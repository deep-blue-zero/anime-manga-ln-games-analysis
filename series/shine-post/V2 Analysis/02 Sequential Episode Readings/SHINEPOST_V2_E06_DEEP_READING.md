## What changes at the mall

Naoki chooses Rio to stand at the center of a new song at the shopping-mall mini-live. Kyoka and Haru have both volunteered. So has Rio, rather grandly, until she understands that the manager means it and that he plans to remove almost all the dancing. She thinks she has cost her more accomplished partners the chance to do what they do best. She runs away, refuses Momiji’s offer to teach her and stops in front of a Hotaru poster. From there the episode goes back to the history of a girl who used to find crowds frightening, won concert tickets for a friend who loved Hotaru, and discovered at that concert that giving people strength was work she wanted to do. It was only after becoming an idol, and comparing her dancing to her teammates’, that she decided the useful thing she could do for them was to keep them from worrying about her. Back in the present she sits on a swing at night, checks the time and heads for the practice room, expecting to find it empty.

Naoki is waiting at the practice room. He recognizes what her show of confidence costs and offers a test she can carry out herself. There is something she can do for a public that her two colleagues cannot do in the same way. He wants her to experience that before he gives it a name. Rio agrees to lead the song. She also goes to Momiji, offers her a pudding from Kantendō and asks for dance lessons if the show goes well. At the mall, even fans fond of Rio expect Haru or Kyoka to center the new number. The three take their places at standing microphones. Rio introduces “Yellow Rose,” a ballad about someone who has kept her weakness from the people she wants to help. There are newcomers who want to know what group she belongs to by the end. The director from the farm shoot, whom Naoki has invited, approves. When the public takes up her name, Rio attempts her customary flourish and bursts into tears. Her two friends are there to receive her. She has done something for this audience that she can finally recognize for herself. The two-thousand-seat Nakano booking still lies ahead. ## What an audience can give back

The most exact reversal in the episode concerns an admission that first sounds like a criticism of other people's leisure. Rio does not understand why anyone goes out to hear music they could hear at home. The crowd makes her uneasy, and the arena seat at the center of Aoi's delight is precisely the place where she feels she might disappear. Her mind changes at the instant the presence of other people makes a perception possible. She can look sideways. What she has felt watching Hotaru is happening, in forms she can recognize, to Aoi and to people whose names she will never know. At the mall there are places from which someone who has barely heard of TiNgS can look down, and after the credits there is just time enough for a performer on that stage to look back up. Her question has become more interesting on its return. There is something a performer can come to know in the company of an audience that even a knowledgeable manager cannot give her in the rehearsal room. The insistence that she learn her strength by experience acquires its full meaning only when we have waited with her to find out how the song has been received. [SPV2-CLM-E06-04, SPV2-CLM-E06-07, SPV2-CLM-E06-11]

That wait makes a difference to the kind of recognition at stake. Miku already liked Rio. People had specifically asked to buy tickets from her before she had this credit. An audience member can call for “Rio-sama,” praise the joke, admire her persistence, or feel personally welcomed by her without being aware of the singing she could bring to the middle of a ballad. The performance gives some of those listeners more precise things to value in somebody they already knew. The newcomers on the upper level have a different experience, the ability to ask who she is after being drawn in by the performance. The director has still another. She has seen a generous, photogenic field report and a piece of professional publicity unusable for the purpose she had in mind. At Naoki’s invitation she encounters the same performer in circumstances that reward a very different kind of candor. Sato does not take back her earlier editorial judgment, and its validity matters. Finding a suitable form for Rio enlarges her possibilities well beyond trying to make every brave failure look like a success. [SPV2-OBS-E06-09, SPV2-OBS-E06-10, SPV2-CLM-E06-09]

Nearly eliminating dance for one number is a particular artistic choice. The stage still needs three people who can coordinate their presence, decide when to come forward in support and move their hands in relation to the same song. The two women at the sides have already done substantial work before the first lyric. Haru tells the people in front that excitement is welcome. Kyoka stakes her practiced public assurance on a new attraction they can discover in the whole group. When they turn the exchange over to Rio, they leave her the time to answer for herself. In a song about having to stop turning one's back on help, being able to occupy center depends on who is standing on either side. Once on that stage, calling to a friend and having the friend answer is part of what giving a public performance consists of. Downstairs, a shopper can respond to the ballad without knowing the history of the pudding or Rio’s request for lessons. Upstairs, the girl who accepted the pudding can hear that same performance with the exchange still in mind. Rio has found a public form for something she first had to ask of one person. [SPV2-CLM-E06-01, SPV2-OBS-E06-08, SPV2-CLM-E06-10]

The episode gives us more information about Rio's capacity for judgment than either generic reassurance or an isolated recording of her singing could supply. She notices exactly how well Haru recovers and Kyoka sustains. She understands why a shopper should take notice of them. At an age when she regarded a schoolbook as one more thing she had failed to manage, she had already found a way to reward a friend's kindness. Her harshest judgments characteristically concern the extent to which her own work counts. There is an observable objection to letting every flattering interpretation win. Asked for a food report she did give Sato material the director felt she must cut. Asked to hold a dance position she has fallen in rooms containing people who did not fall. Those setbacks make her wish for a lesson a good practical decision. They are also the very things the closing credits can show under an expression of friendship. The girl offering to protect others can count the people who rescued her among the people she sings for. She has had a chance to see that they can receive that offer without pretending they never had to help her. [SPV2-CLM-E06-03, SPV2-CLM-E06-05, SPV2-CLM-E06-08]

Two forward-looking judgments need different degrees of belief. ### What the episode changes in the incoming reading

After the street encounter in E05 the principal expected to find out whether Naoki would tell anyone what he had learned and who would take the initiative in helping Rio. The new song turns out to be a proposal he makes in the presence of the whole group. The explanation he owes Rio takes place later, between the two of them, after Momiji has already tried to help. Identifying who initiates each exchange makes the answers more varied than a prediction that a perceptive manager will simply fix a colleague's lack of confidence. The manager has a role to give Rio, the other girls have things to offer in their own right, and an audience can supply the answer to a question she has never before been in a position to ask.
