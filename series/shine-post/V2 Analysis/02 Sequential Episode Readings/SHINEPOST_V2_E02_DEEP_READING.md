# E02 — The audience that makes an idol visible

## What happens

Haru wants a full house at Bright Station. She also wants the person driving her there to stay. In E01 Naoki offered to take the dedicated job if they filled their own theatre. The three set out to sell all one hundred tickets, and Haru later insists on making the show a success and holding him to the job. A ticket lies beside the doughnuts Haru shares with a school friend. The people on the street outside the theatre still have to be persuaded to take one. People on their way to an FFF concert are excited about the performers they already know. Two girls who joined Brightest around the same time as the trio then come by, predict failure and identify problems in all three performers. Three familiar fans stop, greet the performers by name and buy. Beyond that circle, hours of work bring the total to thirty-two. Naoki has spent the time watching where their efforts go. By the time he asks them to get into the car again he has called someone who can give them a different place to stand.

Outside a Yura Yura Sisters concert, their manager Kikuchi gives the newcomers permission to sell and takes an interest in the tickets herself. The singers thank the host group before they approach anyone. People leaving the venue want to know who these costumed strangers are. This time the buyers come to all three, including Rio, who had found it painful to look down the line at the home theatre. The remaining tickets go in about seven and a half minutes. The girls embrace. Naoki goes back to the office to watch footage of their dancing. In the packed theatre the following day he gives three different instructions. Kyoka must carry the group, Rio must stay with it all the way and Haru must show what it means for her to shine. What he has said to Haru in private comes back during the number. The center who used to check on her partners can direct the same attention outward to the people in front of her.

Success comes in stages that different people can witness. An initial group of concertgoers gives the performers mediocre marks. The Yura Sisters recognize how demanding a room Naoki has assembled. During the performance other spectators single someone out. The episode gives weight to their capacity to make that discovery, and to Haru's capacity to respond to being watched. Naoki has already described making this situation possible as the work of a *dedicated* manager. The girls receive no comparable declaration in the closing scene. Haru's final playful assurance belongs to the stage image, and the question of how the three will sustain this performance belongs to the next day of work.

## Two experiments on the same afternoon

The first approach to sales is easily mistaken for a bad lesson in willpower. The trio asks its new manager for his plan, follows the first answer as if there must be another step, then waits through the realization that doing their best *is* the instruction he intends them to hear. What he does in the meantime changes the meaning of the delay. Stand outside their own theatre and some people know where to find them. Expect a procession of uncommitted passersby to have the leisure and interest to stop for a pitch and much of the same effort goes to waste. Both results are visible from Naoki's position to one side of the street. He can see Rio beside her friends and he can also see the relative length of the lines. He does not have to tell the girls that one has been less successful than her neighbors. Several shots make us look at the disparity from her side first. [SPV2-OBS-E02-04]

The returning fans demonstrate what the group already offers. Rin calls Haru Harurun, Miku addresses Rio in the grand style Rio has taught her fans to use, and Una inquires after her own familiar Okyon. The replies are three distinct transactions, among people for whom a nickname is part of what makes stopping worthwhile. Kyoka even has an opportunity to advertise her joke. When Rio says she has had enough and wants a break, Haru turns after her. Kyoka redirects attention to the people still there. This time the cost of putting the immediate job first is visible on both sides. The patrons have waited for a chance to speak to the particular person they came to see. Rio can walk away from the comparison without two sympathetic colleagues following to witness her humiliation. The second outcome is a possible benefit of Kyoka's decision. The priority is all she actually states. Rio's subsequent reappearance with tickets in hand and another direct invitation answers the important question about whether she has abandoned the effort. What she did during every minute away remains unknown. [SPV2-OBS-E02-04, SPV2-OBS-E02-05]

A single sale to someone Haru has approached matters at precisely this point. It keeps the story from treating the old location as incapable of producing a new customer. Naoki changes one part of the experiment without asking the girls to change costume, price or identity. He calls Kiku ahead of the visit. Haru's contribution to this plan can be missed in an account that makes the quick sellout a demonstration of managerial genius. She asks the others to do something before they begin. ## What the fourth key opens

The performance explains its first three conditions by asking us to look from one member to the next. Each has footage to go with the quality Naoki assigns her. Rio works to stay with the number, Kyoka gives the formation a dependable reference and Haru can adjust. A fourth condition requires looking the other way altogether. The critical spectators' ability to discern what they have been shown is indispensable to *this* version of success. Indifference and undiscriminating enthusiasm could hide a particular accomplishment equally well. When Naoki says that people have come who can see through to TiNgS, he gives these people an importance in the event close to that of the performers. There is a reciprocal risk. The idols cannot dictate what an experienced watcher will value and the watcher can always mistake a choice for incompetence, as the first scores and the earlier exchange at the theatre entrance show. [SPV2-OBS-E02-11, SPV2-OBS-E02-12]

Naoki's distinction between watching the other two and watching the audience hardly requires a miraculous change in Haru's physical skill overnight. A girl accustomed to adjusting for others already has a use for her eyes and considerable practice making what she sees count. Changing whom she watches makes something she could already do more visible, to more people. It can also expose the asymmetry her adjustments once helped conceal. A performance built around a steady reference and an unstable counterpart may still place a cost on all three workers. As of E02 nobody has shown how to distribute that cost over a whole season of regular concerts. Objective measures of step accuracy, beat placement and individual vocals would require evidence this independent review does not have. The episode offers a professional explanation and a sequence of pictures through which to test its plausibility. Even if later evidence should revise that explanation, it would remain an important fact that Naoki offered it and that different watchers were able to see different things in the same group. [SPV2-OBS-E02-12]

## Who is speaking to whom

Haru's street pitch often takes the form 来てくれませんか, a negative question about whether someone might do her the favor of coming. A person can accept the invitation or disengage without directly refusing her. The modest reply まあ いいけど leaves distinctly more room for indifference than Rin's happy recognition at the regulars' arrival. Naoki has his own way of economizing on what he asks of a stranger. Speaking to Kiku by phone, he leads up to a small favor with 実はちょっとお願いがありまして. When they meet, her ナオっち puts his formal greetings in a different light. He is asking for something professionally, from someone who remembers which invented sentence endings she used to like. Her current ぞい, her past ざます, the English ticket request and the French farewell make a small history of somebody who enjoys the sound of an entrance and exit. He has enough standing to remark when she speaks ordinarily again. We can read these changes on the page. Their pitches, rhythms and degrees of extravagance in the actual performance await an ear. [SPV2-OBS-E02-04, SPV2-OBS-E02-06, SPV2-OBS-E02-11]

The word 根性論 is a sharp response to Naoki's announcement that the girls should give their all. It names a way of explaining success by sheer determination. When the group discovers what he has arranged, he can put 努力 on both sides of a single formulation about the effort required to keep effort from going to waste. He has earned the repetition. His own work can be quiet enough to conduct over the phone while nobody pays him attention. A second pairing gives that action a larger meaning. 最高の環境を用意する, supplying the best environment, is first presented as a manager's duty and returns as 専属マネージャーの仕事, the *dedicated* manager's work. The addition of one word changes the promise his audience can hear in it. At the office he had used 臨時, provisional. The three girls have no occasion to hear both ends of this comparison for themselves. [SPV2-OBS-E02-04, SPV2-OBS-E02-06, SPV2-OBS-E02-08, SPV2-OBS-E02-11]

Even a very short deictic word can do a surprising amount of work here. In the private advice Naoki says there are people who are watching them, then tells Haru that そっち is where she should look. One needn't give a girl a more complex technical vocabulary in order to ask her to give her attention a different object. Earlier Haru had addressed her two peers as if common agency membership could guarantee harmony. At Bright Station the people available for her to address are strangers, precisely the people who cannot be counted on to like her already. In “TOKYO WATASHI COLLECTION,” a girl lays out her clothes before a first date and stages an imaginary runway show for one. A hundred outfits cannot answer her question, “Which me do you like?” She wants her addressee to take the initiative, keeps something back, then promises her most adorable self yet in the form of a challenge. Getting dressed for a particular person and performing for a paying crowd are very different relationships. Each involves putting a visible version of oneself before someone free to respond unexpectedly. [SPV2-OBS-E02-08, SPV2-OBS-E02-11, SPV2-OBS-E02-12]

Haru's お任せあれ is conspicuous language for giving someone permission to leave a job in her hands. In school, the nearest equivalent was her unwillingness to accept a friend's assessment that *she* was dependable. By the end of the concert she can claim responsibility in front of an audience, and do it in character as Haru-chan. It would go too far to treat the phrase as a diary entry. The recurrence is a way of recognizing her across different settings, including the ones where confidence is part of what she owes the room. [SPV2-OBS-E02-01, SPV2-OBS-E02-09, SPV2-OBS-E02-12]
