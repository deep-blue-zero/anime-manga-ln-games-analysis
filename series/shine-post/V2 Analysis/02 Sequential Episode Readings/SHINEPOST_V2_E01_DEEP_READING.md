# E01 — Why Naoki agrees to start with one hundred

## Causal synopsis

The first concert belongs to Hotaru. A sign identifies the 2018 Budokan finale of her Shining Tour. One girl in the audience grips a pink lightstick and wonders whether she too might become an idol. The episode cuts to a much smaller stage in 2022. Haru Nabatame thanks the fans of TiNgS, promises them more and, once the public exchange is over, wonders if she is suited to the work. Her companions Rio Seibu and Kyoka Tamaki have fans who call them by name. The people who came matter to the three performers. Too few of them come to keep the group going on its present terms.

Yuki, the president of Brightest Production, tells the girls that she is considering dissolving TiNgS. Nine months after forming, they have yet to fill their own hundred-person theater. She offers to reverse her decision if they fill the two-thousand-capacity Nakano Sunplaza at their first-anniversary concert in three months. She also promises them a dedicated manager of exceptional ability. The girls find Naoki Hinase on his way to the agency, welcome him and start describing what they hope to do together. Only then do they discover that Yuki has left the job of persuading him to them. Naoki means to work for the agency. He has specifically ruled out managing idols.

The girls keep after him. Most of what they try is more entertaining than persuasive. Haru eventually tells him the consequences of failure. He asks the group for its highest attendance to date and receives the answer thirty-seven. Even that makes Naoki more inclined to recommend someone else. The opening he can accept is smaller. He agrees to watch their lesson. Their current performance leaves him unconvinced they can meet the challenge, but he asks each girl what kind of idol she wants to become. Haru wants to be a shining signpost for people who love idols, so they can openly say which idols they love. Freedom from embarrassment is one implication of that wish. The answer changes what he is prepared to do. He offers to manage them temporarily, and to make it official if together they fill their own theater.

Their reactions to his decision precede the audience's full explanation. Speaking to Yuki, his older cousin, Naoki says that Haru did not shine when she answered. Yuki names his unusual capacity to see people glow when they lie. For someone with his eyes, the absence of a spectacle can carry conviction. The episode title, 青天国春は《輝かない》, puts that apparent contradiction on screen long before the audience learns its special sense. His own old ambition resembles Haru's. His previous experience has also taught him that pursuing it can hurt other people. The episode ends with a first instruction about facing the regular concert with confidence. The group has secured a working partnership and a nearer target. Both attendance tests still lie ahead.

## Two stages and one spectator

The Budokan sequence makes a specific demand on the viewer's time. It could have established Hotaru's fame with a poster. Instead, it begins in the dark, isolates the light rig and the dated tour sign, brings a bright pink lightstick into the foreground, then lets a solitary figure occupy a spotlight. Hotaru's invitation comes before the first sustained lyric cue. The wide reverse views establish the size of the house. The repeated returns to individual faces establish what a house of that size is made of. At the edges of the stage, dancers have their own postures, looks toward the audience and moments of prominence. On the floor, some spectators wave, some hold their sticks close, and the girl who will ask about becoming an idol watches with her mouth open. Calling this girl an aspiring performer at the first appearance would anticipate the question she has still to formulate. The sequence gives her time to arrive at it. ## Haru’s lucky day

Away from the stage Haru wears glasses and braids. The convenience-store sequence lets her occupy two positions at once. A broadcast describes Hotaru at eighteen, with annual attendance of two million and triple-million single sales. The measurements belong to the announcer’s portrayal of a star. The camera keeps returning to the small object in a girl's hand. The prize stick gives Haru evidence, at the scale of an ordinary day, that something unusually good has happened to her. The broadcast introduces Hotaru’s 前奏曲, credited to her in frame 1148 alongside the arena’s “Sweet Surrender.” The announcement and Hotaru poster give her a much larger example to think with. One piece of good fortune leads her to notice another at the pedestrian crossings. Arriving at the agency, she changes into practice clothes and removes the markers of her school appearance. A message from Kyoka characterizes what awaits as wonderful. Haru signs off in the chat with the same mock-grand instruction to leave things to Haru-chan that she will use at the episode’s end (frame 275). The walk down the corridor supplies enough time to imagine a new costume, a CD debut or television. Even the movement through the hall has acquired the character of a performance before she opens the office door. Calling this mere naivety would discard a practicable part of her make-up. She can make the passage from one piece of work to another enjoyable before anybody promises a reward. [SPV2-OBS-E01-03, 182.95–275.34 s, cues 28–51, sheets 9–12.]

Yuki has been doing a different calculation. Personal fortune cannot alter the number of people who come to see the group. The hinge between Haru’s personal 絶好調, excellent form, and Yuki’s group 絶不調, poor results, turns a joke into an explanation of what group membership entails. Haru tries the entrance and the friendly question again. Yuki gives substantially the same answer. The repetition dramatizes what the girl wishes she could undo. Her recognition that it was no dream makes the others' responses legible as attempts to escape it by different means. Kyoka is ready for the prank reveal. Rio objects that dissolution is unsuitable for someone of her standing. Haru wants a withdrawal and asks the other two to join her. Each response is recognizable again later, when another unwelcome proposition requires a joint response. [SPV2-OBS-E01-04, 275.34–336.69 s, cues 52–76.]

Yuki's distinction between contemplating dissolution and having decided on it opens a narrow space for the girls to act. She has considerable power over the terms of that action. She sets an anniversary deadline, chooses a famous venue, and puts the capacity in the room with them as a number. A group accustomed to thirty or so is being asked for two thousand. They have never filled even the much smaller venue they already occupy. Rio's pride, Haru's momentum and Kyoka's readiness to reason through alternatives cannot make the arithmetic go away. Yuki acknowledges this and introduces a partner. She describes Naoki's ability, mentions his difficult disposition, and gives the girls ample reason to believe he has agreed. She supplies no corresponding evidence of his consent. The resulting misconception is causally productive. It will determine how they approach him and how embarrassing it is when he tells them the truth.

There is more than one defensible assessment of Yuki's conduct at this point. She is extending the life of a struggling act and promising unusually strong professional support. She is also making junior performers carry the emotional work of recruiting a man whose reluctance she anticipates. The later conversation with Naoki establishes that she thought their partnership a reasonable premise for an unreasonable challenge. It does not establish whether she would have carried out the dissolution, or which parts of the threat the girls are meant ultimately to discover. Her description of them as prized protégées is compatible with affection and a willingness to set painful terms. The girls have to make their decisions without the private conversation in which the audience learns how much she values them. [SPV2-OBS-E01-05, 337.65–428.28 and 1225.41–1245.31 s.]

## The audience for a lie

## How to give someone a reason he can accept

Kyoka's objection when the three girls regroup is sensible. Even if Yuki thinks highly of him, should the group really entrust itself to a man who does not want the job? Haru has a reason for trying again. The president does not undertake this kind of thing without thinking; perhaps the refusal is part of what they must get past to reach Nakano. Kyoka draws a different conclusion from a different premise. Their fates are tied up with each other's, so she will go along. She does not first have to agree with Haru about Yuki's wisdom. The initially similar behavior of the two girls therefore conceals an important difference in the people on whom each is prepared to rely. Haru trusts a president's arrangement. Kyoka trusts the people she would have to leave if she walked away.

Three faces appearing in the doorway turn perseverance into a problem of manners as well as strategy. Staring at a man at his desk does not change his answer. Rio offers her pudding if he will become their manager. It costs her enough for the loss of her snack to become visible, so Naoki declines to make her part with it. Her delight at getting to keep it is a particularly generous little joke at her expense. She meant the offer and she still wants her pudding. Kyoka begins to produce another treasured joke, only to find the man already knows what to decline. Haru tries a pierced coin on a thread and an inducement to feel like becoming a manager. His quick refusal punctures the performance. Taken together these attempts give him occasions to refuse different things without rejecting the people trying them. He can be kind to Rio, recognize Kyoka's particular form of humor, and take Haru seriously enough to tell her exactly what work he will do. None of these modest successes is enough to secure the job. [SPV2-OBS-E01-13, 857.84–953.72 s, cues 250–286.]

Only then does Naoki hear what is at stake. His startled question about dissolution is important evidence against the assumption that Yuki briefed both sides. He asks for their highest attendance and learns it was thirty-seven. The earlier estimate of roughly thirty was a statement about their usual draw. The two figures make sense together. Being told the size of the risk does not make him surrender to sympathy. It supplies a reason in his own terms to suggest somebody else. Haru changes the question. Filling Nakano with him is part of what she has decided she wants. She does not prove that another manager would be less competent. Rio volunteers that she wants him too, and Kyoka characterizes the group as poor at knowing when to give up. He refuses once more. Only then does Haru point out, with considerable acuity and some presumption, that やらない, a refusal to do it, leaves open whether he wants to. Her question plays on やりたくない, lacking the desire. The spectator knows a third expression from his memory, やっちゃいけない, feeling he must not do it. Haru has heard the refusal and takes a risk in distinguishing it from reluctance. She has not heard the prohibition he places on himself. The shared choice is real even though the three did not reach it by the same route. [SPV2-OBS-E01-14, 959.65–1018.54 s, cues 288–311.]

Haru finally proposes an action that leaves him free to refuse. Watching the lesson does not commit him to managing its performers. He repeats the limit, and she accepts it while asking him to look at them properly. The maxim about doing everything one can returns from the flashback. There it described the choice that Naoki fears cost people too much. Here it permits a smaller investment with a clear stopping point. His consent to watch shows the limit of reading him as already convinced the first moment he met Haru. In practical terms she has negotiated a way for him to learn something. She has no guarantee that the new knowledge will help her. [SPV2-OBS-E01-15, 1020.54–1049.78 s, cues 312–324.]

## Working together under a condition

Naoki answers with the number one hundred. Yuki has made two thousand the measure of the group's survival. Naoki chooses an immediate target with a known venue, a known capacity and a known shortfall. His formulation includes himself in the attempt. If the regular live sells out, he will take the job officially. No one promises that filling the small house will itself save the group. The decision substitutes a sequence of tasks for a demand to picture a miraculous leap. It simultaneously exposes Naoki to judgment. In his later account to Yuki, a man who began by rejecting these girls has to demonstrate what he can contribute and give them grounds to accept him. A test of the people onstage is also a test of whether they have reason to trust the person who would stand behind them. [SPV2-OBS-E01-19, 1168.36–1212.69 and 1245.31–1259.03 s.]

Without the earlier sequence there would be no reason why the sincerity of this particular answer should matter to him. The ability and the biography work together. A more cynical reading, in which Yuki maneuvered her cousin back into familiar work, explains the engineered meeting but cannot on its own explain the limited commitment he chose to make.
