# Project SEKAI — Nightcord at 25:00 Main Story Phase 04 Deep Reading

## Scope and analytical responsibility

**Phase:** P04 — Origins, recognition, and the decision to intervene  
**Source:** `PJSK:main:nightcode_01_11:01` through `PJSK:main:nightcode_01_15:01`  
**Master episodes:** 12–16

P04 begins where P03 ended: Kanade has driven herself into a psychological and creative collapse after interpreting Mafuyu's statement that K's music was “not enough” as proof that she failed to save her. The phase reconstructs the origin of Kanade's salvation imperative, establishes the developmental history of Mafuyu's identity erosion, and then returns to the present to show how Kanade, Mizuki, and Ena respond once Mafuyu's disappearance threat becomes credible and immediate.

The phase has five linked responsibilities:

1. reconstruct the actual causal history surrounding Kanade's father and distinguish that history from Kanade's guilt-based interpretation of it;
2. determine what Miku's intervention changes—and does not change—about Kanade's binary model of “saving” versus “failing”;
3. reconstruct Mafuyu's earlier selfhood closely enough to explain how a girl with genuine preferences and prosocial aspirations becomes unable to identify what she likes or wants;
4. track how Mizuki and Ena revise their understanding of Mafuyu without falsely granting them access to the audience's full autobiographical evidence;
5. explain why the phase ends not with consensus, but with three different forms of relational response: Kanade's urgent attempt to save, Mizuki's desire to tell Mafuyu that her disappearance would be lonely, and Ena's refusal to participate directly while still showing concern for the others.

P04 is therefore the bridge between **diagnosis and action**. P01–P03 establish that all four members contain forms of endangered selfhood. P04 asks what they do once that recognition becomes actionable.

## Core interpretive claim

P04 reveals that both Kanade and Mafuyu have transformed other people's reactions into substitutes for self-authored value, but they have done so in different ways.

Kanade begins with an authentic childhood desire to make music that makes people happy. Her father's professional collapse does not create this value. Instead, Kanade misreads a multicausal tragedy as a personal moral failure and turns the earlier value into an absolute obligation: if her music once contributed to her father's suffering, she must continue composing until she can make music capable of saving anyone. Her identity becomes conditional on successful rescue.

Mafuyu likewise begins with accessible desires. She is pleased by praise, wants to help sick people as a nurse, begins to say she wants to go to the arcade, and remembers once having things she liked. Her selfhood is not absent from birth. It is gradually displaced. Parents, teachers, and peers repeatedly reward the socially exemplary Mafuyu while gently or casually overriding her own stated preferences. Mafuyu responds by treating other people's happiness and approval as evidence that a choice must be correct. Eventually even sensory and emotional experience becomes externally referenced: if her mother says the cake is delicious, it must be delicious, despite Mafuyu being unable to taste it as such.

The two therefore share a dangerous substitution:

- **Kanade:** “If I can save someone, then my music—and therefore I—has moral legitimacy.”
- **Mafuyu:** “If everyone approves and is happy, then the path—and therefore the self performing it—must be correct.”

P04 begins to loosen both substitutions without resolving either one. Miku does not tell Kanade that she has already saved Mafuyu. She tells Kanade that her music *reached* Mafuyu, that it alone existed inside the Empty SEKAI, and that Mafuyu's deeper wish appears to be to find herself rather than simply vanish. This introduces a less totalizing criterion: partial contact can matter even when rescue is incomplete. Kanade initially converts that possibility back into the language of compulsory rescue, but by episode 15 she can at least name her own project as ego, recognize that her song could hurt Mafuyu, and choose action without pretending certainty.

Mafuyu's autobiographical episode similarly shows that “finding herself” is not the creation of an entirely new person. It is the recovery or reconstruction of access to preferences, feelings, and desires that once existed but became subordinated to external definition.

The phase's ethical center is therefore not omniscient rescue. It is **imperfect people choosing to reach toward one another while recognizing that they cannot fully know or control the other's inner life**. Mizuki models this most cleanly: they explicitly say that only Mafuyu can know Mafuyu, that their intervention may accomplish nothing, but that they are still entitled to tell Mafuyu one fact that belongs to Mizuki—the prospect of Mafuyu disappearing feels lonely.

## I. Narrative and causal progression

### Episode 11 — Kanade's father: talent, professional displacement, and the manufacture of guilt

`幸せになる音楽を` supplies the causal history that P03 deliberately left unresolved.

Two years earlier, Kanade notices that her father has become withdrawn, works late, and appears low in energy. Her response is already recognizable as Kanade: rather than interrogating him, she considers making a song that might cheer him. This is important because the prosocial orientation predates the catastrophic event. The wish to affect another person's emotional state through music is not created by guilt.

The first destabilizing event is professional. Kanade's father reports that a composition passed a competition, but the portion singled out for praise is a phrase Kanade created. The client intends to use that part in a commercial. He thanks Kanade and suggests the success may generate additional work.

The outward story is success. The father's reaction makes clear that the meaning is more complicated.

As more work arrives, clients repeatedly request music resembling the commercial track. The problem is that the admired quality is precisely the quality he associates with Kanade's contribution. He tries to modernize his sound, rejects his own arrangements, works obsessively, and is repeatedly told—implicitly and explicitly—that his established musical language is outdated.

Kanade then gives him a song she made because she thinks he needs encouragement. He is stunned by it. His response contains love, admiration, professional recognition, and pain at once. He tells Kanade she has extraordinary talent, that music seems to love her, and that she should continue creating her own music because people will accept it, enjoy it, and need it.

Nothing in the scene supports the proposition that he wants Kanade to stop creating or that he consciously blames her for his condition. On the contrary, his explicit instruction is to continue.

Soon afterward he collapses into a state of psychogenic memory confusion. The physician explains that the condition is associated with severe psychological stress and may or may not resolve completely.

Kanade finds her father's diary and uses it to construct the explanation that will govern her life.

The diary reveals several interacting pressures:

- clients call his sound old-fashioned;
- he tries to retrain himself technically and aesthetically;
- Kanade's phrase succeeds where his own musical language is rejected;
- new clients request the sound associated with Kanade's contribution;
- he feels that the successful piece cannot honestly be called his own;
- he experiences Kanade's talent as something he cannot reproduce by effort or theory;
- he believes her music can touch people living in the present in a way his cannot;
- he nevertheless feels responsible for producing commercially viable music to support himself and Kanade;
- he questions whether music created under that pressure can still fulfill his ideal of making someone happy;
- he wonders whether Kanade, rather than he, may be capable of realizing that ideal.

This is a multicausal professional and identity crisis. Kanade, however, does not preserve its complexity. She compresses it into personal responsibility: `わたしが……お父さんを苦しめてた……？`

This is one of the foundational cognitive moves in her reconstruction model. Under emotionally catastrophic uncertainty, Kanade prefers an explanation in which she is responsible over an explanation in which causality is distributed, ambiguous, and partly uncontrollable.

The attraction of the guilt explanation is paradoxical. It is devastating, but it also restores agency. If Kanade's music caused suffering, then different music might prevent suffering. If the catastrophe was instead produced by labor conditions, professional insecurity, comparison, personal vulnerability, financial pressure, and circumstances beyond Kanade's control, there is no single reparative action available.

Kanade first turns against the talent itself. She wishes she could not make music. She apologizes to her father and reaches an explicit disappearance state: she thinks that she wants to vanish and wonders whether simply not eating might allow her to disappear.

The mother's music box interrupts that trajectory. It contains one of her father's gentle melodies, a piece both Kanade and her mother loved. Kanade remembers his instruction that she must continue making *her* music.

But she does not recover his words neutrally. She converts them into a penance structure.

Her conclusion is not merely “my father wanted me to keep composing.” It is:

- the song she made for him was not a song that could make someone happy;
- therefore his collapse is evidence of her failure;
- therefore she must learn to create music that can make someone happy;
- ultimately she must create music capable of saving *anyone*;
- she must continue until that obligation is fulfilled.

The original value—making people happy through music—becomes an absolute command.

This **STRENGTHENS** P03's distinction between Kanade's authentic prosocial orientation and her trauma-shaped compulsion. The catastrophe does not invent the value. It destroys the value's ordinary limits.

### Episode 12 — From “saving” to “reaching,” and Kanade's difficulty accepting partial efficacy

`ただひとつの歌` returns to the present immediately after P03's collapse.

Kanade tells her absent father that she tried. She has worked, but her music saved nobody. In her current causal model, the father and Mafuyu are joined as two proofs of the same proposition: Kanade's music causes suffering or fails to prevent it.

She asks whether she still has to continue creating.

Mafuyu's accusation—Kanade wants to disappear more than anyone—returns. P03 had not yet supplied an explicit present-tense disappearance statement from Kanade. P04 changes that evidentiary state. Kanade enters the Empty SEKAI not to search for Mafuyu but to withdraw. She lies down, considers closing her eyes and remaining there, and wonders whether doing so would make things easier.

Together with episode 11's explicit adolescent `もう……消えたいな`, this materially strengthens Mafuyu's perception of Kanade. Kanade's self-erasing work behavior is not the only relevant evidence; under collapse she has also experienced direct desire for disappearance or release.

The intervention comes through music.

Kanade hears Miku singing an old song Kanade created. Miku explains that the song existed in the Empty SEKAI from the beginning. The world generated from Mafuyu's feelings is otherwise empty and white, yet this one composition was present.

The fact destabilizes Kanade's binary model. Kanade has been using:

> saved = meaningful  
> not saved = failure / meaningless

Miku offers a third category:

> **reached**

The song may not have been enough to resolve Mafuyu's crisis, but it reached somewhere that almost nothing else could reach.

Kanade initially rejects the distinction. If Mafuyu was not saved, then reaching her “had no meaning.” This is outcome absolutism: partial efficacy is treated as zero efficacy because it did not produce total rescue.

Miku's answer is relational rather than triumphant. Miku proposes that Kanade's music reached Mafuyu precisely because Kanade contains feelings similar enough to Mafuyu's pain to make contact possible. Miku does not say the two are psychologically identical. Nor does she claim that Kanade can certainly save Mafuyu.

Instead, Miku makes two important claims:

1. Mafuyu has continued creating while suffering because she is trying to find her real feelings and self.
2. Mafuyu's deepest wish is therefore not reducible to “I want to disappear.”

Miku asks Kanade to *find* Mafuyu. When Kanade asks whether her music can save her, Miku explicitly answers that she does not know. The strongest claim is narrower: if something can help find Mafuyu, Kanade's music may be the only thing capable of doing it.

This distinction should remain explicit in later analysis.

**Miku's formulation:** possibility, reaching, finding, uncertainty.  
**Kanade's reformulation:** obligation, rescue, “this time I must save her properly.”

Kanade is renewed, but she is not psychologically cured. She takes a less totalizing piece of evidence and incorporatesates it into the existing salvation imperative.

The episode closes on Mafuyu reading comments about OWN. The audience praises the work, speculates about the creator, and calls OWN a genius. Mafuyu dismisses the responses as useless because none answer the question she is asking: where is the “real me”?

This is another reason popularity or praise cannot simply restore her. External recognition is precisely the medium that has become unreliable as a guide to selfhood.

### Episode 13 — Mafuyu before disappearance: how a self becomes externally authored

`どこにもいない“私”` is the main story's clearest developmental account of Mafuyu's identity erosion.

It begins by establishing something later crisis readings can easily forget: young Mafuyu is not affectless. She proudly shows her parents a perfect test score, enjoys their praise, and resolves to work harder because the positive response makes her happy.

The praise itself is not framed as sinister. The danger lies in the learning structure that accumulates around it.

Mafuyu learns that achievement produces affection, approval, and the identity of `いい子`. She is good at meeting expectations, and people respond warmly when she does. This creates a feedback loop in which external satisfaction becomes increasingly useful as a proxy for internal decision-making.

The middle-school career conversation demonstrates the process.

Asked what she wants to become, Mafuyu gives a clear answer: **a nurse**, because she wants to help people who are suffering from illness.

This matters enormously for reconstruction. The desire to help others is not shown here as something supplied by her mother. The specific occupational preference is Mafuyu's own. There is an accessible prosocial self before the later collapse.

Her mother does not prohibit nursing. She praises it, then suggests a “better” way to satisfy the same stated value: if Mafuyu wants to help sick people, why not become a doctor? Her father immediately validates the suggestion by pointing to Mafuyu's academic ability. Her mother imagines Mafuyu treating the parents someday. Her father promises educational support. Both say they will support her.

The interaction is caring in surface form and autonomy-eroding in effect.

Mafuyu begins to object—`でも私……`—but the objection never becomes a defended preference. Surrounded by a more prestigious, rationalized, warmly supported alternative, she yields. Alone, she tells herself that if her parents support this route, it must be better and will make them happy.

The important mechanism is not crude obedience under threat. It is **substitution of others' approval for internally felt preference**.

The arcade scene broadens the mechanism beyond the family. Classmates invite Mafuyu out. She begins to say that she wants to go. Another classmate interrupts with the assumption that an exemplary student and high-performing club member obviously has no time for something like that. The invitation is withdrawn apologetically.

Nobody orders Mafuyu to stay home. The social role simply speaks before she can finish speaking for herself.

Mafuyu accepts the role and asks to be invited another time.

The doctor aspiration then becomes publicly reinforced. A teacher praises it. Friends call it perfectly suited to Mafuyu because she is kind and takes care of others. Mafuyu observes that everyone is smiling and reasons that if her parents, teacher, and friends are happy, then the path must have been correct.

The feedback loop is now distributed across the social world. This **STRENGTHENS** the P02 conclusion that Mafuyu's family expectations matter, while **REVISING** any overly mother-centric account. Her mother is a major source of normative pressure, but the “good Mafuyu” identity is co-produced by family, school, peers, praise, assumptions, and Mafuyu's own learned method for deciding what counts as right.

The sensory scenes show the cost.

Mafuyu eats a cake her mother says should be delicious but finds that it barely has taste. Rather than treating her own sensory experience as authoritative, she reasons that the cake is probably delicious because her mother said so. She then notices that not only cake but restaurant food and her mother's cooking have recently lost taste.

She tries to solve the problem by doing something she likes and discovers that she cannot remember what she likes.

She listens to a song friends described as bright and fun. She can understand the category cognitively but cannot access the feeling: it is probably a fun song, but she cannot tell.

The sequence maps a progressive collapse of first-person authority:

- What career do I want? → the externally endorsed option becomes “my” dream.
- Do I want to go out? → the role others assign to me answers before I do.
- Is this food delicious? → another person's judgment substitutes for sensation.
- What do I like? → the answer cannot be retrieved.
- Is this music fun? → the social label remains, but the internal feeling does not.
- Where am I? → the self that should integrate preference, sensation, and desire can no longer be located.

This is why “Mafuyu has no self” is too blunt for reconstruction. The episode proves that she once had self-authored preferences and affective access. The more accurate formulation is that her access to internally owned preference and feeling has been progressively attenuated, overridden, or made unreliable.

In the present, Mafuyu says she searched through music again and again but never found herself. She tells Miku that she has had enough.

This makes her creative output structurally different from Kanade's. Kanade composes to save an other and thereby justify her own continued identity. Mafuyu composes to locate a self from which value could be generated at all.

### Episode 14 — Mizuki's inference, Ena's resentment, and the smallest sufficient reason to care

`寂しいなぁ、って` returns to the Nightcord call.

Mizuki hopes the session can be enjoyable after the previous conflict. Kanade is absent. Ena has messaged her but received no response. Mizuki worries that Kanade may finally have collapsed and remarks that one person cannot absorb Snow's entire workload.

Ena remains wounded. She says Snow does not need to be in Nightcord because someone capable of producing music like OWN can succeed alone.

The statement is not neutral organizational analysis. It continues the recognition injury established in P03: if Mafuyu can independently produce something so extraordinary, Ena can protect herself by redefining the relationship as unnecessary.

Mizuki notices that Ena is not acting normally and privately admits that Mizuki is not doing especially well either. Mafuyu's accusation returns.

Mizuki then chooses disclosure.

They tell Ena about overhearing Mafuyu and her mother. The account is significant because Mizuki is working with incomplete evidence but reasons remarkably well from it. They describe Mafuyu's mother as someone who naturally imposes her own values, note that Mafuyu outwardly acted as if it did not bother her, and infer from the Empty SEKAI crisis that this apparent indifference may have been false.

Mizuki first proposes that Mafuyu forced herself to be a “good girl” for her mother, then immediately broadens the hypothesis: perhaps it was not only for her mother. Perhaps Mafuyu kept changing herself to fit everyone around her until she became the universally admired, non-troublesome person others expected, and eventually lost track of everything else.

The audience knows from episode 13 that this is directionally correct. Mizuki does not know the career conversation, arcade interruption, teacher praise, taste loss, or Mafuyu's inability to identify favorite things. The distinction matters epistemically. Mizuki is perceptive, not omniscient.

Mizuki explains the inference through structural analogy. They are not a “good child” in Mafuyu's sense, but they know how painful an atmosphere of required conformity can be.

This **STRENGTHENS** P03's “inverse autonomy” model:

- Mafuyu loses access to self through habitual alignment with expectations.
- Mizuki knows the self they want to preserve and suffers because others pressure them to surrender or justify it.

Ena is surprised because Mizuki usually appears free and happy. Mizuki's reply is also reconstruction-relevant: life is better if one enjoys it; remaining negative forever is not enjoyable. Ena admits that she wants to live happily too.

Mizuki then articulates the phase's least grandiose and perhaps most autonomy-respecting reason for intervention.

If Mafuyu is suffering, reached her limit, and disappears because of it, Mizuki says that would feel **lonely**. Mizuki wants to talk to Snow one more time.

The wording is deliberately modest. Mizuki does not claim to know how to save Mafuyu. They do not say Mafuyu owes Nightcord survival. They do not claim their pain is identical. They identify a fact about their own relational state: Mafuyu's absence would matter to them.

This becomes important in episode 15.

Before they can act, OWN posts multiple new songs in rapid succession. Ena and Mizuki immediately hear a qualitative escalation. The songs feel more disturbing than Mafuyu's previous work. Remembering Mafuyu's statement that she will disappear if she cannot find herself, Ena asks whether she is serious.

Creative output therefore functions as behavioral evidence. Nightcord knows Mafuyu through music well enough to recognize a change in risk state from the work itself.

Kanade returns at that moment.

### Episode 15 — Intervention without consensus

`消えてしまう前に` converts P04's recognition into decision.

Kanade tells Ena and Mizuki that she went to the Empty SEKAI and has decided to make a song for Snow because she wants to save her.

Mizuki learns Miku's interpretation that Mafuyu does not truly want disappearance as an end in itself. Mizuki immediately asks Kanade to take them along.

The reasoning is unusually careful. Mizuki says that going may accomplish nothing and that only Mafuyu can know Mafuyu. This is explicit epistemic humility. Yet Mizuki still believes there is something legitimate to communicate: their own feeling that Mafuyu's disappearance would be lonely.

This is a useful reconstruction template for Mizuki in high-stakes interpersonal situations. When uncertainty is recognized, Mizuki may avoid universal claims about the other person and instead offer a bounded first-person statement: *I cannot know you completely, but this is what your presence or absence means to me.*

Ena refuses.

Her stated reason is that everyone suffers; Snow is not uniquely entitled to intervention. The Empty SEKAI makes Mafuyu's situation unusual, but Ena does not want to enter it for her.

The refusal should not be prematurely reduced to either cruelty or hidden benevolence. Both resentment and a genuine fairness claim are present in the language available here. Mafuyu has hurt Ena, and Ena is not ready to convert recognition of Mafuyu's suffering into direct caretaking.

The group response is important. Mizuki accepts the refusal. Kanade thanks Ena for listening. No one tries to force unanimity.

This makes the decision to intervene itself consistent with the emerging autonomy theme. The others intend to confront Mafuyu's disappearance, but they do not demand that Ena perform solidarity she does not currently choose.

Kanade then begins composing at extraordinary speed because she believes time is running out.

Crucially, she now pauses to consider the possibility that she is repeating the mistake she believes she made with her father. A song intended to help can become pressure. A song made for Mafuyu could make Mafuyu worse.

This is a meaningful increase in moral and psychological reflexivity compared with the younger Kanade of episode 11.

Kanade names the motive directly: her music is made from **ego**—the desire that *her* music make someone happy. She acknowledges that such a song may have no inherent power to save anyone.

This does not dissolve the salvation imperative. She still wants, specifically, to save Mafuyu with her own song. But the action is no longer represented to herself as pure altruism backed by certainty.

Kanade now knows at least four things simultaneously:

1. she wants to save Mafuyu;
2. that desire is partly about Kanade herself;
3. intervention could cause harm;
4. success is not guaranteed.

She acts anyway because Miku's evidence gives her reason to believe the song may be capable of reaching Mafuyu.

This is more mature than her earlier binary, even if still psychologically burdened.

Mizuki hears the finished song and says it will reach Mafuyu.

Ena, who refused the intervention, has stayed awake and working. She asks to hear the song. Her response returns to a motif established earlier in the main story: K's music is warm.

Then Ena tells them to go and asks them to come back safely.

This is a subtle but important relationship state. Ena's refusal remains real. She does not suddenly forgive Mafuyu or join the rescue. Yet the refusal does not equal indifference. She supports Kanade and Mizuki's action at the boundary she can presently tolerate and expresses concern for their return.

P04 therefore ends without emotional homogenization:

- Kanade acts from a reflexive but still compulsive rescue mission.
- Mizuki acts from relational attachment and epistemic humility.
- Ena refuses direct involvement while maintaining care for her teammates.
- Mafuyu remains absent from the decision-making conversation and is escalating toward crisis.

That asymmetry is exactly what P05 must confront.

## II. Kanade's salvation architecture after P04

P04 permits a more precise model of Kanade than any previous phase.

### 1. The prosocial value is authentic

Kanade's desire to make music that affects people positively exists before her father's collapse. She responds to his low mood by wanting to create something for him. Earlier material likewise places happiness-through-music within her childhood understanding of what music can do.

Therefore:

> **Do not model Kanade's care as merely guilt wearing altruistic language.**

The guilt colonizes an existing value; it does not fabricate it.

### 2. Trauma converts preference into obligation

Before the collapse, the structure is:

> “I want my music to make someone happy.”

After Kanade's interpretation of the diary, it becomes:

> “I must make music capable of saving anyone, and I must continue until I can.”

This is a transition from aspiration to compulsory moral identity.

### 3. Kanade prefers over-responsibility to uncontrollability

The actual causal chain around her father is distributed. Kanade simplifies it toward personal guilt.

This creates a recurring prediction:

> When someone she cares about deteriorates after interacting with her work, Kanade is unusually likely to ask what *she failed to do* or what *she must do more of*, even when causality is uncertain.

### 4. Failure first intensifies work

P03 established the immediate pattern:

> guilt → increased workload → self-neglect → rejection of interference → collapse.

P04 explains why. Work is not merely avoidance; it is penance and attempted causal repair.

### 5. Collapse can produce direct disappearance desire

P04 revises the earlier evidentiary picture. Both adolescent Kanade and present Kanade reach states in which disappearance or passive withdrawal from continued existence becomes thinkable.

This should remain state-conditioned. It is not an ordinary baseline trait, but it is a real high-stakes vulnerability.

### 6. Partial efficacy is psychologically difficult for Kanade to accept

Miku says Kanade's song reached Mafuyu.

Kanade's first answer is that this has no meaning if Mafuyu was not saved.

This reveals a dangerous perfectionism about moral outcomes: reaching, helping somewhat, or reducing suffering can feel like failure if the person remains fundamentally unsaved.

### 7. Kanade can become reflexive about intervention

By episode 15 she recognizes:

- the ego inside altruism;
- the possibility of unintended harm;
- the absence of certainty.

She does not stop acting, but her action becomes ethically more self-aware.

This is important for reconstruction outside the original story. Kanade should not be simulated as someone incapable of questioning her own rescue impulse. She can question it deeply. The harder problem is that self-critique does not necessarily release her from the obligation.

## III. Mafuyu's identity erosion: a developmental model

P04 should replace several tempting but inaccurate shorthand descriptions.

### “Mafuyu never knew what she wanted” — REJECT as a historical absolute

Episode 13 shows clear preferences:

- she wants to become a nurse;
- she begins to say she wants to join classmates at the arcade;
- she remembers that she once had favorite things;
- praise and achievement can produce genuine happiness in childhood.

The later inability to answer desire questions is therefore developmental.

### “Mafuyu's mother simply commands her” — REVISE

The mother's influence is central but usually works through caring rationalization, praise, future planning, and superior alternatives rather than direct threat.

This makes the pressure harder to resist because it can be interpreted as love and good judgment.

### “The family alone creates the good-girl self” — DOWNGRADE

Episode 13 distributes reinforcement across:

- mother;
- father;
- teachers;
- classmates;
- friends;
- achievement systems;
- Mafuyu's own learned tendency to read others' happiness as evidence of correctness.

The family is foundational, not exhaustive.

### The role becomes anticipatory

Eventually other people do not even need to give explicit instructions. Classmates assume Mafuyu cannot want to go to the arcade because the exemplary student role already answers for her.

Mafuyu then conforms to the expectation without defending the interrupted preference.

The social identity has become self-maintaining.

### First-person authority weakens across domains

The narrative deliberately extends beyond career decisions.

Mafuyu increasingly cannot rely on:

- preference;
- appetite;
- taste;
- enjoyment;
- aesthetic affect;
- desire.

The cake scene is especially important. Another person's judgment of taste becomes more authoritative than Mafuyu's actual sensation.

This is the endpoint of the same structure seen in the career conversation.

### Prosociality may contain an authentic substrate

Mafuyu's original nursing aspiration is framed as wanting to help sick people.

Later analysis should therefore avoid assuming every act of helpfulness is only compliance with a “good girl” mask. Some prosocial motive may be genuinely hers even if the social role later overdevelops and instrumentalizes it.

This is an important reconstruction constraint.

## IV. Three different models of intervention

P04 gives Kanade, Mizuki, and Ena different positions rather than turning Nightcord into a unanimous rescue collective.

### Kanade — “I must save her”

Kanade experiences Mafuyu's crisis through obligation.

Strengths:

- enormous persistence;
- willingness to act under uncertainty;
- serious attention to the risk of harm;
- ability to create specifically for another person's emotional state.

Risks:

- over-responsibility;
- self-neglect;
- treating incomplete rescue as total failure;
- making another person's survival part of the justification for Kanade's own identity.

### Mizuki — “I can't know her completely, but I can tell her what I feel”

Mizuki's intervention is relational rather than salvific.

Strengths:

- epistemic humility;
- structural empathy;
- respect for the other's unknowability;
- ability to frame attachment without claiming ownership.

Risk:

- selective disclosure may keep Mizuki from exposing how much the relationship actually matters;
- playfulness can conceal seriousness from people who do not read it well.

### Ena — “I am not required to rescue someone because she suffers”

Ena refuses direct intervention.

Strengths:

- preservation of personal boundary;
- resistance to the idea that another person's suffering automatically creates unlimited obligation;
- honesty about unresolved anger.

Risks:

- resentment can make legitimate boundaries indistinguishable from defensive withdrawal;
- artistic humiliation may cause her to devalue a relationship she still cares about.

The group allows all three positions to exist.

That is itself significant. Nightcord's emerging relational ethic is not based on forced unanimity.

## V. Relationship-state changes

### Kanade ↔ Mafuyu

**Before P04:** Kanade experiences Mafuyu primarily as someone she failed to save; Mafuyu experiences Kanade's music as something that once partially reached her but was insufficient to locate herself.

**P04 transition:** Kanade learns that her music had a unique place in Mafuyu's Empty SEKAI. She therefore regains belief in possible contact. However, she continues to translate contact into a duty to save.

**End state:** Kanade is actively moving toward Mafuyu with a purpose-built song, aware of both ego and possible harm.

### Mizuki ↔ Mafuyu

**Before P04:** Mizuki suspects Mafuyu's claim not to care about social pressure was false.

**P04 transition:** Mizuki constructs a broader model of Mafuyu's “good girl” adaptation using the overheard mother interaction and Empty-SEKAI behavior. The audience can verify that much of the structure is directionally accurate.

**End state:** Mizuki wants renewed contact because Mafuyu's disappearance would produce loneliness. The relationship has become explicitly valued.

### Ena ↔ Mafuyu

**Before P04:** Ena interprets the OWN revelation through humiliation and artistic inferiority.

**P04 transition:** Ena hears Mizuki's account, recognizes the escalating danger in Mafuyu's new songs, and asks whether Mafuyu is serious. This demonstrates that resentment has not erased attention.

**End state:** Ena still refuses direct contact or rescue. The rupture is unresolved.

### Kanade ↔ Mizuki

Mizuki moves from monitoring Kanade's collapse to becoming Kanade's chosen companion in the intervention. Kanade accepts Mizuki's reason without demanding a matching salvation ideology.

### Kanade ↔ Ena

Ena refuses the mission, but Kanade does not punish or pressure her. Kanade thanks her for listening. Ena later listens to the finished song, recognizes its warmth, and asks Kanade to return safely.

The relationship retains care despite disagreement.

### Ena ↔ Mizuki

Mizuki shares a more serious piece of their own understanding and explains their analogy to Mafuyu. Ena learns that Mizuki's apparent freedom and cheerfulness do not mean freedom from normative pressure. This slightly deepens Ena's model of Mizuki.

## VI. Epistemic-state ledger

P04 contains unusually important information asymmetries.

### Audience knows

- the full diary-based history of Kanade's father crisis;
- Kanade's adolescent disappearance state;
- Kanade's present withdrawal into the Empty SEKAI;
- Mafuyu's childhood and middle-school progression from self-authored preferences to externally referenced identity;
- Mafuyu's taste and enjoyment flattening;
- Miku's interpretation that Mafuyu's deeper wish is to find herself rather than simply disappear.

### Kanade knows

- her own history and guilt interpretation;
- that an old Kanade song existed in Mafuyu's Empty SEKAI from the beginning;
- Miku's claim that the song reached Mafuyu;
- Miku's belief that Mafuyu is fundamentally searching for her true feelings;
- that Miku cannot guarantee rescue;
- the risk that a song intended to help can harm.

Kanade **does not** receive Mafuyu's episode-13 autobiographical history in this phase.

### Mafuyu knows

- her own developmental memories;
- her failed attempt to find herself through music;
- the external response to OWN;
- her own declared contingency that if she cannot find herself she may disappear.

Mafuyu does **not** know Kanade's father history or the reasoning that produced Kanade's salvation imperative.

### Mizuki knows

- the overheard interaction between Mafuyu and her mother;
- Mafuyu's Empty-SEKAI crisis behavior;
- Mizuki's own experience of conformity pressure;
- Miku's later claim, relayed through Kanade, that Mafuyu does not fundamentally want disappearance;
- that Mafuyu's latest music appears more alarming than before.

Mizuki's broader model of Mafuyu is inference, albeit a strong one.

### Ena knows

- Mizuki's account of the mother interaction and inference;
- Mafuyu's OWN identity and extraordinary ability;
- the threatening escalation in new OWN material;
- Kanade's decision to make a rescue-oriented song.

Ena does not know the full autobiographical or Kanade-family history.

This asymmetry must constrain P05 interpretation and later simulations. The characters cannot reason from evidence that only the audience possesses.

## VII. Behavioral and speech reconstruction extraction

### Kanade

**Under ordinary concern for another person:**
- notices changes in mood or functioning;
- tends to respond by doing or making something rather than demanding disclosure;
- care often becomes practical production.

**Under perceived rescue failure:**
- internalizes responsibility rapidly;
- increases work rather than initially withdrawing;
- may absorb other people's responsibilities;
- deprioritizes food, rest, and bodily state;
- speech becomes shorter and more resistant to interruption;
- can interpret concern as obstruction when mission urgency is high.

**At collapse:**
- can move from overfunctioning into direct disappearance/withdrawal ideation;
- may seek a quiet, low-stimulation place rather than dramatic confrontation.

**When given a plausible path forward:**
- reactivates quickly;
- commits decisively;
- can work at extraordinary speed;
- does not require certainty if she believes there is a meaningful chance of reaching the person.

**New P04 constraint:**
- Kanade is capable of naming ego, unintended harm, and uncertainty;
- therefore high-stakes reconstruction should include self-questioning, not only compulsive certainty.

### Mafuyu

**Earlier developmental baseline:**
- capable of sincere happiness from praise;
- possesses self-authored prosocial desires;
- can want leisure and ordinary peer participation;
- begins to voice preferences before yielding to stronger social definitions.

**Under sustained expectation:**
- external approval increasingly substitutes for first-person preference;
- socially rational alternatives are especially powerful when presented as caring or “better” expressions of her own values;
- she can reinterpret surrender as voluntary correctness if it makes others happy.

**At severe identity depletion:**
- difficulty identifying likes and wants;
- affective/sensory flattening;
- reliance on others' labels for whether something is enjoyable or good;
- terse private language and repetitive searching questions;
- creative behavior becomes an attempt to locate selfhood rather than ordinary self-expression.

**New P04 constraint:**
- do not treat later blankness as proof that all earlier warmth/helpfulness was fabricated;
- at least some prosociality and desire are historically authentic.

### Mizuki

**When reading another person's distress:**
- uses analogy from own experience without automatically claiming equivalence;
- can infer normative pressure with high sensitivity;
- may frame insight lightly before becoming serious.

**When deciding whether to intervene:**
- acknowledges uncertainty explicitly;
- recognizes that only the other person fully knows their experience;
- prefers communicating an owned relational fact (“I would be lonely”) over claiming authority over the other's life;
- asks to participate rather than assuming entitlement.

**When another person refuses:**
- accepts the refusal without coercion.

### Ena

**When hurt by perceived humiliation:**
- converts attachment into exclusionary language (“she doesn't need us”);
- frames talent asymmetry as proof that the relationship is unnecessary;
- can remain highly attentive to the person she claims not to need.

**When crisis becomes credible:**
- concern can surface indirectly through questions and monitoring;
- does not necessarily translate concern into direct caretaking;
- preserves the right to refuse involvement.

**Toward teammates whose choices she does not share:**
- may support them at the boundary of her comfort;
- practical relational care can appear as “go” / “come back safely” rather than emotional declaration.

## VIII. Thematic and structural role

### 1. “Saving” is being challenged by “reaching”

P04 introduces the possibility that a relationship can matter without completing the other person.

Kanade wants total rescue. Miku points to partial contact. Mizuki wants to communicate loneliness. Ena offers support without joining.

The story is building multiple forms of connection rather than one heroic rescue model.

### 2. External approval is unreliable evidence of selfhood

Kanade's father receives professional success that deepens his crisis.

Mafuyu receives universal praise that deepens her alienation.

OWN receives acclaim that Mafuyu experiences as useless.

Recognition is therefore not intrinsically restorative. Its value depends on whether the recognized thing corresponds to a self the person can own.

### 3. Prosocial ideals can become coercive when absolutized

Kanade wants to make people happy.

Mafuyu wants to help sick people.

Neither desire is inherently pathological.

The danger enters when:

- Kanade's desire becomes a compulsory requirement to save anyone;
- Mafuyu's desire becomes a socially optimized career identity that displaces the form she herself chose.

The story is not anti-altruistic. It is attentive to what happens when altruism loses self-authorship or ordinary limits.

### 4. “Good intentions” do not eliminate pressure

Mafuyu's parents can care about her and still contribute to identity erosion.

Kanade can sincerely want to save Mafuyu and still recognize that her intervention might become another source of pressure.

P04 repeatedly separates benevolent intent from benign effect.

### 5. Nightcord's emerging counter-model is plural rather than uniform

The group does not solve the crisis by agreeing on one interpretation.

Kanade, Mizuki, and Ena each preserve different motives and boundaries.

The possibility of a healthier relationship may lie precisely in allowing those differences to exist without turning one member's value system into the law for everyone else.

## IX. Claim transitions from P01–P03

### PRESERVE

**P01:** Nightcord's online collaboration has genuine relational value before the crisis.  
P04 continues to show that even after rupture, presence and absence matter to the members.

**P02:** Mafuyu's different social registers should not be flattened into one “true voice.”  
Episode 13 explains how adaptive social presentation became deeply internalized rather than merely performed on command.

**P03:** Ena, Mizuki, and Kanade have distinct endangered-self structures rather than one shared pathology.  
P04 reinforces the distinction through their three different intervention choices.

### STRENGTHEN

**Kanade's authentic prosocial value predates trauma.**  
Episode 11 provides direct evidence that she makes music to improve her father's emotional state before his collapse.

**Mizuki is unusually sensitive to normative coercion.**  
Episode 14 shows them correctly infer much of Mafuyu's “good girl” mechanism from limited evidence.

**Ena's anger does not equal indifference.**  
Her attention to OWN's escalating songs and her request that Kanade and Mizuki return safely strengthen the distinction.

**Mafuyu's family environment contributes to autonomy erosion.**  
Episode 13 supplies direct developmental evidence.

### REVISE

**P03: Kanade's relation to disappearance was represented primarily as self-erasure through duty.**  
P04 adds explicit disappearance desire in adolescence and a present withdrawal state in the Empty SEKAI. The broader self-erasure model remains valid but is no longer the full account.

**P02: Mafuyu's “good girl” structure was heavily explained through family pressure.**  
P04 broadens the causal model to distributed reinforcement across family, school, peers, achievement, and Mafuyu's own approval-based decision heuristic.

**Kanade's father as the source of the salvation ethic.**  
The father crisis is better understood as the event that absolutizes an existing wish to make people happy, not the origin of prosocial musical purpose itself.

### DOWNGRADE / REJECT

**“Snow/public Mafuyu is simply a consciously fabricated false mask.”**  
Episode 13 makes this formulation increasingly inadequate. The exemplary persona emerges through long-term internalized adaptation and reinforcement. Deliberate impression management may occur, but it is not sufficient as the governing explanation.

**“Mafuyu never possessed stable wants.”** — REJECT.  
Episode 13 directly shows earlier wants and preferences.

**“Mafuyu's mother alone caused Mafuyu's condition.”** — DOWNGRADE.  
The mother's influence is substantial, but the narrative explicitly distributes the reinforcing system.

### OPEN

**Miku's claim that Mafuyu does not truly want disappearance.**  
Miku has privileged relation to the Empty SEKAI and Mafuyu's feelings, making the claim highly significant, but P04 does not yet show Mafuyu herself accepting that formulation. P05 owns the confrontation with it.

**Whether Kanade can shift from “saving” to a less controlling model of “reaching/finding.”**  
Episode 15 increases reflexivity but retains the language and identity investment of rescue.

**Whether Ena's refusal persists once Mafuyu is directly encountered.**  
Do not infer from later material within P04.

## X. P04 ending-state handoff

### Kanade

**Beginning state:** collapsed after overwork; rescue mission appears invalidated; creative meaning threatened.  
**New information:** father's history is reprocessed by the audience; Kanade discovers that one of her songs existed in Mafuyu's SEKAI from the beginning; Miku frames Mafuyu as searching for self rather than desiring disappearance as an end.  
**Choice:** recommit to composing specifically for Mafuyu.  
**Ending state:** urgent, purposeful, more reflexive about ego and harm, but still strongly organized around the duty to save.

### Mafuyu

**Beginning state:** unable to find the self through OWN; disappearance contingency already declared.  
**Developmental clarification:** earlier authentic preferences and affect are established; identity loss is shown as gradual and socially reinforced.  
**Present behavior:** escalating rapid production of increasingly disturbing OWN material.  
**Ending state:** absent from Nightcord's deliberation, high-risk, still searching and nearing the point at which she may act on the disappearance contingency.

### Mizuki

**Beginning state:** troubled, privately reconsidering Mafuyu through own experience of conformity pressure.  
**Recognition:** articulates a strong but explicitly inferential model of Mafuyu's good-girl adaptation.  
**Choice:** intervene because Mafuyu's disappearance would be lonely and because Mizuki wants another conversation.  
**Ending state:** committed to accompanying Kanade while preserving epistemic humility.

### Ena

**Beginning state:** still angry and humiliated by the OWN revelation.  
**New information:** hears Mizuki's interpretation and perceives alarming escalation in Mafuyu's music.  
**Choice:** refuses direct intervention.  
**Ending state:** rupture with Mafuyu remains unresolved, but concern for Kanade and Mizuki is explicit and indifference to Mafuyu is no longer a simple reading.

## XI. Reconstruction handoff to P05

P05 should begin from the following constraints:

1. **Kanade does not possess Mafuyu's full autobiographical history.** Her intervention is based on Miku, the Empty SEKAI song, Mafuyu's prior statements, and Kanade's own salvation schema.
2. **Mizuki possesses a strong structural inference, not omniscient knowledge.** Their intervention should remain grounded in “I think / I feel / I would be lonely,” not perfect diagnosis.
3. **Ena has refused direct intervention.** If her position changes in P05, the change must be earned and located; do not retroactively erase the refusal.
4. **Mafuyu's earlier self is real.** P05 should test whether the crisis is better understood as total absence of self or inability to access and authorize self-owned feeling.
5. **Kanade's song has already demonstrated the capacity to reach without fully saving.** P05 should distinguish what the song actually accomplishes from what Kanade hoped it would accomplish.
6. **The central ethical question is now whether an intervention motivated by love, loneliness, guilt, or ego can reach Mafuyu without becoming another external demand about what Mafuyu must be.**

P04 therefore ends at the threshold of the decisive confrontation. The source has supplied enough history to explain why each member is approaching that confrontation differently. P05 owns whether those differences can become a form of relationship Mafuyu can inhabit without simply replacing one externally authored self with another.
