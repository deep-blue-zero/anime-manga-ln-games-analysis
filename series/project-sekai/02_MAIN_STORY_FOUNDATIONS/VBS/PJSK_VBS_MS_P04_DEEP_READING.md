The resolution turns on a distinction the conflict has made difficult to see: the origin of a musical choice does not permanently determine its present meaning. That correction needs several agents. Kohane identifies pleasure Toya cannot acknowledge, An helps her reach him, the Street singers reopen Akito's interpretation of the breakup, and Ken makes the hidden explanation audible to him. Only then can the pair's restoration become part of a four-person project.

## Rupture through borrowed contempt

Toya's words to Akito echo his father's valuation: their music has no meaning, it is child's play, and RAD WEEKEND is merely a small local event (`14:001:0007–0016`). P03's private admiration of Akito and conviction that he should leave already contradict a straightforward reading of those words as Toya's settled aesthetic judgment. Episode18 confirms that he wants to protect the dream from his allegedly contaminating presence. He uses a hurtful fiction to make an exit he thinks responsible possible.

Akito first asks whether Toya's father has said something again, showing prior knowledge of conflict without proving knowledge of the full childhood history. Once Toya insists on disparagement, Akito treats a normally non-joking person's language as transparent. He says not to return; later he repeatedly denies that Toya is his partner. Yet the memories, distraction, and difficulty continuing alone reveal the attachment beneath that verbal denial (`14:004:0005–0027`, `16:004:0002–0004`). A model that simply retains the insult as a literal change in preference misses the defensive mechanism.

The transcript has an angry physical confrontation cue through “let go,” interrupted speech, and the subsequent exchanges, but it does not supply an unambiguous narrated punch. This analysis does not derive a blow, injury, posture, or facial expression from an uninspected CG link. The rupture can be explained fully through its explicit speech and consequences.

## Why Kohane's intervention matters

Kohane notices the absence, tries a difficult conversation with Toya, and says she worries because they meet often and the boys recognized the girls (`15:004:0012–0025`). She says this before the offender admits that he cut the sound on his own. Her concern is not a reward given only after proof that Akito was innocent of the literal act. It also does not imply approval of his earlier words. Recognition has made the boys significant people in her world, and she acts on that relationship despite remaining discomfort.

An initially treats the issue as the boys' own problem and even teases Akito about his missing partner. She carries resentment from the false sabotage account. The offender's confession revises her factual premise: he had dismissed her as benefiting only from her father, but the rematch changed his judgment, and he now wants to energize events through his own work (`15:004:0031–0042`). She accepts the apology with a condition against repeating it. This is evidence of repair after explicit accountability, not an assertion that competitive settings inherently redeem every offender.

An then gives Kohane the access she lacks by bringing her into Kamiyama High's courtyard. Toya's library committee work and the report that Akito routinely waited for him supply ordinary-life evidence that the partnership was embedded in daily rhythms (`15:006:0002–0005`). The girls' direct description of Toya as enjoying singing is evidence Toya has not integrated into his self-image. His surprise—asking whether he really looks happy—shows incomplete self-knowledge rather than absence of affect (`16:001:0002–0009`).

Kohane reuses her own recently learned decision rule: other people's judgments did not cancel the fact that she herself wanted to sing with An; what does Toya want? She does not know his detailed family cause and does not pretend to diagnose it. He leaves, and she worries she overstepped; An supports persistence. Thus new agency includes an unsuccessful first approach and uncertainty afterward, not effortless transformation into a universal mediator (`16:001:0015–0030`).

## Two cafés, complementary interventions

Street MEIKO uses hospitality to lower friction before interpretation: coffee and cheesecake, a chance to talk, then a glass that resembles an alcoholic drink but contains oolong tea. Akito first refuses milk and sugar in rough speech and corrects himself into polite Japanese (`17:001:0002–0003`). His courtesy toward an adult-like host reappears even in distress. Len speaks from his own local dispute with Rin, naming the loneliness of being apart from one's closest companion. Akito agrees and admits he thought he could reach RAD WEEKEND with Toya (`17:001:0008–0021`).

MEIKO's demonstration does not disclose Toya's secret. It introduces an alternative hypothesis: what seems evident may not be the full truth, so Toya may not have meant his words. Akito himself recognizes that he had equated Toya's lack of joking with literal sincerity (`17:001:0030–0037`). This is an epistemic reopening, not proof of omniscience in the singer. He is already about to act when Ken calls. Akito asks Ken to keep the call connected and goes to the café.

At WEEKEND GARAGE, Toya permits the girls to hear his farewell explanation but explicitly asks that Akito not be told because he does not want Akito's disappointment (`17:002:0008–0016`). Ken has already facilitated the phone connection. Episode19 confirms Akito heard by this route. The story uses a boundary-crossing disclosure to repair the relation; that successful consequence should not erase Toya's stated confidentiality preference. Nor should it become a general endorsement of secretly relaying a friend's disclosures. The exact route is essential to reconstruct what Akito knows and why he can answer a confession not originally addressed to him.

The childhood flashback depicts denied play, prolonged lessons despite finger fatigue, and a mother who recasts paternal professional sacrifice as a reason to comply (`18:001:0004–0022`). Toya's subsequent speech explicitly says he came to dislike music, stopped piano and violin in opposition to his father, and began street music because it would most antagonize him. The audience sees the specific childhood dialogue; the café listeners receive the spoken retrospective summary. Do not give every listener verbatim memory of the flashback.

## Reconstructing seriousness from shared history

Toya's explanation contains its own contradiction. He says he has only escaped and depends on Akito, yet also says Akito gave him enjoyment, a place, his first companion, and a valued dream (`18:001:0027–0054`). He has treated an initial negative motive as if it permanently invalidates every later positive reason. He offers the girls as people fit to pursue Akito's dream, meaning his advocacy for them also functions as a proposed replacement for himself.

Akito answers with the history Toya discounts: relentless practice, continuing beside him after an audience criticized their technique, and believing in a dream others dismissed (`19:001:0008–0029`). The point is not that any quantity of labor mechanically proves a person's inner truth. Akito shows Toya what that labor and companionship have meant to *him*. The dependence becomes reciprocal: the supposedly burdensome partner is someone without whom Akito says he could not have reached the present position. Akito explicitly rejects replacing him (`001:0032`).

The question then changes from “Was your original motive pure enough?” to “What do you want now?” Toya finally admits that he wants to sing with Akito and pursue the dream. His objections linger briefly; the repair is not an instant erasure of self-judgment. Akito insists that Toya is his best partner who merely regards himself as half-hearted, and they agree on renewed practice (`19:001:0034–0054`). This is the durable psychological and relational transition that resolves the phase's crisis.

## Music, ordinary speech, and unit formation

The common song does not itself command a unit merger. After the pair repairs, the singers name the shared wish and invite singing. The transcript gives the ensuing reactions: Kohane feels excitement, An values the sound and enjoyment, and Akito privately considers what collaboration could do. Untitled is then named Ready Steady (`20:002:0022–0031`). The next day's proposal is An's, explicitly based on that experience and shared objective. Toya considers a collective suffix, Kohane supplies SQUAD, and Akito supplies an additional meaning. Group naming thus distributes agency across all four, though An leads the proposal. No lyrical exegesis, musical arrangement assessment, MV visual analysis, or vocal-performance judgment is warranted by a music-video marker alone.

Speech remains relationship-conditioned. Kohane's greetings and questions remain hesitant in awkward encounters, whereas her naming contribution is unsolicited. An is casual and tactile by reported interaction, intense in affection toward Kohane, teasing toward the boys, and practical about café service. Akito's high-stakes confession uses rough emphatic Japanese rather than a new polished emotional register; his switch to polite speech with MEIKO and Ken demonstrates context sensitivity. Toya speaks with formal respect to Ken, blunt defensive dismissal to Akito during the rupture, then unguarded admission under pressure. His final observation that honestly communicating feelings is good invites Akito's dry reminder that he is an ironic person to say it (`20:004:0036–0037`). The story preserves the awkwardness inside progress.
