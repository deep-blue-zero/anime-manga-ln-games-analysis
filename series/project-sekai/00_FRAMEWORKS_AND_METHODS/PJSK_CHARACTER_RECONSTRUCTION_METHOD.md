# Project SEKAI Character Reconstruction Method


## Purpose


This method defines how to reconstruct a Project SEKAI character for plausible use across scenarios ranging from mundane daily interactions to unfamiliar high-stakes situations. Reconstruction is not impersonation by vocabulary alone. It is a conditional model of psychology, knowledge, relationships, context, decision-making, behavior, and speech.


The governing sequence is:
psychology -> goals -> decision -> behavior -> speech.


Do not reverse this sequence by starting from catchphrases and inventing motives to justify them.


## Required foundation


A principal character should not be considered reconstruction-ready until the analysis has established at least:
- starting psychological state;
- foundational relationships;
- major conflicts;
- crucial identity/background facts;
- major state transitions;
- approximate chronology of later evidence;
- sufficient ordinary-life evidence to distinguish daily personality from crisis behavior.


Exhaustive analysis of every story is not required for R3 reconstruction readiness, but the main-story foundation and major developmental events must be understood well enough to prevent state confusion.


## Canonical character package


A mature principal-human character folder normally contains:


CHARACTER_MONOGRAPH
Answers who the character is, why, and how they developed.


RECONSTRUCTION_MODEL
Answers how the character is likely to think, choose, act, and speak under specified conditions.


EVIDENCE_INDEX
Maps claims and behavioral categories to canonical source locators.


STATE_HISTORY
Optional state snapshots when temporal complexity requires explicit historical simulation targets.


Avoid unnecessary proliferation of overlapping psychology, speech, and relationship documents unless independent retrieval need justifies it.


## Character monograph contents


### Identity and background
Roles, family/social position, creative/professional identity, important history, and durable contextual facts.


### Self-concept
What the character believes about themselves. Distinguish this from what the narrative and other characters reveal.


### Values and beliefs
Principles shown through decisions and tradeoffs, not merely stated opinions.


### Needs and motives
Conscious goals and less-recognized psychological needs. Mark inference clearly when motives are not explicit.


### Fears and avoidance
Threats to self-concept, relationships, autonomy, belonging, competence, safety, or identity.


### Defenses and coping
Masking, withdrawal, anger, humor, compliance, perfectionism, avoidance, intellectualization, caretaking, impulsivity, or other recurring strategies.


### Emotional architecture
Which emotions are easily expressed, displaced, suppressed, difficult to name, or only shown around specific people.


### Agency
When the character initiates versus reacts. Identify conditions that increase or suppress initiative.


### Social masks and context selves
Model differences among school, family, unit, public, private, online, performance, stranger-facing, and other recurring contexts.


### Contradictions
Preserve genuine inconsistency, ambivalence, development, and self-misunderstanding. Do not flatten the character into a clean trait list.


### Development
Use explicit state transitions. Later states must not retroactively overwrite earlier psychology.


### Relationships
For each major relationship, record what the other person activates in the character: trust, vulnerability, dependence, rivalry, irritation, admiration, obligation, disclosure, avoidance, or growth.


## Reconstruction model layers


### Layer 1 — Behavioral invariants
Traits that remain relatively stable across states: temperament, social energy, empathy style, attentional tendencies, broad conflict orientation, recurring values, default conversational energy, and habitual decision biases.


### Layer 2 — State-dependent variables
Properties that change meaningfully across development: confidence, self-understanding, autonomy, willingness to disclose, hostility, dependence, hope, emotional regulation, and agency. State variables must be tied to a temporal/source boundary.


### Layer 3 — Context modes
Model distinct behavior in settings such as school, home, unit work, recreation, public space, private space, online interaction, performance, unfamiliar groups, and authority-facing situations.


### Layer 4 — Relationship conditioning
For each important interlocutor or relationship class, record trust, intimacy, vulnerability, dependency, rivalry, authority, safe/unsafe topics, forms of address, teasing norms, conflict style, repair style, and expected disclosure level.


### Layer 5 — Stakes and pressure
Model low, medium, high, and extreme stakes separately. Stress may reveal value hierarchy, but it can also distort ordinary behavior.


## Stakes model


### Low stakes
Primary evidence: area dialogue, casual cards, ordinary event scenes, downtime, shopping, school, practice, meals, hobbies, small inconveniences.


Questions:
- Does the character initiate conversation?
- How do they joke and respond to teasing?
- What interests or bores them?
- How practical or impulsive are they?
- How do they offer help?
- How do they react to minor mistakes or annoyance?
- What conversational silence is comfortable?
- How much information do they volunteer?


Low-stakes evidence is mandatory because it prevents a reconstruction from behaving as though every conversation is a character climax.


### Medium stakes
Primary evidence: interpersonal disagreement, embarrassment, performance pressure, creative conflict, social evaluation, competition, misunderstandings.


Questions:
- Does the character confront, avoid, deflect, appease, joke, withdraw, or seek mediation?
- How quickly do they escalate?
- What makes them apologize or repair?
- What kind of criticism is threatening?


### High stakes
Primary evidence: major identity, family, relationship, career, or psychological crises.


Questions:
- Which value wins when values conflict?
- What risks become acceptable?
- Who receives trust or disclosure?
- Does the character become more direct, more avoidant, more controlling, more self-sacrificial, or more passive?


### Extreme or out-of-domain
Examples include violence, criminal danger, unfamiliar political/social institutions, or crossover conditions with no close source analog. Prediction is allowed but must be marked as extrapolation and constrained by established traits and value ordering.


## Evidence-distance scale


D0 DIRECT
The character has faced essentially the same situation.


D1 NEAR_ANALOG
Very similar pressures and interpersonal structure.


D2 STRUCTURAL_ANALOG
Surface situation differs, but the underlying conflict is well represented in source.


D3 EXTRAPOLATED
Prediction derives from strong traits and priorities but lacks a close scenario analog.


D4 SPECULATIVE
Source constrains the answer weakly. Multiple outcomes may be similarly plausible.


Scenario outputs should become more probabilistic and less linguistically specific as distance increases.


## Relationship-conditioned reconstruction


Never treat a character as having one universal social register. For every scenario, resolve who the other person is to them.


At minimum classify:
- stranger;
- casual acquaintance;
- peer/classmate;
- unit member;
- close friend;
- family;
- authority figure;
- rival;
- disliked person;
- dependent/caretaking relationship.


Where the source supports it, use person-specific models rather than broad classes.


Relationship state is asymmetric. A may trust B more than B trusts A; A may understand B better than B understands A. Simulation must preserve that asymmetry.


## Epistemic constraint


Before predicting behavior, establish what the character knows at the selected temporal state. Do not allow them to:
- reference unrevealed information;
- understand a secret before disclosure;
- behave as if a misunderstanding is already resolved;
- import later self-knowledge into an earlier state.


Psychologically correct behavior with chronologically impossible knowledge is still incorrect reconstruction.


## Behavioral prediction protocol


For each scenario:
1. Select temporal state.
2. Select knowledge state.
3. Resolve relationships to all relevant participants.
4. Resolve context mode.
5. Resolve stakes.
6. Identify immediate goals and competing values.
7. Identify likely perception/interpretation of the situation.
8. Predict decision and observable behavior.
9. Generate speech appropriate to that behavior.
10. Compare with the closest source analogs.
11. Assign evidence distance and confidence.
12. Note plausible alternatives when evidence does not strongly select one outcome.


## Speech reconstruction


Speech should be modeled conditionally, especially in Japanese source work. Track:
- first-person pronouns;
- names/forms of address;
- honorific behavior;
- politeness level;
- sentence endings;
- contraction and informality;
- lexical preferences;
- hesitation and ellipsis;
- response length;
- directness;
- rhetorical questions;
- teasing and insult style;
- emotional intensification;
- internal versus spoken language;
- changes by interlocutor and state.


The preferred model is not "Character X speaks like this." It is a matrix such as:
Character X -> unfamiliar adult
Character X -> classmate
Character X -> unit member
Character X -> specific intimate/rival relationship
Character X -> internal monologue


Textual speech reconstruction should remain distinct from performed-voice reconstruction. Prosody, pitch, breath, timing, laughter, and vocal intensity require sufficient audio evidence and should not be invented from transcript text.


## Decision heuristics


A reconstruction model should include reusable conditional tendencies, for example:
- if criticized by trusted peer versus stranger;
- if another person is distressed;
- if the character is embarrassed publicly versus privately;
- if responsibility conflicts with desire;
- if an authority figure pressures them;
- if a friend violates expectations;
- if the character must choose between honesty and relationship preservation.


These are not hard-coded rules. They are evidence-backed tendencies with exceptions and state dependence.


## Anti-patterns and behavioral constraints


Every reconstruction model must identify what would be surprisingly out of character. Include:
- disclosures unlikely without extraordinary trust or pressure;
- forms of aggression rarely or never used;
- emotions usually displaced rather than verbalized;
- situations in which the character tends to defer;
- situations in which they reliably take control;
- fanon-like simplifications contradicted by source;
- behaviors requiring unusually strong provocation.


Negative constraints are essential for preventing drift during long scenario conversations.


## State representation


A useful state snapshot contains:
STATE ID
Temporal/source boundary
Self-concept
Goals
Active fears
Emotional baseline
Social masks
Primary dependencies
Relationship states
Known information
Unknown/misunderstood information
Available coping strategies
Typical agency level
Stress vulnerabilities
Speech/register changes
Key evidence


State IDs are stable analytical identifiers, not claims that the character changes discretely at every event.


## Reconstruction readiness


R0 INDEXED — source retrieval only.
R1 FOUNDATION — main story and foundational relationships understood.
R2 CHARACTERIZED — psychology and major development modeled.
R3 RECONSTRUCTION_READY — strong state, relationship, low/high-stakes behavior, and textual speech coverage.
R4 MATURE_RECONSTRUCTION — broad longitudinal evidence and contradiction audit.
R5 PERFORMANCE_INFORMED — mature textual model plus substantial voice/performance evidence.


R3 is the practical target for scenario use. Do not delay useful reconstruction until every live-service line is exhaustively analyzed.


## Virtual Singer rule


Do not flatten Miku, Rin, Len, Luka, MEIKO, or KAITO into a single Project SEKAI personality without context. Preserve SEKAI/unit manifestation differences. A Virtual Singer reconstruction should distinguish stable cross-context features from manifestation-specific behavior and explicitly mark what cannot safely be generalized.


## Audit standard


Before calling a model reconstruction-ready, test it against source scenes from at least:
- ordinary low stakes;
- conflict/medium stakes;
- major high stakes;
- more than one important relationship;
- at least one context where the character's public/private presentation differs.


A good reconstruction should explain known behavior before being trusted to extrapolate unknown behavior.
