## 4. From “we have fans” to “what can our music do for someone?”


The opening marks a professional threshold. Leo/need recognizes that a brother and sister repeatedly occupying the front row are not simply audience members but their first identifiable recurring fans. Shiho immediately contextualizes that recognition in professional terms: stable performance and regular live appearances are not the endpoint; if they intend to become professionals, they need audience growth, wider recognition, and eventually original songs and a public-facing identity.


The event then makes this abstract professional question personal. The siblings, Yota and Kano, live apart after their parents' divorce. Their father has remarried. Kano increasingly escapes the discomfort of adapting to the new household by meeting Yota, while Yota concludes that his continued presence may be preventing her from forming a new family bond. He therefore decides, painfully and paternalistically, that they should stop seeing one another.


The writing does not give Leo/need omniscient authority over the family. The band repeatedly recognizes that outsiders cannot know the one correct solution. Their eventual intervention is bounded: invite the siblings to one more show and make a song that may give them something emotionally usable.


## 5. Ichika's creative-method transition


### 5.1 Generic consolation fails


Ichika first tries to write comforting lyrics about separation. She repeatedly rejects drafts because the words feel insufficient for people who are actually grieving a relationship. This is important craft evidence: Ichika is not satisfied by semantic correctness. She asks whether the words can plausibly **reach this particular listener in this particular state**.


### 5.2 Luka redirects her toward autobiographical evidence


Luka does not supply a phrase or formula. She asks what actually supported Ichika when she herself was painfully separated from Saki, Honami, and Shiho.


Ichika remembers that she listened constantly to Miku songs. The genres and lyrical styles varied, so the useful lesson is not imitation. What mattered was the relational activity the songs enabled: while listening she imagined what Saki would like, what sounds would suit Honami, how Shiho might play a bass line, and what it might feel like to be together again.


Music therefore functioned as a **continuity medium for absent relationships**.


### 5.3 Personal memory becomes targeted communication


Ichika realizes that she cannot know the siblings' exact inner worlds, but she does know the structure of missing people who matter and being sustained by memories of them. She writes toward that shared structure: count the happiness created by meeting; carry good memories forward; separation now does not erase the relationship or make future reconnection impossible.


Card `0416` makes the conceptual move explicit: if Miku's songs once helped Ichika survive loneliness, perhaps Leo/need can become something analogous for another listener. Ichika does not claim equivalence or rescue. She decides that at minimum they can **send the song**.


### 5.4 “I can only sing” becomes “I can deliver a song”


Immediately before the final performance, Ichika almost frames music as helplessness: she can only sing. She corrects herself. Singing is not the absence of action if the song has been intentionally made for someone and can be delivered to them.


This is a strong future-state candidate:


> **Candidate:** Ichika shifts from song as personal attachment/expressive refuge toward song as deliberate listener-directed relational action.


No Leo/need successor ID is created until the main-story foundation exists.


## 6. Miku, original creation, and the real-world Vocaloid bridge


EVENT_0056 also clarifies Ichika's relation to Miku at three levels:


1. **Childhood media encounter.** She accidentally discovers a Miku song on her father's tablet, becomes intensely excited, and brings the music into her friendship group.
2. **Coping infrastructure.** During Saki's hospitalization and the later friendship breakdown, Miku songs fill otherwise lonely transit and waiting time.
3. **Present collaborative aspiration.** School-SEKAI Miku sings Leo/need's new song with Ichika and encourages her to sing with “the Miku in your world” too.


The endpoint is concrete: Ichika sees Hatsune Miku software in a music store, remembers being unable to get it as a child, realizes she can now buy it with her own part-time earnings, and takes it with her. This is not merely fandom nostalgia. It opens a new real-world creative toolchain and places composition/Vocaloid practice inside Ichika's reachable future behavior.


## 7. Saki: separation sensitivity and creative response


Saki maps the guest siblings directly onto the emotional centrality of Tsukasa during her illness. Card `0417` is especially useful because Tsukasa refuses to tell her there is an obvious moral answer. Family conditions differ; Yota has considered the cost of his choice; outsiders cannot simply declare that continued contact is always correct.


Saki nevertheless identifies something she can do with her own experience: someday write songs that give someone courage to apologize after a fight. Her creative imagination is concrete, interpersonal, and immediately linked to lived memory.


Ordinary sibling behavior remains intact around the serious material: crying into Tsukasa, sweet curry, teasing, walking home together, and recalling petty fights. The relationship is neither saccharine nor purely dramatic.


## 8. Honami: care as observation, scheduling, and material support


Card `0418` gives exceptionally strong Honami reconstruction evidence. She recognizes Ichika's sleep deprivation through subtle presentation cues and explains that repeated visits to Kanade's home taught her the difference between Kanade when properly rested and after composition-driven late nights.


She then converts care into logistics:
- builds a demanding but feasible rehearsal schedule;
- tracks each member's required practice time and external commitments;
- anticipates fatigue rather than treating motivation as limitless;
- makes soy/oat-pulp cookies, honey-lemon, lemonade, and other food support;
- tries to reduce the bodily cost of the schedule she helped design.


This is a strong example of Honami's care style: **not merely comforting speech but continuous environmental regulation**.

## 9. Shiho: professionalization and trust in Honami


Shiho drives the move from “practice and accumulate live experience” to strategic audience-building. Card `0419` also shows her explicitly praising Honami's operational growth: the schedule is not just neat organization but a model of who needs what kind of practice and how the band can reach a performance goal.


The praise matters because Shiho is selective and concrete. Honami's delighted reaction also gives a useful low-temperature dynamic: she jokes that being praised by Shiho first thing means it will be a good day; Shiho pushes back against her reputation as only a hard-ass while continuing the affectionate exchange.
