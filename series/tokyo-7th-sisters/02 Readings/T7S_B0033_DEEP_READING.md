# T7S_B0033 — friendship without friendliness

## Result

Ume tries to make Emoco publicly approachable by drilling smiles, greetings, and harmless conversation. Her campaign mistakes conventional friendliness for the substance of friendship and projects her own history of painful isolation onto someone who does not want a large social circle. Emoco, meanwhile, can perform a commercial smile but refuses to distribute intimacy as a generic social signal. She finally defines one or two true friends as sufficient and reserves gentle language for them—an indirect reassurance she immediately withdraws into insult. Hina supplies the chapter's interpretive key: Emoco has already been kind within 4U's antagonistic private idiom, and being with them elicits the smile Ume cannot command.

## The accessible member as involuntary intermediary

Ume opens by converting a producer's compliment into evidence that she is 4U's natural star. The street encounter that follows appears to confirm her popularity until the fan asks her to deliver a present to Emoco. Her humiliation is comic, but her complaint identifies real relational labor. Because Emoco seems difficult to approach, fans repeatedly use the visibly friendlier Ume as a postal route. Public accessibility is work, and one member's refusal can transfer that work to another.

The opening therefore gives Ume a legitimate practical grievance before she overreaches. She asks Emoco to change “for my sake,” yet quickly expands inconvenience into a diagnosis: Emoco's personality is broken, socially unfit, and in need of correction. Concern for a colleague becomes a normalization project. Ume assumes that greater popularity, more conversational ease, and more friends form a single scale of human improvement.

Emoco does not offer a clean counterexample of harmless difference. Her insults are deliberately abrasive, she enjoys Ume's embarrassment, and her style can obstruct contact by people who do not already know its rules. The source neither proves that everyone should accept that cost nor shows Emoco becoming more considerate in public. It instead asks whether those consequences authorize Ume to replace Emoco's mode of relation with her own.

## Sociability as a repertoire of techniques

Ume's curriculum is revealingly professional. Smile visibly; greet people; notice a change in clothing; offer a safe compliment; keep inconsequential conversation moving. These are learnable techniques rather than spontaneous evidence of an inner disposition. Ume knows this because she reportedly practiced manufactured smiles in a mirror each night, and Emoco can already produce an appropriate smile for jacket photography because it is work.

The chapter does not expose practiced expression as inherently fake. Ume's approachable idol persona is socially functional, and rehearsal can make difficult conduct available. The problem is the inference that competence in a code obliges continuous use of it. Emoco regards smiling as something cheapened by indiscriminate circulation. She can sell an image under professional conditions while withholding the same sign from ordinary exchange.

Her repeated “failed” smiles make the distinction concrete. Ume perceives contempt regardless of the facial configuration and correctly guesses the thought attached to one attempt. A smile is not a universal packet whose meaning survives context. In this relationship, Emoco's expression is legible through shared history, speech, and intention; changing the mouth alone cannot transform it into warmth.

The greeting exercise produces the same conflict. Emoco claims that three seconds of eye contact already constituted acknowledgement, while Ume demands a spoken form. Ume's norm is more publicly interoperable, but it is not the only imaginable sign. The lesson fails because she evaluates whether Emoco can imitate conventional signals, not how Emoco actually makes recognition available to people she values.

Hina becomes an unwilling instructional prop. Ume points to her habitual smile and casual talk as the model, and both others pinch and examine her cheeks despite her protest. Hina's easy sociability is treated as a bodily resource they can inspect. Yet she is the person who most consistently refuses to rank their styles. She thinks Emoco is already fine, affirms the value of her smile, and ultimately interprets the relation the two combatants cannot name.

## Ume's autobiography inside the cure

Ume repeatedly calls Emoco a friendless loner, but Emoco knows that Ume once cried alone after school and that her later popularity partly operates as a self-authored setting. This knowledge destabilizes their surface roles. The aggressively social reformer and the isolated target are not opposites; Ume is trying to protect Emoco from the pain that organized her own transformation.

When the exercises fail, Ume finally stops performing expertise and states the autobiographical premise. Having no one to talk with, walk home with, or visit places with was lonely. Receiving her first friendship mattered enough to make her cry. Her intervention is intrusive, but the fear beneath it is not vanity alone. She cannot imagine someone inhabiting solitude without sharing the injury she remembers.

That projection also explains why number matters to her. Ume begins with competing fan counts, insists she is popular with everyone in class, and proposes that Emoco could easily accumulate many friends. Quantity protects against the return of abandonment. Emoco answers by rejecting friendship as a countable status good. If it is precious enough to produce tears, she asks, why would displaying the number of friends prove its value?

The response does not establish that Emoco never feels loneliness; the source gives no private account sufficient for that conclusion. It does establish a different desired scale. One or two true friends are enough. She does not need universal accessibility to possess meaningful relation, and institutional standing—she notes that she can become cultural-festival committee chair—shows that sparse intimacy is not equivalent to total social incapacity.

## An idiom that conceals and conveys care

Emoco says she would save gentle words for true friends. In context, the likely candidates are Ume and Hina, but she refuses to certify this directly. Ume first recognizes the implication as unfairly moving, then notices that Emoco still has not spoken gently to her. Emoco answers with another insult and an opaque “it means what it means.” The scene produces reassurance and deniability simultaneously.

That indirection is not empty. Emoco's attacks demonstrate an almost archival attention to Ume: she knows about the earlier classroom tears, the private smile rehearsals, the repeated color of her ribbons, her public self-mythology, and the exact vulnerability underneath the fan encounter. Information can be weaponized, as it is here, but its precision also proves sustained observation. Emoco is not indifferent to Ume; she is intensely oriented toward her.

Ume reciprocates in the same abrasive register. She claims to be reforming Emoco for society, insults her constantly, persists after exhaustion, and immediately resumes their contest when direct vulnerability becomes uncomfortable. Their quarrel is an interaction neither permits to lapse. It maintains proximity while protecting both from the exposure of an uncomplicated declaration.

Hina names what their dyad cannot. She tells Ume that Emoco has continually offered the two of them kind words and that, when she is with them, she displays a wonderful smile. The claim does not require every insult to be secretly benevolent; some are plainly designed to wound or provoke. It asks the reader to interpret the whole patterned exchange rather than isolate its polite content. Participation, detailed attention, selective disclosure, and unforced delight can carry affection even when surface phrasing remains hostile.

This makes the title, “Dear My Friend,” both sincere and structurally difficult to utter. The dedication belongs to a form of friendship that resists the generic language of dedication. Ume cannot teach Emoco to become a friend by copying phrases, because the relation is already present in a highly specific code.

## Public persona and scarce intimacy

4U's work complicates any simple opposition between false business feeling and true private feeling. Ume's practiced smile can genuinely help fans approach her even when it is constructed. Emoco's work smile can fulfill a visual assignment without disclosing intimate warmth. Their professional personae are useful interfaces, not transparent measures of friendship.

At the same time, scarcity gives Emoco's uncommanded expression its force. Hina recalls the smiling jacket photograph, but Emoco explains it as a job; Hina closes instead on the smile that arises with the group. The distinction is not visible configuration alone. It is who requested the expression, for what purpose, and whether its occurrence belongs to a relationship or a deliverable.

Ume's campaign fails to change Emoco, yet it succeeds as an inquiry into 4U. It draws Ume's past loneliness into speech, lets Emoco articulate a theory of selective friendship, and positions Hina as the unit's most capable reader of care. The group remains abrasive, asymmetrical, and publicly inconvenient. Its stability comes not from converting difference into smooth friendliness, but from learning that shared signs mean differently inside and outside the relation.
