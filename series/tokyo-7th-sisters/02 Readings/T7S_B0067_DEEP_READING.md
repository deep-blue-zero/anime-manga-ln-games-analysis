# T7S_B0067 — the magic that does not return

## Result

The injury makes SOL's hidden comparison structure audible. Manon concludes that three members cannot perform because the unit requires all four, yet her reasons soon disclose a harsher belief: Shinju and Tasha are the members audiences truly want, while she and Shirayuki are appendages. Shirayuki rejects that public ranking, but her reassurance cannot reach a person who has made adulthood synonymous with learning to discard the self no market will certify.

The installment places this conflict beside Momoka's 2036 letter to Coney. In the letter, a lost puppy called Coney is returned to an owner who regrets abandoning it; Momoka then begs the absent Coney to return because the children remain without her. The analogy reveals enduring need, but it also makes return the only imaginable proof of love. Momoka's current cancellation decision repeats that arrested model: protect the group from a painful failure by deciding the route before asking what every member wants.

The three-person trial live fails. The audience asks for Shinju, the formation breaks, and Manon flees into black rain after discarding the magical-girl wand that once linked her to Shirayuki and Haru. The recovered movies make the formal descent clear: a luminous childhood pair is separated by an empty white field, then the present collapses into storm clouds and near-blackness. The “magic” that cannot return is not literal supernatural power. It is Manon's fantasy that an external transformation can replace the person she is.

## A 2036 letter converts disappearance into an animal-return story

The episode opens with a dated letter: `2036年2月19日`, from Momoka to Coney. Momoka recounts how the old Nanasta group found a puppy, named it Coney, and cared for it. The original owner, who had abandoned it, returned in remorse. The girls gave the puppy back. Momoka then asks the human Coney to return, fears that Coney abandoned them, and says the children remain children without her (opening sequence).

The puppy is not evidence about why the person left. It is Momoka's analogy, built to create a return narrative where direct knowledge is absent. It contains two incompatible beliefs: abandonment has happened, and remorseful return will repair it. Both keep Momoka's adulthood organized around Coney's future action.

Her wording also preserves a child/adult hierarchy. If Coney returns, the children may again be held inside the former relation. If she does not, their growth remains framed as deprivation rather than a life they can author. The letter is moving because the need is real; it is dangerous because it makes one absent person responsible for ending everybody else's suspended childhood.

## “Four make SOL” can mean solidarity or self-erasure

With Shinju unable to dance, the members consider the festival. Manon says three cannot do it. Tasha agrees that the four are required for SOL. Momoka moves toward cancellation. Shirayuki had wanted to continue, but retracts her preference when directly asked (roughly logs 76–170).

The same sentence—SOL requires four—therefore contains different positions.

- Tasha affirms relational identity: a slot cannot simply be filled or ignored.
- Manon partly affirms relation but also assumes the others have value she lacks.
- Shirayuki has an unspoken desire to proceed and gives it up to preserve apparent unity.
- Momoka treats protection from failure as authorization to close the decision.

Unanimity is not produced by four people saying what they want. It is produced by the least secure member speaking first and another member concealing dissent. The unit's anti-secrecy ideal fails precisely when it is most needed.

## The train conversation reveals Manon's imagined audience

Manon reports overhearing fans who treat Shinju and Tasha as the real attractions and Shirayuki and Manon as lesser additions. The report is direct testimony about what she heard, not a representative survey of SOL's audience. Its force comes from how completely it matches her existing fear (roughly logs 171–245).

She blames herself for Shinju's injury and says she lacks value. Shirayuki rejects the ranking: external opinions change, and a stranger's measure cannot determine their relation. This is ethically sound but pragmatically insufficient. Manon hears Shirayuki as someone who can simply trust herself because she possesses an uncomplicated inner line.

Shirayuki has in fact been changing speech, school behavior, and desire to stay near Manon. Her failure is not false sincerity. It is failure to reveal the history and labor underneath apparent steadiness. As long as Shirayuki offers only confidence, Manon can continue treating her as evidence of an essence Manon lacks.

## Tasha translates old seasons into a duty not to flee

Tasha drinks and remembers the 2034 Nanasta cast and its units. People and seasons change, but the remembered time remains a light. She tells Shirayuki that those left after earlier departures cannot undo loss; what they can do is refuse to run away from the lives still present (roughly logs 246–335).

The formulation does not promise permanence or reunion. It makes memory actionable in the present without requiring restoration. Tasha also acknowledges her own change: school, research, and future travel have redistributed attention. Shirayuki admits missing the earlier group and asks when they all changed.

The answer is not a date. Change occurred through ordinary accumulation. That matters because nobody committed a single betrayal that could be reversed to restore childhood.

## Manon uses the wand as a failed conversion device

In the dressing room Manon holds the magical-girl wand associated with childhood. She wants it to make her an idol, then insists magic does not exist. The exact reconstructed composite shows her in a bright performance costume with eyes hidden beneath her fringe, shoulders closed around the wand. The costume supplies maximum category legibility while the face supplies withdrawal.

This is the episode's central image. Manon does not merely abandon childish fantasy. She asks the object to replace the insufficient person, discovers it cannot, and concludes that the insufficient person must be discarded instead.

The childhood movie complicates that conclusion. Its sampled frames show little Manon and Shirayuki together in matching striped magical-girl costumes, a tiny figure crossing an enormous white field, and the pair reunited. The remembered “magic” was never solitary transformation. It was relational accompaniment and a language in which fear could be carried together.

## The failed live makes the imagined hierarchy materially effective

SOL attempts a three-person Nanasta live. Audience voices call for Shinju. The group loses formation and song continuity, and the performance stops. Manon runs into the rain, discards the wand, and says that only she is not an idol and must become an adult (roughly logs 390–481).

The audience request is established; audience-wide rejection is not. The performance failure is real; proof that Manon lacks talent is not. The missing fourth member, compressed rehearsal, unresolved conflict, and Manon's self-monitoring all contribute. Her conclusion converts a relational/system failure into an essence verdict.

The storm movie moves from layered clouds to hard rain and almost complete black. It does not merely illustrate sadness. It removes the visible field in which Manon had been comparing herself. Yet she continues the judgment in darkness. The examiner no longer needs to be present because its standard has been internalized.

## Rui sees the crisis that Momoka tried to prevent

Rui meets Momoka after the cancellation decision. She notes that the Nanasta office has been intentionally preserved as if waiting for someone and predicts that SOL's concealed problem will worsen. Momoka answers from Coney-derived distance: people must find their own answer (closing sequence).

That pedagogy contains a legitimate defense of agency. Its limit is that questions, information, and presence are also conditions under which people can answer. Momoka has copied the form of Coney's lesson while omitting the reliable companion who remained nearby during many earlier choices.

## Claim changes

1. Momoka's exact `2036年2月19日` letter establishes years of unresolved Coney-directed abandonment fear and a return-centered repair fantasy.
2. SOL's cancellation is not produced by fully disclosed preference: Shirayuki suppresses a wish to continue, while Manon's position is shaped by self-erasure.
3. Manon's audience hierarchy is grounded in a heard fan exchange but cannot be generalized to all reception.
4. Tasha treats changing seasons and departed people as reasons to remain with current relations, not as proof that loss can be reversed.
5. The magical-girl wand is a relational childhood object that Manon misuses as an impossible device for replacing herself.
6. The three-person live fails and Manon flees; the failure does not establish individual incapacity or audience consensus.
7. Momoka's copied self-answer pedagogy is now implicated in managerial distance.
