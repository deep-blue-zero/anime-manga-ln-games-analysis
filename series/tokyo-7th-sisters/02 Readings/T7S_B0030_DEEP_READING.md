# T7S_B0030 — a confession that refuses to choose

## Result

Haru and Kajika are cast as friends who become rivals for the same boy, but their rehearsal cannot preserve the triangle's required opposition. Each tries to surrender the romance for the other's happiness until the male recipient becomes irrelevant. The production rewrites its ending around their friendship, and viewers intensify the girls' pairing rather than the scripted heterosexual couple. The chapter treats acting as relational inquiry: each performer learns how she moves by seeing herself through the other's attention, while a failed imitation of generic rivalry reveals the bond the drama should actually stage.

## A role written close to the performer

The proposed drama reduces the distance between character and actor. Haru's character is cheerful and likes cleaning; Kajika's works in a fish shop. Staff have adapted the roles to recognizable traits so two inexperienced leads can enter them. The remaining obstacle is the climax: neither girl has confessed romantic love, and the plot asks both to address the same boy.

Closeness to the actor is helpful but not sufficient. A character assembled from biographical facts still requires imagined relations, circumstances, and embodied decisions. The Player correctly notes that the preceding scenes may make the final confession intelligible once the performers have inhabited the whole sequence. Coney instead accelerates directly toward an emotionally “real” confession. These approaches establish a tension the chapter never entirely resolves: acting can grow through constructed context, but idol production also rewards shortcuts that recruit the performer's actual attachments.

## The manager as rehearsal object

The Player is made the boy's stand-in without prior permission, first through a surprise double confession and later through Coney's instruction that the girls imagine genuinely liking him. The text marks his alarm, his objection to the lack of authorization, and the professional danger perceived by Musubi. He subsequently agrees to help, but Coney continues to exploit embarrassment as productive material.

Familiarity makes the Player a plausible safe partner for vulnerable practice. It also blurs the relation between manager, performer, and romantic object. The chapter converts that boundary problem into comedy rather than fully examining the asymmetry. Its lack of an authored choice is telling: although the opening asks which girl he will select, neither the source structure nor the later scene gives the Player a decision. The rehearsal uses him as the apparent center, then the performers' relation displaces him.

## Borrowed genres and private imagination

Momoka and Musubi demonstrate two ways of filling an unknown romantic situation. Momoka borrows a spectacular blood-soaked rivalry from media; the others briefly mistake genre familiarity for real-world information. Musubi constructs a quiet sunset walk, suspended eye contact, minute gesture, and a single confession. Her embarrassed denial leaves open whether this is personal fantasy, practiced cultural script, or both. The chapter supports the presence of unusually detailed affect, not a definitive claim about whom she desires.

This contrast prevents “experience” from meaning only direct romantic history. People can draw on stories, observation, fantasy, friendship, and self-projection. The problem is not that a borrowed convention is unreal; it is whether that convention helps the performer perceive a relation she can inhabit.

## Seeing oneself through a friend

At the riverbank, Haru and Kajika are asked to act from the premise that they themselves love the Player. The exercise initially produces focused embarrassment. Its decisive turn comes when Kajika interrupts Haru and addresses her rather than the designated boy. She has perceived Haru's feeling, wished it were different, and hidden her own. Haru answers that she did the same.

The rivals then become advocates. Kajika says she loves Haru too and could sincerely celebrate her happiness; Haru insists Kajika would be the better partner. The escalating mutual surrender becomes comic because no one is any longer making a confession to the supposed object of desire. Yet Momoka's judgment is exact: it is unmistakably “like the two of them.” Their failure to reproduce possessive rivalry discloses an existing mode of love—care that protects the other's future even at personal cost.

That mode has its own risk. Endless self-erasure would leave neither girl able to state a desire. The chapter does not test that risk because the scene is rehearsal, not an actual triangle. What it does establish is that their bond cannot be represented adequately by a story in which one woman must defeat the other to secure the male prize.

Haru and Kajika later explain that each learned her own expressions and gestures from what the other noticed. This is more than copying a friend's notes. A performer cannot directly see her own face and body as another person does; the partner supplies an external perspective. Their acting becomes “their own” through reciprocal observation. Self-expression is thus neither isolated interior truth nor total direction from outside, but an exchange in which another person's knowledge helps make one's latent behavior available for deliberate use.

## Revision and the audience's excess

The production changes the final scene to preserve the riverbank dynamic. The completed work becomes a story about the two girls' friendship and is praised for their endearing performances. This adaptation does not reduce acting to “being themselves.” The Player emphasizes that they use lines, expressions, and gestures as performance, while the script is altered to meet the relation they can render with specificity.

Audience interpretation exceeds even that revision. Momoka reports that online discussion favors a girl/girl pairing over the girl/boy couples and describes the result as a gentle romantic comedy. The source does not settle whether Haru and Kajika's bond is canonically romantic, platonic, performative, or some combination. It does show that removing the man's decisive function makes queer reception structurally available. Friendship, romantic acting, and audience desire overlap without becoming identical.

The chapter's title, “a small love song,” fits this scale. No grand confession produces a canonical couple. Instead, affection appears through close observation, embarrassment, advocacy, revision, and an audience recognizing relational energy that the original premise did not intend to center.

## Exit state

- Haru and Kajika gain a successful dramatic lead credit through roles adapted to their traits and then further revised through rehearsal.
- The Player's use as a romantic stand-in is partially consented to but exposes an unresolved manager/performer boundary that the chapter handles mainly as comedy.
- Media convention, fantasy, and observation all function as possible resources for acting without being equated with lived romantic experience.
- The scripted competition for a boy collapses when each girl prioritizes the other's happiness and addresses her partner instead.
- Haru and Kajika learn their own embodied tendencies through one another's attentive description.
- The production treats failed conformity as diagnostic information and revises the story around the relation the performers can render specifically.
- The male “choice” is never enacted; the drama recenters female friendship, and the audience further opens it to a girls' romantic pairing.
- The source preserves rather than resolves the boundary among friendship, performed desire, and queer reception.
