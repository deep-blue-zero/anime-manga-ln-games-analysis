# T7S_B0026 — the labor behind a “natural” expression

## Result

WITCH NUMBER 4's first dramatic video asks the members to perform against their established types. Its interview frame presents the finished work as polished, cinematic, and emotionally coherent; the flashbacks reveal missed shots, exhausted bodies, borrowed anime poses, inverted books, food distractions, and improvised wind. The chapter's comedy does not puncture the drama as false. It shows that a convincing image can be both thoroughly constructed and capable of disclosing possibilities the performers and audience had not yet recognized.

## A story about making a story

The chapter alternates between a promotional interview and recollected production scenes. That structure gives the audience two works at once: the `SAKURA`-inspired drama and the story WITCH NUMBER 4 tells about making it. The interviewer seeks usable “behind-the-scenes” material, while the members repeatedly encounter the distance between the video's public effect and its practical origin.

This continues B0025's concern with supposedly candid access. Here there is no fantasy that a camera simply finds the authentic person. Roles, storyboards, direction, editing, reaction shots, and later publicity all mediate what becomes visible. Even the interview's disclosure remains curated. When Rona and Hime ask whether Haru's laughter should remain secret for the sake of her image, the interviewer postpones the decision until the article is assembled. Behind-the-scenes truth is itself another produced text.

## Against type—and inside type

The director and Momoka organize the roles around “gap appeal”: Rona, perceived as timid and unathletic, must become physically decisive; Hime, known for rough speech and neighborhood energy, becomes a demure literary girl; Momoka, habitually animated by food and games, must walk through the city in adult melancholy; Haru must contemplate a love she has not experienced. The project expands WITCH NUMBER 4 beyond its established cyber imagery by relocating it to school corridors and ordinary streets.

The apparent reversal never fully escapes the member's known character. Rona's running scenes are achieved through literal overexertion, and her difficulty projecting anger returns her characteristic sensitivity. Hime can hold the book and soften her pose only through the others' detailed instruction, while embarrassment at what her shopping-district neighbors might think keeps her local identity present inside the literary costume. Momoka can produce astonishing basketball competence by inhabiting an anime persona, but food and a rare character good interrupt her melancholy walk. Haru cannot manufacture romantic wistfulness on command.

These failures are not evidence that each girl possesses one true type which performance should preserve. Momoka's anime imitation, for example, produces real athletic action; pretending is one of her actual capabilities. Rona completes repeated runs despite defining herself as unsuited to them. Hime allows the others to shape her posture because she trusts their perception of a beauty she finds embarrassing. The unfamiliar role becomes an experiment: not “who is the real member beneath the image?” but “what can this person do when image, body, collaborators, and circumstance interact?”

## The asymmetry of directed labor

The director's enthusiasm exposes a cost. Rona is selected because the contrast between her public image and strenuous action will be appealing, then repeatedly made to run as new backgrounds inspire additional coverage. Editing can simulate a successful basket, but it cannot simulate away her fatigue and sore legs. “Gap” is therefore not an abstract aesthetic property. It is produced by assigning a body labor that its established image makes especially marketable.

The group does not openly challenge that production relation, and the Player endorses the storyboard as a way to reveal different sides. Yet the chapter keeps Rona's exhaustion visible long after the completed video converts it into effortless vitality. The comic recurrence of another running take prevents the audience from consuming transformation as costless. At the same time, Haru, Hime, and the Player bring water, encouragement, food, and practical assistance. Their care mitigates the work without pretending that care and work are identical.

## Artificial wind, unscripted affect

The climax condenses the chapter's model of representation. The script requires Haru to stand at sunset, surrounded by windborne cherry petals, while contemplating an unknown future love. The natural wind does not arrive. Crew and members use fans and reflector boards to manufacture it; the beautiful atmosphere depends on bodies straining just outside the intended frame.

Haru sees those bodies and begins laughing. The group tries harder, Momoka turns the fans into another game, and the solemn shot repeatedly fails. Only after Haru has laughed herself into exhaustion does the director recognize the relaxed expression he needs. The “natural” wistfulness is neither an expression of the fictional situation nor a transparent revelation of secret romance. It is the residual bodily state created by collective comedy and fatigue.

That discrepancy does not empty the image. Haru's expression can signify within the drama because performance is not required to reproduce its fictional cause. Her collaborators' ridiculous effort materially enables an audience to experience beauty and longing. The finished scene is artificial at every level—acted premise, hand-made wind, distributed labor, selective framing—yet the relation that produces Haru's openness is real.

## Public image as a plural capacity

The interview ends by selling the project through “unexpected sides” of WITCH NUMBER 4. That language risks turning the members into a sequence of consumable contrasts, but the chapter also gives them partial authorship. Momoka initiated the gap premise, the girls coach and support one another, and they decide together how to narrate the shoot. They are not merely objects discovered by a director; neither are they sovereign authors who control every implication.

The resulting image of the unit is plural rather than corrected. Their earlier cyber identity remains valid, and the school drama adds rather than replaces it. The shoot makes room for Rona's persistence, Hime's delicacy, Momoka's embodied mimicry, and Haru's capacity for an affect whose origin exceeds the script. Maturity here means acquiring more performable forms without having to declare any single one the definitive self.

## Exit state

- WITCH NUMBER 4 extends its public repertoire from a cyber-associated unit image into school, street, and dramatic settings.
- Acting against type reveals genuine capacities while retaining the members' familiar dispositions inside each role.
- Rona's repeated exertion makes the bodily cost of marketable “gap appeal” visible.
- Momoka demonstrates that imitation and fandom can generate real competence, not only distraction.
- Hime's embarrassment shows public reinvention remaining answerable to a local community that already knows her.
- Haru's prized “natural” expression is produced by laughter, fatigue, artificial wind, and collective off-camera labor rather than the fictional romance.
- The interview turns production history into another curated representation; disclosure and concealment both participate in image-making.
- Constructedness is not equated with falsity. The chapter defines an idol image as a collaborative artifact able to disclose more than any participant deliberately put into it.
