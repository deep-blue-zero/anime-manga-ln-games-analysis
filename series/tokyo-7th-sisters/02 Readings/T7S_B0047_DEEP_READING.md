# T7S_B0047 — dissolution terms written by spectators

## Result

Nero never directly negotiates reciprocal dissolution. She stakes AXiS, attributes equal resolve to 777☆SISTERS, and allows circulation to manufacture the rule that whichever group loses must end. Haru's signature on a participation contract then supplies a legal-looking anchor for terms she did not authorize. Episode 6 carefully distinguishes presence, signature, inference, and informed consent. It also strips Hime's language of battle down to its emotional source: she does not want domination; she wants the shared future not to disappear. Her proposal to perform alone tries to protect that future by preemptively removing herself, reproducing the very dissolution she fears. Coney refuses to solve the crisis through authoritative reassurance and says the girls' independent judgment was also Seven Sisters' belief. Yet she begins to ask whether autonomy without protective mediation helped produce the earlier catastrophe. The gravely ill Dr.END then literalizes the deadline behind the plan without excusing its coercion.

## Symmetry manufactured through circulation

Nero tells reporters that AXiS will dissolve if it loses and attributes equivalent resolve to 777☆SISTERS. Networks turn the implication into an apparently simple rule: the loser dissolves. Commentators describe the groups as long-term collaborators and frame Nanastar as risking retirement to reverse decline.

No single statement needs to contain the final falsehood. Nero supplies a unilateral stake and a suggestion of reciprocity; reporters, commentators, and audiences complete the structure. Distributed authorship here does not mean democratic legitimacy. It means responsibility can become difficult to locate while the result remains predictable.

The fabricated collaboration history also normalizes coercion. If the groups have supposedly designed the event together, existential stakes become entertainment strategy rather than harm. Nanastar's objection can then be read as post-announcement weakness.

## A signed page is not authorization for every consequence

Haru tells the group that she proposed only a joint live and was promised shared entertainment. They believe her. She then discloses that she signed a contract to participate.

Susu infers that a large cancellation penalty is probably placed elsewhere in the document. The source does not show her reading the exact provision. The group nonetheless correctly recognizes the encounter as designed to make withdrawal costly.

The episode keeps several evidentiary layers separate. It is fact that Haru signed something and says she did not agree to dissolution. It is the public's belief that the signature binds an equal-stakes battle. It is Susu's plausible inference that hidden or unreviewed terms impose monetary liability. None should be silently collapsed into another.

Haru calls herself stupid and wholly responsible. This accepts the manipulator's allocation of agency: because her body signed, every downstream reinterpretation becomes her fault. The group refuses that conclusion and names AXiS' conduct as wrong.

## Hime's proposed sacrifice

Hime accepts the battle, then proposes to perform alone so that only she must leave if Nanastar loses. The offer is loving and structurally self-defeating. To preserve the group, she would first turn its collective action into an individual burden and make one member disposable.

Musubi refuses and returns to the members' original reasons for becoming idols. Those reasons differ, but none began as a desire to attack people whose work was different. She protects the end for which the group exists.

Hime asks what that principle can do against reality. She invokes Seven Sisters, whose ideals did not prevent dissolution after two years, and says the current destruction is what 777☆SISTERS received for earnest effort. The objection is not cynical. It demands that ethical language account for institutions, contracts, and loss.

The two positions remain asymmetrical. Musubi can state what the group must not become; Hime can state what it cannot survive. Neither yet provides a viable act.

## Tears beneath the language of fighting

Hime's anger breaks. She says the members merely worked hard, cannot understand why they deserve this, does not want dissolution, and wants to continue with everyone.

The title locates truth not in a victorious argument but in the affect the argument defended. “Fight” was an emergency form taken by fear of losing ordinary shared time. Recognizing that source does not make defense unnecessary. It prevents defensive militancy from being mistaken for Hime's positive ideal.

Haru and Hime now mirror each other. Haru takes total blame and wishes to submit; Hime offers total sacrifice and wishes to fight. Each tries to make one person absorb a collective threat. The group's task will be to distribute responsibility without erasing anyone.

## Coney's non-directive pedagogy under pressure

The Manager and Coney learn what occurred. Coney blames herself for failing to prevent a predictable attack, but she does not issue the command the Manager wants. She says the girls have always had to think and decide for themselves; this was also “our” belief, extending the principle to Seven Sisters.

Her account corrects a retrospective fantasy. Coney's prior interventions created conditions, asked questions, and provided support, but did not author every resolution the group reached. Restoring her comic confidence cannot substitute for their judgment now.

At the same time, she begins to question the model. She calls the assault the curse of Seven Sisters and wonders what Seven Sisters might have needed a manager to tell them. Autonomy is not repudiated. Its infrastructure becomes visible. People can make their own decision and still require someone responsible for information, alternatives, and protection from capture.

The Manager repeats Hime's earlier wish that Coney restore order as usual. Her refusal makes a difficult ethical claim: support cannot promise that independent choice will be painless or correct. But the story also refuses to let that claim excuse institutional absence.

## The producer's body and the deadline

Coney concludes that the plan exceeds ordinary ambition or status and returns to Dr.END, expecting that even he would not permit what she now suspects. He arrives gasping, coughs blood, collapses, and needs an ambulance.

His earlier insistence that he has little time now has a bodily referent. Mortality helps explain why a producer committed to making the ultimate idol might choose a compressed, coercive shortcut. It does not transfer the cost of his deadline onto performers as legitimate necessity.

The collapse also reverses the apparent power relation. The man behind a vast encirclement is physically dependent on the people he has harmed. How they respond will test whether care must be earned through moral alignment or debt.
