# T7S_B0041 — the third performer no one can see

## Result

A televised haunted-house appearance makes Saori and Ei a popular pair, but its more consequential audience member is a spirit who has followed Ei's work for some time. The fan can speak to her idol only after accidentally entering Saori's body. What begins as contact becomes collaboration: the spirit handles an interview, notices choreography problems, and helps the two prepare a streamed stage. Her growing desire to perform with them, however, makes the possession stronger and Saori weaker. The story treats the supernatural as diegetically real and simultaneously makes it a precise account of idol access. A fan's desire to approach, speak, and participate becomes harmful when another person's body is the access route. The spirit withdraws rather than take Saori's place, but absence is not the final ideal. On show day she returns as an unseen hand that helps lead Saori without occupying her. The public sees two idols; Ei and Saori call it a three-person stage. This solution recognizes fan contribution without confusing proximity with possession. The closing joke—another spirit immediately uses Saori's body to meet Ei—makes the limit explicit: one relationship has learned a boundary, while the system that turns Saori into a conduit remains unresolved.

## Safety infrastructure around an “unbreakable” performer

The opening comedy depends on a serious institutional fact: Saori is injured so often that the Player arranges emergency responders for an amusement-park shoot. The responders already know her, say they frequently transport her, bring two stretchers, and include off-duty colleagues who want to watch because they are fans. The Player tells them that their work allows Saori to remain an idol.

Care and entertainment are entangled before any spirit appears. An emergency system has become routine production infrastructure. The responders' fandom makes cooperation easy, but it also turns a safety assignment into audience access. The program producer initially dismisses the chance of injury as negligible because he does not understand Saori's history. The Player understands the risk yet permits the appearance because Saori insists on participating.

The chapter's physical comedy repeatedly calls Saori unusually resistant to injury. She trips, is struck in what bystanders call an accident, and emerges with a scrape. That resilience should not be treated as a real safety standard. The narrative itself shows Ei expending continuous attention so Saori does not run into hazards. A body that survives harm is not the same as a workplace in which harm has been prevented.

Ei is added to the haunted-house shoot because Shisara says she can handle spirits. Saori imagines Ei driving away malicious entities. Ei repeatedly corrects her: spirits are ordinary presences to her, most are not harmful, and she is not especially powerful. What actually protects Saori is mundane. Ei holds her hand, watches where she is going, and keeps her from getting lost.

This substitution establishes the chapter's ethic. The spectacular explanation is exorcism; the effective practice is attention. Ei does not have to eradicate invisible beings in order to care for the person beside her.

## A camera finds an audience no one counted

The haunted-house camera records spirits that the producer did not believe were present. He immediately recognizes the footage as extraordinary material. Saori and Ei's contrasting behavior—Saori exuberant, Ei unafraid—also receives strong audience response and leads to a streamed two-person stage.

The production therefore converts accidental relation into a format. The pair did not enter the haunted house as a unit concept; market response names their ordinary interaction as valuable “combination.” Saori accepts the new performance quickly. Ei questions what they did beyond walking together, then agrees because they already know how to make a stage together.

One audience member is categorically absent from the ratings account. A spirit living at the attraction has watched Ei's prior streams, including several specific appearances, and follows her back to Nanasta. She is not attracted by this program alone. The haunted-house encounter gives a long-standing fan physical proximity to someone she could previously reach only through distribution.

The spirit's existence should not be reduced to metaphor. Within the chapter she speaks, moves, remembers, observes rehearsals, and makes physical contact. The parasocial reading follows from those literal events; it does not replace them. She is both a diegetic person who has died and an excluded audience member seeking acknowledgment.

## Contact through someone else's body

The fan initially stands beside the stage where Saori and Ei rehearse. Saori cannot see her and nearly crosses the same space. After Saori falls, the spirit begins speaking through her body. Ei recognizes a different interlocutor; the script's speaker label changes while the visual state continues to use Saori's character body and expressions.

The spirit did not intend to possess anyone. Her intense wish to speak with Ei appears to trigger the event. She immediately apologizes and asks how to release Saori. Ei refuses to treat her as malicious, but identifies the central boundary without ambiguity: the body belongs to Saori, who is Ei's important friend.

Saori remains conscious enough to return to the foreground and can apparently hear at least some of what occurs. Once she understands the situation, she says the spirit may remain until they discover how to separate, provided she causes no spiritual harm. This is meaningful consent to continued coexistence after accidental entry. It is not fully informed consent to everything that follows. None of them yet knows that stronger desire may deepen control, weaken Saori, or alter memory.

Saori calls accommodation “fan service.” The joke literalizes a dangerous expectation: the idol's circle owes bodily access to a devoted fan. Saori is generous, but generosity cannot make her body a neutral meeting place between Ei and her audience. Ei's sustained discomfort is not hostility toward the fan. It is awareness that kindness has not solved the conditions of contact.

## The performer who cannot be publicly named

Possession soon enters professional labor. Before a promotional talk show, nervousness unexpectedly brings the spirit forward. She must present herself as Saori because the broadcast is beginning and management does not know what has happened. Ei gives her prompts and redirects the host when the performance of identity falters.

The scene is comic improvisation, but it has real representational stakes. A fan performs paid promotional work through an idol's body without the producer or Player knowing that the credited person is not the current speaker. Saori misses her appearance; the spirit receives no public identity; Ei becomes responsible for concealing the event. Necessity explains the immediate response but does not make the arrangement institutionally sound.

The secrecy transfers care work onto Ei. The Player notices that she looks tired and offers help, but Ei withholds the cause because she expects him to panic. He cannot manage a risk he does not know exists. Saori and the spirit grow comfortable with their alternating presence, while Ei alone keeps asking whether the situation is sustainable.

The spirit is not merely a burden. Close observation makes her a capable rehearsal partner. She identifies the point at which Saori's posture breaks, demonstrates a correction, remembers choreography, and offers to perform if Saori becomes unable to continue. Saori nicknames her Rei and invites further advice. What began as audience attention becomes artistic knowledge.

The three start calling themselves almost a unit. That formulation recognizes labor the public production cannot see: one body is credited, two visible performers appear, and three persons shape the work. The structure resembles other EPISODE 3.0 stories in which “solo” or “natural” performance hides networks of preparation. Here invisibility is literal and legal-social at once. There is no role under which a dead, unseen collaborator can be contracted, credited, or safely staged.

## Care, substitution, and the endangered body

Saori's generosity repeatedly risks collapsing care into substitution. When an accident appears to threaten the spirit's location, she moves to protect her even though the spirit has no ordinary vulnerable body. Saori's physical body takes the impact. Later, when Saori weakens during rehearsal, the spirit offers to take over because she understands how much Saori wants to perform.

Both acts are loving and both confuse whose exposure is at stake. Saori treats her durable body as expendable for a person who cannot be struck in the same way. The spirit treats competent imitation as a way to preserve Saori's intention even if Saori cannot remain present. In each case, the person being protected risks disappearing from the solution.

The rehearsal receives praise from the Player, who does not know that its stronger performance partly belongs to the spirit. Professional approval thus rewards an unsafe arrangement. Saori wants to prove that Ei's support has succeeded; the spirit wants to protect Saori's opportunity; Ei wants the stage to proceed. Shared goodwill makes it easier for each person to overlook the body's warning signs.

The episode marks the threshold through memory. Saori says she is excited to go onstage for the first time, then covers the contradiction when Ei reminds her that she has performed before. Her speech suggests that the spirit's perspective is bleeding into hers, or that control has become uncertain. The source does not provide a clinical mechanism, but Ei correctly recognizes the change as more than harmless alternation.

## A fan who chooses not to take the stage

The spirit understands Ei's fear before Ei says it: growing desire may lead her to take Saori over. She admits that merely watching had once seemed enough, but kindness and collaboration made her want to perform with them. The desire is not condemned as impure. Ei says that she and Saori also came to accept the fan. The danger arises because the only available form of participation uses another person's body.

The spirit decides to leave before the live. She frames the period of happiness as something that could never continue and worries that she has exploited the pair's kindness. There is pathos in that self-exclusion, but it should not be romanticized as the only ethical place for a fan. Her continued possession cannot be justified; her personhood and creative contribution need not vanish with it.

Ei initially cannot articulate the distinction. After the spirit leaves Saori, Ei finds her again at the original stage-side location but can no longer hear her. She finally says what she should have said when dialogue was possible: thank you for being my fan; the contact was confusing, but it made her happy. This is Ei's growth as an idol. She moves from managing an anomaly to directly recognizing the audience member as someone whose regard mattered.

The timing matters. Gratitude comes after the fan has surrendered access. It does not bargain for her withdrawal or make acknowledgment payment for obedience. Ei also apologizes because she did not speak while an answer could be heard. Recognition without reciprocity is incomplete, even when one side is materially invisible.

Ei then feels something touch her hand. She doubts the perception, but the final live gives it practical force.

## Participation without possession

On show day Saori cannot remember the choreography developed while the spirit shared or controlled her body. She trembles and takes Ei's hand. Ei recognizes the warmth as continuous with the touch she felt after saying goodbye. She asks Saori for her other hand; Saori then feels an unseen person holding it. Ei says that person will lead her.

The resulting formation is precise. Saori remains in her own body. Ei remains beside her. The spirit does not speak over Saori or substitute for her performance; she maintains contact as a third point in the chain. Saori and Ei explicitly call the live a three-person stage.

This is not total separation. The fan still crosses the boundary between audience and performance and still supplies labor without public credit. But the mode of contact has changed from occupancy to accompaniment. She can participate without making Saori absent. The narrative's bodily image—one visible friend holding one hand and an invisible collaborator holding the other—turns consent and non-substitution into blocking rather than a slogan.

The live begins late enough to alarm the producer and Player, then receives extraordinary response. The Player senses a stronger presence from the visible pair but does not learn its source. Reports later circulate that the stream contains various spectral images. The source does not establish which spirits appear in which images, so the original fan should not be individually identified in the footage without further evidence.

Public fascination turns the performance back into opportunity. Viewers ask for more, and the producer begins planning separate solo stages for Saori and Ei. A three-person collaborative achievement produces two individual career offers. Once again, institutional categories redistribute collective work into marketable persons.

## “Calling” in both directions

The title can be read across several movements without choosing only one. The idol's distributed image calls to an audience beyond the living people an agency can count. The fan calls to the idol through attention and eventually speech. The stage becomes a vocation or calling for someone who did not have such fulfillment while alive. The act of address also functions almost as invocation: Ei's belated thanks and Saori's need allow an absent hand to return.

The chapter refuses a simple model in which idols transmit and fans receive. The spirit's long attention makes her able to teach posture and remember choreography. Saori's hospitality lets her become a participant. Ei's recognition enables a less harmful relation. Audience labor is constitutive of the performance, not merely a reaction after it.

At the same time, devotion does not generate entitlement. The spirit's sincerity, loneliness, and real contribution cannot authorize her to occupy Saori. The central ethical achievement is not closeness alone; it is finding a form of closeness that keeps all three persons present.

This makes `アイドル・コーリング` an apt final chapter for EPISODE 3.0. Across the unit, performers repeatedly become individuals through relation rather than separation: units absorb distinct skills, friends care without becoming interchangeable, and local stages reach beyond their size. The spirit is the sharpest limit case. She is socially absent, physically dead, and institutionally impossible, yet still alters an idol's work. The story asks not whether such an audience counts, but how counting her can avoid consuming someone else.

## The recurrence the happy ending cannot contain

One week later, Saori trips and another spirit speaks through her body. This new figure also says that meeting Ei is an honor. Ei runs away in exasperation while the spirit admires even her running. The tag restores comedy, but it prevents the first relationship's solution from becoming a system.

Saori is now an involuntary access channel for a potentially unlimited audience. Her initial consent applied to one apparently benign spirit under uncertain conditions; it cannot authorize future possession by strangers. Ei's visibility to spirits may be increasing, or Saori may have become especially permeable, but the chapter does not explain the mechanism.

The recurrence also changes the meaning of success. The first spirit learned to accompany without occupying and helped produce a celebrated stage. Public demand follows. Yet the increased attention may bring more invisible fans whose first act reproduces the original violation. Popularity expands access pressure faster than the characters develop safeguards.

No exorcism, disclosure protocol, medical response, workplace accommodation, or consent procedure exists at the endpoint. The Player still asks whether Saori merely hit her head. Ei's scream is funny because she recognizes that the private care labor will begin again. It is also an accurate statement that the burden has not ended.
