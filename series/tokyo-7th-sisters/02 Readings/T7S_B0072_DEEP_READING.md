# T7S_B0072 — the invisible hand

## Result

The “invisible hand” is not impersonal fate. Episode 3 carefully gives it offices, phones, trade relationships, missing documents, friendly language, and a human intermediary. Dream★Age can suppress or circulate scandal, exchange private stories for access, cancel work through cross-sector ties, and convert Nanasta's inadequate legal structure into dependency. Its power is invisible because responsibility is distributed, not because nobody acts.

The Player initially refuses Reiji Gotoda's partnership. Dream★Age then removes the Rainbow Dome and the group's industry work, after which Gotoda offers protection from the crisis his network helped create. The contract is accepted under manufactured scarcity. The group retains a choice in the thin sense that alternatives can be spoken; the available future has already been narrowed by coercive power.

The Player's own justification gives the capture moral force. He correctly sees that asking the girls to share staff labor can become another way adults offload responsibility. He then overcorrects: shielding them becomes deciding for them, and professional growth becomes eventual departure from the home they chose. The episode's central trap is not adult care itself. It is the conversion of care into jurisdiction.

## Protection is offered after vulnerability is demonstrated

Gotoda introduces himself with a photograph of Sumire and the Player, claims to be suppressing it, praises the AXiS live, and offers Dream★Age partnership. He describes a network that trades access and private stories across industries and treats manufactured scandal as promotion. Afterward Gotoda privately orders the planned operation. Rainbow Dome is canceled and work across the field disappears. Missing ownership/registration documents provide a legal pretext, while coordinated industry withdrawal supplies the practical force. The evidence supports Dream★Age participation and a wider network; it does not make every participating worker equally informed or prove one complete legal conspiracy.

The sequence defines coercive protection: first make independent life unsafe, then present dependence as shelter from “society.” The rescuer and threat are institutionally continuous even when no single sentence admits the whole causal chain.

## Handmade governance meets its unformalized underside

Gotoda's attack succeeds because Nanasta's chosen informality has material gaps. The owner is missing; important registration and shareholder documents cannot be produced; lateral alliances are weak; the Player is overextended. Those deficiencies are not invented by Dream★Age, but Dream★Age weaponizes them.

The episode therefore refuses an easy purity defense. A handmade institution still needs legal ownership, record custody, staffing, privacy practice, and allies. The members' Episode 1 desire to share management was a valid recognition of that need. What fails is not their participation but the absence of durable procedure before a hostile actor arrives.

## The manager turns a shield into a wall

The group considers rebuilding from zero with fans rather than accepting Dream★Age. Hime first voices the reset route. The Player stops them. He argues that shared governance has forced performers to carry staff burdens and limited their potential; adults should shield them so they can fly farther, perhaps eventually beyond Nanasta.

Part of this is accountable insight. He did allow exhaustion and legal fragility to accumulate. But his response silently changes the object of the group's earlier consent. They asked to preserve and co-govern Nanasta. He chooses a partner that will decide their work, because he interprets adulthood as bearing the decision alone. Haru hesitates: her desire is not unlimited reach but a live together, wherever it occurs.

The static state associated with the manager's shielding declaration is less important as emotion than as institutional position. He stands within the group scene while speaking a future for everyone. The episode makes benevolent authorship the hinge through which hostile power enters.

## Gotoda is both believer and operator

Gotoda says the AXiS live moved him and praises the group's sincerity. He also arranges pressure, treats scandal as a tool, and calls the members children who require adult protection from society's invisible hand. Admiration does not mitigate the coercion; it explains why possession can feel to him like preservation.

A later exchange with Sand Mountain Toru links Gotoda to the dissolution of Seven Sisters and to an exile produced by the “Seventh incident.” Exact responsibility remains partly attributed at this point. What is established is that Gotoda understands historical destruction as evidence that straight, unprotected action cannot survive. He will preserve what moved him by placing it under the same kind of control that made its predecessor untenable.
