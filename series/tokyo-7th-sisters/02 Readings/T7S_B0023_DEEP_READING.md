# T7S_B0023 — together only after two people appear

## Result

KARAKURI returns to Grand Fest not to accept the idol category or complete Dr. Serge's command, but to dismantle the mechanism that made perfect fusion the condition of existence. Hitoha and Futaba interrupt their own “Winning Day,” sing alone for the first time, and then choose to sing together again. Separation is neither the cure nor the goal. The performance makes togetherness revisable and therefore chosen.

## Naming the category boundary

The Player says he will not call someone an idol when that person rejects the name. This is a small but significant advance over Coney's recruitment reflex. Shared hot pot establishes relation; it does not purchase categorical authority. KARAKURI can join the same social world while remaining artists by self-designation.

Coney describes the post-collapse field as one in which fixed concepts are changing and girls grow both as idols and as people. She also confirms that one former Seven Sisters member was an orphan and is alive and moving forward. The source does not identify that member. The limited fact matters because it breaks the equation between orphan history and permanent capture without turning a predecessor into a biographical solution for the twins.

## The thread (`イト`)

Three months after the opening interview, Hitoha and Futaba recognize that they have always sung for “someone,” even when they declared the pair self-sufficient. Collaborations were enjoyable. Admitting that fact feels dangerous because changing purpose might negate the selves who survived by insisting on victory.

The hostile interviewer argues that idols and 4U lack pride and resolve. Memories of Ume, Haru, Musubi, and the separate bowls offer another account: a person can revise purpose without making prior endurance unreal. The twins decide to cut the thread that binds them to inherited adult desire and return for a good stage, not to abolish idols.

The interviewer is revealed as a former participant in the Dr. Serge system who engineered the distorted coverage. He treats the twins as the remaining vessel for the dreams of adults whose project failed. “Thread” therefore joins coercion and attachment: an external string manipulates a doll, but relation also links one person to another. The arc cannot solve the problem by cutting every tie. It must distinguish imposed purpose from chosen connection.

## Decomposing the machine onstage

Nanasta performs successfully, while the crowd and online audience remain divided. KARAKURI begins “Winning Day,” the song of empty victory. Then Futaba leaves and Hitoha sings `B.A.A.B.` alone; Hitoha leaves and Futaba sings `-Zero` alone. The reviewed stills show distinct body assets and gestures, not merely swapped face overlays. The production that introduced a fused holographic unit now displays two named performers in sequence.

The solo acts are not framed as technical improvement. Their imperfection is revelatory. The twins deliberately break the perfect two-in-one output to say that even extremely close twins possess feelings that do not become one. They are not dolls carrying other people's wishes. Each asks to be recognized as a person before the pair resumes.

This sequence avoids two easy endings. Permanent separation would make individuality depend on abandoning the sister. Immediate reunion without solo speech would preserve fusion as compulsory. The staged interval lets each be absent while the other continues; their later synchronization becomes an act rather than an ontology.

## Apology without innocence

Hitoha and Futaba acknowledge that changing KARAKURI may hurt fans who loved the old form. They apologize as inexperienced, imperfect people and accept the burden of continuing. The gesture neither grants fans ownership nor declares all reception abusive. It locates responsibility on both sides of a public relation: performers may change, and they remain answerable for how that change reaches people.

The former producer resists, then tells them to protect their own song. His concession does not erase manipulation, but it prevents the antagonist from being reduced to pure external evil. He too must relinquish the dream he placed inside the children.

## Victory after victory ceases to rule

KARAKURI wins the MVA by an overwhelming margin after declaring victory insufficient. Members of the rival groups have voted for one another. The result matters precisely because it is no longer the only authorization to exist. Hitoha and Futaba can now want to surpass Seven Sisters as a future aim they choose rather than an annihilation command inherited from Dr. Serge.

The twins remain frightened that closeness will end in abandonment. They retain “rival” as a defensive boundary. Sawara and Musubi tell the Player that public success does not remove a child's need for adult praise. He runs after them and says, by name, that Hitoha and Futaba worked hard and did well. They cry. The arc places this ordinary adult sentence after the grand award because recognition of performance cannot substitute for recognition of the children who performed.

## Exit state

- Hitoha and Futaba explicitly reject being dolls or vessels for inherited adult purpose.
- Solo songs establish distinct persons; renewed synchronized singing establishes chosen togetherness.
- KARAKURI retains its non-idol category and its sister relation.
- The former Dr. Serge participant is identified as the manipulator of the media crisis and partially relinquishes his claim.
- Cross-voting weakens the contest's zero-sum frame; KARAKURI wins after victory has ceased to govern its right to exist.
- The Player praises each twin by name as a child/person, supplying adult recognition that the award cannot.
- Friendship remains desired and feared; “rival” continues as a protective distance rather than a final social resolution.
