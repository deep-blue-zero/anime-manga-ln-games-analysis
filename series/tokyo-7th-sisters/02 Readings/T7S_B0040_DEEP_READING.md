# T7S_B0040 — a world stage small enough to be home

## Result

Jeda's solo debut is announced for Neo Seventh Dome with full support from Royal Medix, the global record company whose earlier scout she refused. She accepts the opportunity and promises her younger sister abroad the greatest song on the greatest stage. The company then withdraws its support after an internal faction asks why it should help someone who rejected it. Jeda experiences the cancellation not primarily as lost prestige but as her own failure to keep a promise. Her chosen family at Nanasta helps her revise what “greatest” means: the TANIMACHI shopping street that she and her sister already love can matter more than a legendary dome. The replacement live converts a corporate spectacle into a local, transnational family event without shrinking Jeda's global ambition. The street becomes a world stage precisely because her song and its photograph can travel from an embedded community to an absent sister. The repair is real but limited. Royal Medix's retaliation is never reversed, and friends override Jeda's explicit request for solitude in the name of family. The chapter celebrates chosen kinship while leaving the coercive powers of both corporation and care visible.

## A local archive made for someone elsewhere

The chapter begins before the debut offer, with Jeda photographing the TANIMACHI shopping street for her younger sister. The merchants know Jeda by name. She asks permission before photographing them, explains who will receive the pictures, and receives playful consent. This is not an anonymous tourist extracting picturesque surfaces. She is a recognized participant making a local place legible across distance.

Jeda nevertheless uses the language of discovery: the street is full of photogenic subjects, and her sister has become captivated by downtown atmosphere and Japanese culture. The sequence can romanticize the shopping district as “authentic Japan.” Its relational structure complicates that gaze. The shopkeepers answer back, joke, pose, and know that one merchant has an admirer abroad. The image is co-produced rather than taken invisibly.

This consent-forward photography matters within EPISODE 3.0. Earlier, Kyoko's animal-program farewell was captured by secretly running cameras in the name of candid authenticity. Here Jeda repeatedly announces the camera and names its audience. At the final live she asks the whole crowd before making a group image. Mediation does not have to depend on subjects not knowing that they are becoming content.

The photographs perform family maintenance. Jeda's sister cannot share Jeda's daily physical environment, so Jeda sends pieces of that environment outward. She promises to fill her holo-device's memory while traveling Japan. The archive is not a substitute for the sister; it is a repeated practice through which Jeda keeps their worlds in contact.

## The dome as inherited greatness

Nanasta announces Jeda's solo debut as an escalating sequence of superlatives. The achievement is not only singing alone but beginning at Neo Seventh Dome, the venue where Seven Sisters held their farewell live. The Player calls it legendary and imagines Jeda's voice reaching the whole world from there. Venue, predecessor memory, financial scale, and global reach are collapsed into one obvious definition of success.

Jeda initially finds the scale excessive. Sawara notices the material contradiction more bluntly: Nanasta cannot afford such a production. The Player confirms that the agency's finances could not support it and reveals Royal Medix as the enabling patron. A supposedly personal milestone is therefore dependent on capital from a much larger institution.

Royal Medix's interest is not new. Its employee remains impressed by Jeda even after she declined a prior recruitment offer, and has persuaded the company to underwrite the live. The bounded chapter establishes the earlier refusal but does not restate all of Jeda's reasons for it. What it makes unmistakable is the resulting power asymmetry. Royal Medix can transform a Nanasta solo debut into a dome event simply by agreeing to pay for it.

The Player asks Jeda whether she wants the opportunity; she enthusiastically consents. That decision should not be rewritten as vanity or capitulation. The dome is a real chance and a site important to her sister, who loves Seven Sisters. Jeda can accept institutional resources while remaining attached to Nanasta and to her existing relationships. The problem is not scale itself. It is that scale can be granted and withdrawn by actors who treat prior refusal as an injury to corporate entitlement.

## A solo announced collectively

Jeda learns of the debut through a surprise celebration staged by Kajika, Sawara, Mimi, and the Player. Before rehearsal schedules can begin, the women insist on another party at their dormitory. Kajika brings a celebratory fish supplied by her father. Jeda calls Kajika her Japanese younger sister; Kajika's sisters and friend extend the kinship outward.

As in Kazumi's immediately preceding solo chapter, the institutional word “solo” conceals a social production. Jeda will occupy the stage alone, but news, food, rehearsal presence, sponsorship, venue access, and emotional confidence are organized by other people. She explicitly tells Kajika that the celebration gives power to what Kajika calls an already perfect rehearsal.

The parallel also clarifies a difference. Kazumi's crisis concerned whether she could produce the inner state an expert expected to hear. Jeda's preparation receives no comparable correction. Kajika testifies that rehearsal already sounds complete. The threat arrives from outside performance: an institution revokes the conditions under which the planned performance could occur.

Jeda calls her biological sister before celebrating and makes a promise. She will deliver the best song from the best stage, partly for this person who shares her love of Japan and Seven Sisters. The sister's audible side of the conversation is absent; her joy is reported through Jeda's responses. The scene establishes Jeda's interpretation of the call, not an independently voiced reaction.

## Sponsorship as conditional recognition

Royal Medix cancels its support shortly before the live. The Player reports that people inside the company objected to helping someone who had rejected its scout, and that the supportive employee resisted but could not withstand the pressure. This is retaliation disguised as resource allocation. The corporation's recognition of talent is conditional on ownership or compliance: if Jeda does not accept the career path it offers, some decision-makers believe she should not benefit from its infrastructure.

The episode does not flatten every employee into that position. The original contact argues for Jeda and later attends the replacement live. This distinction makes the institutional critique stronger, not weaker. An individual can recognize the injustice, apologize, and remain unable to prevent the organization from enacting it.

Jeda responds by saying the cancellation is her fault. That conclusion accepts the corporation's punitive logic: because she exercised refusal, the resulting punishment belongs to her. The Player does not endorse that judgment, but neither he nor the chapter directly names it as victim-blaming. The narrative moves quickly toward finding another venue.

The loss is material. Nanasta still cannot finance the dome. The company does not restore support, compensate the preparation already undertaken, or answer publicly for the decision. A satisfying local live cannot retroactively make the pressure harmless. “Result all right,” as the Player later calls it, describes Jeda's good outcome; it is not institutional accountability.

## The promise beneath the prestige

After asking to be alone, Jeda is unable to call her sister. She describes herself as a bad older sister because she cannot deliver what she promised. The cancellation hurts through relation. Had the dome represented only status, a smaller venue would simply be a lesser professional result. Because it represents a gift to her sister, Jeda experiences the loss as moral failure.

Mimi separates the sister's likely joy from the venue. Surely, she suggests, Jeda's debut matters wherever it happens. Jeda agrees implicitly but returns to the exact promise: the greatest stage and greatest song. Sawara's solution does not ask Jeda to abandon that language. It disputes the ranking system by which a dome automatically occupies its highest position.

TANIMACHI is the place Jeda and her sister already love. Her sister knows it through the stream of images Jeda sends; Jeda knows it through repeated embodied exchange with its people. A performance there can be “greater” because the venue already contains the relationship for which the promise was made.

The reversal is not an anti-ambition lesson. At the street live Jeda still says her voice will cross seas, mountains, and sky to reach the world. She does not conclude that global circulation is corrupt or that an idol should remain small. The chapter instead unbundles global reach from spectacular infrastructure. A local event can travel through song, memory, and images; a large venue can be emotionally irrelevant despite its capacity.

## Chosen family and the right to intrude

Jeda explicitly asks for time alone after the cancellation. Kajika responds by summoning Sawara and Mimi. Sawara openly describes what they are doing: family enters the heart with shoes on and hugs even when someone wants solitude. The action succeeds. Jeda laughs, speaks, and eventually recognizes a new possibility.

Its success does not turn boundary violation into a universal law of care. Jeda had articulated a clear preference. The others decide that their family status authorizes them to override it. The scene treats loving intrusion as an older sister's prerogative; the same logic could easily deny a distressed person needed space.

The sequence is best read as a negotiated risk inside established relationships, not proof that consent becomes unnecessary among family. Sawara's exuberance is checked by Mimi's calmer inquiry. Jeda is not forced to disclose everything immediately; the fuller account emerges later in the bath. She ultimately accepts the “older sisters” formulation and joins the plan. Yet acceptance after the fact cannot be projected backward as permission already granted.

This ethical friction is consistent with EPISODE 3.0's recurring concern with care. Musubi's collapse showed that unnoticed caretaking can become exploitative. Sakura's encounter with Seven Sisters showed that even benevolent entertainers must eventually respect a stop signal. CASQUETTE'S rescued Chizuru through invasive acts that endangered privacy. Here love works through unwanted proximity, and the chapter celebrates the result without dissolving the question of method.

## A family made through transitive relation

Sawara's “older sisters” argument is comically transitive. If Kajika is Jeda's Japanese younger sister, and Sawara is Kajika's older sister, then Sawara becomes Jeda's older sister too. Mimi is folded into the same unit. Biology supplies the vocabulary, but chosen affiliation supplies the membership.

This family does not replace Jeda's sister abroad. It expands the number of people through whom Jeda can continue being her sister. Kajika mobilizes care when Jeda cannot accept her hand directly. Sawara generates an alternative from knowledge of Jeda's local attachment. Mimi helps distinguish the lost venue from the person the promise was meant to reach.

Before the replacement live, Jeda returns to the moment when she refused Kajika's offered hand. She apologizes and thanks her, then says she loves Kajika and the others. Kajika does not demand repayment, apology, or emotional performance; Jeda's recovered smile is enough for her. This exchange repairs a local rupture rather than pretending it never occurred.

The chapter's title, “Diamond Family,” consequently names more than Jeda's surname. Family is the network that refracts one broken promise into another route. It includes a sister elsewhere, a Japanese sister, self-appointed older sisters, merchants, Nanasta colleagues, and—more provisionally—a corporate employee who attends as a fan when he cannot act effectively as sponsor.

## The photograph that fills memory

The shopping-street audience includes the merchants from the opening, Nanasta friends, the Player, and the Royal Medix contact. Jeda names the street as important to herself and her beloved family, performs her debut song there, and then asks the audience for a group photograph to show her sister. The crowd audibly agrees before she takes it.

The image completes the chapter's opening circuit. At first, Jeda photographs individual local figures to send the texture of the street abroad. At the end, the street looks back toward the camera as a gathered public. Her solo debut becomes a collective portrait.

The photograph also fills the holo-device's memory. An embedded recollection reminds the reader that Jeda once intended to fill it with images for her sister while traveling Japan. The full memory therefore marks the accumulation of a life and relationship, not a technical inconvenience alone. Jeda immediately treats it as a threshold rather than an ending: more memories will begin from here.

No response from the sister follows within the bounded chapter. The analysis cannot claim that she sees the picture, hears the live, or judges the revised promise fulfilled. What the source establishes is Jeda's confident address toward her and a material artifact intended to cross the distance.

## What the replacement does not erase

The replacement live succeeds on its own terms. It turns local attachment into artistic scale, makes chosen family explicit, and gives Jeda a debut she actively wants rather than a consolation she reluctantly accepts. The Royal Medix employee's attendance as an individual fan shows that institutional role does not exhaust personal allegiance.

It does not restore the lost budget, prevent future corporate retaliation, or explain how Nanasta arranged the street event on very short notice. The chapter gives no attendance count, financial result, distribution record, or evidence that the live reaches a world audience. “World stage” is an interpretive description of its relational geography, not a claim about measured circulation.

The story also leaves intact Jeda's tendency to translate another party's coercion into her own failure. Friends relieve the immediate guilt by changing the venue, but no one explicitly tells her that rejecting recruitment did not make her responsible for Royal Medix's reprisal. The familial solution protects her from one consequence while the underlying power remains.

The chapter therefore does more than oppose a warm community to a cold company. Both formations can cross personal boundaries. The company treats refusal as disloyalty and controls resources; family treats a request for solitude as an invitation to intervene. The decisive difference in this instance is not that family never exerts pressure. It is that its intervention returns agency to Jeda: she identifies the new venue, apologizes and thanks on her own terms, addresses both local and distant audiences, and chooses the meaning of “greatest.”
