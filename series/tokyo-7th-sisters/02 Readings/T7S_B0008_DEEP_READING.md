# Tokyo 7th Sisters — B0008 deep reading

## The prince she remembered was kind: Sui recovers care without surrendering every boyish form

## 0. Result, scope, and integrity

B0008 closes the complete nine-episode native Main chapter `300080`, マイ・プリンス・フォーエバー. 神城スイ begins by calling herself a girl who uses `ボク`, excels at swimming, and intends to become “a boy above boys”—a prince strong enough to protect everyone. She also knows that she has forgotten why she wanted that role and what she meant to do with it. The chapter first narrows princehood into victory: when the Player reaches a drowning child before her, she treats rescue as defeat and pursues him through repeated performance contests. Its decisive recollection restores what the win/loss model omitted. The admired childhood boy not only defeated a larger aggressor; he understood that opponent, learned the motive behind the aggression, and became his friend. Sui recognizes that she remembered strength and forgot kindness to both the threatened girl and the defeated boy.

The source simultaneously makes Sui's ideal a first love. Musubi interprets her boyish identification as a way of hiding embarrassment about a boy who remained in her heart, Hime calls her an ordinary girl in love, and Sui accepts that she had been in love. That is a consequential participant interpretation which Sui partly adopts. It is not omniscient proof that every boyish word, aspiration, bodily discomfort, or practice was false. The chapter itself prevents that total conversion: after the diagnosis Sui still uses `ボク`, chooses a cool song for boys because she wants to sing it at full force, preserves the feeling and effort that formed her, and receives Hime's final reciprocal address as `王子様`. The resolution is gender-constraining and heteronormative in several peer statements, but its own remainder is plural.

Sui's recruitment is more self-propelled than Coney's initial maneuver. Coney crookedly converts Sui's challenge to the Player into a contest against his idols, but Sui later sends the challenge letter, requests the rematch, discloses the childhood history, chooses the final song, accepts defeat, and asks the Player to help make her a full-fledged girl in the immediate idol-development context. The chapter treats his acceptance and their handshake as her Nanasta entry, and Hime says they will work together from now on. No formal contract, ordinary lesson, audience-facing debut, stable labor, or complete performance is shown. Each contest and assent remains local.

The Player's prince-like status is ethically mixed. He acts first to rescue a drowning child and later offers attention and recognition. He also repeatedly reaches toward Sui after she has explicitly said that men must not touch her. Her reflexive punches are harmful, she immediately apologizes, and the response persists even after cognitive insight; none of those facts makes his boundary failure disappear or makes the punch harmless. The coda refuses a cure narrative: Sui accepts the handshake, strikes reflexively again, apologizes again, and Musubi observes both that the “male allergy” remains and that the Player has not learned.

## 1. Descriptive reconstruction

### 1.1 A protection ideal has lost its own history (`201000701`)

Sui introduces herself through conjunction rather than disguise: `一応、女の子`, swimmer, faster than boys, and determined to become a prince-like “boy above boys.” Her prince is energetic, radiant, strong, and able to protect everyone. She immediately marks a gap: she no longer remembers why she wanted to become one, what she hoped to do, or what she felt then. [E01]

At the pool, the Player notices Sui while scouting. A child calls for help. Sui begins to intervene, but the Player jumps in fully clothed and reaches him first. She praises the act, then converts it into a contest she lost and promises to defeat him next time so she can become the prince who helps everyone. [E02] The rescue supplies real prince-like conduct; Sui's competitive grammar makes priority rather than the child's safety the measure afterward.

Coney hears `王子様`, calls the sound nostalgic, and refuses to explain. She later says a girl once called “prince” was her acquaintance and that meeting another at Nanasta feels like fate. [E02, E15] This is a two-step clue about Coney's past, not an identified person or established Seven Sisters relation.

### 1.2 Coney turns rivalry into recruitment, and Hime begins from gender policing (`201000702`–`201000703`)

Before meeting Sui again, Hime says a woman using `ボク` cannot be cute and expresses personal dislike of princes. The Player internally notices the inconsistency with Hime's own `オレ`, but no one corrects her aloud. [E03] At the pool Sui renews her challenge, initially proposing swimming. Coney calls competing in one's own specialty unfair, then defines idol production as the Player's specialty: defeating one of his idols in performance would therefore defeat him. The Player explicitly calls the device crooked. Sui accepts it and says she will sing; Hime reluctantly accepts Coney's “ore/boku battle.” [E03]

The performance is omitted. The next episode opens with Sui's loss, which she accepts. Asked what prince means, she gives a reduced account: a cool, strong boy who defeated a bully. Hime asks what a girl would do by becoming him. The Player offers Sui a hand; Sui says a man must not casually touch her, strikes reflexively, apologizes, and runs. [E04] The source establishes both the explicit boundary and the harmful reaction. Neither licenses the other.

### 1.3 A self-authored rematch makes body conflict explicit (`201000704`–`201000705`)

Sui sends a written challenge and asks the group to return to the pool. Hime volunteers herself and Musubi; Musubi protests Hime's use of her name but accompanies the group. Sui explicitly asks for another performance contest. [E05] The second performance is again omitted. Sui loses and says a prince must not lose or cry “like a girl.” Coney and Musubi respond by calling her body, atmosphere, and song female and cute. Sui says she is already troubled that her body is becoming more feminine, equates the female category offered to her with weakness, and insists on the strong prince. [E06]

Hime asks whether Sui's prince was only strong and points to the towel the Player has kept offering. Sui refuses the towel and repeats that a man must not touch her; the Player persists because she might catch cold. Sui strikes him, apologizes, calls the response an “allergy,” and runs crying. [E06] Hime's question productively dislodges strength from princehood. The Player's concern does not override Sui's stated control over touch.

### 1.4 The remembered prince cared for the opponent too (`201000706`)

Hime and Musubi search for Sui. Musubi asks why she admires the prince so deeply; Sui agrees to tell them. [E07] In elementary school, a transferred boy appeared exceptional to her. A larger, rough boy was persistently bothering a girl. The admired boy fought him fairly and won, and Sui vowed to become strong enough to protect everyone. Hime's prior question lets her recover the omitted aftermath: the winner spoke to the aggressor, understood that he was acting roughly because he liked the girl and did not know what to do, and became his friend. He had been kind not only to the girl but to the opponent. Sui explicitly says she forgot that important fact by thinking only about strength and victory. [E08]

The account also contains attraction: Sui felt the boy was unlike other boys from the first meeting, breathed quickly near him, could not think, and sometimes could not sleep. Musubi calls this love. Sui first distinguishes wanting to be him from loving him. Musubi then proposes that the boy remained so embarrassingly present in Sui's heart that she tried to become him to conceal the fact, and calls this the source of the touch response. Sui answers, `ボク、恋してたんだ`. [E08–E09]

This exchange establishes a newly accepted self-description, but the causal explanation remains attributed to Musubi and seconded by Hime. Musubi's own history gives her real experience with self-presentation and hidden desire; it may also encourage overconfident projection from one structure onto another. The chapter supplies no independent narrator who resolves every aspect of Sui's gendered aspiration.

Hime calls Sui a normal girl in love. When embarrassment overwhelms her, Hime proposes a performance and Musubi agrees to sing there; Sui quietly says yes. [E09] The song is omitted.

### 1.5 Peer advice constrains Sui, but her final test preserves choice (`201000707`–`201000708`)

Sui says the unseen song helped. Musubi calls it a lovely girl's song made cute by love and says Sui had been deceiving herself by trying to become prince. Hime treats storybook princes as overbearing rescuers, tells Sui to leave that role to others, and says she should become what she herself can be. [E10] The advice opposes victory obsession and makes room for receiving care, but it also assumes the available self is properly female and the prince role properly belongs elsewhere.

Sui says she no longer knows what she can become. Hime tells her to express herself fully once more before someone who will receive it—the Player. [E10] Sui does not merely accept a feminized song chosen for her. She requests a last duel and chooses “the song he was good at,” immediately correcting the phrasing to a boys' song sung by boys, because she wants to sing a cool song with full force. Hime, Musubi, and the Player accept the terms. [E11] This is the chapter's clearest action-level counterexample to total conversion.

### 1.6 Cool and cute are joined, entry is accepted, and the reflex remains (`201000709`)

The final performance is omitted. Sui concedes complete defeat and thanks the group. In one branch the Player credits Nanasta's daily practice; in the other he calls her clean admission “man among men,” leaving Sui unsure whether to be pleased. Both branches converge before his consequential judgment: Sui drew out the song's appeal better than anyone, was cool and cute, and showed herself worthy of aspiring to princehood. [E12] The statement joins rather than orders the two modes.

Sui says the Player resembles the childhood boy and that a prince-like person is now nearby. The self who was excessively fixated on becoming prince is gone, but she explicitly says the old feeling will never be forgotten and that all her effort remains in her. She decides next to “properly fall in love”; she has no present target but believes she can now face the feeling. [E13]

She then asks the Player, `ボクを、女にしてくださいっ！` The surrounding recruitment/performance exchange and his reply make the operative request idol/girl development, while the group comically hears sexual implication. He promises to make her a full-fledged girl and offers a handshake. Sui accepts with `よろしくお願いします`, reflexively strikes him, and apologizes. Musubi says the “male allergy” is not yet cured and wonders whether the Player lacks learning ability. [E13–E14] The source treats the exchange as accepted Nanasta entry, not as literal sex, cure, or an exhaustive contract.

Coney then says she once knew another girl called prince but supplies no name. Sui greets Hime as princess; Hime rejects that title yet says they will work together and answers with `王子様`. [E15] Their reciprocal naming transforms initial gender policing into peer recognition without deleting the prejudicial route by which they arrived there.

## 2. Analytical findings

### 2.1 Princehood is an ethical aspiration before it is a gender verdict

At entry, `王子様` joins gendered identification, athletic competition, protection, radiant style, and an unknown remembered origin. No single term exhausts it. Later romance changes the meaning of the ideal, but does not make its aspirational or ethical dimensions retroactively unreal. The childhood account's decisive correction is not “the prince was male.” It is that Sui copied his victory while forgetting his care, understanding, and reconciliation.

This distinction matters because the chapter's strongest moral content is portable beyond masculinity. The admired boy intervenes, protects, learns why the aggressor acted badly, and restores a relation. Sui's opening wish to protect everyone preserved one part of that model; her loss fixation had reduced the means to strength and winning. Remembering the defeated opponent converts princehood from dominance into relational responsibility.

### 2.2 The love diagnosis is adopted testimony, not omniscient identity adjudication

Musubi's reading fits several facts: Sui's somatic attraction, sleeplessness, embarrassment, touch reactivity, and eventual statement that she was in love. Sui's adoption gives it high evidentiary weight for the first-love recognition. It is still a proposed causal totalization when Musubi says she became the boy to hide the boy in her heart. Neither Musubi nor Hime can prove that every use of `ボク`, every athletic practice, discomfort with feminization, or prince aspiration has that sole cause.

The source itself supplies contrary remainder after the diagnosis. Sui keeps `ボク`; chooses a boys' song as her fullest self-expression; wants coolness as well as receiving cuteness; preserves her formative feeling and effort; and is welcomed by Hime as prince. The most faithful formulation is therefore asymmetric: love is newly recognized and changes Sui's self-understanding, while boyish identification is revised, bounded, and retained in action rather than exposed as wholly counterfeit.

### 2.3 The chapter's femininity resolution is real, coercive in implication, and incomplete

Hime and Musubi repeatedly treat body, attraction, song, and embarrassment as proof of a normal girlhood that displaces princehood. Sui herself asks to be made a woman and decides to face love. Those facts make a femininity turn real; analysis should not evade them. Yet “girl” is frequently delivered through external classification—`どう見たって女`, “your body is properly female,” “you are more of a woman than anyone”—at the exact moments when Sui voices conflict.

The ending does not require choosing either wholesale affirmation or wholesale rejection of that turn. Sui participates in it, peers constrain it, and the final composition remains mixed. Her self-authored boy-song request and Hime's `王子様` answer are not minor jokes to discard; they are the chapter's internal counterevidence to an exclusive “true girl replaces false boy” thesis.

### 2.4 The Player is prince-like in care and deficient in boundary learning

The rescue supports Sui's later association between the Player and her prince. His recognition of coolness and cuteness also lets her carry more than one mode. But the ethical content she recovers—care that understands another person—sets a higher standard than intention alone. She twice tells him not to touch her; after a renewed refusal of the towel he continues toward contact, and at the final handshake he again fails to account for the known response. The chapter itself lets Musubi name his failure to learn.

Sui's punches remain harmful conduct, marked by his injury and her immediate apologies. Calling the response an allergy and later explaining it through shame does not excuse it. The appropriate paired judgment is: her bodily boundary should have been respected, and striking another person is not made benign by that violation. The continuing reflex after insight also shows that a cognitive reframe is not an embodied cure.

### 2.5 Competition becomes a vehicle for self-authored expression

Sui initially uses victory as ontology: the winner is closer to princehood. Four performance/song events then occur, all elided. The first two produce losses and crisis; the third helps her feel clearer after disclosure; the final one lets her choose the material and receive a judgment that joins cool and cute. By the final duel, the contest matters less as proof of superiority than as a structured request to express the self she still wants to carry.

Because every act is omitted, the chapter establishes decisions, reported effects, and attributed judgments—not singing timbre, technical quality, choreography, audience response, or comparative performance truth. The Player's statement that Sui drew out the song best is character testimony inside the scene, not an independently heard conclusion.

### 2.6 Hime becomes a bridge without becoming an infallible interpreter

Hime begins by denying cuteness to a `ボク`-using girl and asking why a visible girl would become prince. She then asks the question that repairs Sui's memory—whether the admired boy was only strong—searches for her, offers a final self-expression test, agrees to Sui's chosen boys' song, and closes with reciprocal role recognition. Rivalry becomes a bridge because Hime can contest Sui in Sui's own forceful register.

The development does not purify Hime's advice. Calling Sui a normal girl in love and telling her to leave prince work to others retain gender essentialism. The final `王子様` matters precisely because it exceeds those earlier claims: Hime recognizes a peer role she initially rejected, even as the chapter never explicitly retracts the assumptions behind the rejection.

## 3. Counterreadings and adjudication

### 3.1 “Sui was never really a prince; she was only hiding a crush”

This is close to Musubi's explicit explanation and Sui accepts the love component. It overreaches when it erases the rest of the source. The aspiration predates the current discussion, organized sustained swimming effort and protection language, retains value after recognition, determines Sui's chosen final song, and survives in Hime's final address. Current adjudication: first love partly motivates and recontextualizes princehood; it does not exhaust it.

### 3.2 “The chapter straightforwardly affirms a transmasculine identity”

Sui's `ボク`, wish to be a boy above boys, discomfort as her body becomes more feminine, resistance to female classification, and persistence of boyish forms make this a plausible contemporary resonance. The admitted source does not give Sui a transgender identity term or a stable declaration that she is a boy; it repeatedly calls her a girl, has her accept first love and ask to become a woman, and ends in a mixed rather than settled state. Current adjudication: preserve the gender conflict and avoid cis-normalizing certainty, but do not assign an identity the text does not name.

### 3.3 “The Player cures Sui through romantic recognition”

His rescue and final recognition matter, and Sui calls him prince-like. Yet she denies having a current love target, the recruitment request is framed as development, every performance is elided, and the touch reflex persists immediately afterward. Current adjudication: he supplies a relational occasion for reinterpretation and entry; no cure, mutual romance, or complete identity resolution is shown.

### 3.4 “Repeated losses prove Sui lacks idol ability”

The contests are not shown, their rules and judges are not established, and the Player finally says she drew out the song's appeal better than anyone. Loss records the chapter's competitive outcome, not a technical evaluation that can be independently verified. No stable talent, incapacity, or performance-quality claim is available.

## 4. Linguistic, visual, and performed-media state

### 4.1 Textual voice

Sui's `ボク` is stable from prologue through entry. Forceful imperatives, challenge language, sports confidence, repeated `王子様`, and embarrassed stammering coexist rather than form a before/after binary. Her most important revisions are self-corrections: from the admired boy's song to “a boys' song sung by boys,” and from abandoning excessive fixation to preserving the original feeling and effort. Hime's `オレ` makes her policing of Sui's `ボク` a pointed asymmetry; the Player notices it, while the chapter uses their eventual princess/prince exchange to turn opposed roles into peer address.
