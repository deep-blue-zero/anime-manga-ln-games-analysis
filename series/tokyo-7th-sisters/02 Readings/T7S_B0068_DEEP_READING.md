# T7S_B0068 — fall in love with the self that remains

## Result

The fifth installment corrects two forms of abandonment produced in the name of autonomy. Shinju tells Momoka that a person who is lost needs help, not another demand to find an answer alone. Rui tells Manon that no adult category, talent, or experience can let a person escape failure, regret, loneliness, or the self who bears them. Together the conversations relocate agency: it is not isolation, invulnerability, or successful transformation, but the ability to own a desire while other people remain available as witnesses and companions.

Momoka and Shinju finally name their inability to cry after Coney disappeared. They had not trusted that Coney left without abandoning them, so grief could not become loss; it remained a suspended verdict awaiting return. Momoka understands that life continues without making the loss unreal. Rui gives Manon the corresponding memory practice: do not lock the past in a protected room; burn it again and again until it becomes something that cannot melt. Memory becomes durable by being metabolized, not sealed.

The title's “fall in love” is therefore broader than romance. Manon's shy promise of a future date with Rui matters, but it comes after she recovers Haru's childhood words: believe the self who did not flee and who is here now; an idol does not have to be an idol; you are fine as yourself. Love is permission to remain a non-interchangeable person rather than qualification for a role.

## Reading position and evidence boundary

B0064–B0067 were closed before this installment; the final episode was withheld. Both attached transcripts were read at invocation, including the fourteen-page Haru/childhood sequence. Two blocking movies and the recurring inline loops were source-bound; the blocking pair was hash-verified and sampled. The sampled first movie shows food, an amusement ride, and a night drive, while the second revisits the childhood pair and Haru's address. These stills support montage/form observations, not full timing, sound, or romantic-performance claims. All 426 voice-reference pages remain unauditioned.

## Shinju refuses distance disguised as pedagogy

The group searches for Manon. Injured Shinju confronts Momoka. Momoka repeats that Manon must find her own answer; Shinju answers that a lost friend needs someone to go to her. She says Coney is gone and tells Momoka to wake up (opening through roughly log 74).

The confrontation is physically marked by an effect and shocked responses, but the available static/text evidence does not securely identify the exact contact. The analysis therefore does not claim a slap or specify who struck whom. What is explicit is Shinju's verbal intervention and Momoka's destabilization.

Shinju points to the office and Coney's glasses as preserved objects of waiting. Momoka has maintained an address where return remains imaginable. Preservation has become stasis because current relationships are governed as interim conditions until the absent adult repairs them.

The charge also turns back on Shinju. She changed from calling Momoka by name to calling her “manager,” making institutional address a wall against the intimacy in which loss could be spoken. Both women protect themselves by converting relation into role.

## Not crying is failed certainty, not absent love

Momoka later tells Shirayuki that she knew Shinju could not cry after Coney disappeared. Momoka could not cry either. Others could because they trusted Coney had not abandoned them; Momoka and Shinju could not make that assumption (roughly logs 241–316).

This is a participant interpretation, not a clinical rule. Within the episode, inability to cry indexes suspended knowledge: if Coney may return, grief could be betrayal; if she abandoned them, grief could admit worthlessness. Emotion is blocked by an unresolved account of what the departure means.

Momoka recognizes that loss does not disappear and that people nevertheless proceed into another season. She asks Shirayuki to say everything she feels, because Momoka and Shinju once failed to do so. She then joins the search.

The managerial correction is concrete. Momoka does not produce Manon's answer. She changes the conditions around answering: adults will go toward the lost person, peers will disclose, and the institution will not treat isolation as proof of agency.

## Tasha returns Shinju to first-person story

Tasha follows Shinju and challenges the protective formality between Shinju and Momoka. Shinju explains that “manager” is easier than the old intimate name. Tasha says the matter is not somebody else's verdict but Shinju's story, and that Shinju has companions who have watched that story with her (roughly logs 317–372).

The formulation joins ownership and relation. “Your story” does not mean only you may affect or understand it. It means other people may witness, question, and accompany without replacing the first-person position from which desire must be spoken.

The reconstructed Tasha state is an ordinary adult outfit and open, direct expression rather than an idol costume. Her authority in the scene comes from long co-presence, not stage rank.

## Rui's “date” supplies care without demanding disclosure first

Rui finds Manon alone in the rain. She calls their time a date, gets Manon dry clothes and food, and moves through an amusement ride and night drive before the decisive conversation. The recovered movie samples emphasize abundance, shared motion, and travel through the city (roughly logs 373–445).

The sequence does not make consumption a cure. Rui first restores warmth, food, and mobility. She does not require Manon to explain herself before receiving care. The playful “date” reframes rescue without converting Manon into a dependent child.

Manon asks what adulthood feels like. Rui says adults can produce endless beautiful platitudes, then refuses to do so. She narrows the answer to one difficult claim: nobody escapes the self. Talent and experience do not remove failure, regret, or loneliness; those things remain part of one's life. If Manon does not believe herself, no external certification can complete the work (roughly logs 446–477).

This is not a doctrine of self-sufficiency. Rui is saying it while sitting beside Manon after finding, clothing, and feeding her. Self-belief is relationally supported without being transferable.

## Haru's old spell was never role conversion

The attached childhood sequence returns to a young Manon and Shirayuki facing a large audience. Haru tells them to believe the selves who did not flee and who are here now. She calls the advice a spell and says `アイドルは、アイドルじゃなくてもいい`. The remembered meaning resolves as `君は、君でいい` (attached transcript, logs 482–495; return at primary logs 498–500).

The paradox is precise. An idol role is legitimate when it carries the person rather than requiring that person to disappear into the role. “Not idol” does not abolish idol work. It refuses to make successful category performance the condition of human value.

Manon had remembered the wand and forgotten the sentence. The crisis therefore does not require childhood to return unchanged. It requires recovering the relation through which the object once meant trust rather than transformation.

## Burn memory instead of locking it away

Rui tells Manon not to lock the precious memory in a small internal room. Burn it repeatedly. It may eventually become something that cannot melt (roughly logs 501–509).

The metaphor joins the arc's temperature systems. Locked memory is preserved but inert, like Momoka's office. Fire risks alteration. What survives repeated use is not an untouched archive but material strong enough to enter new situations.

This is also Rui's implicit account of Seven Sisters. She does not live by restoring the old unit or denying its importance. She carries its relational capacities into orphanage, talent, travel, and this one found child. Her adult multiplicity is evidence that a past role can be burned into future practice.

## Manon returns with desire rather than proof

Manon says she must go back. She addresses Rui by her real name and asks for another date after she becomes an adult. Rui accepts the possibility and says her life contains too many precious things to choose one; the world can be beautiful even after desert (roughly logs 510–545).

The exchange has romantic coloring, but the source does not establish an ongoing relationship or fixed orientation. Its immediate function is temporal: Manon can now imagine adulthood as another meeting rather than the death of childhood.

At Nanasta, Shirayuki sees the others return. She tells Momoka that a manager belongs with everybody and should remain while the younger members speak. Four bodies reassemble before the final decision is known (closing sequence).

## Claim changes

1. Shinju explicitly rejects “find the answer alone” as an adequate response to a lost friend and identifies Momoka's preserved office/glasses as Coney-directed stasis.
2. Momoka and Shinju's inability to cry is linked by Momoka to uncertainty about whether Coney abandoned them; the interpretation remains participant testimony.
3. Momoka revises management from distant answer-demand to search, presence, and invitation to disclose.
4. Tasha defines first-person story ownership as compatible with companion witness.
5. Rui finds and materially cares for Manon before offering an account of adulthood that includes rather than removes failure, regret, and loneliness.
6. Haru's childhood “idol need not be idol” maxim is recovered as `君は、君でいい`, rejecting role-based self-replacement.
7. Memory is revised from locked preservation to repeated transformative use; Manon chooses to return without first proving idol legitimacy.
