## Factual reconstruction

### EV-B0101-001 — being indulged is not the whole play relation

**SOURCE-ESTABLISHED NARRATIVE FACT.** With lessons off, the Player considers lunch near TANIMACHI shopping district and encounters the group playing in a park. Shirayuki is Alice, Sui the White Knight, Manon the Cheshire Cat, Susu the Dormouse and Hime the Queen of Hearts. Sui promises protection; Manon supplies grinning warnings. Susu sleeps rather than actively sustaining the fiction. Hime's threat to put tofu into her mouth exposes ordinary identity inside the assigned royal role; Sui tells her to act properly. The Player identifies characters from both Alice books, not one verified faithful adaptation. [A:P0–23]

Hime says Shirayuki requested the game. Manon explains her participation as reciprocity: Shirayuki previously accompanied her search for magical items. Sui enjoys the knight role and says acting has helped her understand chivalry. When she and Hime quarrel about their habitual roles, Shirayuki objects; they retreat into assurances that this is acting, not a real fight. Manon calls them proper older sisters in front of Shirayuki. The Player attributes their competence partly to Sui's care and Hime's experience with siblings; that is his explanation. [A:P19–33]

Shirayuki asks the Player to join. He agrees, framing participation as occasional care by the manager responsible for her, then resents receiving a minor playing-card-soldier role. Hime and Sui use that role to order and tease him; he agrees to try. Shirayuki says his acting lets her play with confidence. By dusk Sui's improvisation has expanded the game, including the Player's invented monster-Coney threat. The Player proposes stopping and resting at the office; Shirayuki agrees. No literal monster, sword battle or magical kingdom is established. [A:P34–42; B:P1–12]

### EV-B0101-002 — disclosure changes the meaning of an enjoyable afternoon

**SOURCE-ESTABLISHED NARRATIVE FACT.** Over juice, Shirayuki apologizes for involving the others. Sui notices her changed manner; Manon asks why play requires apology. Shirayuki explains that she wanted more than amusement: memories of her northern home made her lonely, and Tokyo's still-unfamiliar streets sometimes made her intensely anxious. Playing together in Wonderland was meant to dispel that anxiety. [B:P14–24]

She reports that an older sister, now living abroad, used to read Alice picture books to her. She loved the narrated world and imagined adventures there when sad. This is testimony about her remembered care, not a directly shown flashback or the sister's independent account. No personal name or present contact is supplied; the reported older-sister figure is not automatically merged with another established older companion. Shirayuki also says she loves the weak but earnest playing-card soldiers. That revises the Player's assumption that assigning him this role meant giving him an unimportant part. [B:P25–28]

Shirayuki concludes that she cannot always flee into Wonderland: she should grow up, stop escaping and stop involving the Player. These are her proposed standards of maturity, not the narrative's established requirement. [B:P29–30]

### EV-B0101-003 — refuge becomes something one may share

**SOURCE-ESTABLISHED NARRATIVE FACT.** Hime rejects the demand for solitary endurance: adults also involve friends in play to manage uncertainty. The Player agrees, then describes idols as people who dispel others' anxiety. That is an attributed vocational ideal, not proof that all idols or audiences experience this effect. Shirayuki still asks whether the group disliked being involved. Hime says unfamiliar roles can be enjoyable and compares the activity to the Nanasta stage; Manon explicitly says she enjoyed it. Sui resumes teasing Hime's queen performance. Their answer includes personal enjoyment rather than only duty to a younger child. [B:P31–41]

Manon proposes taking people into their own country—inviting everyone to the Wonderland Shirayuki loves. Hime generalizes this as fans wanting to play in an idol's world. Shirayuki adopts the possibility of enjoyment for herself and others; the Player promises help. Sui continues her knightly protection, and Hime objects to the sword idea. Susu falls asleep over her juice; Manon's alarm and Hime's exasperation return the scene to ordinary group disorder. The Player uses being drawn into others' lives as a counter to homesickness; Shirayuki agrees and promises to take them someday to her home, her real Wonderland. No itinerary, consent to a particular trip or public performance follows. [B:P42–58]

## Interpretation and alternatives

**BOUNDED INFERENCE — CL-B0101-001.** The chapter corrects an asymmetry in Shirayuki's account of play. She assumes her need makes participation a burden, while the reader has already seen Manon's reciprocal motive and Sui's genuine investment. Hime and Manon's later testimony does not invent that enjoyment from nothing. The Player's minor-role disappointment receives a parallel correction: a supposedly low-status role is one Shirayuki values. Care works here partly through revising the receiver's estimate of what others give up. [A:P19–41; B:P7–9,16–30,37–41]

Rival: the older members simply indulge a child and then reassure her. Their initial language supports some asymmetry, and teasing the Player is not identical to Shirayuki's experience. But Manon's explicitly reciprocal reason, Sui's reluctance to stop and the ensemble's improvisation resist reducing every participant to altruistic accommodation. Conversely, local enjoyment does not establish blanket permission for future demands or perfect knowledge of everyone's feelings.

**THEMATIC INTERPRETATION — CL-B0101-002.** Wonderland moves through three forms without requiring the earlier ones to become shameful: a world received through remembered reading, a privately imagined refuge, and a shared world to which others might be invited. Hime challenges the moral hierarchy in which adulthood means never needing such refuge. Manon's proposal gives Shirayuki an active hospitable role without demanding that she abandon the stories that comfort her. Her home-region promise brings the imagined and actual places into relation rather than replacing one with the other. [B:P25–36,42–56]

Rival: idol work simply converts private distress into a service owed to audiences. The Player's broad definition of idols makes that pressure possible, and no later work arrangement is shown. Yet this chapter explicitly retains Shirayuki's own enjoyment and others' willingness to join; the invitation is not presented as a quota or compulsory performance. The supported result is a new possible relation to need, not the elimination of vulnerability through usefulness.

**THEMATIC INTERPRETATION — CL-B0101-003.** Role-play permits identity leakage rather than requiring flawless impersonation. Tofu, recurring prince/queen preferences, affectionate teasing and Susu's actual sleep all disturb the fictional roles, but those disturbances make the group legible as companions. The ending's disorder prevents the new idol-country idea from resolving into solemn professional doctrine. This claim concerns dialogue, reported action and script order, not the unauditioned comic timing or performers' vocal affect. [A:P8–33; B:P1–9,38–56]

Retrospectively, B0079 also distinguishes supported care from unilateral self-sufficiency, while Episode 5.0 separates adulthood from abandoning relation. Those are comparisons across admitted evidence, not proof that B0101 causes the later choices or supplies their chronological origin. Earlier prospective readings remain unchanged. Shirayuki's dialect is visible in this chapter's written language; no actor accent, pitch or enduring performance baseline is inferred from text.
