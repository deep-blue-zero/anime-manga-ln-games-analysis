# T7S_B0066 — a senior who refuses the idol name

## Result

The visitor is Wakaoji Rui, one of Seven Sisters, but she introduces her current life through an alias, a joke about kidnapping, and work that moves talented people across borders. She does not describe herself as an idol. Her presence gives Episode 5.0 an adult counterexample to Manon's category panic: a former idol can remain the same person while changing names, countries, occupations, and public visibility.

Momoka cannot yet use that example honestly. She recognizes Rui, presses for the true reason for the visit, and then tells SOL that the stranger was a sportswear representative. Her lie is protective in intention but reproduces the structure that already isolates the four: an adult sees consequential information and decides the younger people should not carry it.

The resulting fracture becomes physical. Shinju metabolizes unresolved grief into harder rehearsal; Manon reads Shinju's discipline as the standard of a real idol and tries to erase her own insufficiency through effort. She accidentally injures Shinju. No malicious intent is established. The causal point is that concealed histories and unspoken comparison can still enter bodies, schedules, and risk.

## Three names do not imply three people

The visitor already knows SOL's debut, eighth live, and Shinju's history. She calls the kidnapper association a joke and says she performed at Nanasta long ago, although she did not regard what the group did as idol work. Momoka recognizes her as `若王子ルイ` (roughly logs 0–92).

Rui explains that `姫宮ソル` is useful in her current business. She scouts people with uncommon skill—engineers, researchers, creators—and connects them to places where their capacities can grow. Her routes run between developing and developed regions and across national borders. She has no family whose name she must preserve, so aliases are practical (roughly logs 93–151).

The account is direct self-report, not an audited employment history. It establishes a recognizable practice: she relocates opportunity and people rather than preserving a fixed public self. The “kidnapper” name condenses how that work may look from outside, but this installment does not yet establish orphanages, financing, consent procedures, or outcomes.

Rui's identity structure is formally important. `若王子ルイ`, `姫宮ソル`, former Seven Sisters member, and international scout are not revealed as masks hiding one final true occupation. They are successive or context-specific routes through which one person acts. That is precisely the form of adulthood Manon cannot imagine for herself.

## Seven Sisters continues as dispersed lives

Rui gives a partial report of the other members. MiTO is publicly known. Mana moves through international finance, and Rui sometimes meets her abroad. Kurt is an action-film lead in the Kowloon sphere. Memoru may be helping Kurt while avoiding public attention. Rui and Momoka do not know where Coney is (roughly logs 152–205).

These reports are attributed and uneven. They establish continued lives and some occupations; they do not provide comprehensive current status or direct testimony from the absent women. The absence of Coney is distinctive because Momoka has been searching.

The old unit has not survived as an organization, yet its people are not null. Their paths are dispersed, non-equivalent, and partly connected. This turns dissolution from an image of annihilation into a history with incomplete continuities.

## Momoka asks the right question and gives the wrong account

Rui says she came because of SOL. Momoka presses: that is not the whole reason. The conversation cuts away before the answer becomes available to the younger group. When Rui leaves, Momoka tells SOL she was a sports manufacturer checking tracksuits. Shinju and Tasha clearly suspect the explanation (roughly logs 206–251).

The lie protects information about Seven Sisters and possibly Coney. It also withholds information about Tasha's opportunity and SOL's relation to the visitor. Momoka treats concealment as a way to keep younger people from being destabilized. The immediate result is not stability; it is asymmetrical suspicion.

Shinju invokes a group promise not to hide things from one another. Momoka refuses to answer today. Shinju then narrows the request: if anyone is leaving, at least say goodbye. Momoka asks whether Shinju wants Coney to return. Shinju says she does not know. Momoka says she does, always.

The exchange exposes two different needs. Shinju asks for departure to become communicable. Momoka wants one particular return. Neither can yet say the grief beneath those positions.

## The pact is stronger as accusation than practice

The group's anti-secrecy pact appears morally clear, but every member already violates it in a different register. Momoka conceals Rui. Tasha has not disclosed the international approach. Shirayuki withholds her concern about Manon. Manon conceals her self-rejection. Shinju channels fear of departure into discipline rather than saying what she needs.

The pact is not false. It identifies a standard they cannot currently enact. Its failure shows that openness requires more than a rule against secrets; it requires a relation in which a changed desire can be spoken without immediately becoming abandonment.

## Manon sees Shinju's wound as a credential

Festival rehearsal intensifies. Shinju pushes through fatigue and emotion. Manon sees the seriousness of a former 777☆SISTERS member and calls it the behavior of a real idol. She answers by practicing harder, again denying the “magic” of her earlier self (roughly logs 252–319).

This is not simple admiration. Shinju's unresolved grief is misread as professional essence. Manon cannot see the need underneath the discipline, so she copies the pressure rather than the responsibility.

During rehearsal Manon collides with or otherwise causes Shinju to fall, injuring her ankle. Medical advice prohibits intense movement for about a month, placing the festival in doubt (roughly logs 320–337).

The source establishes accident and consequence, not intent. Manon is responsible for an unsafe act without having wanted harm. The distinction will matter: guilt may identify a real obligation to repair, but it does not prove that she is uniquely unworthy or that the group would be better without her.
