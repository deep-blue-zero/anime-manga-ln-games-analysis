# T7S_B0038 — a wildflower instead of a smile machine

## Result

Nanasta forms a seven-person unit to inherit the small local stage that its increasingly successful acts can no longer regularly occupy. Management chooses the membership and a trial deadline but asks the performers to author their own purpose. Their first concept—making flowers of smiles bloom in everyone—becomes a demand Tomoe believes she can meet only by erasing herself into Haru. The others eventually learn that “be yourself” can be another coercive instruction when a frightened person does not yet know what that self can do. They wait through a delayed live, let audience members leave, and transfer the mud on Tomoe's ruined costume to their own bodies through an embrace. Her failed solitary comedy then becomes funny as shared improvisation. Tomoe revises the unit's grand promise: before making anyone else smile, the seven can try to stand like roadside flowers in rain, wind, mud, small venues, or even an empty room. Leadership goes to the member who makes insufficiency speakable. The chapter's solidarity is costly and sometimes reckless, but it defines a unit as the place where people may remain unfinished together rather than a machine for producing compulsory happiness.

## A succession problem at the local stage

The Player begins with institutional arithmetic. `777☆SISTERS` now appears at large venues more often than at Nanasta's streamed live; Le☆S☆Ca and QOP can also support major solo shows. QOP, Ci+LUS, and CASQUETTE'S have demonstrated increasing capacity to formulate their own concepts and directions. Growth has made the established performers less available to the place from which they grew.

The proposed seven-person unit is partly an answer to that success. Tomoe, Xiao, Madoka, Saori, Miu, Monaka, and Rishuri have accumulated individual work and have coordinated across jobs, but do not yet have a formal unit debut. The Player expects them to remain active around Nanasta, compensate for one another's weaknesses, and govern more of their work themselves.

This is both succession and labor allocation. A local venue needs a new large act because prior acts have moved into a more prestigious circuit. The seven receive an opportunity, but they also fill an operational gap. Calling the choice recognition of their maturity does not eliminate the institution's need for replacement capacity.

Management's process is mixed. The Player chooses the membership and establishes that the group should exist before asking what it should be. Each member is invited to accept, but the social setting makes dissent difficult; Tomoe agrees only after Madoka says they might become like 777☆SISTERS. Once they accept, however, the Player refuses to supply a ready brand. They must determine what they want, devise a concept, and later pass a trial live before the arrangement becomes durable.

The chapter explicitly names this as a developing Nanasta practice. Rishuri calls attention to how newer units have begun forming their own direction. The performers jokingly debate whether “do what you want” is Player-ism, Nanasta-ism, or Coney-ism. This personalization shows how institutional culture is remembered through charismatic leaders, but the actual scene distributes authorship away from them. Coney and the Player become reference points; neither attends the conversation that decides what the seven will be.

## The meeting that succeeds by failing to stay on task

The first planning session appears unproductive. Xiao proposes a martial-arts unit no one else can execute. Monaka proposes changing professions every appearance. Saori repeatedly imagines jobs in which she is thrown. Tomoe can think only of playing an unemployed person; Madoka's horn would prevent her from singing. A discussion of a spy concept drifts into Monaka's detective job, and darkness falls before the group notices.

Yet this failed meeting performs the work its agenda cannot directly name. Every impractical proposal discloses a member's habits, work history, humor, fear, or desire. Rishuri recognizes that a unit concept cannot be separated from learning who would have to inhabit it. She refuses the group's easy casting of her as leader because formal hierarchy would precede sufficient mutual knowledge, then proposes a camp where they can continue talking.

Changes in address become a small governance test. Rishuri asks to use first names and to reduce honorific distance, but not everyone can or wants to make the same transition. Madoka prefers calling others older sister. Tomoe finds direct address difficult. Xiao has already used names without honorifics. Rishuri tells Tomoe to take her time. Closeness is proposed, not standardized.

At camp, hunger redirects discussion into shared cooking; shared food redirects it into biography. Tomoe came hoping to become a socially successful, sparkling person. Xiao crossed into idol work as mental training after a martial-arts master told her that technical mastery had outpaced her interior development. Monaka is pursuing treasure. Madoka admired 777☆SISTERS and wanted to change her fear of appearing before people; she initially refused the Player's scout, then called after him because she did not want to keep stopping before every desired path.

The source does not provide an equally extended origin account for every member here, and no symmetry should be invented. The important formal result is that the concept arrives through conversation rather than a marketing exercise. Saori says Madoka's smile makes flowers bloom in an audience. The phrase catches among the group: they will become seven performers who make flowers of smiles bloom in everyone.

Tomoe remains silent. The convivial process has produced a shared phrase without detecting that one participant hears it as a threat. Consensus is real but incomplete when a person cannot yet voice what agreement will demand of her.

## The excluded member inside “everyone”

The proposed concept directs every smile outward. The performers will make “everyone” smile; Tomoe immediately excludes herself from that universal audience. She believes an idol must already possess a beautiful public smile before she can transmit one. Because she describes herself as gloomy, socially incapable, physically awkward, nauseated, vocally unstable, and fundamentally out of place, the concept seems to prove that she is the group's defective part.

This repeats the problem exposed in the preceding `スマイル` chapter at a new institutional level. An apparently benevolent commitment turns involuntary affect into a performance metric. Sakura felt guilty because six entertainers could not make her smile; Tomoe feels guilty because she cannot guarantee smiles in others. The target and producer occupy opposite positions in the same economy of compulsory happiness.

Tomoe first tries to locate an individual technique. Xiao successfully incorporates martial-arts forms into her local stage and receives audience excitement. Tomoe attempts a one-line comic act and fails. She then reuses a “loner” costume that had worked in a prior joint routine with Shisara and fails again. She correctly realizes that the earlier response was relational: Shisara's departure from her normal seriousness generated contrast, while Tomoe alone could not reproduce the same event.

This does not establish that Tomoe is inherently unfunny. It establishes that a response created between performers, context, and audience cannot be extracted as one person's portable property. The chapter later proves the point when Tomoe's muddy arrival becomes funny through the others' timing and participation. Her early error is seeking a solo asset for a collective vocation.

The repeated failure intensifies self-surveillance. Tomoe watches herself in a reflective surface, classifies the image as an ugly duckling or even an ugly amphibian, and searches for a market category—rock, comedy, cuteness—that could make the wrong body useful. The problem is no longer merely what she wants to perform. It is whether the person she sees can count as a person from whom joy is allowed to come.

## Admiration as model and self-erasure

Haru is the figure through whom Tomoe entered idol desire. Watching Haru's stream, she observes a smile answered by an audience and concludes that this is what blooming smiles must mean. Admiration offers a visible possibility of connection. It also supplies a completed person with whom Tomoe can replace the self she distrusts.

An embedded recollection shows that this is not a new conflict. Tomoe once reproduced a Haru song with the same choreography, breath, and expression. Murasaki acknowledged stable, high-quality singing but called the performance a copy. Shisara and the Player argued that the girl Tomoe wished to reach had admired Tomoe, not Haru; technical replication could not carry a feeling Tomoe refused to author.

The judgments in that recalled scene are evidence of the characters' evaluation, not independent acoustic verification. What matters here is that Tomoe remembers the critique and repeats the strategy anyway. “Be yourself” has never told her how to produce a self she considers performable. When a deadline approaches, imitation is not ignorance but the only safe form she thinks remains.

She copies Haru's bright greeting, energy, and presumed audience security in group rehearsal. The others immediately recognize that something is wrong. Saori asks why she changed and says that imitation is even less likely to make anyone smile. She too admits that the unit concept frightens her: no one knows whether she can deliver happiness, so she intends to meet the uncertainty with everything she has.

Saori's appeal is sincere, but Tomoe hears another capable person telling an incapable one to do what she cannot. Her outburst—why does everyone order someone who cannot do a thing to do it?—identifies an important limit of authenticity discourse. A demand to expose “all of yourself” can be as coercive as a demand to copy. The fact that friends value a hidden self does not make access to it effortless or owed.

Saori later recognizes this. She tells the others that she may have forced something Tomoe found genuinely unbearable. Miu answers that Tomoe also did not want to live as a copy. The group holds both claims instead of settling for a slogan: imitation is self-erasure, but authenticity cannot be extracted on schedule.

Haru's most consequential contribution is consequently not a performance for Tomoe to reproduce. When others fear Tomoe will not return, Haru insists that Tomoe will because this is Tomoe's story. She grants narrative confidence without volunteering as substitute. Shisara uses the same formulation at the unit's beginning. The admired senior's ethical role is to refuse possession of the junior's future.

## Solitude mistaken for professionalism

Tomoe responds to conflict by asking the Player for exclusive use of the camp during the week before the trial live. She says she needs individual practice, hides her distress during his later check-in, and perfects the Haru imitation in isolation. Her stated aim is not selfish escape. She is terrified of burdening the unit and believes a professional must arrive with a finished contribution.

The arrangement exposes a management gap. The Player honors her request and calls to ask about progress, but the group format requires relational rehearsal, and Tomoe's reassurance is false. The chapter does not show a safeguarding conversation, a weather contingency, or a process for distinguishing productive solitude from withdrawal. Respect for autonomy without enough relational knowledge leaves distress legible only after crisis.

Heavy rain produces landslides around the camp on the morning of the live. A police officer explicitly says that buses and cars cannot pass and instructs Tomoe not to go out on foot or bicycle. She takes a bicycle anyway. The attempt is courageous in the narrow sense that she acts despite fear, but it is also unsafe and against direct emergency advice. The story's later celebration of her injuries should not erase that the institution could have cancelled, delayed, or treated physical safety as sufficient reason not to perform.

Tomoe falls, rolls down an embankment, injures herself, bends the bicycle, and covers Rishuri's handmade costume in mud. The ruined garment becomes proof, in her mind, that she destroys other people's labor before the unit has even debuted. She asks Saori to have Haru replace her—the person whose style she copied should also occupy her slot—and begs the others' forgiveness.

This proposed substitution reveals what imitation has done. Tomoe now understands herself as an interchangeable weak implementation of Haru. The unit refuses that functional logic. Its members delay the live not because no competent performer is available, but because the relation they agreed to enact contains Tomoe specifically.

## Waiting is an action with costs

Police contact Nanasta after Tomoe leaves the restricted area. Shisara and Mimi search and locate her. Shisara initially says that everyone is waiting; when Tomoe refuses to return, Shisara answers a call from Rishuri and claims Tomoe has not yet been found. The deception lets Tomoe hear the other members' messages without making her immediately respond to them.

Those messages do not say she owes the group a performance. Rishuri says they are waiting. Miu says they have not even worried together yet. Madoka and Monaka say that without Tomoe, the group has not begun. Saori apologizes for the confrontation and says she is waiting not for a generic seventh performer but for Tomoe.

This is less coercive than a demand, but it is not pressure-free. The venue is waiting, an audience is departing, collaborators are absorbing delay, and Tomoe knows six people have attached their own decision to hers. Shisara's statement that no one will force her is an ethical intention rather than a description of a socially neutral choice.

The group accepts material consequences. By the time Tomoe arrives, many spectators have left. Madoka worries that the Player and audience will be angry; Saori says they should apologize and work harder afterward. Rishuri and Monaka say they do not regret the delay because it was their decision. Solidarity is not made frictionless by narrative permission. It spends time, goodwill, revenue, and opportunity, and the members claim those costs rather than assign them to Tomoe alone.

Shisara also improves on the harshest version of her prior “lift the cap” speech. She admits that she still does not know the correct path, sometimes advances one step and retreats three, and continues looking for moments when she can laugh from within. She tells Tomoe to decide for herself what the future Tomoe and Tomoe's smile will be. Although Shisara again locates the final decision in the individual, the waiting group makes clear that deciding need not mean solving alone.

## Mud as a relation written onto costume

Tomoe first meets a small flower while she is stranded and muddy. She calls both of them misplaced, then becomes unsettled by the plant's upright growth in an uncelebrated location. The flower does not become beautiful by escaping the mud. Its location is part of the form that attracts her attention.

When Tomoe reaches the venue, her companions embrace her. In doing so they transfer mud from her costume to theirs. Rishuri jokes that Tomoe has stamped a face across her chest. The image converts damage from a private mark of failure into a shared visual condition. The pristine costumes Rishuri designed to express a future unit acquire an unplanned record of what the unit has already done.

This redistribution is more consequential than verbal reassurance. Tomoe cannot stand beside six polished performers as the singular dirty member whose mistake must be overlooked. Their embrace makes her accident part of everyone's staging. No one can restore the original costume before the live, so they change what counts as presentable.

The source risks romanticizing injury and deprivation when Haru says that idols are always muddy and hurt. Idol labor should not require preventable danger, and Tomoe's disobedience of emergency guidance is not a best practice. Yet Haru's narrower point resists a perfection standard: visible damage does not cancel idol status. She and the Player recognize the juniors not because suffering authenticates them, but because they continue to be performers without hiding every trace of difficulty.

Onstage, the group narrates the mud instead of concealing it. Saori mentions her own muddy shoe; Miu corrects the scale of the story; Tomoe admits falling bodily into the mud; Rishuri turns the transferred face print into a joke; Madoka asks for a print; Xiao offers a calligraphic martial-arts substitute. The audience laughs.

Tomoe's earlier solo jokes failed, but this exchange works because comedy is now distributed. No member has to manufacture the response alone. Timing, correction, callback, costume, accident, and familiarity circulate across seven speakers. What Tomoe could not possess as a technique emerges as a relation.

The audience's laughter is not unambiguously gentle—spectators also laugh when Tomoe says that “someone like me” will be an idol—but Tomoe participates in framing the declaration. She no longer promises to resemble Haru. She says this visibly awkward, muddy person is the one who will try. The performance turns a deficit she had hidden into an address she controls.

## Wanting to smile instead of owing a smile

When Tomoe arrives coughing and apologizing, the others laugh at the anticlimax; she begins laughing with them. Asked what she wants, she says that she wants to smile. She may not yet smile well or be able to make someone else smile, but she wants to learn to laugh with these people. The distinction between present capacity and desired direction releases her from having to counterfeit completion.

The concept is not abandoned. Tomoe later says making others smile remains the destination. What changes is its temporal and ethical status. It is a horizon the group may approach, not a service-level guarantee each member must already embody. A person who cannot smile on demand is included among those for whom the unit should make space.

Rishuri's response similarly withdraws certainty. She does not know whether she can make anyone smile either; the group has not reached the starting line. This admission changes leadership from expertise delivered downward to shared exposure to not knowing.

Tomoe's revised image is a roadside wildflower: struck by rain, wind, and mud, it does not lower its head; on a tiny or empty stage it stretches upright and sings toward the wind. The formulation retains a resilience ideal and could itself become another demand never to bow. Its improvement lies in scale. The flower is not responsible for causing a whole field to bloom. It practices presence in the place it actually occupies.

The small and empty stage also answers Nanasta's succession problem. This unit is not simply a junior copy of 777☆SISTERS waiting to graduate into larger venues. It can value the local and unattended site that success tends to leave behind. “Blooming” is detached from audience size and immediate applause.

The members choose Tomoe as leader because she articulates this revised purpose. Her social anxiety, nausea, failed comedy, withdrawal, and uncertainty are not magically removed. Leadership does not reward the most conventionally confident member. It assigns coordination to the person whose crisis exposed what the group must not demand from its members.

At the chapter boundary the seven have not spoken a unit name. Tomoe sees the flower again and announces that she has an idea, but the text withholds the name itself. Later nomenclature must not be imported as if it were uttered here. The bounded ending preserves naming as Tomoe's next act rather than a fact already delivered by the institution.

## An unfinished collective

The chapter does not resolve whether the trial live formally passes, though the Player describes the audience response positively and the performers believe the unit can continue. It shows no complete song, choreography, post-event audience accounting, or repair of the delay. The represented success is governance: the seven choose to wait, accept the loss, perform their altered material condition, and revise their purpose.

That governance remains imperfect. Tomoe is selected into a group before the group knows her; consensus misses her silence; management permits isolating preparation; emergency safety is overridden; advice about authenticity becomes pressure; a live audience pays part of the cost. The chapter does not offer friendship as a system that prevents harm.

It offers something narrower and stronger. A unit can make a member's private failure interrupt the concept itself. Instead of removing Tomoe so the promise remains intact, the group changes the promise so Tomoe's actual experience can become constitutive. The wildflower does not replace the flower of smiles. It grounds it in a collective capable of getting dirty before asking anyone else to bloom.
