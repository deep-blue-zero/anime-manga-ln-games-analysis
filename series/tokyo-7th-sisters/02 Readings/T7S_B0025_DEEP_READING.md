# T7S_B0025 — Nanasta is what the girls do together

## Result

EPISODE 3.0 opens by asking what Nanasta has become after the increased attention produced by Grand Fest and the confrontation with KARAKURI. A documentary crew proposes to answer with a fixed, unattended camera: observe an ordinary day inside the studio and capture the group's supposedly unadorned self. The day refuses that container. Work disperses the idols across schools, shoots, lessons, a neighborhood event, a fan meeting, and finally an improvised outdoor live. By the time Coney orders the fixed camera carried backstage, “Nanasta” no longer names a room that contains the girls. It names a portable practice of mutual coordination that lets them move beyond it.

## The impossible “natural” image

The documentary premise contains a productive contradiction. Its producers want a friendly, relaxed, undecorated image, so they remove the camera operator and leave a fixed camera in the studio. Yet the camera's presence still changes what it records. Musubi forgets it, becomes embarrassed when reminded that her quarrel with Susu was captured, and begins retrospectively judging whether she appeared acceptably “natural.” Coney repeatedly evaluates ordinary conversation as usable footage. The episode therefore does not treat mediation as a transparent window onto an authentic private self. Naturalness is itself a public performance category, and an idol's awareness of being seen can recede without disappearing.

That tension becomes sharper when Coney insists that the camera be taken to the outdoor venue so it can record backstage. Her comic appetite for footage expands the authorized gaze from a stationary common room into the mobile spaces surrounding labor. The script does not stage a refusal, but it preserves the ethical pressure: the same day that celebrates greater visibility also discusses unwanted touching, fan-service boundaries, and the capture of private talk. Public recognition creates relation and opportunity; it also makes access to the girls' bodies and offstage selves something the institution must negotiate.

## Fame appears as changes in ordinary circulation

The chapter measures Nanasta's post–Grand Fest growth through small disturbances rather than a ranking announcement. Sui is recognized at Shinju's school. Haru and Shizuka notice strangers recognizing them in the street. Susu is approached less often by model scouts because her face is already legible as an idol's. A station poster circulates through fan photographs online. Requests for signed material increase. Kajika meets a grandparent who watched a live with a grandchild, while Hime is presented to a baby as a possible future model.

These examples widen both space and time. Nanasta now reaches schools, streets, stations, homes, and a shopping district; its audience runs from children to grandparents and imagines another generation of idols. Sui's handshake is especially precise. She treats accepting a boy's hand as personal growth, while Shinju distinguishes that consensual request from an attempted skirt-lift she prevented. “Being reachable” is not an unlimited claim on the idol. The chapter places increased accessibility beside the need to discriminate welcomed contact from violation.

## Labor, school, and the misleading ease of ensemble comedy

The opening itinerary makes the group's expansion legible as labor. Individual and unit jobs have become common enough that a full-group stream is now rare. Several members move between school and idol work; Sui also keeps her club commitment. Homework after rehearsal, travel between locations, autograph production, fittings, and equipment handling sit beside the visible performance. The lively relay of arrivals and departures makes this density feel easy, but the schedule exposes the infrastructure beneath that ease.

The jokes about laziness do not simply oppose workers and shirkers. Sawara and Momoka's unusable concierge-AI data turns their unruly dispositions into a comic refusal of frictionless automation. Shinju, by contrast, functions as the actual concierge: she remembers schedules, anticipates Sawara's delay, and recruits the Player to enforce the fitting. Musubi teaches classmates during spare time. Sumire directs the final equipment check. Care and managerial competence are distributed through the group, often among the younger girls, rather than monopolized by the adults.

The sister motif reinforces this distribution. A fashion shoot asks Sui and Shinju to imitate generic “sisterly” intimacy, but Shinju cannot derive that abstraction from the idiosyncratic Harumi household. Shizuka proposes that all of 777☆SISTERS are sisters. The chapter's later action supplies a better definition than the photo concept: sisterhood is not a prescribed pose or permanent physical closeness, but the ability to anticipate needs, correct one another, divide work, and reunite across separate lives.

## The overflow problem

The fan meeting produces the chapter's only crisis because Nanasta's new scale makes two valid obligations collide. More people arrive for the handoff event than can be served within the allotted time. Ending the line protects the scheduled, professionally filmed stream; continuing it honors the fans who came for direct contact. The story refuses to identify either audience as less real. Rona has seen online viewers anticipating the full-group live, while Shizuka insists on reaching the last person physically present.

Coney's proposal—to move the live to KUONJI Central Park—solves the conflict by changing the container rather than sacrificing one constituency. The improvisation is characteristically reckless, but it is not magic. Coney and the Player take responsibility for permissions and venue preparation. Haru, Shizuka, and Susu remain with the fans. Kajika contacts Sawara; Hime contacts Sui; Sumire contacts Musubi and then directs the loadout; Rona and Kajika assemble costumes and makeup. Shinju immediately revises the travel plan. Even Musubi, after correctly identifying the formation problem created by the larger stage, starts running because she wants the larger collective performance.

Coney notices that everyone has begun moving before she can issue orders. That is the decisive evidence of development. Earlier Nanasta stories repeatedly required an adult or lead member to gather uncertain individuals into action. Here leadership creates the conditions in which the members can initiate, specialize, and coordinate for themselves. The Player's claim that he is a manager “for everyone” does not make him the source of their desire. It commits him to clearing a path for a desire they have already expressed.

## From fixed camera to moving institution

The chapter begins with a technological fantasy of total legibility: leave a camera in one place and an ordinary Nanasta will reveal itself. Its form then becomes a relay narrative whose center is repeatedly empty because the idols' work exceeds the studio. The camera must move because the institution has already moved.

Coney's closing declaration gives the spatial argument its future tense. The girls cannot be contained in the small box called Nanasta; someday they will travel far enough to cross the blue sky. This does not discard the studio. The outdoor live succeeds because habits learned there—shared responsibility, candid correction, comic tolerance, and trust under impossible schedules—remain operative elsewhere. Nanasta becomes portable precisely because it has become internal to the group's relations.

The final `KILL☆ER☆TUNE☆R` cue is therefore less a musical conclusion than a public proof of assembly. The source establishes that the performance begins after the hurried transfer and the familiar `We are! 777☆SISTERS!` circle. The performed audio was not reviewed, so no claim is made about vocals, arrangement, crowd acoustics, or execution. What the page sequence proves is that twelve separate itineraries can converge without surrendering their separateness.

## Exit state

- Post–Grand Fest attention is materialized through recognition, posters, autograph demand, diversified jobs, and a multigenerational audience.
- Greater visibility produces both welcome relation and new pressures around touch, fan service, privacy, and backstage access.
- Full-group gathering has become exceptional because individual and unit careers now coexist with school and ordinary obligations.
- Managerial and caring labor is distributed among members; the girls can mobilize before Coney gives instructions.
- A conflict between in-person and online fans is resolved by expanding the performance space rather than ranking one audience beneath the other.
- The Player and Coney increasingly function as institutional enablers: they secure permission, infrastructure, and protection for member-initiated action.
- Nanasta is redefined from a building that houses the group into a repeatable coordination practice that travels with it.
- The chapter's skyward promise opens EPISODE 3.0 as a question of how far differentiated units and persons can travel while remaining capable of return and recombination.
