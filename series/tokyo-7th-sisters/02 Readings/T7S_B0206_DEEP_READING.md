# Tasha's names for closeness and an unfinished future

Tasha repeatedly tries to give closeness an intelligible form: a word from home, the status of a lady, a school essay, a title learned from fiction, an imagined occupation, footprints and the colour of evening. These are active attempts to name what the manager means to her, not seven confirmations that he has accepted the resulting roles. She can reject an address, withdraw her own proposal and hold affection for her homeland alongside enjoyment of her present surroundings. The dialogue gives these choices their small scale: what she calls someone, what she asks for, what she thinks she will want. Its future tenses do not resolve adult residence, family permission or the international research possibilities already retained at the later Main Episode 5.0 horizon.

Each case gives Tasha the only supplied dialogue and native speaker. That form makes her self-account unusually direct while leaving the manager's contribution available through uptake, implication or report. A pleasing stroke/pat and present return companionship have more local support than a merely imagined outing, but neither supplies an independent manager interior or an unlimited future commitment. The distinction between an affection that is spoken and an arrangement that has been agreed is central to the portfolio.

## A — Teaching can ask for recognition

A, P1-3, turns being taught into an offer to teach. Tasha says that the manager regularly explains things she does not know; because that makes her happy, she wants to give him a word from her own country in return (P1). Knowledge is not only a deficiency that the manager repairs. It becomes something she can contribute, with the explanation's value grounded in her gratitude rather than a certified lesson outcome. At P2 she glosses `ダラゴーイ` as a term for an important person and applies it emphatically to him. The evidence supports her katakana word, her gloss and her particular use. It does not independently settle a language's lexical rules, the word's grammatical gender, a named country or her nationality.

The last question is more exposed than the confident gloss. She asks what an important person is called in Japanese and whether he will tell her when she someday becomes his own `ダラゴーイ` (P3). His importance to her is an assertion; her importance to him is made into a hoped-for future condition. That asymmetry prevents converting the exchange into a mutual declaration. The question also links knowledge to recognition: she wants the corresponding word to arrive with a place in his affections, not simply as an item of vocabulary. No answer or promise is supplied, and nothing in the park background proves this future has arrived. D's title experiment offers a formal comparison of address-making, without establishing that either occurrence taught the other.

Her difficulty is not an empty inability to imagine adulthood: she has competing attachments to put into it. She describes present idol work with the manager, expects that she will someday return to her country, and immediately says that idol work is enjoyable and she does not want to go back (P2). The expectation and the reluctance are both hers; neither becomes a final migration decision.
