# T7S_B0029 — ordinary is a form that can keep generating

## Result

Sawara convenes a family meeting to produce a “new” Sanbon Ribbon, invokes the danger of complacency, and proposes escalating names, fictional identities, circus spectacle, and space travel. The sisters' one plausible experiment—a radio-style conversation—quietly repeats a game Sawara used to carry them through a rainy childhood afternoon. The chapter distinguishes renewal from novelty. Sanbon Ribbon can change media and invent new material because its ordinary family relation is generative; it does not need to replace that relation with branding that merely announces progress.

## Growth as an empty imperative

Sawara's opening argument sounds professionally serious: satisfaction with the present means an end to development, so the unit must remain conscious of growth. Kajika is impressed because it does not sound like her sister. The apparent seriousness collapses when the proposed destination is space and the unit names become `Code Melodious`, `Z`, and other borrowed sequel markers.

The comedy identifies a familiar weakness in growth rhetoric. “Move forward” can command agreement before anyone has specified a lack, a need, or a desired direction. Shinju initially asks the essential question: their activity is going reasonably well, so what problem would the new form solve? Sawara never answers it. Her proposals accumulate signs of magnitude—future origin, galactic office, acrobatics, wire flight—without producing a reason for change.

This does not make Sawara's imagination worthless. She notices possible formats, catalyzes conversation, and can write an eye-catching proposal. But ideation without constraint transfers practical cost to her sisters and staff. Shinju repeatedly supplies feasibility analysis: Kajika and she cannot safely reproduce Sawara's acrobatics, flying still burdens the suspended performer, and an invented title can create unwanted public identity. The unit's creativity depends on this tension between expansion and limit.

## Names that escape their maker

The “galactic emperor incident” shows that playful invention has social consequences. Sawara once told a story about being an emperor; Kajika believed it, wrote about it at school, and brought classmates home. Shinju then acquired the unwanted title “the galactic emperor's little sister” in the shopping district. Sawara remembers only ordinary play, Kajika barely remembers the aftermath, and Shinju retains the burden of a label she did not choose.

The anecdote is comic, but it clarifies why Shinju resists a “future idol” persona. A public category need not be true to organize how other people address someone. Nor does the originator retain control once it circulates. Her preference for no special title is therefore not a lack of imagination; it is informed resistance to being made legible through somebody else's improvisation.

## The new program that already happened

Radio becomes the first proposal all three can plausibly share. It allows them to work together and extend their existing streams beyond song. Sawara immediately performs a mock broadcast. Kajika's nervous self-description as the middle dumpling and her slide from rain (`ame`) to candy (`ame`) expose the difficulty of producing spontaneity on command. Shinju succeeds by attending to frogs and the meaning rain has for them. The material comes not from a new persona but from what she already notices and cares about.

Sawara ends the “program” as abruptly as she began it when the rain stops. Kajika then remembers an earlier shelter in which Sawara invented `Harumi Television`, interviewed her, proposed an impossible glass-harp segment, and ended when the sun returned. The present radio experiment is structurally the same event. Sawara converts waiting into episodes; Kajika responds through associative warmth; Shinju supplies skeptical continuity and unexpectedly detailed speech. The unit's proposed new medium is an iteration of the sisters' long-standing way of making time together.

Whether Sawara consciously entertained her younger sisters in the childhood scene remains unresolved. Kajika interprets it as care; Shinju suspects Sawara was simply relieving her own boredom. The distinction need not be settled. Shared play can serve the initiator and care for others at once. What matters is that an ordinary disposition repeatedly makes a temporary common world during enforced waiting.

## “As usual” after time has passed

Kajika and Shinju conclude that the existing Sanbon Ribbon is best: Sawara, Shinju, and Kajika together, as usual. “As usual” does not mean no historical change. The flashback itself records different ages, dependencies, and vocabularies. Shinju is now able to identify the old interaction as a pattern; Kajika can propose translating it into radio; the trio already occupies an idol institution rather than a playground shelter.

Ordinariness is the persistence of a relation across those changes, not the freezing of its members. This distinguishes the chapter from a conservative return to family essence. Its most promising formal innovation arises precisely because the existing relation has enough duration to contain repetition with difference.

The final gag reintroduces institutional risk. Sawara submits the spectacular `Code Melodious` proposal while claiming the three discussed it, and the Player starts making calls before verifying that claim. Shinju predicts the project will collapse once budget and schedule force reflection, which it does within hours. The harmless outcome should not obscure the authorization failure: an appealing pitch and growth vocabulary briefly outrun member consent and feasibility review.

## Exit state

- Sanbon Ribbon's current activity is not shown to have a substantive deficit requiring rebranding.
- Sawara's imagination generates possibilities but needs Shinju's feasibility and consent checks to avoid transferring cost to others.
- Shinju's resistance to invented titles is grounded in prior experience of an unwanted public label, not mere rigidity.
- A radio format emerges as plausible because it lets all three participate and draws on their distinct existing dispositions.
- The present mock broadcast repeats a childhood rainy-day game, making continuity itself a source of new form.
- Kajika's reading of Sawara's play as care and Shinju's reading of it as self-entertainment remain compatible rather than conclusively resolved.
- “Ordinary” names a durable, changing relation—not stagnation or the absence of invention.
- Sawara's falsely collective proposal and the Player's premature uptake expose a minor but real institutional need for consent and feasibility before promotion.
