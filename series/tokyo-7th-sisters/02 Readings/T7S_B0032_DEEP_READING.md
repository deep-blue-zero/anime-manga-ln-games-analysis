# T7S_B0032 — a band that lets its members leave

## Result

An overheard phone call leads QOP to believe that Furb is losing a male beloved, when she is actually mourning a music shop's relocation. The mistake exposes two truths the correction does not cancel. First, the band understands loyalty as chosen rather than compulsory: its members are prepared to release Furb from the camp and even from QOP if another relation becomes more important, asking for communication rather than ownership. Second, the supposedly lesser object of grief is an indispensable part of musical life—a local network of maintenance, specialist knowledge, and conversation. The chapter uses romantic projection to test the band, then refuses the hierarchy that would make romance significant and material artistic infrastructure trivial.

## What the camp actually rehearses

The chapter begins with a conventional improvement project. Furb, Murasaki, and Yumeno have arranged a weekend camp at Shizuka's studio-equipped villa, bring Matsuri as the Player's delegated guardian, rehearse intensively, and imagine that stronger playing might lead to large festival appearances. Yet almost no technical musical problem becomes the narrative conflict. The camp's greater function is to place the four members in a temporary household where they cook, wash dishes, notice one another's silences, and confront an imagined fracture in the group.

This domestic labor is not separate from band formation. Matsuri cooks curry after practice; Murasaki and Yumeno clean; members negotiate breakfast and later converge on helping prepare another meal. The villa lets them experience QOP not only as performers synchronized onstage but as people maintaining the conditions under which the others can work and rest. The title's “queens” are therefore tested less by virtuosity than by whether they can respond ethically to a member whose life may lead elsewhere.

Matsuri's chaperone status initially makes her appear the stable adult. She cooks, accepts responsibility from the Player, and knows the context of Furb's distress. But she also supplies the speculative claim that the absent “important person” is a man, invites the others into it, and later calls them children for worrying about a separation her own inference invented. Murasaki's reminder at the end restores accountability. Care and maturity are practices, not fixed ranks: the nominal guardian can create the confusion, while the younger members can articulate the chapter's most generous response.

## A romance produced by missing information

The overheard half of Furb's call contains all the signs needed for a farewell plot: someone is going far away, she will no longer be able to visit casually, she calls the separation lonely, and a later message fixes the departure date. None of these signs is false. The error lies in supplying a romantic human referent before asking what kind of relationship the language describes.

Matsuri justifies the inference through a generic model of the teenage girl. Furb may publicly seem devoted only to bass and music, she reasons, but she is still a high-school girl with time outside the band's view. That observation properly acknowledges an unseen private life; it also treats heterosexual romance as the expected explanation for an emotionally serious attachment. Yumeno's alarm at the “shadow of a man” and her instant construction of rivalry amplify the presumption into comedy.

The others repeatedly say they should not pry into a matter between the people concerned, then continue to watch, infer, and intervene. Their restraint is sincere but unstable because uncertainty has already become a shared story. This matters to the band portrait. Affection makes them attentive to small changes in Furb, but attention without knowledge can turn another person's opacity into material for projection. The chapter does not punish them harshly; it lets the misreading reveal values while making its origin legible.

Yumeno's reaction retains a further ambiguity. Her routine declarations of attraction to women, distress at a possible male rival, and later delight at Furb's praise make her desire overt within the chapter's comic register. That does not convert every band relation into a settled romance, nor does it identify an exclusive couple. Her escalating fantasy instead refuses heterosexuality as the only available grammar: once the “rival” becomes Furb's bass, Yumeno simply recasts an instrument as an intimidating beloved and answers by committing herself more fully to guitar.

## Permission to choose otherwise

When the three finally confront Furb, their advice is immediate: if someone important is leaving, she should abandon the camp, go after him, and speak honestly. Matsuri assures her that no one will blame or stop her. Murasaki later extends the hypothetical beyond the weekend. If Furb ever finds someone important enough to leave the band for, she should consult QOP—but the members would not claim the authority to forbid her.

This is stronger than a declaration that QOP will never separate. The band proves its bond by declining to make the bond coercive. Furb's membership matters enough that her departure would hurt, but her companions place her capacity to choose above continuity at any price. Consultation is requested because a shared project creates obligations of communication and transition, not because the group owns the future of each member.

Furb responds with a present-tense commitment. She has staked herself on bass and cannot now imagine anyone she would pursue at the cost of QOP. “Now” is crucial: the statement confirms her priority without turning it into an eternal contract. Yumeno's answer—she will stake everything on guitar so that Furb cannot ignore her—translates attachment into artistic challenge. Their relation is sustained not by making exit impossible but by becoming musicians each other actively wishes to remain beside.

The imagined crisis also produces reciprocity. Murasaki tells Furb to speak if something happens; Furb immediately requires the same of Murasaki. The latter jokes that disclosure will depend on circumstances, then relents. The exchange shifts concern away from a uniquely vulnerable Furb toward a bandwide norm: none of them should be expected to remain legible without being asked, and none is exempt from the difficult work of telling the others what may change their shared life.

## The shop is not a punchline

The reveal—that a music store is moving to larger premises—deflates the invented romance, but it does not expose Furb's sadness as absurd. She relies on the shop for checking her bass's condition, its unusually broad stock, and conversations with staff about obscure studio musicians. It is simultaneously technical service, archive, educational network, and social space. Distance threatens the casual frequency through which that relation works.

Murasaki explicitly observes that this loss may be more troublesome than the imagined problem with a man. The line blocks a simple hierarchy between “real” personal feeling and comic attachment to an object. Furb cares about her bass, but her grief is not literally for an inert instrument alone. It is for an ecosystem of people, expertise, access, and accumulated familiarity organized around music.

The shop's expansion also makes the event ethically mixed. Strong sales and larger premises are good for the business, so Furb decides a regular customer should celebrate. Economic success at one scale nevertheless removes local access at another. Her mature response does not require pretending the loss disappears; she can support the shop's flourishing while remaining lonely about what its relocation changes for her.

This material ground matters to QOP's ambitions. Festival dreams and disciplined rehearsal depend on maintained instruments, specialist retail, people willing to discuss neglected musical histories, borrowed studio space, meals, schedules, and supervision. The chapter's modest crisis inventories the infrastructure underneath rock autonomy. A band that appears self-contained onstage is actually held up by a dense network of labor and places.

## Turning a false story into a possible song

After the misunderstanding is resolved, Furb wonders whether QOP could make a song about parting—specifically, one that would not be soggy or sentimental. The chapter does not show such a song being written, so the thought is an artistic possibility rather than a completed work or confirmed origin story. It nevertheless explains what the mistake has generated.

The band has rehearsed separation affectively without separating in fact. Its members have felt fear, jealousy, generosity, embarrassment, relief, and renewed commitment around a referent they got wrong. Those feelings can remain usable once the plot that summoned them collapses. QOP's imagined unsentimental farewell song would not deny loss; it would translate loss into the group's own forceful idiom, just as the chapter translates an awkward rumor into clearer terms for voluntary belonging.

The final return to curry and collaborative cooking is consequently more than reset comedy. After imagining the household dissolving, the members choose the next mundane act that keeps it going. Their camp succeeds even though the source supplies no technical assessment of improved playing: they have practiced how a shared project survives private opacity, possible departure, and the refusal to possess one another.
