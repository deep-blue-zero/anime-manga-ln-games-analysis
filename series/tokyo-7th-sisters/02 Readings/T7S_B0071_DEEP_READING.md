# T7S_B0071 — work enters the home

## Result

The group unanimously accepts the Rainbow Dome live, but the episode immediately distinguishes shared assent from shared security. Professional success has entered school corridors, family businesses, dorm arrangements, private photographs, and the language through which strangers classify the members. “Work” can no longer be left at the stage door.

Haru is publicly described as a successor to Seven Sisters and refuses the label. Her objection is not ingratitude toward the predecessors; it is resistance to a category that turns a relation to history into a replacement claim. The same revival that opens Rainbow Dome also creates copies, markets, and pressure on unknown younger idols. Nanasta helped reopen the field, but it cannot govern every consequence of that success.

The managers' exhaustion and lack of organizational protection make the group's handmade ideal vulnerable to a flattering institutional argument: the Player “raised” them, therefore he should build a larger structure worthy of them. The episode does not yet expose the full proposal. It shows why it will be tempting. Protection, staffing, privacy, and scale are real needs; the danger will lie in who receives authority when those needs are met.

## Revival produces succession pressure

Young would-be idols treat the older Seventh Dome as history and the new Rainbow Dome as the future. Their aspirations establish that the idol field has revived, but the conversation also exposes a copy effect: once one route is publicly legible, institutions and individuals reproduce its surface.

Haru encounters the harsher form in a school corridor. Others call 777☆SISTERS the successors to Seven Sisters. ## A global live is collectively desired

In a flashback six days earlier, all twelve accept the offer through the group's established “want” procedure. They understand the dome as a future landmark for young idols and compare its worldwide scale to Seven Sisters' 2031 Christmas broadcast. This is informed aspiration at the level the scene supplies: the venue, visibility, and symbolic opportunity are known and desired.

The vote does not include the later contract conditions or threat structure. Consent to the live cannot prospectively authorize every institutional means used to produce it. That distinction becomes essential once an outside partner presents itself as the only way to preserve the event.

## Privacy and care become organizational questions

Paparazzi pursuit reaches Haru's daily route. Sawara temporarily moves into the girls' dorm to protect the family shop and her sisters from attention. Sanbon Ribbon's family-facing work is no longer simply charming content; visibility has consequences for people who did not choose the same job.

Hime, Momoka, and Sawara recognize that success can feel lonely and burdensome. The episode refuses a clean opposition between public achievement and private authenticity. Work funds and enlarges their lives, while its demands rearrange residence, family exposure, and available attention. A responsible institution must therefore protect boundaries without treating the protected person as property.

## Professional competence coexists with managerial collapse

The NI+CORA shoot shows Musubi and Susu functioning with considerable autonomy. A producer praises the Player as the person who “raised” Nanasta and asks why he has not created a larger organization. The praise narrows twelve people's development into one adult's authorship at the exact moment that adult is physically failing.

Haru later cleans Nanasta, asks Coney to keep watching, and finds the manager asleep or collapsed from exhaustion. The old HoloCom, kept inside a lunch box, returns as an unresolved object. The juxtaposition makes the missing infrastructure visible: one individual cannot remain the sole shield, scheduler, witness, and keeper of memory.

The episode does not establish that corporate partnership is unnecessary. It establishes why “necessary support” must not be allowed to answer the separate question of control.

## A second numerical ideology appears among trainees

A cynical trainee treats family-supported idol activity as “playing house” and defines work through numbers. Shinju is hurt but does not convert injury into domination. The scene anticipates an institutional logic that will later make numerical output the only recognized sincerity.

Yet the trainee's material concern is not wholly false. Some people cannot afford indefinite aspiration, and “job” can name a boundary that protects life from a failed dream. The episode preserves that pressure before any antagonist weaponizes it. Critique of numerical reduction must not become contempt for people who need wages, limits, or an exit.
