## Factual reconstruction

### 1. A desired approach fails before it becomes audible

Coney conducts a demanding lesson. Haru and Tomoe are both exhausted, but Tomoe reaches nausea first and names her “go-home club” baseline as incompatible with the workload. Afterward, Tomoe repeatedly tries to offer Haru a new towel. Her speech never rises above the surrounding conversation before Haru leaves for a meeting with Musubi. Tomoe then performs a falsely easy, belated version of the offer after Haru is gone and admits that she failed to speak to another idol again.

The scene distinguishes lack of social execution from lack of social intent. Tomoe bought or brought a clean towel, tracks Haru's need, and tries several times to give it. Her care is materially prepared before it is communicatively successful. The Player notices only after the opportunity has passed and asks what she was muttering; he does not mediate the missed exchange.

### 2. Idol status has not supplied the belonging Tomoe expected

Alone in the park, Tomoe says she believed becoming an idol would place her among sociable idols. Several months later, she judges herself unable even to hold a normal conversation. She inventories appearance as though qualifying as “cute” should mechanically yield friendship, then treats the failure as evidence that the world is hopeless. Tea and a cactus become comic substitutes for human reciprocity.

Her self-account is not reliable evidence that nobody has tried to befriend her. It is evidence of the model she is using: visible idol qualification should produce automatic access, and the absence of effortless access becomes a verdict on the self. The chapter immediately complicates that model by making the expression that reaches others the one she performs when she believes she is alone.

### 3. Private improvisation makes an audience before Tomoe knows one exists

Tomoe improvises a self-denigrating song, naming herself the apostle of non-communication, less than even an ugly duckling, and unable to become popular. She jokingly announces it as her own lyric and composition, fantasizes that it will outsell Seven Sisters, and then calls the performance empty. Haru has returned within earshot. Two young men also hear the voice, call it extremely cute, and infer that the singer may belong to nearby Nanasta.

This is not a hidden “true self” replacing an artificial idol. The song is itself a shaped performance: rhyme, exaggeration, announcement, imagined market success, and despair are interleaved. What changes is the immediate social condition. Without a visible audience to anticipate, Tomoe can turn shame into form. The passersby respond first to the voice, not to institutional packaging or visual recognition.

### 4. Praise is initially parsed as hostility

The next scene begins with people gathered outside Nanasta. Haru tells Tomoe that they came for her song. Tomoe first assumes she must have inspired resentment, then treats Haru's explanation as a cruel joke and the crowd as collective bullying or an anti-Tomoe group. Hime's rough compliment does not immediately dislodge that frame. Haru urges Tomoe to greet them and physically pushes her forward; Tomoe protests the push but then says she will go out.

The audience is real, but proof does not enter an empty interpretive field. Tomoe's existing expectation of ridicule determines what admiration initially looks like. Haru's confidence supplies access to the encounter, yet the means are not neutral: encouragement and bodily pressure coexist. The text does not support turning Haru into either pure rescuer or deliberate aggressor.

### 5. Recognition raises the stakes faster than it builds capacity

Once the visitors identify Tomoe as the park singer and cheer, she briefly accepts that someone other than her cactus may be a fan. She adopts a large stage greeting and imagines becoming a “real swan.” That surge does not stabilize her body. As she attempts to begin, dizziness and nausea intensify. Haru offers a retreat; Tomoe refuses and then vomits in front of the crowd.

The failure is public and physical, not a lack of will. Tomoe chooses to continue after Haru makes withdrawal available, but her choice does not confer performance capacity. The chapter therefore refuses the simple sequence “external validation → self-belief → successful debut.” Recognition can be accurate and still overload the person receiving it.

### 6. The accident does not erase the voice, and the voice does not erase the accident

Tomoe continues singing by improvising the disaster itself: a once-in-a-lifetime chance turned into the worst accident, a desire to disappear, the cactus, and a hope for future happiness. Hime cannot find an adequate response; Haru describes Tomoe as having overextended herself. Arriving late, the Player hears the song without knowing who is singing and calls the voice cute. Haru identifies the singer as a talented, cute idol, while Tomoe ends by drinking tea to soothe her stomach.

The ending supplies corroboration from three differently placed listeners: passersby who heard the park song, Haru who knows the singer, and the Player who hears the post-accident improvisation before identification. Their agreement supports a textual claim that Tomoe's voice is perceived as appealing. It does not provide acoustic verification, prove a stable talent essence, cure her social anxiety, or transform public humiliation into uncomplicated triumph. Her last action is bodily recovery, not a confident encore.

## Analytical findings

### Communication is an uneven channel, not a binary trait

Tomoe cannot deliver a towel across a few feet, yet can project an improvised song far enough for strangers to locate its institution. The contrast does not mean that song is her authentic language and speech is false. Each channel redistributes risk. Direct conversation requires an answer from a known person; solitary improvisation initially permits no answer at all. The audience forms after the expressive act, which is why it can precede her defensive forecast.

### Recognition is evidence, not treatment

The chapter gives Tomoe unusually strong counterevidence to her belief that she cannot reach people: strangers seek her out, cheer, and ask for the song again. Her threat model nevertheless remains active, and the encounter ends with a severe somatic response. A later portfolio must test whether recognition becomes usable knowledge or remains an event she can explain away.

### Self-deprecation has both protective and productive functions

Tomoe's comic despair anticipates humiliation and lets her author it first. It also becomes the material of an actual song that listeners value. The productive result does not make the defense harmless: once an audience is present, the same form carries a wish to disappear and sits beside physical collapse. The chapter makes creative agency and self-injury adjacent without declaring them identical.

### Nanasta is porous to accidental public formation

The audience is not assembled through a scheduled live or managed campaign. A voice crosses the park, listeners infer the nearby institution, and the institution's threshold becomes an improvised venue. This expands Nanasta's public function while exposing its lack of preparation: neither staff nor peers appear to manage the crowd, protect Tomoe's transition, or establish a safe performance condition before she is pushed forward.

## State handoff

- **Tomoe:** now has externally corroborated evidence that her singing reaches people, including listeners who do not first know her identity. She remains unable to treat positive attention as safe, experiences severe public-performance nausea, and ends without a stable social or stage breakthrough.
- **Haru:** recognizes an underseen peer capability and acts as its advocate. Her support includes both reassurance and unexamined physical/social pressure.
- **Nanasta:** becomes discoverable through unscheduled local circulation of a member's voice. Its threshold can host spontaneous audience formation, but this case exposes weak transition and safeguarding practice.
- **Open tests:** whether Tomoe can offer care directly; whether she can perform with a known audience without collapse; whether peers learn to scaffold rather than propel; whether she can retain praise as evidence; and whether self-authored comic song expands beyond anticipatory self-erasure.
