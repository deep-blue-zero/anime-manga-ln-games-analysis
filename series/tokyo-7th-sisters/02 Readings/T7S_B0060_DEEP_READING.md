# T7S_B0060 — the absolutely invincible party

## Result

A proposed HoloCom game about the Seven Sisters prompts the group to imagine themselves as heroes, enemies, and marketable character types. Mito then remembers a childhood game with Nicole. She objected that killing minor enemies could not produce peace for those excluded from it; Nicole accepted the premise and proposed a party that would make minor enemies and the demon king smile through Mito's song. The fantasy's founding ideal is therefore jointly made: Mito supplies the ethical objection, Nicole converts it into collective form and exuberant action.

Adult Mito wonders whether that exchange became the Seven Sisters. Nicole says she has forgotten and redirects attention to a costume. The episode preserves the resonance but refuses a certified origin myth. Its closing move is more important: Mito addresses the recipient and offers Nicole as someone who may make that person a little happier. Memory does not restore an untouchable monument. It transmits a fallible person through an invitation to future relation.

## A game inside the game

Mito frames the episode retrospectively as an “incident.” Rui arrives excited because a HoloCom Seven Sisters game has been proposed, though she was distracted by costume ideas and retained few details. Mana identifies her family's Misonoo Electronics as the developer of the first HoloCom unit, and Memoru describes the platform as globally ubiquitous.

The exchange establishes a material institution without making the Seven Sisters owners of it. Their identities can be translated into game roles by an electronics company and consumed through a common platform. Rui's incomplete briefing allows the members to fill the absent design with fantasies of bosses, allies, powers, and costumes. Their speculative play is another act of self-authorship inside a commercial proposal.

## Mito's excluded enemy

The group's role-play triggers Mito's childhood memory. In a game with Nicole, she asks whether defeating small enemies and a demon king truly makes a peaceful world. Peace for the victor would exclude the beings defined as disposable obstacles. Nicole does not dismiss the objection. She proposes making the small enemies and demon king smile with Mito's song instead.

The ethical premise originates in Mito's refusal of a world divided into savable party and killable remainder. Nicole supplies a practical-poetic transformation: make a party, sing, dance, and produce smiles rather than victory through elimination. Their “absolutely invincible party” is invincible by changing the game's objective, not by acquiring overwhelming force.

This childhood ideal should not be sanitized. Making everyone smile can become an expansive claim over unknown people, and the other memories show Nicole coercing those nearest her. Later story has already forced that ideal toward bounded responsibility. EPISODE 0.0 nevertheless proves it was a real aspiration rather than a retrospective slogan invented by successors.

## A distributed origin, not a sole founder

The scene is easily misread as proof that Nicole invented the Seven Sisters alone. Its actual sequence distributes authorship. Mito identifies the moral defect. Nicole accepts it, imagines a group response, and names the pair invincible. Song belongs specifically to Mito in the proposal; collective form and confidence belong strongly to Nicole; the later group contains four additional authors whose practices the previous episodes have established.

Adult Mito asks whether this was the origin of the Seven Sisters. Nicole does not confirm it, saying she forgot and turning to Mito's costume. The deflection can express embarrassment, genuine forgetting, or refusal of solemnity; the source does not choose among them. Mito's “perhaps” remains an interpretation inside the work, not documentary proof of direct causation.

## Costume, stage, and continuity of modes

The adult group moves from backstage speculation into public costume and performance. As in Rui and Memoru's episodes, costume change does not reveal a false self replacing a true one. It is a coordinated transition into the mode required by the stage.

Mito's narration then summarizes the group's ordinary life: they sing, dance, make noise, and make people laugh; sometimes it goes well and sometimes it does not. “Invincible” is not empirical freedom from failure. It is Nicole's name for a practice that can continue through imperfect outcomes.

That distinction prevents the memory from becoming tragic dramatic irony alone. The group will eventually dissolve, so institutional invincibility is false. Yet the ideal has effects beyond institutional survival, as the already-consumed 2034 story demonstrates. A practice may fail to preserve its original organization and still remain causally active in people who received it.

## Mito hands Nicole to the recipient

The closing address changes the archive's function. Mito asks the recipient to accept Nicole, who will probably make that person a little happier. She does not promise universal salvation or provide a complete biography. The “little” bounds the claim; “probably” preserves uncertainty; encounter replaces mastery.

Mito is therefore not only the remembered companion. She becomes narrator and custodian, selecting what another person should receive while preserving Nicole's unpredictability. The person transmitted is affectionate, generative, forgetful, and boundary-crossing across the six memories. The gift is not a purified icon.
