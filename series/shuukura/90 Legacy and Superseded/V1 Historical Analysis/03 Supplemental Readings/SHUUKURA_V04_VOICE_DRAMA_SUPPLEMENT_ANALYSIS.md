# SHUUKURA V04 Voice-Drama Supplement Analysis

# XVI. Volume 4 voice dramas: what kind of supplementary text are they?

KADOKAWA's campaign explicitly distinguishes the materials.

The publicly released **「放課後、不真面目な私たち」** is labeled **書き下ろしボイスドラマ**—a newly written voice drama.

The second uploaded file is explicitly **「仙台さんと卒業式のその後で」試聴版**—a trial/sample of a limited drama. KADOKAWA's campaign states that quiz participants could unlock limited voice-drama material and publicly posted the full newly written drama plus the trial clip on April 18–19, 2024. [![](https://www.google.com/s2/favicons?domain=https://prtimes.jp&sz=128)プレスリリース・ニュースリリース配信シェアNo.1｜PR TIMES](https://prtimes.jp/main/html/rd/p/000014601.000007006.html?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

That means we should not treat these two the same way.

**「放課後、不真面目な私たち」 is an original audio-script supplement.**

**「仙台さんと卒業式のその後で」 is primarily an adaptation/performance text built from the climactic Volume 4 material.**

That distinction determines their analytical value.

---

# XVII. Haneda's Volume 5 afterword tells us exactly why the original voice drama exists

I checked the Japanese Volume 5 EPUB in your archive directly.

Haneda writes that she was fortunate enough to **write the scripts herself** and deliberately packed them with:

> 「宮城と仙台の声で聴きたい台詞」

lines she wanted to hear **in Miyagi and Sendai's voices**.

She specifically says the scripts include things the two often say, and that she even made Sendai speak with **「にゃん」** attached to her sentence endings.

Then she jokes that she wanted Miyagi to do the same but couldn't make it work because Miyagi becomes **難攻不落**—“impregnable,” essentially impossible to crack—in situations like that.

This tells us something important about canon weight.

These dramas aren't merely publisher advertisements in which actors happen to improvise cute dialogue.

They are **author-scripted character-performance experiments**.

Their purpose is partly:

> What happens when verbal traits that exist silently on the page become physically audible?

That's analytically valuable even when they add little plot.

---

# XVIII. 「放課後、不真面目な私たち」 is a voice-characterization piece by design

Even without pretending I possess a complete transcript, the authorial statement lets us classify the work quite confidently.

Haneda deliberately selected:

-   familiar Miyagi/Sendai verbal behavior;

-   lines she particularly wanted voiced;

-   deliberately playful material such as Sendai's 「にゃん」.


So the drama's main contribution is probably not:

> previously unknown plot information.

It is:

> **performative confirmation of character voice.**

That matters unusually much for *Shuukura* because prose voice has been one of its central formal achievements from Volume 1.

Miyagi and Sendai differ not merely in what they believe.

They differ in how they **occupy language**.

Miyagi tends toward:

-   denial;

-   clipped resistance;

-   negative formulations;

-   commands;

-   annoyed corrections;

-   saying an intimate word while denying that she will say it.


Sendai is more:

-   conversationally fluid;

-   teasing;

-   adaptive;

-   persistent;

-   capable of making emotionally dangerous statements sound casual.


The voice drama takes those textual patterns and asks Lynn and Kana Ichinose to turn them into **sound**.

That gives it high character value even if its plot contribution is small.

---

# XIX. The title 「放課後、不真面目な私たち」 is itself a miniature thesis about their private relationship

“After school, the unserious us” is a very good title.

Sendai's public identity has been constructed partly around being:

-   competent;

-   accommodating;

-   academically capable;

-   socially reliable.


Miyagi similarly inhabits an ordinary student identity, although less polished.

Their private after-school world is where the socially regulated versions loosen.

There they can be:

-   petty;

-   childish;

-   provocative;

-   ridiculous;

-   sexually curious;

-   annoyed;

-   lazy;

-   playful.


The drama's very premise therefore foregrounds something we've been analyzing throughout the novels:

> **Miyagi's room permits versions of them that school does not.**

“Unserious” does not mean insincere.

Quite the opposite.

One of *Shuukura*'s central paradoxes is that the girls often become **more emotionally truthful while behaving more absurdly**.

The ridiculous command can reveal more desire than a polite conversation.

The teasing response can reveal more attachment than a formal declaration.

Being “不真面目” together is one route toward being less socially performed.

---

# XX. The 「にゃん」 joke is surprisingly diagnostic of Sendai

Haneda's comment about making Sendai talk like a cat sounds like pure bonus-material silliness.

It also fits Sendai extraordinarily well.

Sendai is the more **performatively flexible** of the pair.

She can inhabit:

-   good-student Sendai;

-   socially pleasant Sendai;

-   annoyed private Sendai;

-   teasing Sendai;

-   obedient Sendai;

-   sexually forward Sendai;

-   apparently ridiculous roles imposed by Miyagi.


One of her abilities is:

> **I can enter the game and then manipulate the game from inside.**

That's exactly why the original command structure gradually ceased to guarantee Miyagi dominance.

Miyagi says:

> perform X.

Sendai often does—

and then discovers how far she can push X.

So of course Sendai can be made to attach 「にゃん」 to her sentences.

The author finding **Miyagi** impossible to force into the equivalent joke is equally appropriate.

Miyagi resists externally authored presentation.

Remember the Volume 3 cultural-festival material: even being told to **smile appropriately as a café server** irritates her.

Sendai is highly adaptive.

Miyagi is highly resistant.

The audio-drama joke takes that serious personality contrast and converts it into comedy.

---

# XXI. This also complicates “authentic Sendai versus fake Sendai”

Back in Volume 2, Miyagi hated the overly pleasant Sendai she encountered during their attempted ordinary friendship outing.

We argued then that it would be too crude to say:

> public Sendai = fake
> private Sendai = real.

The voice-drama premise reinforces that.

Sendai's flexibility is not merely a mask imposed by society.

It is also something she **enjoys using playfully** in intimate situations.

She can perform because performance itself is part of her personality.

The key variable isn't whether Sendai is performing.

It is:

> **Who chose the performance, and for whom?**

Public Sendai adapts partly because she needs social stability.

Private Sendai can play because she trusts Miyagi enough to be ridiculous.

Those are psychologically different uses of the same ability.

---

# XXII. The voices matter because the novels are full of things that look similar on paper but should sound different

Consider Miyagi saying:

> 「仙台さん」

That can mean:

-   irritation;

-   warning;

-   curiosity;

-   embarrassment;

-   desire;

-   reassurance;

-   don't leave;

-   stop;

-   continue;

-   I have no idea what to say.


Likewise Sendai's:

> 「宮城」

can be:

-   teasing;

-   gently corrective;

-   frustrated;

-   confrontational;

-   intimate;

-   uncertain.


Prose supplies surrounding interior narration to disambiguate these.

Audio strips that support away.

The actor has to do the work through:

-   timing;

-   pitch;

-   hesitation;

-   emphasis;

-   breath;

-   softness;

-   speed.


That makes the voice drama an unusually interesting **stress test of Haneda's characterization**.

Can these two be recognizable without their internal monologues?

The fact that Haneda herself wanted to hear particular recurring lines strongly suggests that was part of the pleasure of writing the scripts.

---

# XXIII. 「仙台さんと卒業式のその後で」 has a different value: it externalizes the climax

The second file is not best treated as additional plot.

Its title is the same as Volume 4's climactic Chapter 9.

And the available file is specifically marked:

> **試聴版**

trial/sample.

So we should treat it as an **adaptation fragment**, not as though we possess a full separate story. [![](https://www.google.com/s2/favicons?domain=https://prtimes.jp&sz=128)プレスリリース・ニュースリリース配信シェアNo.1｜PR TIMES](https://prtimes.jp/main/html/rd/p/000014601.000007006.html?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

This makes it interesting for almost the opposite reason from 「放課後、不真面目な私たち」.

The original drama adds voice to an author-created supplementary situation.

The graduation drama takes an existing scene whose meaning depends enormously upon Miyagi's interior narration and asks:

> **What does this conflict sound like if all the reader receives is what the girls actually say?**

That is potentially revealing.

---

# XXIV. The graduation climax is almost entirely about what Miyagi cannot say aloud

Recall how much of the prose scene exists inside Miyagi's head.

She knows:

-   she doesn't want Sendai to disappear;

-   she fears the Sendai she cannot observe;

-   she has been collecting “last memories”;

-   she has failed to erase those memories;

-   she is jealous of an imagined future she cannot control;

-   she wants Sendai to show visible sadness;

-   she is preparing to terminate the relationship precisely because she cannot trust its continuation.


But what does she actually say?

Things like:

> return the necklace.

> the deadline has expired.

> we agreed this ends at graduation.

> I won't see you anymore.

The gap between:

> **internal meaning**

and

> **audible dialogue**

is enormous.

A voiced adaptation makes that gap impossible to ignore.

The listener does not directly hear Miyagi's elaborate theory of abandonment.

They hear a girl trying to convert heartbreak into administrative language.

That's extremely *Shuukura*.

---

# XXV. Sendai's vocal role in that scene is structurally the opposite

Sendai's high-school arc has increasingly involved forcing the implicit question into audible form.

She asks versions of:

> Do you want me here?

> Will you see me afterward?

> What do you actually want?

Then she finally presents the room.

This makes her the character whose greatest narrative function is often:

> **turn Miyagi's unspoken attachment into a decision Miyagi must answer.**

In prose, Sendai sometimes feels overbearing because we can see all of Miyagi's discomfort and resistance internally.

In audio, I would expect the balance to shift depending strongly on delivery—but without a reliable transcript/performance read I won't pretend to tell you exactly how Lynn and Ichinose play every beat.

What we can say is that **voice makes Sendai's method much more naked**:

she talks,

Miyagi resists,

Sendai keeps the interaction moving.

That is the central power dynamic we've been tracking since Volume 1.

---

# XXVI. “Shiori” is especially important as something spoken

The graduation scene contains perhaps the definitive collapse of the school naming rule.

Sendai calls:

> **志緒理**

The significance on the page is obvious.

But names are inherently sonic.

A given name being **heard** carries something prose description cannot fully reproduce.

For three volumes:

> 志緒理

has effectively been forbidden territory between them.

Sendai can touch Miyagi intimately before she is allowed to call her Shiori.

The instant school ends, the old institutional prohibition loses its legitimacy.

Sendai weaponizes the name to make Miyagi stop walking away.

In audio adaptation, that is exactly the kind of moment for which having an established actress becomes useful: the formal significance of the name becomes embodied as somebody physically saying it.

Again, because your current file is only the trial version and I do not have a reliable transcript of its exact excerpt, I am distinguishing here between **the source scene the drama adapts** and what I can verify as present in this particular 1:46 clip.

---

# XXVII. The two audio pieces therefore complement each other unusually well

Conceptually, they represent the two poles of the high-school relationship.

### 「放課後、不真面目な私たち」

The private after-school world.

Their idiolect.

Play.

Commands.

Teasing.

The versions of themselves permitted inside the exceptional space.

### 「仙台さんと卒業式のその後で」

The exceptional structure expires.

School ends.

The contract ends.

The name rule breaks.

Miyagi tries to terminate the relationship.

Sendai replaces the old spatial structure with a shared home.

In other words:

> **one audio piece celebrates the old private language; the other dramatizes the moment that language ceases to be enough.**

That's a very fitting pair for Volume 4.

---

# XXVIII. The audio's biggest analytical contribution is actually negative

By “negative,” I mean it tells us what disappears when prose interiority disappears.

*Shuukura* is built on alternating first-person narration.

The reader constantly knows:

> what she says
> versus
> why she tells herself she said it.

Audio dialogue strips away the second half.

That creates an experiment:

> **How misleading would Miyagi and Sendai seem if we only heard them?**

The answer is probably: extremely.

Miyagi sounds much more hostile and detached than her actual attachment warrants.

Sendai can sound much more confident than she actually feels.

This mirrors the entire social problem within the story.

Maika hears Miyagi say:

> I don't know Sendai well.

She cannot hear:

> Sendai has become part of what constitutes me as a person.

The voice dramas place **us** temporarily closer to Maika's epistemic position.

We hear what outsiders could hear.

We lose access to the rationalizations and fears underneath it.

That makes voice adaptation particularly thematically appropriate for this series.

---

# XXIX. And this brings the Maika SS and audio material together

At first they look unrelated.

One is Maika's quiet interior monologue.

The others are promotional voiced scenes.

But together they ask the same question:

> **What can another person know about Miyagi and Sendai from what becomes externally observable?**

Maika has:

-   glances;

-   reactions;

-   weird defensiveness;

-   information leaks;

-   Sendai's unusual interest.


The voice-drama listener has:

-   spoken words;

-   pauses;

-   tone;

-   verbal habits.


Neither has unrestricted access to the protagonists' interiority.

The first four novels trained us to rely heavily on internal narration.

These supplements remind us:

> relationships also acquire an external form.

And by Volume 4, that form is becoming increasingly difficult to disguise.

# XXX. A corpus correction: you do **not** quite have the complete Volume 4 audio set

This emerged while checking the audio's provenance.

Haneda states explicitly in the Volume 5 afterword:

> **「ボイスドラマは……限定のものを含めて３本あります」**

There are **three** voice dramas in total, including the limited material.

Your current archive contains:

1.  the full public **「放課後、不真面目な私たち」**;

2.  only the **試聴版** of **「仙台さんと卒業式のその後で」**.


The surviving secondary archive record identifies another quiz-limited newly written piece as:

> **「夏追う冬、残された時間と私たち」**

and describes it as supplemental material situated around Volume 4 Chapter 8, **「私と宮城に残された時間」**. [![](https://www.google.com/s2/favicons?domain=https://www.bilibili.com&sz=128)Bilibili](https://www.bilibili.com/video/BV1gDePzeEAy/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

I would give the **existence of a third drama high confidence**, because Haneda herself says there were three in the Japanese afterword we possess. The title/content identification of the missing one relies on a surviving secondary mirror rather than the currently accessible official campaign page, so I would mark that specific identification as slightly lower-confidence than the first two.

The practical archival status is therefore:

> **one complete Volume 4 drama held; one partial/trial drama held; at least one additional limited drama absent.**

And since your graduation file is only the public trial, the **complete version of that dramatization is absent too**.

So I would *not* mark the Volume 4 voice-drama corpus complete.

---

# XXXI. How much does the missing audio matter?

For our **literary interpretation of the high-school arc**, not much.

Volumes 1–4 are overwhelmingly the governing text.

The audio cannot plausibly overturn:

-   Miyagi's abandonment structure;

-   Sendai's pursuit strategy;

-   the collapse of the ¥5,000 system;

-   the university problem;

-   the meaning of the necklace;

-   the roommate proposal.


For **character voice and performance analysis**, however, the missing material matters more.

Haneda explicitly wrote these scripts around things she wanted to hear the actresses say.

That means they're unusually concentrated evidence for:

-   characteristic speech;

-   teasing cadence;

-   how much emotional meaning can be carried by repeated phrases;

-   the difference between Miyagi's resistance and Sendai's adaptive playfulness.


So I would assign:

> **low plot loss, modest character-text loss, moderate performance-analysis loss.**

Nothing here should delay the literary synthesis.

But I would preserve a placeholder in the archive for the missing audio if it ever becomes obtainable.
