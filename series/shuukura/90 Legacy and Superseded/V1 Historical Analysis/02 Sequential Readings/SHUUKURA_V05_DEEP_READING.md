# SHUUKURA V05 Deep Reading — Transcript Extraction

# 『週に一度クラスメイトを買う話』Volume 5

## \+ 「ワニの憂鬱」 / 「黒猫の期待」

### When the contract disappears, intimacy becomes harder

Volume 5 is an unusually good test of the high-school synthesis we just established. Volume 4 seemed to solve the practical problem: the ¥5,000 relationship could die because Miyagi and Sendai had chosen to continue as roommates.

Volume 5 immediately demonstrates that this was only a logistical solution.

The old contract was restrictive, evasive, and frequently unhealthy—but it also supplied something both girls desperately needed: **a grammar for proximity**. It answered questions they otherwise had to answer themselves.

When can Sendai enter Miyagi's room? When she is called.

Why is she there? Because she was paid.

May Miyagi give her an unusual order? Yes, within the rules.

Why is Sendai obeying? Because that is the arrangement.

What happens when the afternoon ends? Sendai goes home.

Cohabitation abolishes almost all of that at once. Now Sendai is there every morning. There is no money. Neither owns the other's time. Each has a private bedroom. Nothing says they may kiss; nothing says they may not. There is no natural point at which Sendai leaves, because this is Sendai's home too.

Sendai captures the paradox early in the book: they have become physically so close that it feels **too close**, yet when she backs away the resulting distance feels **too far**.

That is Volume 5's central problem.

The high-school arc asked how they could create intimacy through artificial rules. The university arc begins by asking:

> **Can they tolerate intimacy when the rules no longer tell them what they are allowed to want?**

The answer, at least initially, is no.

So they start making rules again.

---

## The ¥5,000 was an excuse—but it was also infrastructure

One of the smartest things Volume 5 does is refuse the easy retrospective interpretation that the money was *only* a dysfunctional relic.

Sendai had wanted the transactional relationship gone. Yet almost immediately after moving in together she thinks:

> 「今は五千円があればと思う。」

Now she sometimes wishes the ¥5,000 still existed.

That is not because Sendai wants to be bought again. She misses what the money **authorized**.

She explicitly recognizes that once the ¥5,000 vanished, she also lost what felt like her rights to:

-   enter Miyagi's room;

-   touch her;

-   kiss her;

-   receive commands;

-   occupy the peculiar relational position they had spent years constructing.


“Roommate” grants enormously more physical access in one sense—they share an address—but much less **relational permission**.

This is one of the major revisions Volume 5 makes to our synthesis:

> **The contract was an alibi for intimacy, but alibis can become rituals, and rituals can become a shared language. Removing the lie does not automatically teach the people who used it how to speak honestly.**

That is why they almost immediately reproduce the old structure in domestic form.

They establish household rules: don't enter each other's rooms without permission, don't let friends stay overnight, notify the other about late returns or overnight absences, and eat together when their schedules allow.

The most revealing negotiation concerns meals. Sendai cautiously proposes eating together **once a week**.

Miyagi effectively asks: why only once?

If they tell each other when they'll be late, they can eat together more often.

That tiny exchange may be the cleanest statement of how far Miyagi has come.

The original title premise was:

> once a week, Miyagi buys Sendai's time.

The new domestic rule becomes:

> **whenever possible, let's eat together.**

One purchased afternoon has started expanding toward ordinary daily recurrence.

---

## But the old command system immediately reappears inside the new rules

The household rules include a punishment:

> if one of them breaks a rule, she has to do one thing the other says.

That is almost comically transparent.

They have abolished:

> money → command

and reinvented:

> broken household rule → command.

This is psychologically revealing because **both girls miss the old mechanism**.

Sendai discovers that violating a rule could earn her a “punishment” from Miyagi and is perversely tempted by the possibility because it would restore a familiar structure.

Miyagi later stretches the definition of a broken promise so that she can claim a punishment right against Sendai.

Neither wants to return to being buyer and purchased classmate.

Both nevertheless want a mechanism that permits:

> *I may ask something unusual of you now, and neither of us has to explain why.*

The ¥5,000 disappears.

**Contractual thinking does not.**

And that substantially complicates the Volume 4 conclusion. “Roommate” isn't the end of their defensive structures. It is the newest one.

---

# The apartment solves Miyagi and Sendai's opposite definitions of an unhappy home

The paired LN chapters **「宮城に言うおかえり」** and **「仙台さんに言うおかえり」** are probably the strongest purely literary construction in Volume 5.

Sendai's relationship with 「ただいま」 is heartbreaking in an understated way.

At her family home, saying *I'm home* creates an expectation:

> somebody should answer.

Repeated silence therefore carries rejection.

When Sendai first lives alone in the new apartment, she actually enjoys saying 「ただいま」 to an empty room because an empty room **cannot fail to answer her**. There is nobody from whom she ought to expect warmth.

Then Miyagi is going to arrive.

Suddenly Sendai wants something better than the safety of no expectation.

She wants:

> a home in which someone answers.

Miyagi comes from the opposite side.

Her old home was:

> a place where somebody was supposed to be there, but that somebody did not come home.

Her new definition is explicit:

> this home is different; someone besides her lives here, and that person **will come back**.

The mirrored chapters therefore complete the domestic thesis we identified earlier.

Miyagi's wound says:

> People leave.

Sendai's says:

> People may be present without making you feel welcomed.

The simplest household phrases answer both:

> **ただいま。**
> **おかえり。**

Miyagi even realizes that saying 「おかえり」 gives her a peculiar sense of purpose: there is now somebody for whom her *welcome home* is needed.

The romance has moved a long way from the ¥5,000 precisely because its most important new language consists of words children learn before they understand romance.

---

# The shared apartment makes them equal residents—and private rooms become the new border

Volume 4's shared-home proposal appeared to remove Miyagi's old spatial dominance.

That prediction proves correct.

In the high-school arrangement, Sendai entered **Miyagi's room**. Miyagi controlled access.

Now each woman has her own bedroom.

That equalizes sovereignty—but produces another problem.

Sendai finds Miyagi's bedroom almost impossibly distant despite sleeping literally on the other side of the wall.

The wall becomes one of Volume 5's most effective spatial images:

> **maximum physical proximity, minimum assumed permission.**

Sendai can hear Miyagi through it.

She knows Miyagi sleeps immediately beyond it.

But she may not cross the doorway without permission.

In one of the better inversions of the old relationship, Sendai now starts manufacturing ridiculous excuses to gain access. She asks to borrow books or a dictionary she does not genuinely need. Miyagi recognizes that some of these excuses are excuses and nevertheless participates.

This is essentially the ¥5,000 logic reduced to miniature:

> We both know I want to come in.
> We both know you know.
> Nevertheless, let's give the entrance another stated purpose.

Even after years together, they are extraordinarily attached to **plausible deniability**.

---

# “Roommate” becomes a protective fiction more important than its literal meaning

Volume 5 makes the function of the new label much clearer.

When Sendai wants greater physical intimacy, Miyagi objects:

> roommates don't do this.

Sendai responds with deliberate sophistry:

> nothing about being roommates prevents them from doing it.

Technically, Sendai is right. “Roommate” describes shared housing, not a complete interpersonal code.

Psychologically, Miyagi is the one who understands what the word is actually doing.

After their major intimate encounter she thinks of **ルームメイト** almost as a **ticket** guaranteeing four years together.

If that category disappears, she fears the four-year future may disappear with it.

This is crucial.

“Roommate” is not merely Miyagi refusing to admit that they are romantically involved.

It is another **attachment contract**.

High school offered:

> until graduation.

University offers:

> four years as roommates.

Miyagi can tolerate relationships if she can give them a container.

The label therefore has contradictory functions:

It understates their intimacy.

It also protects the continuity of their intimacy.

That is why simply saying “they should admit they're girlfriends” would miss the psychological problem. For Miyagi, a vaguer emotional category might actually feel **less secure** than a concrete housing arrangement with a four-year duration.

---

# The former ¥5,000 becomes communal property

Volume 5 extends the money motif beautifully.

When they need an electric kettle and other shared items, Sendai proposes using the accumulated money.

Miyagi objects:

> that's Sendai's money; Miyagi gave it to her.

Sendai insists:

> **「二人のお金だから。」**
> It's our money.

This is an enormous conceptual change.

The money has progressed through:

**Miyagi's money → payment to Sendai → Sendai's preserved archive → housing capital → shared household fund.**

It is no longer an instrument proving asymmetric authority.

It is becoming **the commons**.

They use what once documented private domination to purchase mundane infrastructure for collective life.

An electric kettle may sound absurdly unromantic compared with jewelry or kisses. That's precisely why it matters.

The high-school relationship converted money into exceptional intimacy.

The university relationship converts that same money into:

> **appliances.**

The romance is becoming domestic property.

---

# The crocodile, black cat, and platypus form a surprisingly elegant genealogy of the relationship

Volume 5's object system becomes even clearer when the two commemorative SS are added.

**「ワニの憂鬱」** gives Miyagi's old crocodile tissue cover a hilariously grandiose personality. It considers itself Shiori's first and greatest admirer, resents Sendai for forcing the move, resents the black cat for receiving Shiori's attention, and imagines Sendai as somebody trying to steal **“my Shiori-chan.”** Yet beneath the comic possessiveness, it notices something important: Miyagi looked anxious while packing it, and the crocodile simply wants to get out of the box and comfort her. Shuukura - LN05 Release SS - ワニ…

The crocodile is therefore almost perfectly suited to represent the **old Miyagi room**:

possessive, longstanding, resistant to change, attached to Shiori, suspicious of Sendai, but fundamentally oriented toward remaining beside her.

**「黒猫の期待」** comes from a completely different emotional register. The black cat was chosen by Sendai and brought into Miyagi's old room. It also dislikes the dark box, because confinement reminds it of the bag in which it was originally transported. But when Miyagi packs it beside the crocodile, the darkness is suddenly **not lonely**. The cat becomes excited about the move partly because Miyagi has told it that moving means seeing Sendai. Shuukura - LN05 Release SS - 黒猫…

That produces an almost perfect miniature of the domestic theme:

> **The same cramped box feels different when someone else is inside it.**

Place matters.

Company changes what the place means.

And the two objects are literally packed together:

-   the object from Miyagi's pre-Sendai domestic life;

-   the object Sendai chose for Miyagi.


The old home and the new relationship are not discarded separately.

They are transported in the same box.

Then the main novel adds a third plush object: the **platypus tissue cover**, purchased for the shared apartment using their shared money.

That gives us a neat material progression:

**crocodile:** Miyagi's old private world.

**black cat:** Sendai's gift entering Miyagi's private world.

**platypus:** something created directly by their shared household.

The platypus is particularly valuable because neither “owns” its symbolic origin. It exists because **they went shopping together for their home**.

Later Miyagi moves it into Sendai's private room, and Sendai begins reading its presence as evidence that Miyagi may return there.

A ridiculous tissue cover has become a small diplomatic mission crossing the bedroom border.

That is extremely on-brand for this series.

---

# Cohabitation does not make ordinary conversation easy

Volume 5 also challenges one assumption that could arise from the high-school arc.

Miyagi and Sendai are intensely compatible in certain ways.

They are not obviously compatible in all of them.

At one point Miyagi thinks that with Maika, conversation can simply continue without much effort. With Sendai, they don't necessarily have enough common interests to fill time naturally.

That is important.

The books are not arguing:

> these are soulmates because they like all the same things.

They often don't.

Sendai can find Miyagi's movie boring.

Miyagi can dislike Sendai's suggestions.

They have different clothes, tastes, social styles, and ways of spending time.

Yet Sendai later reflects that she would rather watch another boring movie with Miyagi than make some potentially more entertaining plans with her new university friends.

That reveals the actual basis of their compatibility:

> **the activity is frequently secondary to the presence.**

This is romantic, but it deserves a counter-reading.

It can also become narrowing.

A relationship in which the other person's mere presence outweighs most external opportunities can turn into dependency if neither develops an independent adult life.

Volume 5 wisely begins giving them those independent lives.

And neither handles it especially well.

---

# University creates the most frightening thing possible: versions of each other they cannot observe

The second half of Volume 5 repeatedly uses the vocabulary of **knowing**.

The chapter titles themselves increasingly announce the problem:

「私の知らない宮城」
the Miyagi I don't know.

「宮城の全部を知りたい」
I want to know all of Miyagi.

「私の知らない仙台さんと私の知らない私」
the Sendai I don't know and the me I don't know.

That progression is deliberate.

Cohabitation produces an apparent paradox:

> They see each other more than ever, but there is now **more of each person to be ignorant of**.

Different universities create separate social worlds.

Sendai acquires new friends.

Miyagi continues seeing Maika.

Sendai begins working.

Neither can rely on the ambient shared high-school environment to infer what the other is doing.

That intensifies a theme that was already present in Miyagi's attachment style:

> **The unknown version of the person I love feels dangerous because I cannot secure my place inside that version's life.**

And now Sendai feels the same thing.

---

# Sendai's tutoring job is not just a jealousy plot

The tutoring material is one of Volume 5's most interesting choices because it turns something formerly private into something ordinary.

During high school, “private tutor” was Sendai's excuse for spending summer with Miyagi.

Now tutoring becomes:

> an actual paid job.

This is almost a desacralization of their history.

Miyagi's distress isn't primarily about the specific middle-school student being a credible romantic rival.

It's worse than that.

The role itself is being **reused**.

Miyagi imagines Sendai:

-   sitting beside another student;

-   explaining work;

-   using the same teaching voice;

-   occupying a situation strongly associated with their private summer.


Something that belonged to their exceptional relationship has entered the marketplace.

That is why Miyagi thinks she could tolerate almost any other part-time job more easily.

The conflict is about **relational uniqueness**.

Miyagi has always needed proof that what she receives from Sendai is not interchangeable with what Sendai gives everyone else.

Tutoring someone else threatens exactly that.

But Sendai's job has an equally important meaning from Sendai's perspective: **adult independence**.

She wants income.

She wants the ability to support herself eventually.

She does not want to be forced back into her family home after university.

So when Miyagi asks her to quit, Sendai refuses.

That refusal is important and healthy.

Sendai accommodates Miyagi in many things, but there remains a real independent self that Miyagi cannot simply command into compliance.

If Sendai abandoned economic independence because Miyagi disliked her having a life beyond the apartment, the shared home would become a trap rather than a choice.

The novel seems aware of that tension.

---

# Miyagi's jealousy is increasingly about replacement rather than sex

Miyagi eventually acknowledges that different universities are something she can, with effort, accept. She can imagine what Sendai is doing there by connecting it to known parts of her.

Tutoring is harder because it maps too directly onto Miyagi herself.

What she fears is almost:

> **Someone else is being placed into my old position.**

This connects directly to her abandonment psychology.

Replacement is especially threatening because it converts:

> *you left*

into:

> *you found another version of me.*

That is also why her fixation on making Sendai remember promises becomes stronger.

Miyagi does not merely want Sendai nearby.

She wants to be **non-fungible**.

---

# Sendai is jealous of exactly the same thing from the other direction

Sendai keeps thinking about Maika.

But again, the important question isn't whether Sendai thinks Maika and Miyagi are secretly romantic.

She envies **access**.

Maika knows:

-   how Miyagi behaves at university;

-   what Miyagi looks like when she smiles there;

-   aspects of Miyagi's ordinary life that Sendai cannot witness.


Sendai asks Miyagi to smile for her specifically because she knows Miyagi smiles elsewhere.

That leads to an ugly but revealing little sequence in which Sendai physically manipulates Miyagi's mouth into a smile and gets bitten hard enough to leave a lasting injury.

The scene isn't admirable behavior from either girl.

Its emotional logic is transparent:

> Miyagi wants access to the Sendai who might be reproduced in somebody else's life.

> Sendai wants access to the Miyagi whom Maika receives outside their home.

They both want **exclusive registers of the other person**.

This is the same desire that drove Miyagi's fascination with angry or unpolished Sendai in the high-school arc.

Now Sendai has caught the disease.

---

# The piercing is the most important object in Volume 5

The evolution from earlier bodily marks to Miyagi's earrings is one of the strongest motif developments so far.

Earlier Miyagi wanted to pierce **Sendai**.

Sendai refused because that degree of bodily permanence was too much.

Volume 5 reverses the direction.

Miyagi asks Sendai to pierce **her**.

And the motive is not primarily fashion.

Miyagi wants Sendai to remember.

More specifically, she wants Sendai to remember their promises whenever she sees the alteration Sendai herself made to Miyagi's body.

This produces a much more complex kind of possession.

Earlier:

> Miyagi wants to leave her mark on Sendai.

Now:

> **Miyagi asks Sendai to leave Sendai's mark on Miyagi.**

Miyagi is effectively saying:

> I will carry physical evidence that you changed me, so you must remember that you changed me.

There is vulnerability in that.

There is also manipulation.

Miyagi wants Sendai to experience responsibility, even guilt, when seeing the earrings. She wants the act to be memorable precisely because Sendai was the person who caused the pain and permanent alteration.

So this is not simply:

> romantic matching jewelry.

It is **attachment anxiety translated into the body**.

And Sendai quickly gives the mark her own possessive interpretation, privately thinking of it as something resembling a sign that Miyagi is hers—even while knowing that isn't literally true.

Possession has now become reciprocal.

---

# Miyagi simultaneously wants the piercing seen and hides it from Sendai

This contradiction is particularly good characterization.

The entire purpose is for Sendai to remember.

Then Miyagi starts hiding her ears from Sendai.

Why?

Because Sendai looks.

Sendai calls the earrings cute.

Sendai's attention turns the abstract symbolic mark into **being seen**.

Miyagi can tolerate:

> carry Sendai's mark.

She finds it much harder to tolerate:

> stand in front of Sendai while Sendai openly appreciates what she sees.

That distinction recurs throughout the series.

Miyagi often desires intimacy in indirect material form more readily than she tolerates direct positive appraisal.

Praise strips away the hostile or contractual disguise.

---

# The platypus moves into Sendai's room at exactly the right moment

After the piercing, Miyagi brings the shared platypus tissue cover into Sendai's private room.

Sendai doesn't need another tissue box.

That is not the real function.

The shared object becomes evidence:

> Miyagi entered this room.

And eventually:

> Miyagi may enter this room again.

The object is therefore doing what the ¥5,000 notes once did.

It turns uncertain future presence into something tangible.

*Shuukura* continues to be a story in which the characters are better at communicating through household clutter than through declarative language.

---

# Their old intimacy returns because both of them keep engineering excuses for it

Volume 5 is full of scenes where one girl claims to be done with the old dynamic and then constructs a mechanism that restores it.

A rule is broken.

A punishment is owed.

Miyagi accepts Sendai's clothes or makeup, then argues that reciprocity means Sendai should do something she asks.

Sendai says she will obey anything if Miyagi will issue an instruction.

Miyagi insists that Sendai phrase a desire as a **request**.

Their vocabulary is changing slowly:

**command → punishment → exchange → request.**

That is meaningful.

When Sendai wants Miyagi to kiss her, Miyagi rejects an insufficient formulation and makes her ask properly:

> 「キスしてください。」

Only then does Miyagi agree.

This is one of the healthier developments in the volume because it introduces something their original contract deliberately avoided:

> **Say what you want and ask the other person to choose whether to give it to you.**

Of course, they do not suddenly become good at this.

But the possibility now exists.

---

# Miyagi is learning that a request is emotionally more dangerous than a command

Commands assume authority.

Requests admit dependence.

If Miyagi commands:

> kiss me,

the resulting act can be attributed to the rule.

If Sendai says:

> please kiss me,

and Miyagi does it, Miyagi has to own her answer.

That is one reason the gradual migration from command to request matters more than the physical acts themselves.

The core development isn't “they become more sexually intimate.”

It's:

> **they are slowly becoming responsible for admitting that they want the intimacy.**

This will make the volume's climax particularly complicated.

---

# The first explicitly sexual encounter is about knowledge as much as desire

Chapter 9, **「宮城の全部を知りたい」**, is the volume's decisive escalation.

Sendai's desire is framed repeatedly in epistemological terms.

She wants:

-   reactions nobody else knows;

-   a voice Miyagi has never let anybody hear;

-   bodily responses Miyagi herself has barely discovered;

-   increasingly exclusive knowledge.


This is the sexual continuation of a desire present since Volume 1.

Miyagi wanted:

> the Sendai nobody else receives.

Now Sendai wants:

> **all of Miyagi.**

Sexual intimacy turns this metaphor almost literal: what part of this person can I know that nobody else knows?

That gives the scene considerably more thematic purpose than simple erotic escalation.

But it also makes Sendai's possessiveness more dangerous.

The wish to “know everything” about another person can collide very quickly with that person's right to remain partially unknowable.

And here it does.

---

# The consent problem in Chapter 9 is the most serious one in the series so far

This scene requires precision because the text supports several facts at once.

Before the escalation, Sendai asks for permission. Miyagi does not simply remain passive: she instructs Sendai to turn off every light and later reflects that she understood what Sendai wanted and **decided to allow it**.

Miyagi establishes limits during the encounter.

She does not want clothing removed.

She rejects certain touching.

She rejects marks.

Sendai respects several of those boundaries.

So it would be inaccurate to characterize the entire encounter as something Miyagi never consented to.

But there is a specific moment that cannot be waved away.

During a more intimate act Miyagi says:

> 「……やっぱり、やだ」

roughly, **“Actually, I don't want this.”**

Sendai's internal response is effectively that Miyagi's physical arousal appears inconsistent with rejection and that, in the moment, she cannot make herself stop. She continues, adding only a condition along the lines of “tell me if you really can't.”

That reasoning is ethically indefensible.

Physiological arousal is not consent.

Earlier general permission does not eliminate the ability to withdraw permission.

And making Miyagi produce some stronger category of refusal after she has already said 「やだ」 improperly shifts responsibility back onto the person setting the boundary.

This is more serious than some of their earlier loophole-testing because the text gives Sendai an explicit verbal withdrawal and then shows her reasoning past it.

Miyagi's Chapter 10 perspective complicates the **character interpretation**, but not that normative point. Miyagi retrospectively understands the encounter as something she knowingly permitted, found pleasurable, and does not frame as an assault or unwanted violation. She says there was no money, no exchange, and no easy “it just happened” excuse: she understood what Sendai wanted and chose to allow it.

That tells us how **Miyagi** integrates the event.

It does not retroactively turn Sendai's response to a specific 「やだ」 into good consent practice.

This distinction is worth preserving:

> **Textual fact:** Miyagi initially consents and later interprets the overall encounter as chosen.

> **Textual fact:** she verbally withdraws/rejects a particular continuation, and Sendai continues.

> **Inference about Haneda's intent:** the novel appears more interested in their mutual desire, shame, boundary ambiguity, and poor communication than in framing the scene as traumatic violation.

> **Ethical judgment:** Sendai nevertheless crosses a meaningful consent boundary at that moment.

Volume 5 therefore does not solve the power problems of the high-school relationship by making intimacy reciprocal.

It proves that **mutual desire and ethical conduct are separate questions**.

That is one of the sharper conclusions the series has earned so far.

---

# Miyagi's aftermath is not regret over pleasure—it is terror at having become observable

Chapter 10's title is excellent:

**「私の知らない仙台さんと私の知らない私」**
*The Sendai I Don't Know and the Me I Don't Know.*

Miyagi's shock isn't primarily:

> I hated what happened.

She explicitly acknowledges the opposite.

She had not known her body would respond that way.

She had not known she could make those sounds.

She had not known intimacy with Sendai would feel that good.

And she suspects—without having a comparison—that the specificity of the experience has something to do with the fact that it was **Sendai**.

That means the encounter reveals two unknown people simultaneously.

Sendai discovers a Miyagi nobody—including Miyagi—had known.

Miyagi discovers herself as someone capable of wanting and responding that intensely.

This is intolerable partly because **Sendai now knows it too**.

Miyagi repeatedly wishes she could erase Sendai's memory.

That's perfectly consistent with her old relationship to marks and memories.

The problem isn't merely:

> something happened.

It's:

> **somebody else now possesses evidence about who I am that even I did not possess yesterday.**

For Miyagi, that is an extreme loss of control.

---

# Miyagi finally runs to Maika, and this tells us something important about the two relationships

Unable to face Sendai the next morning, Miyagi goes to Maika and asks to stay with her.

This is a useful reminder after the intensity of the central relationship:

> **Maika remains a functioning secure friendship.**

Miyagi can seek refuge there.

Maika does not demand immediate total disclosure. She does correctly point out that letting a conflict fester makes reconciliation harder.

Miyagi lies, telling her that the roommate is a relative.

The old secret therefore survives university.

In fact, it becomes harder to explain.

At high school Miyagi only had to conceal:

> I see Sendai after school.

Now she must conceal:

> **I live with Sendai.**

The shared apartment normalizes their relationship privately while making its social explanation more elaborate.

The Volume 5 subtitle, **「ふたりの秘密は一つ屋根の下」**, is very apt. Their secret has acquired an address.

---

# Maika once again identifies the relational shape while supplying the wrong category

Maika wonders whether the supposedly troublesome roommate is actually Miyagi's **boyfriend**.

She also suspects the piercing may have something to do with this hidden romantic partner.

As during the cultural-festival material, Maika's heteronormative hypothesis is wrong in identity but increasingly correct in structure.

Miyagi is:

-   secretly cohabiting with someone;

-   emotionally entangled with that person;

-   physically marked through that relationship;

-   fleeing to Maika after a crisis of intimacy.


“Secret partner” is getting harder to distinguish from the truth.

Only **Sendai** remains cognitively excluded.

---

# Maika touching Miyagi's ear gives us a clean control experiment

Maika casually touches the pierced ear.

Miyagi immediately recognizes that it feels completely different from Sendai touching the same place.

This is important evidence because the series contains so much physical interaction between female friends that physical contact alone cannot carry our interpretation.

The variable isn't:

> ear touching.

The variable is:

> **Sendai.**

Miyagi's body assigns different meaning to the same physical action depending on who performs it.

That makes the specificity of her attraction unusually clear without needing any identity label.

---

# Sendai's new friend and student also begin broadening the world beyond the dyad

Volume 5 introduces an important structural change that shouldn't be lost beneath the romance.

Sendai now has an independent university identity.

**Komatsu Mio** begins connecting her to new people and eventually the tutoring opportunity. She represents a social world Miyagi does not possess direct access to.

**Hanamaki Kikyou**, Sendai's middle-school student, provides another useful contrast. Sendai has to figure out an actual professional teacher-student distance because this is the first time “tutoring” really is what the word claims to mean.

When asked why she became a tutor, Sendai explains that she enjoyed teaching a **friend**.

That friend is Miyagi.

The irony is wonderful.

Sendai can call Miyagi a friend when she needs a socially intelligible explanation for a stranger.

She still cannot comfortably make “friend” the governing category of the relationship itself.

“Friend” is now something like a **public alias** for the much stranger truth.

---

# Sendai's financial independence is one of the healthiest things happening in the volume

This matters because it cuts against a purely codependent reading.

Sendai wants to earn money partly so that after university she will not have to return to the family home.

She is building:

-   skills;

-   savings;

-   professional responsibility;

-   an independent future.


That means her life with Miyagi has the potential to remain **chosen** rather than economically compulsory.

Miyagi's jealousy therefore produces a genuine developmental test.

Can Miyagi tolerate a Sendai who has enough autonomy to leave and **still chooses to return home**?

That is exactly what Miyagi has never trusted.

Preventing Sendai from building outside independence would create the illusion of security.

Allowing it creates the possibility of something better:

> voluntary recurrence.

Volume 5 does not resolve that issue, but it places it at the center of the university arc almost immediately.

---

# The volume repeatedly asks whether they can let one another remain partly unknown

This may be the deepest thematic development beyond the high-school synthesis.

Miyagi's fear says:

> If there is a version of Sendai I don't know, perhaps that version does not need me.

Sendai increasingly thinks:

> If there is a version of Miyagi Maika knows and I don't, perhaps my access is not special enough.

Sexual intimacy temporarily appears to offer one solution:

> acquire knowledge nobody else possesses.

But that is not a sustainable answer.

No matter how intimate they become, Sendai will still have:

-   university classes Miyagi doesn't attend;

-   students Miyagi doesn't teach;

-   friends Miyagi doesn't know;

-   private thoughts Miyagi cannot read.


Miyagi will have the same.

So the real adult task emerging from Volume 5 is not:

> **know everything.**

It is:

> **trust what you cannot know.**

That is exactly the skill both girls are weakest at.

---

# 「ワニの憂鬱」 and 「黒猫の期待」 are comic, but they sharpen the transition surprisingly well

The two commemorative SS should not be treated as literal omniscient testimony; their plush narrators are deliberately whimsical.

But as paratext they are unusually well placed.

The crocodile experiences moving as **displacement**. Sendai has changed Shiori's future, displaced the old room, introduced a rival black cat, and seemingly stolen attention. Yet the crocodile also declares that wherever Shiori goes is where it belongs. Shuukura - LN05 Release SS - ワニ…

That is almost the old attachment world learning that:

> continuity does not require unchanged geography.

The black cat has the opposite orientation. It is frightened of confinement, but once it discovers the crocodile beside it, the box stops feeling lonely. It then looks forward to finishing the move because moving means seeing Sendai. Shuukura - LN05 Release SS - 黒猫…

So the two SS quietly state the Volume 5 domestic thesis before the protagonists can:

> **A place can change completely and remain tolerable if somebody familiar is there with you.**

For Miyagi and Sendai, “home” is being transferred from architecture to relationship.

# U35's visual program also changes with the story

Volume 5's illustrations reinforce the structural break.

The color material prominently places Miyagi and Sendai inside the **shared domestic environment**, especially the kitchen/household space. The black-and-white images repeatedly select moments involving private rooms, the platypus, grooming/clothes, food, and bodily closeness.

The ¥5,000 note is no longer the dominant visual object.

That makes sense.

Their material vocabulary has changed from:

> money + Miyagi's bedroom

to:

> **kitchen + shared objects + two bedrooms + bodies learning a new distance.**

The art therefore participates in the same shift as the prose: the relationship is no longer visually legible as one girl visiting another.

They occupy the same home.

# Volume 5's architecture

The chapter sequence is especially coherent when read around three changing questions.

The first movement asks **how close roommates are allowed to be**. The money is gone, household rules replace it, separate bedrooms become borders, and the girls discover that “doing nothing” can become an implicit rule even when nothing formally prohibits touch.

The middle movement asks **how much of the other person's outside life must belong to me**. Sendai develops university relationships and work; Miyagi develops her own routines with Maika. Jealousy focuses less on conventional romantic rivals than on other people gaining access to versions of Miyagi or Sendai unavailable inside the apartment.

The final movement asks **how much of the other person can I know**. Piercing, bodily marks, requests, private rooms, sexual intimacy, unknown reactions, and the aftermath all converge on the impossibility of complete knowledge.

That gives the book a clean conceptual progression:

> **distance → independence → knowledge.**

And underneath all three:

> **trust.**

# Updated Miyagi after Volume 5

The most important development in Miyagi is that she can no longer rely entirely on the fiction that intimacy happens *to* her through the contract.

She increasingly participates in constructing it.

She enters Sendai's room.

She creates exchanges.

She asks Sendai to pierce her.

She uses the old punishment system.

She leaves marks.

She asks for shared meals.

She permits a sexual encounter knowing what Sendai intends.

Her Chapter 10 recognition that she cannot blame the event on money, momentum, or exchange is significant.

Miyagi is beginning to own:

> **I chose something because I wanted something.**

But the cost is severe shame and retreat.

Her dominant fear is also changing subtly.

High-school Miyagi principally feared:

> Sendai will leave.

University Miyagi increasingly fears:

> **Sendai will become a person whose life continues beyond my ability to understand or influence it.**

That is a harder problem, because cohabitation cannot solve it.

Her current developmental task is therefore less “allow Sendai to stay.”

Sendai is staying.

It is:

> **allow Sendai to be independent while staying.**

---

# Updated Sendai after Volume 5

Sendai remains the more natural initiator and practical architect.

She establishes household structures.

She builds an adult social life.

She works toward financial independence.

She creates excuses to reopen private-room access.

She openly asks for things more often than Miyagi does.

But Volume 5 also exposes the danger in her central drive more clearly than any previous volume.

Sendai wants **more Miyagi**.

More access.

More reaction.

More knowledge.

More permission.

And when she feels she has enough evidence of Miyagi's desire, she can begin treating Miyagi's resistance as something to interpret rather than something simply to respect.

That is the ethical failure at the center of Chapter 9.

Sendai's emotional intelligence becomes dangerous when it turns into:

> *I know what you really mean better than your words do.*

She often genuinely does understand Miyagi's defensive behavior.

That does not grant her authority to override Miyagi's stated boundary.

So Sendai's developmental task is becoming very precise:

> **learn to pursue Miyagi without treating superior knowledge of Miyagi as permission to decide for her.**

That is a much more difficult problem than “be more assertive.”

Sendai is already assertive enough.

# Revised series thesis through Volume 5

The four-volume high-school movement ended with a prediction:

> ordinary intimacy may be harder for them than exceptional intimacy.

Volume 5 confirms it almost immediately.

The shared home should make everything easier.

Instead, the absence of the old excuse makes every act more legible.

A kiss without ¥5,000 is simply a kiss.

Entering the other's room is simply choosing to enter.

Eating together is something they both want.

Waiting for 「おかえり」 means expecting someone to return.

Jealousy can no longer be blamed on the odd buyer-purchased arrangement.

Sexual desire can no longer be quarantined inside a high-school game.

So the university arc begins by creating another set of excuses:

> roommate.
> household rule.
> punishment.
> broken promise.
> borrowed book.
> shared object.
> four years.

The story's central mechanism has survived the death of the title premise.

The revised thesis is therefore:

> **Shuukura is not really about whether Miyagi and Sendai can remove the structures they use to avoid emotional vulnerability. It is about whether each successive structure can become less coercive and more openly chosen until ordinary mutual commitment no longer requires an excuse.**

Volume 5 is not yet that endpoint.

In some ways it is messier than Volume 4.

But that's exactly why it works.

Graduation did not magically heal two girls whose attachment patterns had been built over years.

It only removed their most convenient explanation.

Now they have to discover whether a room in which someone always comes home can become a real home—or simply another highly elaborate contract.

And the most consequential line of development is no longer whether Sendai will come back.

She does.

The new question is:

> **Can Miyagi let Sendai leave the apartment each morning, become someone Miyagi cannot completely know, and still trust that the person who says 「ただいま」 that evening chose to return?**

That is a much richer problem than the original ¥5,000 premise, and it gives the university movement a strong reason to exist beyond merely extending the romance.
