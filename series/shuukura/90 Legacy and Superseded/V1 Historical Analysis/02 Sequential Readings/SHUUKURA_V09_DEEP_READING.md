Volume 9 changes my interpretation of the university arc more than I expected. Volume 7 was about **pace and the right to refuse**. Volume 8 was about **remaining connected through absence**. Volume 9 asks the next, harder question:

> **Can Miyagi and Sendai tolerate difference?**

Not merely physical separation, but the existence of another autonomous consciousness whose preferences, boundaries, friendships, memories, desires, and decisions are not identical to one's own.

The volume's great paradox is that **Miyagi—the character most associated with possession—begins demanding that Sendai become harder to possess**.

And the internal childhood story reveals why Sendai finds that demand extraordinarily difficult.

---

## I. Miyagi has discovered the fatal problem with perfect obedience

One of the series' oldest fantasies is:

> If Sendai does what Miyagi says, Sendai is Miyagi's.

Initially the ¥5,000 contract made this reassuring. Commands created a visible causal relationship. Miyagi did not have to wonder why Sendai was there.

By Volume 9, that logic is collapsing under its own success.

Sendai will accept almost anything Miyagi asks.

She lets Miyagi:

-   choose;

-   command;

-   mark;

-   determine activities;

-   determine food;

-   shape physical interaction;

-   claim her as 「私のもの」;

-   and increasingly decide what counts as acceptable behavior between them.


And Miyagi discovers that she **doesn't actually like the endpoint of that process**.

The crucial problem is epistemic.

If Sendai always says yes, what does Sendai's yes tell Miyagi?

Very little.

If she eats whatever Miyagi selects, Miyagi cannot learn what Sendai wants to eat.

If she goes wherever Miyagi wants, Miyagi cannot learn where Sendai wants to go.

If she accepts every command, Miyagi cannot identify Sendai's limits.

If she says she likes whatever Miyagi likes, Miyagi cannot tell where Miyagi ends and Sendai begins.

So Volume 9 produces one of the most important reversals in the entire series:

> **Miyagi starts wanting Sendai to disobey her.**

Not constantly. Miyagi still enjoys obedience enormously.

She wants something more contradictory:

> I want you to obey me because you choose to.
> Therefore I need proof that you are capable of not obeying me.

That contradiction is psychologically sophisticated.

A person who *cannot* refuse cannot provide the reassurance Miyagi actually wants.

---

## II. The zoo makes “preference” into a form of personhood

The zoo material initially looks like another domestic/date sequence following the aquarium.

Its deeper purpose is different.

At the aquarium, Miyagi learned to say:

> I had fun.

At the zoo, Miyagi begins asking:

> **Did you have fun? What did you want?**

She chooses the tiger partly because Sendai once said she liked cats.

That is surprisingly important.

Earlier Sendai habitually modeled Miyagi's preferences and then constructed plans around them. Now Miyagi is building part of an outing around something she thinks **Sendai might independently enjoy**.

But Sendai repeatedly gives her the same frustrating answer in different forms:

> If Miyagi is happy, that's enough.

Even when asked which animal she most wants to see again, Sendai ultimately routes the answer back through Miyagi: she wants to see Miyagi looking at the shoebill.

That is sincere.

It is also exactly the problem.

Sendai later internalizes Miyagi's shoebill interest so thoroughly that she can say:

> 「宮城が好きなハシビロコウは、私の好きなものにもなった。」

The shoebill Miyagi likes has become something Sendai likes too.

We should resist an overly simplistic reading here.

**Relationally acquired preferences are not fake preferences.**

People really do:

-   acquire foods from partners;

-   start liking music because someone important showed it to them;

-   become interested in places because they associate them with a loved person;

-   inherit rituals, jokes, habits and aesthetics.


We've already seen this with the rare cheesecake.

So Miyagi's eventual rule—

> 「私と同じっていうのは禁止」
> You're not allowed to answer “the same as me.”

—is developmentally useful but philosophically too severe if interpreted literally.

Sendai does not need preferences uncontaminated by Miyagi.

Nobody has a pristine, pre-relational self like that.

She needs the capacity to say:

> I like this because you introduced it to me, **and now I own that liking**.

Or:

> You like this. I don't.

Or:

> I don't know.

What frightens Miyagi is not influence.

It is **indistinguishability**.

---

## III. 「お互いの体温以外を知らなすぎる」 is almost a summary of the entire problem

Miyagi reaches a wonderfully compressed realization:

> 「お互いの体温以外を知らなすぎる。」

They know too little about each other besides each other's body temperature.

That line retroactively comments on the whole series.

These two women know things about one another that many conventional couples would consider profoundly intimate.

They know:

-   physical responses;

-   embarrassment;

-   jealousy;

-   private rooms;

-   bodily habits;

-   family wounds;

-   domestic routines;

-   where the other sleeps;

-   how the other sounds when emotionally overwhelmed.


Yet Miyagi does not reliably know:

> What's Sendai's favorite animal?

> What's her favorite color when “Miyagi's color” is excluded?

> What doesn't she want Miyagi to do?

> What does Sendai want when Miyagi's happiness isn't available as the answer?

Volume 9 therefore returns to something Sendai herself asked for on her birthday in Volume 7:

**ordinary information.**

The novels continue making the case that intimacy isn't just the depth of extraordinary access.

It is also the breadth of mundane knowledge.

---

## IV. Miyagi eventually makes one of her clearest requests for Sendai's autonomy

The most important conversation comes after Miyagi has attempted an extreme command partly because she wants to find something Sendai will finally reject.

Sendai effectively indicates that she would rather not do it but would comply because Miyagi ordered her.

Miyagi calls the command off.

That is significant.

She realizes:

> **Compliance will not answer the question she is asking.**

If she commands Sendai to reveal a boundary and Sendai obeys by crossing her own boundary, Miyagi has learned nothing except that Sendai obeys.

Later, with the cat-shaped chopstick rests in front of them, she finally formulates what she wants:

> 「仙台さんは本当に嫌なら嫌ってちゃんと言って」

If you really don't like something, say that you don't.

Then she broadens it:

> 「私といるときに嫌なことがあったら嫌だって言ってよ。あと、本当に好きなものも教えて。私と同じっていうのは禁止」

Tell me if there's something you don't like when you're with me. And tell me what you really like. “The same as me” isn't allowed.

Sendai asks why.

Miyagi answers:

> 「ただ仙台さんのこと知りたいだけ」

I just want to know about you.

And, under pressure:

> 「……興味があるから」

Because I'm interested in you.

That may be a more meaningful declaration for Miyagi than some conventional romantic lines would be.

Miyagi has spent much of *Shuukura* wanting **access** to Sendai.

Here she says she wants **knowledge of Sendai**.

Those aren't the same thing.

---

## V. The internal 「在りし日の私たち」 reveals why Sendai has such difficulty answering

This is where Volume 9 becomes much more psychologically ambitious.

The first half of the internal bonus opens:

> 「好きなものはお姉ちゃんだった。」

The thing/person Sendai liked was her older sister.

Young Hazuki's family is organized around her exceptionally capable older sister **Mutsuki**.

Mutsuki receives:

-   parental attention;

-   questions about dinner;

-   recognition for academic success;

-   the position of normative daughter.


Hazuki increasingly experiences herself as outside that family circle because she cannot remain identical to Mutsuki.

A 91 on a mathematics test is objectively excellent.

It is worthless to Hazuki because Mutsuki got 100.

The problem isn't:

> Am I doing well?

It becomes:

> **Am I the same as my sister?**

When Hazuki's performance diverges from Mutsuki's, parental attention declines.

And she constructs a devastating childhood rule:

> **Sameness produces belonging. Difference produces exclusion.**

This gives adult Sendai's accommodation an origin much older than Miyagi.

---

## VI. The fried-shrimp scene is almost a miniature theory of Sendai's damaged preference formation

Hazuki likes **エビフライ / ebi fry**.

Her mother no longer asks Hazuki what she wants for dinner.

She asks Mutsuki.

Mutsuki says:

> ebi fry.

Hazuki knows Mutsuki's actual favorite is meatballs and strongly suspects her sister is using her privileged position to get Hazuki the meal Hazuki likes.

Importantly, I don't read Mutsuki as the villain here.

The likely intention is caring.

Mutsuki seems to be trying to use the attention she receives to give Hazuki something.

The family structure surrounding that gesture is the problem.

Because Mutsuki repeatedly says:

> **I like ebi fry**

on Hazuki's behalf, Hazuki eventually loses confidence in the ownership of her own preference.

Was ebi fry really Hazuki's favorite?

Was it Mutsuki's?

Did Hazuki decide she liked it because Mutsuki kept naming it?

Did she once genuinely like it and then stop?

The answer becomes inaccessible.

That is startlingly relevant to adult Sendai.

When Miyagi asks:

> What do *you* like?

Sendai's internal machinery keeps answering:

> What does the person who matters to me like?

That isn't merely romantic devotion.

It's an old strategy.

---

## VII. The personality quiz makes the childhood mechanism almost explicit

Young Sendai receives an ordinary personality test from a classmate.

She cannot answer it.

Why?

Because questions about herself immediately trigger:

> **How would Mutsuki answer?**

That may be the most important developmental detail we have received about Sendai.

The socially competent girl who later becomes excellent at:

-   smiling;

-   adapting;

-   reading people;

-   responding appropriately;

-   becoming easy to be around


develops against a background in which **self-report itself has been destabilized**.

She is not simply hiding an inner catalogue of preferences.

The act of asking:

> What do *I* want?

has been contaminated by comparison with an attachment figure.

That radically sharpens our interpretation of Sendai's public adaptability.

---

## VIII. Adult Sendai is repeating the Mutsuki strategy with Miyagi

This is an inference, but I think the structural evidence is very strong.

With Mutsuki:

> If I become enough like her, perhaps I will belong.

With Miyagi:

> If Miyagi wants it, I can want it.
> If Miyagi likes it, I can like it.
> If Miyagi orders it, I can accept it.
> If Miyagi needs me, I will organize myself around being what she needs.

There is a huge difference, of course.

Miyagi is not Sendai's mother.

Their relationship contains genuine mutual desire.

Sendai genuinely enjoys many of the things she accepts.

She is not merely performing submission while secretly hating everything.

That distinction matters.

But Volume 9 suggests that Sendai's ability to enjoy accommodation and her childhood survival strategy have become interwoven.

That makes her earlier Volume 7 fear—

> 「私は宮城に侵食されすぎている。」

Miyagi is occupying too much of me—

considerably more serious.

She already has a history of trying to secure attachment by allowing another person to determine the shape of her self.

---

## IX. Sendai does have a self; the problem is that she habitually routes it through attachment

I would push against a possible reading that Volume 9 reveals Sendai to be “empty.”

She isn't.

We can identify genuine Sendai preferences all over the series.

She likes:

-   marked occasions;

-   preserving memories;

-   cooking;

-   bodily intimacy;

-   teasing;

-   extending shared time;

-   being physically close;

-   making plans;

-   bringing people into experiences;

-   sometimes styling Miyagi;

-   learning things;

-   and, overwhelmingly, Miyagi herself.


She is capable of saying no to people outside the relationship, as the Mio SS makes unusually clear.

The problem is narrower:

> **Sendai has trouble locating herself when the answer might place her in opposition to the person whose attachment matters most.**

That's different from having no self.

And it makes her problem more interesting.

She doesn't need to invent Sendai Hazuki.

She needs to stop treating **difference from a loved person as a threat to Sendai Hazuki's continued belonging**.

---

## X. 「小松澪と謎多き女」 proves that Sendai's inability to refuse is attachment-specific

The Mio supplemental story is extremely valuable here.

From Mio's perspective, Sendai is almost absurdly difficult to invite anywhere.

She refuses:

-   mixers;

-   shopping;

-   Sunday plans;

-   repeated ordinary invitations.


Mio has become close enough to jokingly ask whether Hazuki secretly dislikes her, and Sendai answers that if she disliked Mio she wouldn't spend time with her at all. Yet Mio notices something very precise: Sendai is friendly, smiles at everyone, gives off a “good person” aura, but **does not let people closer than necessary** and rarely goes out with friends. Shuukura - LN09 Release SS - 小松…

That rules out a very simple explanation of Sendai's Miyagi behavior.

Sendai is **not a woman constitutionally incapable of saying no**.

She tells Mio no constantly.

She can refuse a mixer effortlessly.

She can reject a shopping invitation.

She can prioritize her own schedule.

The collapse of refusal happens primarily around **Miyagi**.

That makes it less a generic passivity trait and more a specific attachment pathology—or, more neutrally, an attachment-specific surrender of autonomy.

---

## XI. But Mio also complicates our optimistic reading of Volume 8's social expansion

Volume 8 suggested Miyagi and Sendai were slowly building a larger relational world.

That's still true.

But Mio's SS shows how incomplete the process remains.

Mio perceives Sendai as:

> friendly but difficult to access.

Her university friends get a polished, pleasant Sendai who nevertheless protects an enormous amount of her private time.

Mio even wonders whether there must be a boyfriend because otherwise Sendai's habitual unavailability makes little sense. Sendai denies having one, and Mio cannot find any candidate in the university social world. Shuukura - LN09 Release SS - 小松…

We know the missing variable.

Sendai's life is increasingly organized around:

> Miyagi + home.

That can be read positively as prioritization.

But because Sendai has already worried that Miyagi occupies too much of her, we should not automatically celebrate every rejected outside invitation as romantic devotion.

The question remains:

> **Is Sendai choosing Miyagi from a sufficiently large life, or gradually making Miyagi the life from which every other choice is derived?**

Volume 9 deliberately leaves that tension open.

---

## XII. Miyagi's childhood story gives us the mirror pathology

The second half of **「在りし日の私たち」** begins with broccoli.

Young Miyagi remembers her mother making a cream stew with broccoli resembling a small forest.

Miyagi said the broccoli wasn't tasty.

Her mother looked sad.

After her mother's departure, Miyagi begins engaging in the kind of counterfactual thinking common to children confronted with an incomprehensible loss:

> If I had said it was delicious, maybe the story would have had a happy ending.

Intellectually, middle-school Miyagi knows this isn't causal.

Emotionally, broccoli has become linked to:

-   guilt;

-   maternal absence;

-   the fantasy of a correct action that could have prevented loss.


That's already revealing.

But then the story goes considerably further.

---

## XIII. Middle-school Miyagi shows us that she was never simply socially incapable

A friend comes to Miyagi's home.

They:

-   play games;

-   drink soda;

-   eat snacks;

-   discuss books;

-   disagree about literature.


Miyagi can say straightforwardly that she dislikes a popular fantasy novel because too many characters die and she doesn't consider the result a sufficiently happy ending.

This is important.

Her later difficulty with Sendai isn't explained by:

> Miyagi has always been unable to make friends.

She can make friends.

She can state preferences.

She can entertain someone.

She can disagree.

The problem emerges **after the friend leaves**.

Two pairs of shoes become one.

The room becomes perceptibly larger.

The empty space—**余白**—becomes oppressive.

Miyagi explains why she doesn't like inviting friends over:

> being with someone is enjoyable, but the room feels emptier once that person leaves.

That is almost the complete psychological foundation of the adult relationship.

---

## XIV. Miyagi's core attachment proposition is finally stated without disguise

Later, she reads an old picture book about a repeatedly reincarnating cat who eventually discovers love through a white cat.

The relationship is happy.

Then both cats eventually die.

Miyagi can understand why her mother called that a happy story.

She still rejects the ending.

She formulates her own ideal:

> 「私のハッピーエンドは、なにも欠けないほうがいい。」

Her happy ending is one in which nothing is missing.

Then the truly decisive statement:

> 「なくなるようなものだったら、最初からないほうがいい。」

If something is going to disappear, it would be better for it not to exist from the beginning.

There it is.

Almost the whole Miyagi Shiori problem in one sentence.

The ¥5,000 contract.

The rules.

The marks.

The four-year limit.

The fear of photographs.

The difficulty with birthdays.

The obsession with promises.

The need to define Sendai as hers.

They are different implementations of one childhood wish:

> **How do I love something without allowing it to become something I can lose?**

---

## XV. This also clarifies why memory frightens Miyagi so much

The childhood material adds another dimension to our Volume 8 photography analysis.

Miyagi tries to recreate her mother's scrambled eggs.

She cannot remember the seasoning.

She recognizes:

> 「反芻しないものは忘れてしまう。」

Things that aren't repeatedly recalled are forgotten.

Her mother's food once had a precise taste.

Eventually the taste becomes fog.

All that remains is:

> it was good.

And that hurts.

This produces an impossible relationship to memory.

Miyagi fears **remembering**, because memory preserves what has vanished.

She also fears **forgetting**, because forgetting means the vanished thing disappears again inside her.

Sendai's impulse is almost the reverse:

> photograph it; commemorate it; preserve it; repeat it.

Neither strategy solves mortality or change.

Volume 9 increasingly makes their relationship a negotiation between those two theories of memory.

---

## XVI. Food is now carrying both childhood wounds at once

The series' food motif becomes much more substantial after 「在りし日の私たち」.

For young Sendai, food is mediated through an unequal family structure.

Her mother asks Mutsuki what she wants.

Mutsuki may answer on Hazuki's behalf.

Hazuki eventually cannot tell whether a favorite food belongs to her.

For young Miyagi, food is disappearing maternal memory.

Mother's stew.

Mother's scrambled eggs.

The housekeeper's food.

Then convenience food eaten in an empty home.

So what do adult Miyagi and Sendai repeatedly do?

They eat together.

And Sendai cooks.

That means their ordinary meals are correcting *different* injuries simultaneously.

For Miyagi:

> someone is here and food recurs.

For Sendai:

> she is now the person creating the meal, outside a family table whose hierarchy once erased her preference.

The New Year's hot pot in Volume 9 is particularly good for this reason.

Miyagi barely has a family-hot-pot tradition because her father was rarely present and a communal hot pot makes little sense alone.

Now she and Sendai sit around one.

The scene isn't dramatically sentimental.

That's exactly what makes it work.

It isn't trying to recreate Miyagi's lost family.

It creates a different household ritual.

---

## XVII. Valentine's Day turns food into co-authorship rather than remembrance

The handmade chocolate sequence has a similar function.

Miyagi initially sees handmade chocolate as inefficient.

Objectively, it is.

Buying chocolate is easier.

But Sendai likes events.

She wants to make something together.

And while forming the truffles, the two women begin modifying what the other has made—Miyagi accidentally suggests animal shapes; Sendai adds details.

The object becomes neither:

> Miyagi's product

nor:

> Sendai's product.

It becomes something they authored through interaction.

That is a much healthier material symbol than a bruise.

The relationship no longer has to prove itself exclusively by one person leaving a mark on the other.

They can make something **third** that exists because both were there.

---

## XVIII. Volume 9 finally reveals one very clear Sendai preference: she likes occasions

This is easy to overlook because Sendai keeps claiming she has no meaningful preferences independent of Miyagi.

She plainly does.

Sendai likes:

-   Christmas;

-   New Year's;

-   Valentine's Day;

-   birthdays;

-   photographs;

-   planned outings;

-   commemorative objects.


She likes **ritualization**.

Miyagi has historically distrusted exactly that.

A special date becomes a high-resolution memory.

A high-resolution memory becomes something that can later hurt.

So there is a genuine difference between them.

Sendai:

> Let's make this day identifiable.

Miyagi:

> If we make this day identifiable, losing it will hurt more.

Volume 9 doesn't solve that by making one philosophy win.

Instead, the BOOK☆WALKER story finds a much better synthesis.

# XIX. 「私たちが過ごすなんでもない日々」 may be the best possible coda to the nine LN volumes

It is 11:21 p.m.

Nothing important is happening.

No birthday.

No holiday.

No graduation.

No ¥5,000.

No aquarium.

No zoo.

No dramatic revelation.

Sendai simply does not want to leave Miyagi's room.

So she starts **shiritori**.

Miyagi refuses.

Sendai persists.

Miyagi finally agrees on an absurdly Miyagi-specific condition: only things that fly.

She wins almost immediately because Sendai answers **シジミ / shijimi clam** and clams famously do not fly.

It is trivial.

That's why it matters.

Afterward Sendai finally states what she actually wants:

> 「なんでもない日に夜更かしして、一緒になにかするのって楽しくない？」

Isn't it fun to stay up late and do something together on an ordinary day?

Then her narration gives the real thesis.

There are far more **なんでもない日 / unremarkable days** in a year than commemorative ones.

She cannot turn every one into an unforgettable memory.

So she wants:

> **more time in which she and Miyagi simply spend ordinary days ordinarily together.**

That is an extraordinarily fitting endpoint for the currently published LN corpus.

---

## XX. The opposite of ¥5,000 turns out to be wasted time

The original relationship was aggressively quantified.

One week.

One visit.

¥5,000.

Commands.

Start.

Stop.

Go home.

There was always a mechanism explaining why time belonged to them.

By this BOOK☆WALKER story, Sendai's desire is basically:

> **Can we waste another thirty-nine minutes?**

Miyagi finally says:

> 「……十二時になったら、自分の部屋に戻ってよ」

Go back to your room at midnight.

Sendai promises.

That's still Miyagi.

There is still a boundary.

Still a time.

Still a negotiated endpoint.

But compare it with Volume 1.

Nobody purchases the interval.

Nobody needs a plausible social excuse.

Nothing needs to happen inside it.

They are preserving time precisely by **not requiring the time to justify itself**.

If I had to choose one scene to represent the whole series' development, I might choose this rather than any of the explicit romantic encounters:

> **11:21 p.m., two roommates arguing about whether a clam can fly because one of them doesn't want the ordinary evening to end.**

That is very close to the antithesis of the original ¥5,000 arrangement.

---

## XXI. But the BOOK☆WALKER story also refuses to let us pretend their boundary problem has disappeared

There is an important complication.

Sendai asks for a kiss as the price of returning to her own room.

Miyagi refuses.

Sendai then initiates one herself and intensifies it until Miyagi physically pushes her away.

So even this beautifully domestic little story contains the recurring flaw:

> **Sendai still frequently treats Miyagi's no as a stage of negotiation.**

That cannot simply be folded into “playful couple behavior.”

It belongs to the same ethical pattern we've been tracking.

The ordinary-time theme is lovely.

The consent practice remains defective.

Both are true simultaneously.

---

# XXII. In fact, Volume 9 is a regression in some consent areas

This is where I would revise some optimism from Volume 8.

Volume 8 gave us what looked like a clean photography rule:

> don't photograph Miyagi without permission.

Volume 9 demonstrates that the rule has not actually become internalized.

At New Year's, Sendai photographs Miyagi without prior permission.

Miyagi objects.

When she eventually permits **one**, Sendai takes more.

Then Miyagi later photographs a sleeping Sendai, who tells her to delete it.

Miyagi refuses because Sendai did the same thing.

That isn't reciprocal consent.

It is **reciprocal violation**.

“you violated my boundary, so I may violate yours” is not the mature endpoint of the photo motif.

The zoo later gives us a better version—asking, negotiated photography, each woman photographing the other—but Volume 9 makes clear that this progress is unstable.

---

## XXIII. The sleeping scene is one of Sendai's clearest ethical failures in the series

There is considerably less ambiguity here than in some of their sexually charged negotiations.

Miyagi establishes the penguin as a physical boundary in the bed.

She falls asleep.

Sendai knows the boundary is there.

Sendai crosses it.

She kisses and touches a sleeping Miyagi.

The next morning, when Miyagi suspects something happened, Sendai denies it.

I would classify that as a straightforward violation.

Miyagi is unconscious.

There is no contemporaneous consent to infer.

There was an explicit border.

And Sendai lies afterward.

What's especially interesting is that Sendai later decides she **shouldn't form a new promise with a half-asleep Miyagi** because Miyagi isn't sufficiently alert.

So Sendai understands that a semi-conscious person cannot meaningfully enter a new contractual commitment.

Yet she has already treated Miyagi's sleeping body as accessible.

That contradiction exposes a persistent weakness in Sendai's ethics:

> **she still gives formalized promises more moral weight than moment-to-moment bodily autonomy.**

That is precisely backwards.

---

## XXIV. This is an important counterargument to praising *Shuukura*'s consent writing too uncritically

The series is unusually interested in consent as an idea.

It has produced remarkably good lines such as Volume 7's:

> if wanting something doesn't require a reason, not wanting something shouldn't require one either.

Volume 9 explicitly develops:

> tell me when you dislike something.

It recognizes the distinction between compliance and preference.

It explores whether old promises bind future selves.

It distinguishes desire from permission.

All of that is sophisticated.

But the **behavioral consequences lag dramatically behind the conceptual insight**.

Sendai repeatedly:

-   initiates after refusal;

-   escalates until physically stopped;

-   reads previous patterns as present authorization;

-   treats Miyagi's underlying attraction as more authoritative than Miyagi's immediate words.


Miyagi has her own failures:

-   retaliatory photography;

-   coercive commands;

-   deliberate testing of boundaries;

-   possessive entitlement.


So I would not describe the series through Volume 9 as having arrived at a mature consent model.

A more accurate formulation is:

> **It has become increasingly capable of diagnosing the problem while its protagonists remain strikingly bad at living according to what they have learned.**

Whether later material gives that problem sufficient consequence remains an open question.

---

# XXV. Miyagi's command experiment is nevertheless a major ethical advance

The command she ultimately cancels is important precisely because it fails.

She wants Sendai to produce a refusal.

So Miyagi invents something she expects Sendai to resist.

Sendai essentially responds:

> I would rather not, but if you order me to, I will.

At that point Miyagi realizes the experiment is epistemically broken.

If she makes Sendai violate her own preference in order to prove Sendai has preferences, the command destroys the evidence it was intended to produce.

So she calls it off.

This is one of the clearest signs that Miyagi's relationship to power has changed since Volume 1.

Originally:

> **obedience was proof.**

Now:

> **obedience can conceal the truth.**

That's enormous.

---

## XXVI. The relationship now needs the other's “no”

This may be Volume 9's most important adult insight.

The word **no** is not simply a barrier to intimacy.

It makes intimacy meaningful.

If Sendai can say:

> I don't like that.

then:

> I like this

has more information in it.

If she can say:

> I don't want to go there.

then:

> I want to go with you

becomes more meaningful.

If she can say:

> no

to Miyagi,

then:

> yes

can become evidence of choosing Miyagi rather than merely submitting to Miyagi.

This gives the series a surprisingly strong answer to its original contract.

The ¥5,000 arrangement guaranteed compliance.

But guaranteed compliance is ultimately incapable of giving either woman what she actually wants:

> **proof of freely chosen importance.**

---

# XXVII. Miyagi's own childhood makes this realization even more striking

Remember Miyagi's original psychic solution:

> If I can prevent departure, I cannot be abandoned.

The adult version initially becomes:

> If Sendai obeys, Sendai remains.

Volume 9 begins teaching her something much harder:

> **A Sendai incapable of leaving is not the Sendai whose staying can reassure me.**

This connects directly to the return motif.

Early in the volume, when Sendai asks what Miyagi would do if she didn't come home, Miyagi answers:

> 「……帰ってくるって言ったじゃん」

You said you'd come back.

That's significantly healthier than:

> You aren't allowed to leave.

Miyagi is beginning to rely on **Sendai's word** rather than direct physical control.

The Maika SS later confirms that this is not a momentary accident.

---

# XXVIII. 「宇都宮舞香と面白ガール」 gives us the cleanest outside confirmation of Miyagi's progress

Maika notices something odd.

Miyagi hasn't returned to her family home for:

-   Golden Week;

-   summer vacation;

-   winter vacation.


She stays in the apartment she shares with Sendai instead. Shuukura - LN09 Release SS - 宇都…

During the year-end period, however, Sendai is working at the café.

So Miyagi is temporarily alone.

Maika asks why she didn't return home.

Miyagi explains:

> Sendai isn't that busy. She'll be home tonight.

Maika immediately understands the emotional structure:

> 「仙台さんが帰ってくるから、今は一人でも寂しくないってことだ」

So because Sendai is coming home, being alone now isn't lonely.

Miyagi naturally denies this and insists she is “enjoying being alone.” Shuukura - LN09 Release SS - 宇都…

Maika is almost certainly closer to the truth.

And this is an enormous contrast with middle-school Miyagi.

---

## XXIX. Young Miyagi experiences one pair of shoes as abandonment; adult Miyagi experiences it as temporary absence

This may be one of the clearest ways to express her entire university arc.

Middle-school Miyagi:

> My friend left.
> There is one pair of shoes.
> The room has too much empty space.
> I hate having people over because the emptiness afterward becomes worse.

Adult Miyagi:

> Sendai isn't here.
> She is working.
> **She'll be back tonight.**

The physical circumstance is nearly identical:

> Miyagi is alone in the home.

The psychological meaning has changed.

Why?

Because absence has been inserted into a credible pattern of return.

This is exactly the transformation Volume 8 began establishing.

Volume 9 confirms it externally.

---

# XXX. Maika remains one of the healthiest people in the series because she doesn't confuse care with information rights

Maika notices that Miyagi seems slightly strange.

She considers:

> Should I ask?
> Should I leave it alone?
> Should I message?
> Should I call?

Then she chooses the phone call partly because hearing Miyagi's voice will tell her whether something is seriously wrong.

When Miyagi remains evasive, Maika doesn't interrogate her.

She jokes.

Miyagi jokes back.

Miyagi's voice becomes brighter.

That's enough. Shuukura - LN09 Release SS - 宇都…

This continues Maika's long-running ethical function.

She repeatedly demonstrates:

> **You can care about somebody without demanding full access to the cause of every emotional change.**

That's still a lesson Miyagi and Sendai badly need.

---

# XXXI. The two V9 SS form an excellent pair

Mio sees Sendai from outside.

Maika sees Miyagi from outside.

And each perceives something the protagonists themselves struggle to articulate.

Mio sees:

> Sendai is friendly but strangely unavailable.

Maika sees:

> Miyagi can tolerate being alone because she trusts Sendai will return.

Those observations sharpen the central contrast.

**Sendai's growth problem** is differentiation:

> Can I maintain a life in which Miyagi is central without making Miyagi the answer to every possible question?

**Miyagi's growth problem** is secure continuity:

> Can Sendai leave my sight without becoming someone who has left me?

Volume 9 makes both clearer than ever.

---

# XXXII. The names remain a wonderfully irrational fault line

Mio naturally calls Sendai:

> **葉月**

and wants to call Miyagi:

> **志緒理ちゃん**.

For ordinary university friends, first-name address can be socially negotiated without carrying the metaphysical weight it carries inside the central relationship.

Naturally, both Miyagi and Sendai become jealous about outsiders using the names.

This reveals that the name problem has never really been about Japanese address norms alone.

It is about exclusivity.

Miyagi and Sendai have invested their given names with such extraordinary private significance that seeing someone else use them casually feels almost like theft.

The absurdity is useful.

A university friend can say “Hazuki.”

Miyagi has had sex with Hazuki and still finds saying “Hazuki” emotionally explosive.

That's *Shuukura* in miniature.

---

# XXXIII. “The real Sendai” remains a dangerous idea

At the zoo, Miyagi wants photographs of Sendai when Sendai is not giving her polished social smile.

She wants:

> 本当の仙台さん

the “real Sendai.”

I understand what Miyagi means.

She wants something not optimized for:

-   public expectation;

-   social friendliness;

-   Miyagi's preferences.


But we should resist Miyagi's own binary.

The socially competent Sendai is also Sendai.

Her smile isn't automatically fake because she knows how to produce it.

Her accommodation isn't automatically false because it is relational.

The developmental goal shouldn't be:

> strip away every performed layer until a pure authentic Sendai is found underneath.

There probably is no such pristine self.

A better formulation is:

> **Sendai needs authorship over all her modes.**

She can be:

-   polished university Sendai;

-   teasing roommate Sendai;

-   sleepy Sendai;

-   jealous Sendai;

-   accommodating Sendai;

-   stubborn Sendai.


The ethical question is whether those voices remain hers.

---

# XXXIV. Volume 9's actual enemy is not influence; it is self-erasure

This distinction matters considerably.

Healthy relationships change people.

Miyagi now:

-   eats differently;

-   celebrates things she once ignored;

-   considers zoos;

-   understands makeup products;

-   thinks about another person's preferences;

-   imagines future Valentines.


Sendai now:

-   likes shoebills;

-   likes blue partly because of Miyagi's sapphire;

-   possesses new domestic habits;

-   has increasingly narrow priorities around home.


Those changes are not automatically bad.

The danger begins when either woman reaches:

> **I cannot identify any desire that survives disagreement with you.**

That is where influence becomes engulfment.

Volume 9 finally gives Miyagi enough insight to notice this in Sendai.

---

# XXXV. It is fitting that Miyagi sees the problem before Sendai does

This initially seems ironic.

Miyagi is the possessive one.

Why would she become the advocate for Sendai's independence?

Because Miyagi's possessiveness ultimately requires a **subject**, not an object.

She doesn't merely want:

> something called Sendai in the apartment.

She wants:

> Sendai Hazuki specifically choosing her.

The more Sendai behaves like an obedient extension of Miyagi, the less satisfying that choice becomes.

So possessiveness accidentally discovers its own limit.

Miyagi realizes:

> If I erase Sendai's separateness completely, there is no longer another person whose choice can prove that I am special.

That's one of the sharpest psychological turns in the series.

---

# XXXVI. Sendai's sexual behavior reveals the opposite side of the same problem

Sendai sometimes uses physical intimacy to **avoid self-definition**.

When Miyagi presses her about her preferences and boundaries, Sendai can redirect toward touch and kissing.

At one point she essentially recognizes that if she keeps kissing Miyagi:

> Miyagi cannot keep asking questions.

That is revealing.

Sexual intimacy—supposedly their most private form of knowing—becomes a way to **avoid being known**.

That wonderfully complicates the assumption that more physical access necessarily means greater emotional transparency.

Sometimes they know each other's bodies precisely because bodies are easier than answers.

---

# XXXVII. This is another reason the sexual explicitness is not the mature part of Volume 9

The thematically mature question isn't:

> How sexually intimate have they become?

It's:

> **Why can Sendai expose her body more easily than state a favorite animal?**

Or:

> Why can Miyagi command an intimate act more easily than say “I'm interested in you”?

Or:

> Why can they recognize each other's physical responses yet struggle to negotiate a clean no?

Volume 9 repeatedly turns mundane biographical knowledge into the more difficult intimacy.

That is one of *Shuukura*'s strongest literary instincts.

---

# XXXVIII. Miyagi's Valentine's decision shows her protecting memory again

During Valentine's Day, Miyagi chooses not to let the day become dominated by further sexual escalation.

We've seen this before with the aquarium.

She wants:

> Valentine's Day to remain about the chocolate they made.

That is another quiet change in her relationship to memory.

Miyagi once wanted special events to be less memorable because memory creates future vulnerability.

Now she sometimes wants to **protect the specific identity of a good memory**.

That isn't avoidance of memory anymore.

It's curation.

The difference is significant.

---

# XXXIX. Future recurrence is now everywhere, even though Miyagi still fears permanence

The volume repeatedly generates next times.

Another zoo visit.

Another aquarium.

Next Valentine's Day.

Another return from work.

Spring break evenings.

Miyagi still struggles with indefinite promises.

But compare her to the middle-school girl who says:

> If something will disappear someday, it would be better never to have it.

Adult Miyagi is now deliberately accumulating things that could someday disappear.

That may be the most fundamental way Sendai is changing her.

She hasn't become comfortable with impermanence.

She has begun choosing valuable things **despite impermanence**.

That's more psychologically credible than simply curing her fear.

---

# XL. The four-year “roommate” container is still alive, but it is being quietly undermined by habit

Miyagi continues using the university years as a safe horizon.

When imagining future outings, she can still think:

> there's plenty of time before graduation.

That matters.

She hasn't suddenly accepted permanent partnership.

But everyday behavior keeps creating a contradiction.

They now structure life around:

-   returning home;

-   birthdays next year;

-   Valentine's next year;

-   future outings;

-   future meals;

-   future work schedules;

-   mutual social obligations.


“Roommate” still says:

> four years.

Their routines increasingly say:

> **again.**

And “again” is exactly how permanence can emerge without anyone having to pronounce “forever.”

---

# XLI. U35's illustrations quietly support this shift toward observation and ordinary presence

Volume 9's visual material is much less interesting to me as “romantic illustration” than as an extension of the volume's gaze motif.

Repeatedly, the illustrations isolate:

-   one woman watching the other;

-   photographs and phones;

-   ordinary winter clothing;

-   café/social encounters;

-   sleeping or unguarded expressions;

-   zoo imagery;

-   food and Valentine's material;

-   physical proximity in domestic space.


The visual question is increasingly:

> **What version of you am I seeing?**

That fits the prose perfectly.

Miyagi wants an unperformed Sendai.

Sendai wants images of Miyagi she can preserve.

Mio encounters the polished university Sendai but knows there is something behind the smile.

Maika listens to Miyagi's voice for the emotional information Miyagi won't state.

Volume 9 is saturated with acts of looking, listening, recording and interpreting.

Its protagonists' next task is learning that observation cannot replace asking.

---

# XLII. Updated Miyagi after Volume 9

Miyagi is now much more psychologically advanced than her behavior sometimes suggests.

Most significantly, she understands that:

**possession cannot substitute for personhood.**

She wants Sendai's:

-   likes;

-   dislikes;

-   genuine refusals;

-   independent enjoyment;

-   memories;

-   history.


She can say:

> I'm interested in you.

She can plan something based upon what Sendai might enjoy.

She can call off a command when she realizes compliance would not reveal Sendai's real preference.

She can tolerate Sendai going to work because she believes Sendai will come home.

She can imagine another Valentine.

She can preserve good memories instead of only fearing the pain they may later cause.

But her progress is uneven.

Miyagi still relies heavily on:

-   「私のもの」;

-   marks;

-   exclusivity;

-   jealousy;

-   asymmetric command structures;

-   promises as reassurance.


Her great contradiction now is:

> **She wants an autonomous Sendai whom she can still call hers.**

The next stage of her growth requires learning that these ideas can coexist only if “mine” means relational importance rather than jurisdiction.

---

# XLIII. Updated Sendai after Volume 9

Volume 9 gives Sendai perhaps the most important backstory refinement she has received.

Her accommodation isn't merely:

-   social skill;

-   romantic devotion;

-   sexual preference;

-   fear of upsetting Miyagi.


It likely draws upon an old childhood equation:

> **similarity to the important person = continued belonging.**

That makes many adult behaviors newly legible.

Why does she like what Miyagi likes?

Why does she struggle to say no to Miyagi while rejecting Mio effortlessly?

Why does Miyagi's request for an independent preference feel almost impossible?

Why does Sendai sometimes prefer physical intimacy to answering questions about herself?

Why did the thought that Miyagi was “invading” too much of her become frightening?

Because Sendai has already lived one version of identity-through-another-person.

Her task is not to love Miyagi less.

It is to acquire enough confidence that she can say:

> **I disagree with you and I'm still staying.**

Or:

> **I don't want this and I still love you.**

Or simply:

> **I like this because I like it.**

---

# XLIV. The childhood bonus reveals that their wounds are almost mathematically complementary

The pairing is exceptionally well constructed.

Young Sendai learns:

> **Become like the person you need so you will not be excluded.**

Young Miyagi learns:

> **Do not love something that can disappear, because its absence will hurt more than never having it.**

So adult Sendai tends toward:

> merging.

Adult Miyagi tends toward:

> controlling distance and endings.

Sendai's danger is:

> **I will become you so you cannot reject me.**

Miyagi's danger is:

> **I will possess you so you cannot leave me.**

And Volume 9 begins forcing each woman toward the other's missing principle.

Miyagi tells Sendai:

> **Be different from me.**

Sendai keeps teaching Miyagi:

> **Something can leave the room and still come back.**

That's a beautiful reciprocal architecture.

---

# XLV. Their healthiest future therefore requires two propositions that would once have terrified them

For Sendai:

> **Difference does not mean exclusion.**

For Miyagi:

> **Absence does not mean abandonment.**

Put those together and you get something very close to secure adult attachment:

> You may want something I don't want.
> You may go somewhere I do not go.
> You may have friends I do not control.
> You may tell me no.
> You may be absent tonight.
> And none of those facts automatically means the relationship has ended.

Neither woman can consistently live that way yet.

But Volume 9 makes the destination unusually clear.

---

# XLVI. Revised series thesis through the end of the published LN material

Our previous formulations still hold, but Volume 9 lets me sharpen the architecture considerably.

The high-school arc asked:

> **Can artificial structure make unsafe intimacy tolerable?**

The answer was yes—but only temporarily.

Volumes 5–6 asked:

> **Does desire survive when payment and school disappear?**

Yes.

Volume 7 asked:

> **Can refusal occur without becoming abandonment?**

Sometimes, and they begin learning how.

Volume 8 asked:

> **Can attachment survive work, friends, public space and absence?**

Increasingly yes.

Volume 9 asks:

> **Can attachment survive the other person's difference?**

That remains the current frontier.

And this may be the deepest question yet because **difference is what makes choice possible**.

A person identical to you cannot meaningfully choose against you.

A person incapable of refusal cannot give a fully informative yes.

A person whose preferences are simply your preferences cannot show you what it means to accommodate you.

A person prevented from leaving cannot reassure you by returning.

So the original fantasy of perfect control has become self-defeating.

Miyagi wanted certainty.

What she actually needs is something certainty would destroy:

> **another free person repeatedly choosing her.**

---

# XLVII. The central paradox of *Shuukura* is now much clearer

Miyagi has spent nine volumes trying to obtain proof that Sendai cannot leave.

Sendai has spent nine volumes trying to obtain proof that Miyagi cannot reject her.

But if either receives absolute proof, the relationship loses the thing both actually value:

> **choice.**

The most meaningful evidence Sendai can give Miyagi is:

> I could have gone somewhere else, and I came home.

The most meaningful evidence Miyagi can give Sendai is:

> I can refuse this thing and still want you here.

And the most meaningful evidence either can give the other is:

> **I remain myself beside you.**

That is why I would now summarize the university arc with two formulations:

> **The opposite of abandonment is not obedience. It is return from freedom.**

And:

> **The opposite of possession is not distance. It is difference that remains in relationship.**

---

# XLVIII. Why 「私たちが過ごすなんでもない日々」 is the right final note before synthesis

After nine volumes of:

-   money;

-   contracts;

-   commands;

-   marks;

-   birthdays;

-   gifts;

-   photographs;

-   sex;

-   jealousy;

-   promises;

-   fear of disappearance;


the supplementary story ends up asking for something radically modest:

> stay awake a little longer.

Not because today matters.

Because today **doesn't**.

That is the final inversion of the original premise.

Once:

> Wednesday mattered because ¥5,000 made it theirs.

Now:

> an ordinary spring-break night matters because they are together—and it doesn't even have to become a memory worthy of preservation.

Sendai's most mature temporal desire is no longer:

> Let's make every day special.

It is:

> **Let's have enough ordinary days together that some of them are allowed to remain ordinary.**

For a series whose original relationship existed only in exceptional, purchased time, that is an exceptionally elegant place to stop the LN close-reading phase.

The next step in the agreed sequence should indeed be the **LN-through-Volume-9 synthesis** before we cross into WN-only continuity.
