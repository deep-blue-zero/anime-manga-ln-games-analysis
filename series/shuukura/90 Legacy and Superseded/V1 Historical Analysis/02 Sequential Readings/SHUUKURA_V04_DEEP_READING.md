# SHUUKURA V04 Deep Reading — Transcript Extraction

# 『週に一度クラスメイトを買う話』Volume 4

## The Death of the Contract and the Invention of the Roommate

### Scope

This pass covers the complete Japanese **Volume 4**, including the interlude **「宮城は甘くて痛い」** and the concluding **番外編「この部屋に春が来て仙台さんは」**. I am treating the uploaded main-series EPUB as the governing text; the archive identifies it as the canonical Volume 4 holding, published April 19, 2024. MANIFEST

I am **not yet incorporating** the Volume 4 Kakuyomu SS 「宇都宮舞香の懐疑心」 or the voiced material. Those belong in the next supplementary pass. That distinction matters because Volume 4 itself already forms a remarkably complete end to the high-school movement, and I want its conclusions to stand on their own before using Maika or the voice performances to refine them.

Haneda calls Volume 4, in the afterword, **宮城と仙台が高校を卒業する一つの区切り**—a point of demarcation created by Miyagi and Sendai graduating from high school.

That understates what the book actually accomplishes.

Volume 4 does not merely graduate them from school.

It **destroys the conceptual machinery with which they have understood their relationship for three volumes**.

The ¥5,000 loses its power. The school rules expire. “Until graduation” arrives. “Classmate” becomes factually obsolete. Miyagi tries to carry out the ending she has promised herself since almost the beginning of the relationship.

And the relationship survives all of it.

The volume's governing movement is therefore:

> **the contract ends because the relationship no longer needs it—but neither girl is psychologically capable of living without another structure to replace it.**

So they invent a new one.

Not lovers.

Not even friends.

**Roommates.**

That is both genuine progress and an extraordinarily *Shuukura*\-like form of avoidance.

---

## The ¥5,000 finally admits that it has stopped working

Volume 4 makes explicit what Volumes 2 and 3 had been demonstrating behaviorally.

Miyagi thinks:

> **「命令の対価として渡す五千円は効力を失いつつある。」**

The ¥5,000 she pays as compensation for commands is **losing its efficacy**.

This is one of the most important statements in the first four books because it comes from Miyagi herself.

The original system was conceptually simple:

Miyagi pays → Sendai comes → Miyagi commands → Sendai obeys.

By winter of their final year, essentially every causal connection in that chain has become false.

Sendai comes because she wants to see Miyagi.

Miyagi calls because she wants Sendai there.

Sendai frequently invents or solicits the command she wants Miyagi to issue.

Physical intimacy occurs without payment.

Domestic care occurs without commands.

Both girls rearrange schedules around one another.

The ¥5,000 still changes hands, but increasingly resembles **ceremonial paperwork for a relationship that exists independently of it**.

Volume 4 then reveals something darker beneath Miyagi's continued attachment to the payment ritual.

She wonders what would happen if she simply did not pay Sendai. Then she thinks:

> **「五千円を渡さない私に価値があるとは思えない。」**

She cannot believe that a Miyagi who does not hand over ¥5,000 possesses enough value for Sendai to keep coming.

That radically deepens the money motif.

For three volumes, Miyagi appears to be the powerful one because she is the buyer.

The buyer's private fear is:

> **Without payment, why would you choose me?**

The ¥5,000 was never only an instrument of domination. It was an insurance policy against Miyagi's belief in her own replaceability.

If she pays, Sendai's presence has a cause.

If she doesn't pay, Sendai's presence would have to mean:

> *I came because you are worth coming for.*

And Miyagi finds that much harder to believe.

This is why the title premise has always contained an inversion. Miyagi buys Sendai partly because she cannot imagine herself as someone Sendai would voluntarily choose.

# Sendai's answer is not healthier than Miyagi's—just different

Volume 4 also gives us perhaps the starkest articulation yet of Sendai's own attachment strategy.

After Miyagi offers the winter tutoring arrangement in exchange for a kiss, Sendai reflects that she has become disproportionately inclined toward Miyagi. Previously, she thought she should correct that imbalance.

Now she decides the opposite:

> if the balance is already badly broken, don't restore it; break it further. Make Miyagi tilt toward her just as much—or more.

This matters because it prevents an overly flattering interpretation of Sendai as the emotionally healthier pursuer who simply keeps rescuing Miyagi from avoidance.

Sendai is much more self-aware than Miyagi.

But her conclusion is not:

> I should develop a secure relationship with her.

It is closer to:

> **If I'm this dependent on her, I want her equally dependent on me.**

That's possessive.

It's also psychologically coherent.

Sendai has spent much of her life maintaining belonging by adjusting herself to other people. Miyagi has become one of the very few people around whom Sendai experiences intense desire without performing the socially optimized version of herself.

She therefore does not want to regulate the attachment downward.

She wants reciprocity through **mutual destabilization**.

Miyagi wants guarantees that Sendai cannot leave.

Sendai wants Miyagi attached enough that she will not want to.

Different mechanisms, same anxiety.

---

# The winter arrangement replaces money with kissing—and proves that exchange itself is the defense

Miyagi finally asks Sendai to tutor her during winter vacation.

Of course, she cannot simply say:

> Come because I want you here.

So she constructs an exchange.

A kiss in return for tutoring.

Sendai then makes kissing a condition of the tutoring days.

This could superficially look like progress because the relationship has moved from money to physical affection.

Psychologically, it is almost the same structure.

They still need:

> **X in exchange for Y**

because exchange keeps voluntary desire from standing naked.

The fascinating thing is that both now know what is happening.

Miyagi knows the ¥5,000 is losing effectiveness.

Sendai knows the kisses are not really “payment.”

Neither stops negotiating.

The object of exchange changes because **the exchange structure itself is emotionally protective**.

Money gave them a reason to be together.

Now desire itself gives them a reason to be together.

They still pretend it is commerce.

---

# The shared night is the volume's domestic turning point

The overnight sequence is far more important than its erotic material.

They make stew together.

Miyagi participates in the cooking rather than merely ordering Sendai to prepare food.

Sendai bathes in Miyagi's home.

She puts on Miyagi's clothes.

She uses Miyagi's shampoo.

They study together in sleepwear.

They prepare separate sleeping spaces, then gradually abandon that separation.

Sendai returns from the bath and says:

> 「ただいま」

Miyagi does **not** answer:

> 「おかえり」

That absence is beautifully chosen.

The vocabulary of home is almost available.

Not quite.

We've been tracking a complementary domestic wound since Volume 2:

**Miyagi lives in a home where people do not reliably return.**

**Sendai lives in a home where returning does not reliably produce emotional welcome.**

During this night, they almost improvise the missing household ritual between them.

Sendai says *I'm home.*

Miyagi cannot yet say *welcome home.*

But everything else they do increasingly resembles the life for which those words would be appropriate.

---

# Darkness makes their attachment unusually clear

After they lie down, Miyagi eventually enters Sendai's futon.

Her rationalizations are characteristically bad.

It's cold.

Another body is useful.

She might as well make use of someone who is already there.

Yet her narration makes the actual mechanism obvious.

When Miyagi closes her eyes, Sendai's warmth becomes indistinct and she feels alone again.

Then Sendai says:

> 「宮城」

The voice makes Sendai's presence real.

This continues a motif stretching all the way back to Volume 1, when Sendai reading aloud evolved from humiliation into emotional regulation.

Miyagi needs sensory confirmation that Sendai is still there:

-   voice;

-   warmth;

-   touch;

-   smell;

-   objects;

-   marks.


Her possession is not principally about abstract superiority.

It is about **making absence impossible to mistake for presence**.

That's why the impending graduation is so destabilizing. School has provided ambient evidence of Sendai's existence even on days Miyagi doesn't call her.

After graduation, that background reassurance disappears.

---

# Volume 4 is also the point where their ethical problems become genuinely symmetrical

This is worth treating seriously rather than romanticizing because the book itself does.

Sendai continues to push past boundaries. She leverages tutoring, uses ambiguity around permission, leaves marks, and sometimes interprets Miyagi's failure to stop her as sufficient reason to continue.

Miyagi explicitly rejects that logic at one point:

> **「言わないからしていいわけじゃない。」**

Not saying “stop” does not mean Sendai gets to do it.

That is one of the clearest consent principles the novels have articulated.

But the crucial development is that **Miyagi has begun reproducing Sendai's behavior**.

During the shared night, Sendai tells Miyagi she has not given permission for a particular touch. Miyagi responds by trying to make Sendai say that it is allowed. Elsewhere, Miyagi engineers situations in which Sendai must affirm or reject an escalation.

They are learning one another's methods.

That is emotionally significant and ethically dangerous.

The power relationship has unquestionably become more reciprocal.

It has **not** become proportionately healthier.

Earlier, Miyagi's command authority created the obvious asymmetry. By Volume 4, both girls have enough confidence in the other's attraction to exert pressure.

This is one reason I would resist a fandom reading that treats increasing mutuality as equivalent to increasing health.

What they are acquiring is **agency**.

What they still lack is a mature language of mutual negotiation.

Those are different achievements.

---

# Sendai understands why she keeps forcing Miyagi to choose

She actually comes unusually close to diagnosing her own pattern.

Sendai does not know how many broken promises remain before Miyagi finally leaves.

There is no visible “score gauge” telling her how much Miyagi will tolerate.

So she repeatedly constructs scenarios in which **Miyagi must make the decisive move**.

That explains much of Sendai's behavior across the first four volumes.

She advances.

She provokes.

She narrows the possibilities.

Then, near the important threshold, she makes Miyagi choose.

This lets Sendai reassure herself:

> Miyagi could have left.
> Miyagi didn't.
> Therefore Miyagi wanted this too.

Sometimes that reasoning is justified.

Sometimes Sendai has heavily manipulated the circumstances preceding the “choice.”

Volume 4's climax will reproduce exactly this pattern on the largest scale yet.

---

# The black cat is a small domestic surrogate for Sendai

Sendai gives Miyagi the black-cat plush as a return gift for the necklace.

Again, neither wants the exchange interpreted too romantically.

Yet Miyagi puts the cat beside her pillow.

She talks to it while alone.

She moves it around depending on what she is doing.

By the final bonus chapter, the black cat becomes one of the things Miyagi explicitly decides to take with her when she moves.

This gives the object a clear relational function.

When Sendai is absent, something **chosen by Sendai specifically for Miyagi** remains in the room.

It is a portable trace of voluntary care.

That distinction matters because the necklace works in the opposite direction.

The necklace is Miyagi's mark on Sendai.

The black cat is Sendai's presence beside Miyagi.

Their objects are doing emotional work their language still cannot.

U35's illustrations reinforce this emphasis: the black cat receives a dedicated illustration, while the volume's visual program repeatedly selects domestic, vulnerable, and transitional scenes rather than merely the most provocative encounters.

---

# “Say you hate me” is Miyagi trying to outsource the breakup

Near the end of the exam period, Miyagi commands Sendai:

> **「大嫌いって言って」**

Say you hate me.

This looks cruel and arbitrary until Miyagi's later narration tells us exactly what she wanted.

She describes **嫌い** as a potential:

> **記憶の消しゴム**
> an eraser for memory.

That deliberately recalls the earlier eraser motif.

In Volume 1, an actual eraser became the object Sendai used to prevent Miyagi from erasing their relationship.

Now Miyagi wants a verbal eraser.

If Sendai will say:

> I hate you,

Miyagi can tell herself that separation has become unavoidable.

Then she doesn't have to choose abandonment.

More precisely:

> **she can perform abandonment while imagining herself as the abandoned one.**

This is psychologically perfect for Miyagi.

Her deepest fear is not simply losing Sendai.

It is **having to trust Sendai enough to remain vulnerable to being left later**.

A rejection now would allow Miyagi to escape uncertainty.

Sendai refuses to give her one.

Instead of an eraser, Miyagi receives another hug and another future meeting.

The relationship keeps acquiring memory faster than Miyagi can delete it.

---

# Sendai has become part of Miyagi's body in an almost literal psychological sense

One of Miyagi's strongest self-observations comes while grocery shopping.

She realizes:

> **「いつの間にか仙台さんは、私という人間を構成する一部になっている。」**

Sendai has somehow become a part of what constitutes Miyagi as a person.

The prose then makes that statement concrete.

Sendai has marked:

-   Miyagi's internal calendar;

-   her eating habits;

-   her taste;

-   what groceries she buys;

-   what drinks she keeps available;

-   what meals she considers normal;

-   what an empty apartment feels like.


Miyagi recalls that after her mother left she forgot something basic:

> food prepared by another person tastes better.

Sendai brings that knowledge back.

This is not simply “Sendai is a good cook.”

She reactivates a form of domestic experience associated with Miyagi's pre-abandonment childhood.

That makes food one of the series' deepest attachment languages.

---

# The grocery-bag thought is almost embarrassingly domestic

Miyagi buys more food now because Sendai eats with her.

The bag becomes heavy.

She thinks it would be convenient if Sendai were walking beside her and carrying half.

Then immediately begins constructing reasons why that cannot happen.

She would need to modify the rules.

There's not enough time left before graduation.

She doesn't actually want to shop with Sendai anyway.

And yet she keeps thinking:

> it would be easier if Sendai carried half.

Later, without knowing Miyagi has thought this, Sendai independently suggests:

> Why don't we go shopping together?

This is exactly the kind of mutuality the alternating POV handles particularly well.

Neither girl knows that they are imagining the same ordinary life.

The reader does.

And what they are converging upon isn't another kiss.

It's:

> **buy groceries together and split the bags.**

That is much more threatening.

---

# 肉じゃが is important because it is boring

Sendai eventually makes **肉じゃが** from what Miyagi has bought.

The meal becomes memorable.

That is almost the opposite of an event-driven romance climax.

肉じゃが is quintessentially ordinary home cooking. The scene's significance is precisely its lack of spectacle.

Miyagi has spent years eating food according to an instrumental logic:

> acquire calories, eat whatever is convenient.

Sendai turns dinner into:

> preference, anticipation, labor, conversation and memory.

The relationship is increasingly constructing what might be called **domestic expectation**.

Miyagi doesn't merely enjoy Sendai's cooking.

She begins to expect that eating with another person is what a satisfying meal feels like.

She later acknowledges the frightening consequence:

> if she stops seeing Sendai, eating will probably become boring again.

Sendai has changed the baseline.

That is much harder to erase than a bruise.

---

# Valentine's Day completes one of the cleanest symmetries in the series

Volume 1's Valentine sequence was deeply asymmetrical.

Sendai brought Miyagi chocolate as part of the ordinary activity of making sweets for friends.

Miyagi reacted badly because “friend” was threatening.

A year later:

**Miyagi buys Sendai Valentine's chocolate.**

**Sendai makes chocolate specifically for Miyagi.**

And both immediately lie about what the act means.

Miyagi:

> It caught my eye while shopping.

Sendai, when Miyagi assumes she must also have made chocolate for Hamina and the others:

> essentially lets that false assumption stand.

In reality, with school effectively over, Sendai no longer has the old “I made some for my friends anyway” excuse.

The chocolate is for Miyagi.

This is beautiful because both girls have arrived at the same psychological maneuver from opposite directions:

> **perform singular romantic attention; deny singular romantic intention.**

Neither can comfortably say:

> I thought of you specifically.

Their behavior says it anyway.

---

# The fact that Miyagi becomes nicer frightens Sendai

This is one of Volume 4's subtler achievements.

For several meetings, Miyagi becomes:

-   less irritable;

-   less demanding;

-   more conversational;

-   more permissive about kissing;

-   less likely to issue strange commands.


This should delight Sendai.

It terrifies her.

Sendai thinks something is wrong.

And she's correct.

Miyagi isn't simply becoming emotionally healthier.

She has decided that the relationship is almost dead.

Therefore she can afford to be generous.

This is **farewell kindness**.

Miyagi thinks:

> If I'm going to lose you shortly anyway, perhaps I can allow myself a few more good memories.

That's why Sendai becomes suspicious of the exact version of Miyagi most other people would find easier to deal with.

There is also a lovely inversion of Volume 2 here.

In Volume 2, Miyagi rejected **pleasant social Sendai** because she wanted the difficult, unregulated Sendai specific to their private relationship.

Now Sendai rejects **unusually pleasant Miyagi** because she wants the difficult, prickly Miyagi she actually knows.

Both girls have reached the point where:

> **the other's inconvenience is part of what authentic intimacy feels like.**

This isn't an argument that mistreatment is romantic.

It means neither wants a sanitized performance designed for comfortable social consumption.

Sendai can tell when Miyagi is acting like someone preparing a beautiful goodbye.

She would rather have the annoying Miyagi who expects to see her next week.

---

# Miyagi begins collecting “last memories”

After the examinations, Miyagi gives herself permission to do things that previously frightened her.

Chocolate.

Another movie.

Dressing more carefully than usual.

Public handholding.

Initiating kisses.

She even chooses the same meeting time and place as their summer movie outing.

Sendai recognizes the pattern before Miyagi admits it.

This isn't simply Miyagi becoming braver.

She is **re-enacting their relationship as a farewell ritual**.

The emotional logic is:

> We're ending anyway.
> Therefore these acts cannot create dangerous expectations of continuation.

It's a powerful inversion of her earlier fear of memorable dates.

Before, Miyagi resisted making memories because future loss might make them painful.

Now that she believes loss is certain, she permits herself a controlled final archive.

The problem is that memory does not obey her.

By graduation day she realizes the Valentine's chocolate and movie kisses are not fading.

They have become **more vivid** because she keeps remembering them.

Her final-memory strategy backfires.

---

# The public handholding is more consequential than another kiss

After the movie, Miyagi simply takes Sendai's hand.

Sendai is almost bewildered.

And Miyagi gives the rationale:

> graduation is almost here, so it doesn't matter if someone sees them.

Again, the deadline paradoxically creates freedom.

But there's a deeper point.

Handholding in public violates the old architecture more thoroughly than private sexual behavior ever did.

A private kiss can remain:

> weird thing we do under our private rules.

Two girls walking through town hand in hand is socially legible as ordinary intimacy.

Miyagi has spent three volumes terrified of precisely that kind of legibility.

Now she permits it because she thinks its consequences have an expiration date.

The nearer the relationship approaches death, the more it briefly resembles a conventional romance.

---

# Sendai recognizes the movie date as a funeral

The most impressive part of this sequence is Sendai's reaction.

Miyagi initiates kisses.

This is something Sendai has wanted for a long time.

Instead of simply accepting the windfall, Sendai becomes increasingly uncomfortable.

She realizes:

> this doesn't feel like Miyagi wanting a new kind of relationship.

It feels like Miyagi **giving Sendai what she has wanted before the relationship ends**.

Sendai eventually pushes Miyagi away and says, in effect:

> You don't have to force yourself.

That is meaningful growth.

Sendai still wants Miyagi.

But she doesn't want a version of intimacy whose true purpose is:

> *Here, take everything you wanted so I can disappear afterward.*

She wants the Miyagi who will continue being difficult tomorrow.

That places **continuity above gratification**, just as Volume 2 did after their near-escalation at the end of summer.

The governing romantic value of *Shuukura* remains remarkably consistent:

> not the biggest intimate act, but the existence of a next time.

---

# Graduation makes Miyagi's entire theory of memory collapse

The graduation-day opening is superb.

Ami openly sobs because she will be separated from Maika and Miyagi.

Miyagi comforts her.

Miyagi says they can still see each other.

Summer exists.

They can make plans.

And meanwhile Miyagi is internally preparing to cut Sendai out of her life forever.

The contrast is brutal.

Ami can say:

> Don't leave me.

Miyagi cannot.

Maika notices that Miyagi herself looks close to tears.

Miyagi denies it.

Then she actually cries.

This is an important culmination of the supporting-friendship comparison we've been tracking since the first Maika SS.

Ami experiences separation anxiety and **expresses it directly**.

Nobody abandons her for doing so.

Maika responds with plans.

Miyagi responds with reassurance.

The social world gives Miyagi a live demonstration of what she cannot do with Sendai:

> **tell somebody that separation hurts and negotiate another meeting.**

Instead, Miyagi spends months building an elaborate termination protocol.

Her problem isn't that no other relational language exists.

She is surrounded by it.

She cannot trust it with Sendai.

---

# Graduation also exposes Miyagi's counterfactual thinking

During the ceremony, Miyagi wonders:

> If we had been in the same class again, would I have been different?

> If I had broken the school rule and talked to Sendai publicly, could I have learned to trust her?

These are emotionally understandable questions, but I don't think the text supports Miyagi's implication that school structure is the main source of her distrust.

The different classes helped them compartmentalize.

Their secrecy certainly reinforced the problem.

But Miyagi's core difficulty predates Sendai:

-   her mother left;

-   her father is unreliable as a presence;

-   promises are associated with disappointment;

-   she prefers endings she can control.


A different class assignment might have altered the expression of the relationship.

It would not simply have cured that architecture.

This is one place where Miyagi's own causal theory should be challenged.

She habitually looks for an external rule she could have changed because rules are more manageable than attachment wounds.

---

# Sendai breaks the final school rule the instant school ends

After graduation, Sendai catches up with Miyagi.

Miyagi ignores her.

So Sendai loudly calls:

> **志緒理**

Miyagi stops.

It's a wonderfully economical transition.

The old rules say:

-   don't talk publicly;

-   don't use Shiori;

-   keep the relationship divided from school life.


Sendai's response is basically:

> school is over.

The institutional structure that justified the rules has disappeared.

Miyagi nevertheless tries to preserve them a few minutes longer, even ordering Sendai to walk behind her.

Sendai technically complies by walking **slightly** behind—effectively beside her.

The spatial image is almost comic, but symbolically exact.

Miyagi wants:

> hierarchy and separation.

Sendai keeps positioning herself:

> **next to Miyagi.**

That is where the volume is heading.

---

# The necklace versus the envelope is the climax of the entire high-school arc

Back in Miyagi's room, she asks Sendai to return the necklace.

The command to wear it lasted only until graduation.

Therefore, according to Miyagi:

> the command has expired;
> the necklace has expired;
> the relationship has expired.

She plans to discard the necklace and never see Sendai again.

This is Miyagi trying to achieve an impossibly clean symbolic death.

Sendai's answer is a **spring-colored envelope**.

Inside:

> a floor plan.

Two bedrooms.

Shared kitchen.

Shared dining space.

Bathroom.

A home suitable for two people.

Sendai says:

> live with me.

This is one of the smartest narrative pivots the story could have made.

It doesn't solve the relationship by having Sendai say:

> Be my girlfriend.

Instead, she offers another **structure**.

---

# Sendai's proposal is romantic, practical—and deeply manipulative

It should not be romanticized into a flawless grand gesture.

Sendai has already:

-   searched for the apartment;

-   involved her parent;

-   arranged the housing;

-   decided the geographical compromise between their universities;

-   used Miyagi's accumulated money toward Miyagi's share;

-   done all this without Miyagi's consent.


Then she presents two options.

**Necklace:** Sendai will disappear completely.

**Envelope:** Miyagi will live with her.

That's an ultimatum.

Sendai even knows Miyagi well enough to construct the alternatives so that the second choice will be emotionally almost irresistible.

This is Sendai reproducing the central technique Miyagi used from the beginning:

> **define the available choices so the other person can “choose freely” within a structure you designed.**

The purchased classmate has learned the buyer's method.

That is not healthy communication.

It is also not meaningless coercion.

Miyagi has an actual opportunity to refuse. Sendai says she will accept the necklace choice and disappear.

The scene's tension lies precisely in the coexistence of:

> genuine agency
> and
> carefully engineered pressure.

That ambiguity is very consistent with their relationship.

---

# Sendai has turned Miyagi's ¥5,000 into a home

Then comes the revelation that completes the money motif.

Miyagi asks who paid her share.

Sendai says:

> **the piggy bank.**

Every ¥5,000 note Miyagi gave her.

Sendai never spent them.

She tells her parent that the money had been entrusted to her by Miyagi and uses it toward the shared residence.

This is an extraordinary symbolic reversal.

Consider the full circuit of money across the first four volumes.

Miyagi's father earns money while being largely absent.

A portion reaches Miyagi as material provision.

Miyagi gives ¥5,000 to Sendai in exchange for presence.

Sendai refuses to consume the money and preserves it as an archive of shared time.

At graduation, those accumulated payments become **capital for a shared home**.

So:

> **absence produces money → money purchases presence → preserved money records presence → recorded presence finances a home in which presence becomes ordinary.**

That is one of the most elegant long-form motifs in the series so far.

The original transaction has literally funded its own abolition.

Miyagi believed the notes proved:

> Sendai came because I paid.

Sendai uses the same notes to say:

> **I'm putting them back into our future.**

The payment was never what she wanted.

The accumulated time was.

---

# Miyagi does something cleverer than choosing the envelope

Sendai says:

> choose the pendant or the envelope.

Miyagi reaches toward the table.

She takes:

> **the necklace.**

For a moment it appears she has chosen the ending.

Then she walks behind Sendai and puts the necklace back around her neck.

And agrees to live with her for four years.

This is extremely important.

Miyagi does not simply obey Sendai's binary.

She **subverts it**.

The offered symbolism was:

> necklace = past / ending
> envelope = future / continuation.

Miyagi instead says, through action:

> **I will carry the old relationship into the new one.**

She keeps the necklace on Sendai **and** accepts cohabitation.

The past is not erased.

The relationship is transformed.

This is the exact opposite of the memory-erasure strategy Miyagi had spent the volume pursuing.

She doesn't obtain the eraser.

She stops trying to use one.

---

# But Miyagi immediately creates another expiration date

Her acceptance is:

> **「四年間だけだから。」**

Only four years.

This prevents us from treating the climax as total psychological resolution.

Miyagi hasn't suddenly acquired secure attachment.

She has replaced:

> until graduation

with:

> until university graduation.

The temporal container simply moves outward.

This is exactly what someone with her psychology would do.

She can accept a future if the future has edges.

“Forever” is unimaginable.

“Four years” can be tolerated.

So the climax represents real growth:

> she chooses four years of uncertainty instead of immediate controlled loss.

But the defense remains:

> **timebox the attachment so permanence never has to be promised.**

This gives the university arc a clear future problem without requiring us to import anything from later volumes.

At some point, four years will also become short.

---

# “Roommate” is the perfect successor to “classmate”

This may be the volume's most conceptually interesting move.

Miyagi asks:

> You would live with someone who isn't even your friend?

Sendai responds, essentially:

> roommates don't have to be friends.

That is completely *Shuukura*.

For three volumes they have needed categories that permit intimate behavior without acknowledging emotional meaning.

They have used:

-   classmate;

-   purchased classmate;

-   tutor;

-   buyer and bought;

-   command-giver and command-follower.


Now school has ended.

“Classmate” literally no longer describes them.

So they acquire:

> **roommate.**

Roommate is a logistical category.

It permits:

-   daily presence;

-   shared meals;

-   shared groceries;

-   coming home to one another;

-   household routines;

-   knowing schedules;

-   living inside the other's ordinary life.


And crucially it does **not require them to say they are friends or lovers**.

They have found a label materially more intimate than friendship while semantically less emotionally committing.

That is ingenious avoidance.

The relationship does not finally receive a romantic name.

It receives **a housing classification**.

---

# This will also fundamentally change the power structure

Miyagi's apartment has been central to the original hierarchy.

It is Miyagi's territory.

Sendai enters when called.

Miyagi pays her.

Miyagi defines the rules.

Sendai leaves.

A shared apartment destroys that architecture.

Neither girl will be:

> host and visitor.

They will both be residents.

There will be no natural “Sendai goes home now” endpoint to an encounter because **Sendai will already be home**.

This matters enormously.

The old spatial structure allowed intimacy to be bracketed:

> this happens in Miyagi's room during purchased time.

A shared home makes the exceptional ordinary.

And it removes one of Miyagi's primary mechanisms of control: access.

The high-school relationship depended on Miyagi opening the door.

The university relationship will begin with **both possessing a key**.

That is a massive structural equalization.

The irony is that Sendai creates this more equal future through an extremely controlling unilateral housing decision.

Again, progress and dysfunction arrive together.

---

# Given-name intimacy still loses to cohabitation

Almost immediately after Miyagi agrees to live with Sendai, Sendai asks:

> Can I call you Shiori now?

Miyagi:

> **No.**

This is almost comic in its consistency.

They can:

-   share a home;

-   intertwine finances;

-   plan four years together;

-   have extensive physical intimacy;

-   alter university decisions around proximity;

-   possess keys to the same apartment.


And **志緒理** is still too intimate.

That confirms something we've suspected since Volume 1.

Given-name address isn't merely another form of closeness.

For Miyagi it signifies **recognized interpersonal status**.

Physical intimacy can remain exceptional.

Cohabitation can be rationalized as logistics.

“Shiori” says:

> I occupy a socially intelligible intimate position in your life.

Miyagi still cannot comfortably permit that.

The body and household have raced far ahead of language.

---

# The final hand squeeze remains one of Haneda's preferred forms of confession

After the roommate agreement, Sendai holds Miyagi's hand.

Miyagi squeezes back.

Volume 3 ended on a similar reciprocal gesture.

That's significant.

The novels repeatedly reserve their clearest mutuality not for the most sexual acts but for **small responses freely returned**.

A command can manufacture an action.

A kiss can be provoked.

An ultimatum can constrain choice.

But when one hand squeezes another and the other hand squeezes back, very little machinery is available to explain it away.

It simply means:

> **I am answering you.**

That is why these tiny acts often feel more emotionally definitive than the erotic escalation around them.

---

# The bonus chapter reveals what Miyagi's abandonment psychology does to objects

**「この部屋に春が来て仙台さんは」** is an excellent epilogue because it refuses to jump immediately into the new apartment.

Instead, Miyagi must pack.

Packing is almost psychologically designed to torture her.

Every item requires the question:

> Take it or leave it?

Miyagi finds this disproportionately difficult.

She even says that leaving books behind makes her feel guilty.

Then her anxiety supplies the obvious association:

> someday Sendai might similarly sort Miyagi into the category of things to leave behind.

This confirms something important.

Miyagi's abandonment sensitivity does not begin and end with Sendai.

She has generalized **being left** into an emotional structure so deep that even choosing objects for a move activates it.

No wonder a relationship involving uncertainty becomes intolerable.

Selection itself feels dangerous.

---

# The family photo album explains Miyagi's fear of memory better than almost anything else

During packing she encounters an old photo album.

Her mother appears:

-   in infancy photos;

-   birthdays;

-   kindergarten events;

-   school ceremonies.


Then, at some point:

> her mother disappears.

Her father also disappears from the photographs.

Eventually the photographic record itself stops.

This is a powerful image because Miyagi's autobiographical archive contains a literal **rupture**.

The album teaches:

> people can occupy every page of your life
> and then suddenly occupy none.

This adds depth to everything we've seen concerning:

-   commemorative dates;

-   marks;

-   calendars;

-   anniversaries;

-   photographs;

-   memories;

-   Miyagi's desire not to label experiences as special.


A special memory isn't merely pleasant.

It creates a page from which someone can later disappear.

Miyagi's response was to stop producing the album.

With Sendai, she has effectively begun making another one—in:

-   ¥5,000 notes;

-   objects;

-   meals;

-   tastes;

-   bodily marks;

-   movies;

-   phone calls.


And now she is choosing which pieces of that archive move with her.

---

# The first manga is transformed from humiliation device into family heirloom of the relationship

While packing, Miyagi finds the manga she made Sendai read aloud during one of their earliest encounters.

Originally it was an instrument of control.

Miyagi wanted to embarrass Sendai.

Now Miyagi sees it and immediately remembers:

> this is the book Sendai read when she first came here.

She places it with the things that may travel to the new home.

Again, the function has inverted.

What began as:

> proof that I can make you do something

has become:

> **proof that you were here.**

This is the same transformation the money undergoes.

*Shuukura* repeatedly takes objects created by domination and turns them into archives of attachment.

---

# Moving out may be Miyagi's largest act of development so far

Miyagi originally expected to remain in this apartment indefinitely.

It is the home her mother left.

It is the home her father rarely inhabits.

It is the home in which Miyagi learned to live alone.

It is also the home where Sendai first entered and gradually transformed Miyagi's expectations of domestic life.

Now Miyagi chooses to leave it.

That should not be reduced to geography for university.

Symbolically, Miyagi is leaving the physical space in which she has spent years waiting for absent family.

She's not restoring the old family.

She's building another household.

That is enormous.

Her original domestic model was:

> a home is a place from which people disappear.

The new experiment will be:

> **a home is a place deliberately shared with someone who chose to be there.**

That is the biggest wager Miyagi has made in the series.

---

# Sendai's phone call gives us the language of the new relationship

Sendai offers to help Miyagi pack.

Miyagi refuses.

Then Sendai says:

> **「先に向こうで待ってるから」**

I'll be waiting over there ahead of you.

Miyagi answers:

> 「うん」

Then they both say:

> 「またね」

That exchange is almost the direct opposite of the graduation contract.

“Until graduation” meant:

> there will be a final meeting.

**またね** assumes:

> there will be another meeting.

It is grammatically mundane and psychologically revolutionary.

Miyagi's relationship with Sendai is beginning to rely on the ordinary future tense of people who expect to see one another again.

---

# The last sentence resolves the high-school home motif

The epilogue eventually states explicitly:

> the life in which Miyagi gives Sendai ¥5,000 and issues commands is over.

But:

> **Sendai is not over.**

And Miyagi looks toward:

> **「いつも誰かがいる部屋で。」**
> a room where someone is always there.

That is the answer to four volumes of domestic loneliness.

Not:

> a room I control.

Not:

> a room where someone visits when paid.

Not:

> a room where my father might eventually come home.

A room where:

> **someone lives too.**

That may be the single most important emotional image in the first four volumes.

---

# The old home problem is solved differently for each girl

Their compatibility now becomes unusually clear.

Miyagi's household taught her:

> people leave.

Sendai's household taught her:

> people can be physically present without making you feel chosen.

The shared home potentially answers both.

For Miyagi:

> Sendai will ordinarily be there.

For Sendai:

> being there is something she and Miyagi deliberately chose.

This doesn't guarantee the arrangement will be psychologically healthy. In fact, cohabitation could intensify:

-   jealousy;

-   dependence;

-   territoriality;

-   fear of outside relationships.


But thematically it is an almost perfect environment in which to test whether they can convert **ritualized attachment into ordinary coexistence**.

The bedroom experiment is about to become a household experiment.

---

# Miyagi and Sendai are also becoming more alike

One of the subtler developments across Volume 4 is that the two girls increasingly borrow one another's worst and best habits.

Miyagi becomes more capable of:

-   initiating physical affection;

-   asking Sendai to stay;

-   calling;

-   creating ordinary plans;

-   acknowledging that time together is enjoyable.


But she also learns to exert pressure more like Sendai does.

Sendai becomes more capable of:

-   providing practical care;

-   planning a concrete future;

-   recognizing when physical gratification would damage continuity;

-   respecting some forms of Miyagi's vulnerability.


But she also becomes more like Miyagi in her use of **designed choices and possession**.

The roommate ultimatum is essentially a Sendai-authored command structure.

This mutual contamination is one reason their chemistry feels psychologically persuasive.

They don't simply “heal” each other.

They change each other.

Some changes are good.

Some are not.

---

# Miyagi after Volume 4

Miyagi's central wound remains abandonment, but her strategy changes dramatically.

At the beginning of the volume, she still believes the safest outcome is:

> end first.

She asks Sendai to hate her.

She imagines erasing memories.

She prepares graduation as a controlled death.

By the end, she chooses:

> **four more years.**

That's meaningful growth.

She doesn't acquire faith in permanence.

She acquires enough tolerance for uncertainty to postpone her defensive ending.

Her most revealing realization is perhaps not the roommate decision itself but the thought that Sendai has become part of what constitutes her.

Miyagi understands that erasure would no longer simply remove another person.

It would remove habits, tastes, memories and expectations that have become part of her own life.

She therefore faces the real problem:

> attachment cannot be undone by deleting the person once the relationship has changed the self.

Her biggest unresolved weakness is trust.

She still wants:

-   deadlines;

-   categories;

-   tangible proof;

-   control over access;

-   exclusivity.


But she has finally chosen **continuity without certainty** over certainty through separation.

---

# Sendai after Volume 4

Sendai is now unquestionably the principal architect of continuity.

She:

-   wants winter meetings before Miyagi asks;

-   refuses to say she hates Miyagi;

-   wants to know the examination result;

-   wants geographic proximity;

-   keeps the necklace on;

-   preserves every ¥5,000 note;

-   searches for a home;

-   proposes cohabitation;

-   financially commits the accumulated money to it.


This goes far beyond “the girl being paid.”

Sendai is now actively constructing the material conditions under which the relationship can survive.

Yet the volume gives us good reason not to idealize her.

She consciously wants Miyagi more dependent.

She pushes boundaries.

She creates false binaries.

She arranges a shared home before receiving consent.

Her confidence is often the mechanism by which the relationship survives Miyagi's fear—but that same confidence repeatedly reduces the space Miyagi has in which to make unpressured choices.

Sendai's developmental task is therefore becoming clearer:

> **learn how to pursue without engineering the answer.**

She is excellent at creating momentum.

She still needs to learn how to tolerate uncertainty after asking.

---

# The visual program supports the shift from erotic exception to domestic future

U35's Volume 4 illustrations are especially interesting in aggregate.

Yes, there is intimate imagery. But the book also chooses to visualize:

-   the black cat gift;

-   household and sleepwear vulnerability;

-   phone contact;

-   ordinary food;

-   the girls facing one another at close range;

-   Miyagi in the transitional school period;

-   and, repeatedly, the two girls in school uniforms near the end of high school.


The cover itself places them together in uniform against a bright, almost springlike environment. That is appropriate for a volume whose climax is not fundamentally the most sexually intense scene but **two classmates walking out of the institution that made them classmates**.

The visual emphasis supports the prose's movement:

> bedroom intimacy is no longer enough to define the relationship.

The next image has to contain a world outside the bedroom.

# The architecture of Volume 4

The volume is remarkably coherent when viewed as the dismantling of successive excuses.

**「これは仙台さんへのお願いじゃない」** begins with Miyagi unable to call a request a request. The money is already failing, so she uses kissing as exchange.

**「今の宮城が許してくれること」** gives Sendai's response: rather than reduce her attachment, she wants Miyagi to become equally attached. Mutual dependency becomes an explicit goal.

**「仙台さんが知っていること」** shows Miyagi realizing Sendai manipulates her into choices while Maika's presence in Miyagi's room demonstrates how thoroughly that room has become Sendai-coded.

**「冬休みも宮城は不機嫌だ」** turns an overnight visit into an experimental household, complete with cooking, bathing, shared scent, sleeping proximity, touch and conflict over boundaries.

**「仙台さんはいつだって優しくない」** makes Sendai's presence the thing that renders darkness and solitary meals tolerable. New Year separation demonstrates that Miyagi now waits for Sendai's contact even when her father is physically home.

**「宮城は甘くて痛い」** reveals that Sendai has preserved the ¥5,000 notes and openly wants the relationship to survive graduation; bodily marks are simultaneously proof and social risk.

**「宮城に会う理由」** returns them to the bookstore where everything began and makes Sendai confront how ridiculous the need for excuses has become.

**「仙台さんから欲しいもの」** reveals Miyagi's deepest financial anxiety—that without ¥5,000 she may possess no value to Sendai—and converts “I hate you” into the imagined eraser that might permit escape.

**「私と宮城に残された時間」** is the farewell-performance chapter: Valentine's reciprocity, Miyagi's unusual kindness, university results, another movie, public handholding and Miyagi's attempted collection of final memories.

**「仙台さんと卒業式のその後で」** lets the deadline finally arrive and proves it powerless. The necklace and envelope transform termination into cohabitation.

Finally, **「この部屋に春が来て仙台さんは」** moves Miyagi physically out of the abandoned-family room and toward a home where Sendai is no longer visitor, employee or purchased classmate.

The sequence is almost architectural:

> **room → contract → deadline → collapse → new room.**

---

# What Volume 4 does to the first three volumes

I would now slightly revise our earlier series map.

**Volume 1:** The ¥5,000 contract lets intimacy occur without voluntary attachment being acknowledged.

**Volume 2:** Both girls manipulate the contract so that intimacy can continue after it has ceased to be genuinely transactional.

**Volume 3:** The relationship becomes too large for the room, school hierarchy and graduation deadline; it leaks into public life and future planning.

**Volume 4:** The deadline arrives, the contractual identity dies, and they discover that the relationship survives without it. Rather than name the attachment emotionally, they rebuild it materially as **cohabitation**.

That is a very strong first movement.

And it confirms something I suspected when we initially discussed whether *Shuukura* deserved inclusion in this project:

the ¥5,000 premise is not the story's real engine.

It is a **temporary emotional technology**.

Haneda can let the gimmick expire because what has grown inside it is now capable of generating plot by itself.

---

# Final assessment

I think Volume 4 is the strongest volume so far.

Not because it contains the largest romantic escalation, but because it makes almost every major motif from the previous three books converge on the same decision.

Money.

Memory.

Food.

Home.

Objects.

Names.

Marks.

Promises.

School hierarchy.

Graduation.

Abandonment.

Choice.

All end up inside the necklace-and-envelope scene.

The most elegant transformation is the ¥5,000 itself.

The series begins with Miyagi using money to ensure that someone will enter her empty home.

The high-school arc ends with Sendai using that preserved money to create:

> **a home they will enter together.**

And Miyagi does not finally overcome her fear by believing:

> Sendai will never leave me.

She's nowhere near capable of that.

She does something smaller and more psychologically credible.

She decides:

> **I'll risk four years.**

That is enough.

For Miyagi Shiori, four years of voluntarily accepted uncertainty is vastly more courageous than a confession would have been.

And the final bonus gives the high-school movement its cleanest possible closing line in conceptual terms:

> the ¥5,000 life is over; the commands are over; **Sendai is not over**.

The original room acquired Sendai once a week.

The next room will have someone there every day.

That is the real graduation.
