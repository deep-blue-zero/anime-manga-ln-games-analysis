# SHUUKURA V06 Deep Reading — Transcript Extraction

# 『週に一度クラスメイトを買う話』Volume 6

## \+ 「私たちのサイダーとオレンジジュースの行方」

## \+ 「不平と不満と白川亜美」

### Desire stops being an accident

Volume 5 ended with a serious destabilization of the new domestic order. Miyagi and Sendai had abolished the ¥5,000 contract, moved into a shared home, and discovered that none of those changes automatically taught them how to handle desire without an alibi. Their first major sexual encounter demonstrated the danger particularly clearly: mutual desire was real, but desire did not make their communication or consent practices good.

Volume 6 is the first sustained attempt to live **after** that knowledge.

Its central development is easy to state but enormous for *Shuukura*:

> **Desire is no longer plausibly accidental.**

Sendai finally knows she loves Miyagi.

Miyagi increasingly initiates touch herself.

Both become explicitly jealous.

Both create situations merely because they want the other's company.

Their bodies become less something acted upon by “orders” and more something each actively seeks.

Yet almost none of this becomes ordinary declarative language.

Sendai does not confess.

Miyagi still rejects the idea that Sendai herself might be “special.”

They continue manufacturing rules, bargains, promises, outings with pretexts, and exceptions that permit them to do what they cannot simply say they want.

The result is a volume about **self-knowledge outrunning interpersonal courage**.

The contract is dead. The excuse is not.

But for the first time, the excuses are beginning to become unnecessary.

---

# The morning after Volume 5 changes the meaning of Miyagi's flight

The volume's appended story, **「ルームメイトであるべき私たちの朝」**, is worth reading almost as a framing device for everything that follows.

It rewinds to the morning immediately after the Volume 5 sexual encounter.

Miyagi barely sleeps. She cannot reconcile what happened with the category she has constructed for them:

> roommates are not supposed to do this.

Her instinct is familiar: create distance before the emotional consequences become uncontrollable.

So she leaves for Maika's.

But there is one extremely important difference from high-school Miyagi.

She tells the black cat that she will be back soon.

And as she leaves the apartment, she quietly says:

> 「行ってきます」

That phrase matters enormously.

Miyagi is fleeing.

But she is **not ending the relationship**.

In earlier volumes, Miyagi repeatedly protected herself by imagining the relationship's conclusion in advance. Graduation was always coming. Sendai would eventually disappear. Better to keep control over the conditions under which that disappearance occurred.

Here Miyagi does something structurally different:

> she creates distance **inside an assumed return**.

She can leave home without imagining that leaving means abandonment.

Sendai's side of the story completes the point. She has prepared breakfast for two. Eating alone immediately feels wrong. She expects Miyagi to need some time, but once she realizes Miyagi has actually gone out, she wants her back with painful immediacy.

The shared apartment has changed both of their baselines.

Solitude used to be ordinary.

Now it is an absence.

That gives Volume 6 one of its deepest questions:

> **Can distance become something that occurs within attachment rather than evidence that attachment is ending?**

The entire volume gradually answers yes.

# Sendai finally knows

The first chapter contains one of the largest internal developments in the series:

> 「私はきっと。ずっと長い間。宮城が好きだった。」
>
> 「仙台葉月は宮城志緒理が好きだ。」

Sendai stops equivocating internally.

She loves Miyagi.

And crucially, she recognizes this not as something that began after moving in together or after their sexual encounter. She understands retrospectively that the feeling was already present during the ¥5,000 era.

The money had functioned as a **覆い**, a covering.

That gives us a useful correction to the interpretation of the original contract.

It did not create their attachment.

It did not merely conceal their attachment from outsiders.

It concealed each girl from **herself**.

The ¥5,000 allowed Sendai to explain:

> Why do I keep going back?

Because Miyagi pays me.

Why do I tolerate this strange girl?

Because we have an arrangement.

Why do I care whether Miyagi wants me there?

Because that determines whether the arrangement continues.

Why does graduation frighten me?

Because the arrangement will end.

Once graduation removes the structure, those explanations stop working.

By Volume 6 Sendai has finally reached the proposition that the contract spent years preventing her from articulating:

> **I went because I wanted Miyagi.**

This makes Sendai considerably more self-aware than Miyagi for the remainder of the volume.

But self-awareness is not yet honesty.

# Why Sendai does not confess is more complicated than cowardice

It would be easy to accuse the novel of artificially prolonging the romance here. Sendai knows she is in love. Miyagi is obviously attached to her. They live together. They have had sex. Why not just say it?

Volume 6 gives Sendai a surprisingly good reason—and then immediately complicates it with a much less noble one.

Sendai worries that confessing immediately after what happened in Volume 5 would make:

> 「好き」

function as a retrospective justification.

The sequence could become:

> I sexually pushed past a boundary → but actually I loved you → therefore what happened was okay.

Sendai senses, correctly, that this would be morally suspect.

Love does not retroactively sanitize conduct.

That hesitation is one of the more responsible thoughts she has had about the relationship.

But it is only half the explanation.

Sendai also recognizes that the ambiguous relationship she already possesses is extraordinarily valuable.

She can:

-   live with Miyagi;

-   eat with her;

-   kiss her;

-   touch her;

-   receive Miyagi's increasingly voluntary touch;

-   spend Sundays with her;

-   buy things for her;

-   become part of her daily life.


A confession introduces the possibility of an answer.

And an answer might destroy the arrangement.

Sendai therefore reaches an uncomfortable conclusion:

> she can tolerate not being Miyagi's girlfriend more easily than she can tolerate losing Miyagi.

So the silence is simultaneously **care and self-interest**.

That distinction matters.

Sendai's restraint is more ethical than simply forcing a confession into the aftermath of Volume 5.

It is not selfless.

She is also preserving access.

That ambiguity is very characteristic of *Shuukara*: healthier motives do not magically replace unhealthy ones. They accumulate beside them.

---

# Maika finally crosses the membrane

Volume 6 makes Utsunomiya Maika much more important by allowing the two previously separate social worlds to touch.

Sendai goes looking for Miyagi at Miyagi and Maika's university.

She meets Maika.

And the secret partially collapses.

Sendai tells her that she and Miyagi live together.

The irony is wonderful: after everything that has happened, Sendai has to translate their relationship into socially intelligible vocabulary, so Miyagi becomes her **friend**.

The strange private relationship that spent years refusing the word 友達 now uses “friend” as its public alias.

Maika's reaction is analytically valuable because she has known Miyagi through an entirely different relational context. Sendai immediately discovers that there exists a version of Miyagi whom Maika knows and she does not.

And that bothers her.

This continues Volume 5's epistemological problem:

> **Intimacy does not eliminate the existence of versions of another person that belong to other relationships.**

Sendai experiences Maika as evidence of that fact.

Maika knows a Miyagi who chats ordinarily, socializes ordinarily, and exists outside Sendai's exceptional private world.

Sendai simultaneously feels superior—*I know things about Miyagi Maika could never imagine*—and jealous—*Maika knows things I do not*.

That is not merely romantic rivalry.

It is rivalry over **relational knowledge**.

---

# Maika remains the series' best control case for care without entitlement

The interlude **「宇都宮舞香の戸惑い」** is especially useful because it lets us see how a comparatively secure relationship handles exactly the problems Miyagi and Sendai mishandle.

Maika realizes that Miyagi has hidden something significant from her.

She is confused.

She is somewhat hurt.

She is curious.

But her conclusion is essentially:

> everyone has things they do not want to say, and being Miyagi's close friend means respecting Miyagi's reasons for not saying them.

That is an enormously important contrast.

Miyagi and Sendai often translate closeness into:

> If I am important to you, I should know.

Maika instead operates from:

> **Because you are important to me, I do not automatically own the information you are protecting.**

This is not emotional indifference. Maika very clearly cares.

She is simply capable of allowing affection and opacity to coexist.

The distinction becomes more important as *Shuukura* increasingly organizes Miyagi and Sendai's desire around exclusive knowledge.

Maika is almost a living counterargument to the proposition that intimacy is measured by how completely one person possesses access to another.

---

# Maika also proves that domesticity itself is not romantic

There is a subtle but important moment in Maika's interlude when she comes home, says 「ただいま」, receives an answer from Miyagi, and thinks that she could probably live comfortably with Miyagi too.

This matters because it prevents us from treating every domestic signifier as automatically romantic.

Sharing:

-   meals;

-   rooms;

-   household chores;

-   「ただいま／おかえり」;

-   ordinary time


does not intrinsically constitute romantic love.

Maika could plausibly do many of those things with Miyagi.

The difference between Maika–Miyagi and Sendai–Miyagi therefore has to be located elsewhere.

And Volume 6 increasingly locates it in:

-   bodily desire;

-   exclusivity;

-   jealousy;

-   symbolic marking;

-   erotic knowledge;

-   naming;

-   the desire to monopolize certain versions of the other;

-   and the fear of the other person becoming irreplaceable.


The novel is becoming quite precise about the difference between **intimacy** and **romantic/sexual attachment**.

---

# Miyagi finally has to close the distance herself

One of Miyagi's most important realizations comes while walking home with Sendai after Maika engineers their reconciliation.

Miyagi remembers that during high school, whenever she created distance, Sendai usually closed it.

That has been the relationship's engine for years.

Miyagi withdraws.

Sendai returns.

Miyagi imposes a rule.

Sendai finds a loophole.

Miyagi says enough.

Sendai asks for slightly more.

Miyagi threatens the ending.

Sendai manufactures continuation.

Volume 6 finally puts Miyagi in a situation where she recognizes:

> **This time I have to move too.**

That is an enormous development.

It does not mean she immediately becomes emotionally articulate.

Her methods remain bizarre.

But increasingly, Miyagi is no longer merely the gatekeeper whom Sendai persuades into intimacy.

She becomes an **initiator**.

That shift is central to everything that happens later in the volume.

---

# “I wanted to see Miyagi” still sounds impossible to Miyagi

On the walk home Sendai tells her:

> 「もう一日待てないくらい宮城に会いたかった」

She wanted to see Miyagi so badly that she could not wait another day.

Miyagi essentially refuses to believe her.

This is one of the clearest statements yet of Miyagi's underlying self-concept.

Her reasoning is not:

> Sendai is lying because Sendai is generally dishonest.

It is closer to:

> **There is no coherent reason someone should want me that much.**

Miyagi knows herself as:

-   selfish;

-   difficult;

-   controlling;

-   emotionally withholding;

-   someone who pushes Sendai away.


Therefore voluntary attachment becomes cognitively suspicious.

This is the deepest problem beneath Miyagi's obsession with rules.

A rule can explain why somebody remains.

Payment can explain it.

A promise can explain it.

Roommate obligations can explain it.

Four years on a lease can explain it.

But:

> *I wanted to see you.*

requires Miyagi to accept that **she herself may be sufficient cause**.

She still cannot do that.

---

# The earrings transform from a mark into a trust instrument

Volume 5 established the piercing as reciprocal possession.

Miyagi once wanted to mark Sendai.

Instead, she eventually asked Sendai to pierce her.

Volume 6 develops this into something substantially more interesting.

Sendai uses her own tutoring income to buy Miyagi replacement earrings.

That distinction matters.

She does not want the gift financed by her parents.

She wants these earrings to be materially connected to the independent adult self she is building.

She chooses small silver plumeria flowers.

Miyagi later discovers some associated meanings such as 気品 and 内気な乙女, but Sendai's hidden reason is more significant: she has researched plumeria jewelry as carrying a wish for the happiness of an important person.

She does not tell Miyagi that.

The gift therefore contains an unspoken confession.

But Sendai's internal logic immediately contaminates the apparent purity of that gesture.

She wants Miyagi to be happy.

She also admits that if Miyagi someday loves somebody else, she does **not** think she could genuinely wish for that happiness.

So the earrings become a substitute:

> if I become too jealous and selfish to wish for your happiness, perhaps the thing I gave you can carry that wish in my place.

And buried beneath even that is another desire:

> perhaps Miyagi will remain too 内気—too unable to confess—to successfully give herself to someone else.

The plumeria is therefore almost a perfect Sendai object.

It is simultaneously:

**care, blessing, memory, independent adult labor, possession, fear, and contingency planning.**

Sendai's love has matured enough to imagine Miyagi's happiness.

It has not matured enough to accept being excluded from that happiness.

---

# “Swear on the piercing” is the new ¥5,000

This is one of the most revealing structural transformations in Volume 6.

The girls increasingly use the piercing and earrings as a token for promises.

Miyagi makes Sendai promise not to do strange things.

Sendai jokingly asks whether she should swear on the piercing.

Miyagi takes those promises seriously because the piercing is special.

The mechanism should look familiar.

Earlier:

> ¥5,000 establishes the rule.

Now:

> the piercing authenticates the promise.

This is why I would not say that *Shuukura* has abandoned contractual thinking.

Instead, its contractual grammar is being **moralized**.

The old token authorized asymmetrical control:

> I paid, therefore I may command.

The new token authorizes reciprocal trust:

> you made this mark, therefore I expect you to keep the promise attached to it.

That is significantly healthier.

But Miyagi still needs something concrete because ordinary trust remains too abstract.

She cannot yet comfortably operate from:

> Sendai said she would stop, therefore I trust Sendai.

She needs:

> Sendai said she would stop **and swore on this materially important thing whose meaning I understand**.

The physical object continues doing emotional work language cannot yet carry.

---

# The bedroom border survives cohabitation

One of Volume 5's best discoveries was that separate bedrooms became the new equivalent of Miyagi's old apartment door.

Volume 6 preserves this.

Despite everything they have done together, Sendai has still not casually acquired free access to Miyagi's new bedroom.

When Maika visits, Miyagi becomes acutely conscious of the fact that Sendai's **first** entrance into her room should not happen accidentally as part of a three-person gathering.

That is remarkable.

Miyagi is reserving a milestone without being able to say that she is reserving it.

And Sendai senses the discomfort.

Rather than forcing the issue, she redirects the gathering into her own room.

That is a small but meaningful sign of maturation on Sendai's side.

Earlier Sendai frequently treated Miyagi's hesitation as an obstacle to interpret and maneuver around.

Here she recognizes:

> this entrance matters to Miyagi;

and she leaves it unrealized.

Physical access is becoming something that can be **preserved for later rather than seized because an opportunity exists**.

---

# Jealousy becomes reciprocal enough that Miyagi can finally identify its direction

Volume 6 contains a lot of jealousy, but we should resist treating jealousy itself as automatic proof of romance.

Friends can be jealous.

Possessive people can be jealous without romantic attraction.

Fear of replacement can exist in many attachments.

What matters here is the constellation surrounding the jealousy.

Miyagi is jealous while also:

-   wanting Sendai's touch;

-   initiating kisses;

-   wanting exclusive bodily knowledge;

-   caring intensely about who gets to use Sendai's given name;

-   worrying that Sendai's attention might move elsewhere;

-   wanting Sendai's body specifically rather than generic affection.


Within that broader evidence, jealousy becomes diagnostically useful.

And the key shift is that Miyagi eventually understands what she had previously displaced onto Maika.

At first the problem feels like:

> Sendai is taking Maika.

Then Miyagi realizes the more frightening truth:

> **Maika might take Sendai.**

That is a major piece of self-knowledge.

Miyagi does not merely want her existing friendship protected.

She wants Sendai's attention oriented toward her.

---

# The given name becomes scarce property

The best symbolic development in Volume 6 may be **葉月**.

Maika and Sendai begin developing a friendship of their own.

Sendai suggests Maika might eventually call her Hazuki.

Maika refuses.

Her reasoning is exceptionally perceptive:

> Miyagi does not call Sendai “Hazuki,” so Maika does not want to take that step before Miyagi does.

Maika effectively recognizes an intimacy boundary that Miyagi herself has never openly declared.

This produces a fascinating paradox when Sendai asks Miyagi again to call her Hazuki.

Miyagi refuses precisely because she now understands that if **she** begins calling Sendai Hazuki, Maika may gain permission to do the same.

So the name has become simultaneously:

> too intimate for Miyagi to claim
> and
> too intimate for Miyagi to share.

That is quintessential *Shuukura*.

Miyagi protects an intimacy by refusing to enact it.

Her logic is:

> If I never take possession of this thing, nobody else can receive it through me.

“Hazuki” becomes a scarce relational resource.

This is also why Sendai calling Miyagi **志緒理** during sexual intimacy matters so much.

Miyagi can permit the given name inside an exceptional erotic state.

She still cannot normalize it over breakfast.

Once again:

> **exceptional intimacy is easier than ordinary intimacy.**

A name spoken during sex can be quarantined.

A name spoken every morning would redefine the relationship.

---

# The cat outing is one of the series' funniest examples of needing an excuse

Sendai encounters a calico cat.

Later she wants to go outside with Miyagi.

So she invents a mission:

> let's find the cat.

She knows there is no serious reason to expect the cat to be found.

That is almost beside the point.

She needs a reason to ask Miyagi to go somewhere.

Sendai thinks explicitly that she wishes they could simply go out without requiring one.

And naturally, when Maika later hears about these Sunday excursions, she supplies the category Miyagi refuses to use:

> that's basically a date.

More specifically, it resembles the sort of idle date two people living together might take because they have nothing else to do.

Miyagi denies it.

But Maika's language is important because it reveals how little of the “romance problem” remains invisible from outside once the bizarre private contract is removed.

From an ordinary observer's perspective:

Two young women live together.

They repeatedly spend Sundays together.

They go walking for no practical reason.

One buys the other jewelry.

They cook for each other.

They are visibly preoccupied with one another.

The category Miyagi insists upon—**roommates**—is becoming descriptively inadequate.

---

# Even the animal metaphors reproduce their relationship

Miyagi eventually decides Sendai resembles a dog.

More specifically, after looking for an appropriately elegant large dog, she lands on a **borzoi**.

Sendai says Miyagi is a cat.

The symbolism is almost too neat.

Sendai-as-dog reflects:

-   pursuit;

-   responsiveness;

-   returning when called;

-   her history of obeying Miyagi's commands;

-   her stronger outward sociability.


Miyagi-as-cat reflects:

-   territoriality;

-   selective affection;

-   resistance to being summoned;

-   approaching when she chooses;

-   retreat after overstimulation;

-   intimacy governed by permission.


But the scene does something more interesting than merely assigning archetypes.

Miyagi gives Sendai the command:

> 「お手」

Sendai obeys.

Then she uses the proximity to pull Miyagi closer.

That tiny gag captures years of their relationship.

Miyagi supplies the formal permission.

Sendai decides what to do with the opening.

The old pattern—

> **Miyagi controls permission; Sendai controls momentum**

—is still alive.

It is just becoming playful enough that both can see it.

---

# Domesticity becomes increasingly difficult to separate from love

Volume 6 quietly deepens the apartment's meaning.

During a storm Miyagi admits that frightening weather bothers her. Sendai offers her room.

They watch a movie.

They hold hands.

Miyagi falls asleep.

Eventually she stays in Sendai's bed.

The critical development isn't sleeping beside one another. They have already done more intimate things.

It is that Sendai **keeps the promise** not to turn the situation into another sexual encounter.

Miyagi notices.

And she says, internally, that this makes her trust Sendai more.

That is one of the most consequential statements in the volume.

Trust is no longer theoretical.

Sendai demonstrates:

> proximity does not automatically become escalation.

Miyagi learns:

> I can enter Sendai's space, become vulnerable, fall asleep beside her, and still retain a boundary.

The relationship desperately needs that lesson after Volume 5.

---

# Illness reverses their family histories

Shortly afterward, Sendai becomes sick.

The resulting care sequence is ordinary enough to be almost radically intimate for this series.

Miyagi buys supplies.

She stays beside Sendai.

She worries.

She tells Sendai that if she catches the illness, Sendai can take care of her in return.

The reciprocity matters.

Sendai contrasts this with being sick in her family home, where care was comparatively minimal and emotionally thin.

Miyagi's presence keeps unpleasant thoughts away.

Meanwhile Miyagi has already revealed that she does not particularly want to return to her father's home during summer. Her father is often absent anyway.

The apartment is therefore increasingly becoming something stronger than a student arrangement.

It is a place where each receives a form of care her original home failed to reliably supply.

Miyagi's old home problem was:

> somebody should return, but does not.

Sendai's was:

> people may technically be there without making the place emotionally welcoming.

The new home increasingly answers both through mundane acts:

food, illness, weather, waiting, shopping, temperature settings, sleeping, returning.

The more banal the act, the more consequential it becomes.

---

# “What we eat doesn't matter. Sitting here does.”

Miyagi reaches one of her clearest domestic realizations in Volume 6:

> what they drink or eat is not actually the important part.

The important part is:

> **sitting there together.**

This is almost the inverse of the original ¥5,000 relationship.

In high school, the relationship required a highly specific external reason for shared time.

Now the content of the time is becoming irrelevant.

They do not need an exciting activity.

They need **co-presence**.

This is why scenes involving pudding, tea, stew, cheesecake, juice, breakfast, and pancakes matter so much.

Food has ceased being merely a hospitality motif.

It is becoming a method of answering:

> Will you still be here when nothing special is happening?

---

# Miyagi's deepest defense now becomes explicit: let the relationship be special, not Sendai

Chapter 10 gives us perhaps the clearest statement yet of Miyagi's adult defensive strategy.

She increasingly knows that her relationship with Sendai is special.

She can tolerate that.

What she desperately resists is the proposition:

> **Sendai herself is special to me.**

Why distinguish them?

Because a relationship can be time-boxed.

“Roommate” means approximately four university years.

A living arrangement has structural termination.

It can be remembered afterward as something unique that nevertheless ended when it was supposed to.

But if Sendai herself becomes irreplaceable, Miyagi loses that protection.

Miyagi thinks in terms of the inevitability of endings:

> nothing lasts forever.

Therefore she tries to locate exceptional value in the **temporary arrangement**, not in the person.

This is psychologically sophisticated.

She is effectively saying:

> I can survive losing a special period of my life.
> I do not know whether I can survive losing a special person.

So “roommate” is no longer merely a euphemism for girlfriend.

It is an **expiry mechanism**.

It promises intimacy while protecting Miyagi from having to imagine an indefinite future.

---

# Miyagi becomes an active sexual subject

The major erotic reversal of Volume 6 occurs when Miyagi wants to know what **Sendai** is like under intimate touch.

This development is much more important than “they have sex again.”

During Volume 5, Sendai's desire took the dominant investigative form:

> I want to know Miyagi.

Now Miyagi develops the same hunger.

She wants:

-   Sendai's reactions;

-   Sendai's voice;

-   Sendai's pleasure;

-   bodily knowledge nobody else possesses.


And she specifically recognizes that ordinary jealousy is feeding this desire.

If other people can possess parts of Sendai's social world she cannot control, perhaps Miyagi can possess something they do not have:

> **exclusive embodied knowledge.**

That is not simply sexual curiosity.

It is attachment anxiety being converted into epistemology.

Miyagi wants proof that there exists a Sendai available only to her.

---

# Touch is functioning as anxiety regulation

Miyagi explicitly notices that the more of their bodies are touching, the more her anxiety diminishes.

That is one of the most revealing psychological statements in the entire series.

Physical intimacy is not operating only as pleasure.

It is also:

> **evidence of present orientation.**

When Sendai is touching Miyagi, Sendai cannot simultaneously be disappearing into some inaccessible other relationship.

When Miyagi touches Sendai and receives a response, the other person becomes immediately legible.

Body replaces uncertainty.

That explains why both girls are increasingly attached to marks, reactions, earrings, bites, kisses, warmth, bodily traces, and remembered sensations.

They are trying to solve an abstract problem—

> Do I matter enough?

—with tangible evidence.

That works in the moment.

It cannot fully solve the underlying problem.

No amount of exclusive bodily knowledge can make another person completely knowable or permanently available.

Volume 6 seems increasingly aware of that tension.

---

# The second major sexual encounter is more reciprocal—but not cleanly healthy

The comparison with Volume 5 is important enough to be precise.

Miyagi initiates the Chapter 10 encounter.

She asks Sendai to let her touch her.

Sendai agrees.

Miyagi is therefore not a passive recipient this time. Her desire is explicit enough that she is willing to construct the encounter herself.

That is genuine development.

But Miyagi also uses leverage.

She effectively tells Sendai that if Sendai refuses her now, Miyagi will never allow Sendai equivalent access again.

That introduces coercive pressure.

Sendai later thinks she would have agreed even without the threat because she was happy Miyagi wanted her.

That tells us Sendai's actual desire.

It does not make Miyagi's bargaining method ideal.

There is another ambiguous moment when Miyagi wants to undo more clothing and proceeds despite Sendai not giving a particularly clean affirmative response.

Again, the series has not suddenly acquired exemplary consent practices.

But something important has changed.

When Sendai eventually says:

> 「ストップ」
> 「もうむり」

Miyagi stops.

She apologizes.

And later, when Sendai wants to reciprocate and Miyagi says variations of:

> not now;
> I don't want that now;
> get off,

Sendai does not reason from Miyagi's physical response that her words must be false in the way she did in Volume 5.

That is real improvement.

There is still an earlier scene in the volume where Miyagi tells Sendai to stop touching/get off and Sendai turns it into a じゃんけん game before withdrawing. That remains ethically poor: an immediate boundary should not need to win a game to become effective.

So I would resist either extreme interpretation.

Volume 6 does **not** demonstrate that their consent problem has been solved.

It does show the narrative actively revising their relational grammar.

The trajectory is becoming:

> ambiguous resistance as something to overcome
> → explicit stopping increasingly determines the outcome.

That is meaningful progress, but incomplete progress.

---

# The ethical reversal is especially important for Miyagi

Miyagi's immediate response to Sendai's explicit stop distinguishes her from Sendai's Volume 5 failure.

This does not establish Miyagi as morally superior in general. She has plenty of controlling behavior of her own.

But it reveals an important difference in how the two girls have historically handled resistance.

Miyagi's most dangerous tendency is often:

> construct conditions that pressure Sendai into choosing what Miyagi wants.

Sendai's has often been:

> infer Miyagi's deeper desire and privilege that inference over Miyagi's surface statement.

Those are related but not identical failures.

The healthier future would require both corrections:

Miyagi must stop turning affection into leverage.

Sendai must stop treating her ability to “read” Miyagi as permission to override her.

Volume 6 begins moving in both directions.

---

# Miyagi discovers evidence that she affects Sendai

The emotional importance of Miyagi giving Sendai pleasure should not be reduced to sexual conquest.

Miyagi has spent years struggling to believe that Sendai genuinely wants her.

Now Sendai's body gives Miyagi an involuntary answer.

Miyagi is almost overwhelmed by the evidence.

That is why she becomes so fixated on Sendai describing what she feels.

She wants verbal confirmation too.

Sendai eventually tells her that Miyagi feels better than doing it herself.

For Miyagi, that information is almost terrifyingly powerful because it answers the question she refuses to ask directly:

> **Am I uniquely capable of affecting you?**

Sex becomes another substitute for confession.

Sendai cannot yet say:

> I love you.

Miyagi cannot yet ask:

> Do you love me?

So they obtain a narrower certainty:

> nobody else knows this reaction.

The problem, of course, is that erotic exclusivity cannot indefinitely substitute for emotional declaration.

But at this stage it is one of the few forms of certainty Miyagi will accept.

---

# Sendai wants the trace to remain too

Afterward, Sendai does not want the evidence of Miyagi's touch simply washed away.

That completes the reciprocity of the mark motif.

Early *Shuukura* was dominated by Miyagi's desire to leave marks on Sendai.

Then Miyagi asked Sendai to pierce her.

Now both increasingly want:

> traces proving the other person has acted upon them.

Possession has become bilateral.

That is both more equal and potentially more codependent.

Equality does not automatically make a possessive structure healthy.

What changes is that neither person is merely object.

Both now say, in behavior if not in declarative language:

> **I want to change you, and I want evidence that you changed me.**

---

# “Hazuki” remains impossible even during the most intimate moment

Sendai asks Miyagi to call her Hazuki.

Miyagi refuses:

> 「やだ。よばない」

But internally she can think the name.

This is another exquisite example of the series separating **internal recognition** from **social enactment**.

The name exists inside Miyagi.

She simply refuses to release it into shared reality.

And her reason is now revealingly possessive: allowing herself to say Hazuki might make the name available to Maika too.

Meanwhile Sendai repeatedly calls her **Shiori** during their intimate encounter, and Miyagi does not stop her.

The paradox is almost comical:

> Sendai may say “Shiori” during sex.
> Miyagi cannot say “Hazuki” over ordinary life.

Again the hierarchy of difficulty is inverted.

Sex is exceptional.

Given-name normality is continuous.

The latter frightens Miyagi more.

---

# Sendai nearly confesses—and deliberately doesn't

Immediately after the encounter Sendai starts:

> 「あのさ、宮城。私──」

The implication is obvious.

She stops.

Chapter 11 then confirms that she is relieved she did not finish.

This is where Sendai's restraint becomes difficult to categorize morally.

She has genuine reasons to fear that confession might overwhelm Miyagi.

She also knows Miyagi might run again.

But she is simultaneously becoming increasingly comfortable living inside a situation where she receives many of the privileges of romance without having to risk romantic rejection.

That raises a serious question for Sendai's development:

> **At what point does protecting Miyagi from a confession become protecting Sendai from an answer?**

Volume 6 does not resolve that.

It deliberately leaves the two motives fused.

---

# Chapter 11 gives the volume its most important ordinary breakthrough

The morning after the second major encounter, Miyagi is deeply embarrassed.

Sendai kisses her.

Miyagi asks why.

And Sendai answers:

> 「したかったから」
> 「それだけ」

Because I wanted to.

That's all.

I think this is one of the most important lines in *Shuukura* so far.

Compare it with almost the entire history of their relationship.

Why did Sendai come over?

¥5,000.

Why did Miyagi issue an order?

Because she paid.

Why did Sendai obey?

Because those were the rules.

Why are you touching me?

Punishment.

Why are you kissing me?

Exchange.

Why are we going outside?

The cat.

Why are you in my room?

Movie.

Why did you buy this?

Earrings.

Why did you do that?

Because you let me do this.

And now:

> **I kissed you because I wanted to kiss you.**

No contract.

No punishment.

No equivalence.

No loophole.

No debt.

No external cause.

The act refers directly to desire.

That is the relational language these two have been avoiding since Volume 1.

---

# Miyagi's answer is equally understated

Sendai asks, in effect, whether Miyagi would rather she were not there.

Miyagi cannot produce an equivalent declaration.

Instead she says:

> 「一人で食べるより、二人で食べたほうがいいと思ってる」

She thinks eating together is better than eating alone.

For another romance protagonist this might sound comically insufficient.

For Miyagi it is enormous.

She is saying that Sendai's presence **positively changes ordinary life**.

Not an exceptional afternoon.

Not sex.

Not an order.

Not the security of the lease.

Breakfast.

This is about as close as Miyagi can presently get to:

> I want you here.

And because the statement is about an indefinitely repeatable mundane act, it may actually be more threatening to her than an erotic declaration.

---

# Volume 6 answers Volume 5's question about independence

At the end of Volume 5 I framed one of the new university questions as:

> Can Miyagi let Sendai leave the apartment, become someone Miyagi cannot completely know, and still trust that she will return?

Volume 6 gives us the beginnings of an answer.

Sendai:

-   has university friends;

-   continues tutoring;

-   forms an independent relationship with Maika;

-   earns her own money;

-   exists outside Miyagi's direct observation.


Miyagi dislikes almost all of this at some level.

But she does not successfully eliminate any of it.

Instead, she begins developing another source of security:

> **Sendai returns.**

That is much healthier than:

> Sendai has nowhere else to go.

The relationship remains possessive, but voluntary recurrence is slowly replacing enclosure.

---

# The BOOK☆WALKER SS is almost pure thematic distillation

**「私たちのサイダーとオレンジジュースの行方」** looks trivial on the surface.

They need drinks.

That's basically it.

Which is why it is analytically excellent.

Miyagi notices they are out of cider and orange juice but does not want to admit she wants something else, because she anticipates Sendai proposing a late shopping trip.

Later, Sendai is away at university and learns Miyagi is shopping.

She rushes home and asks Miyagi to wait a few minutes at the supermarket.

She literally runs because she wants to join her.

That alone represents an enormous distance from Volume 1.

Nobody is buying the other's time.

Sendai is spending effort merely to catch a grocery errand.

Then comes the best line of the SS:

> 「でも、ゴールは遠いほうがいい。」

The goal is better when it is farther away.

Sendai deliberately extends their route through the store by adding ingredients for stew.

The practical objective is the checkout.

The emotional objective is:

> **make the walk together last longer.**

This is almost the inverse of the original title premise.

Once, their time together was precisely measured:

a specific day, a specific payment, an implicit ending.

Now Sendai wants inefficient shopping because efficiency would make shared time end sooner.

The old relationship asked:

> How much time does ¥5,000 purchase?

The new relationship asks:

> **How slowly can we reach the register?**

That is a lovely piece of miniature writing.

---

# The grocery trip is also the closest thing yet to effortless dating

Sendai still technically manufactures a reason.

They need groceries.

They need drinks.

They might make stew.

But the excuse has become almost transparently irrelevant.

The relationship is moving through stages:

> necessary reason
> → dubious reason
> → obviously invented reason
> → reason neither person really believes
> → no reason.

The cat outing belongs around the third stage.

The supermarket SS is around the fourth.

Chapter 11's:

> 「したかったから。それだけ」

briefly reaches the fifth.

The formal progression of *Shuukura* may ultimately be less about “will they confess?” than:

> **How much intimacy can survive once every excuse has been removed?**

---

# 「不平と不満と白川亜美」 gives us the healthiest treatment of distance in the corpus so far

The Volume 6 Kakuyomu release SS changes perspective to Shirakawa Ami and is chronologically set around late April, so I would treat it as a side-angle on the early university transition rather than a literal post-Volume-6 continuation.

Its opening thesis is remarkably direct:

> 「何キロ離れていても友だちは友だち。
> どんなに遠くても変わらない。
> 私たちは友だちだ。」

No matter how many kilometers apart they are, friends are friends.

Yet Ami immediately adds the important qualification:

> distance still sucks.

She misses Maika.

She misses Miyagi.

University is enjoyable and she has new friends, but that does not make the disappearance of the familiar high-school trio painless. Shuukura - LN06 Release SS - 不平…

This is a wonderful control case for Miyagi.

Ami experiences something structurally similar:

> people I love have gone somewhere else.

She does **not** respond by concluding that the relationship is ending.

She complains.

Loudly.

She says she wants them back.

She tells Maika she is bored without them.

She makes Maika promise to return during summer.

And then she trusts the promise.

That is almost everything Miyagi has spent six volumes struggling to do.

---

# Ami can say “I miss you” without converting longing into control

This distinction deserves emphasis.

Ami does not suppress attachment.

She is not the “healthy” character because she cares less.

She repeatedly says that being far apart is unpleasant and that she wants the old group together again.

But she also explicitly recognizes that Maika and Miyagi chose their own paths and she does not intend to interfere with those choices. Shuukura - LN06 Release SS - 不平…

That is an adult relational skill Miyagi lacks:

> **I dislike the consequence of your autonomy without believing your autonomy is a wrong against me.**

Miyagi's attachment anxiety frequently transforms:

> I don't want you to leave

into:

> I need a structure that makes leaving less possible.

Ami can remain with:

> I wish you weren't leaving.

She lets the feeling remain a feeling.

That is much harder than the story's comedy makes it look.

---

# Ami and Maika also model curiosity without epistemic entitlement

Naturally, the topic turns to Miyagi's mysterious roommate.

Miyagi has claimed she lives with a relative.

Ami jokingly proposes the obvious rom-com explanation:

> perhaps “the relative” is actually a secret boyfriend.

This continues the useful heteronormative camouflage we've seen before. The social world is increasingly capable of correctly identifying the **shape** of Miyagi's situation while supplying the wrong gender/category.

More importantly, Ami and Maika both notice how suspicious Miyagi is.

She:

-   avoids talking about the roommate;

-   refuses to show pictures;

-   dodges questions about the apartment;

-   will not invite them over.


Ami describes the roommate as effectively covered in mosaic.

They are extremely curious.

And then they stop.

The reason is explicit:

> this is not something they absolutely need to know, so Miyagi's feelings matter more than their curiosity. Shuukura - LN06 Release SS - 不平…

That makes the SS an almost perfect companion to Volume 6's main epistemological theme.

Miyagi and Sendai repeatedly think:

> If I care about you, I want more access.

Ami and Maika demonstrate:

> **If I care about you, I can tolerate the fact that some access is not mine.**

The series is quietly giving its protagonists healthier alternatives through their supposedly secondary friendships.

---

# “Wait for me” is the language Miyagi has not learned yet

Toward the end of the SS, Maika promises that she will come home during summer.

Ami effectively answers:

> I'll be waiting.

And she trusts her. Shuukura - LN06 Release SS - 不平…

The language is striking beside the main novel.

Miyagi has spent years trying to reduce the uncertainty between departure and return.

Ami demonstrates the simplest alternative:

> **wait.**

Waiting accepts two realities simultaneously:

The other person is absent.

The relationship still exists.

That is essentially the adult attachment skill Volume 6 is trying to teach Miyagi.

The release SS therefore isn't disposable comedy. It gives us an external model for the exact developmental task happening inside the central romance.

---

# The high-school trio is undergoing its own coming-of-age separation

The SS also keeps the university arc from becoming too narrowly about Miyagi and Sendai.

Ami's sadness is real because graduation fragmented a social unit that once required almost no effort to maintain.

Friends used to be:

> nearby by default.

Now friendship requires:

-   calls;

-   schedules;

-   holidays;

-   travel;

-   waiting;

-   deliberate reunion.


Ami's final wish is essentially that although they are no longer high-school students, she wants them to meet again and do stupid high-school things together. Shuukura - LN06 Release SS - 不平…

That is an understated coming-of-age theme.

Adulthood does not necessarily destroy old intimacy.

It removes the institutions that automatically maintained it.

The same thing happened to Miyagi and Sendai when high school ended.

The difference is that Ami and Maika have enough ordinary relational vocabulary to survive that transition without inventing a new contract.

---

# U35's illustrations reinforce the broadening of the relationship

Volume 6's visual program is noticeably less claustrophobic than the early novels.

The interior illustrations repeatedly move between:

-   Sendai and Maika's first contact;

-   Miyagi and Sendai in close domestic proximity;

-   shared movie/room scenes;

-   illness and care;

-   Sendai with the calico;

-   the three-person Maika–Miyagi–Sendai gathering.


That matters formally.

For much of the high-school arc, Miyagi and Sendai's relationship existed inside a sealed private chamber.

Volume 6 increasingly places their intimacy in a world containing:

> friends, streets, university, work, animals, errands, bedrooms belonging equally to both women, and shared social space.

The relationship is becoming less secret in the deeper sense.

Not everyone knows what it is.

But it can no longer exist entirely outside society.

That is necessary if it is ever going to become an adult relationship rather than an indefinitely preserved secret ritual.

---

# Volume 6's chapter titles reveal what the book is actually about

The repeated vocabulary is striking:

**knowing Miyagi.**

**getting closer to Miyagi.**

**the distance Sendai chose.**

**what Sendai doesn't want Miyagi to know.**

**trusting Sendai a little more.**

**how Miyagi sees me.**

**what Miyagi wants to know.**

**wanting to touch Sendai more.**

The volume is organized less around conventional romantic milestones than around **epistemology**.

What can I know about you?

What may I know?

What do I want you not to know?

Who else knows you?

Who gets to call you what?

What can your body tell me that your words will not?

What does someone else receive from you that I do not?

And underneath all of those:

> **Can I remain secure while part of you remains inaccessible to me?**

Neither girl can yet answer that comfortably.

---

# Updated Miyagi: from gatekeeper to participant

Volume 6 is probably Miyagi's biggest development in agency so far.

She does not become verbally straightforward.

But behaviorally, she increasingly chooses.

She:

initiates touch;

initiates kisses;

actively seeks Sendai's body;

admits that being apart feels wrong;

spends more time in the shared space;

trusts Sendai more when promises are kept;

cares for her when sick;

lets her own absence become temporary rather than terminal;

gets jealous because she does not want Sendai's attention transferred elsewhere;

and agrees to another shared outing.

Most importantly, she starts confronting the fact that **Sendai herself** may matter.

Her last major defense is sophisticated:

> the relationship may be special, but Sendai must not become special.

That distinction is unlikely to remain sustainable.

Miyagi's developmental problem is no longer primarily:

> Can I tolerate Sendai being close?

She can.

It is now:

> **Can I accept that losing Sendai would matter beyond the expiration of our current arrangement?**

That requires abandoning the fantasy that every important relationship can be made safe by assigning it an ending date.

---

# Updated Sendai: love recognized, love still partially possessive

Sendai's internal uncertainty about her feelings is basically over.

That is the biggest change.

She loves Miyagi.

She knows it.

Her problem is now what to do with that knowledge.

She shows real improvement:

She respects some boundaries she once would have maneuvered around.

She understands that confession cannot ethically function as retroactive justification.

She preserves Miyagi's bedroom threshold rather than exploiting it.

She builds an independent adult life.

She keeps a vulnerable promise during the storm.

She increasingly states simple desire rather than disguising every action as obligation.

But her love remains intensely possessive.

The plumeria exposes this beautifully.

Sendai wants Miyagi happy.

She cannot tolerate imagining Miyagi happily in love with somebody else.

She wants unique access.

She wants bodily traces.

She envies Maika's knowledge.

She wants Miyagi to call her Hazuki.

She is tempted to treat exclusive knowledge as proof of exclusive status.

So Sendai's next developmental problem is not discovering love.

It is:

> **learning that loving Miyagi does not entitle her to complete knowledge, exclusive access, or control over what Miyagi's future can contain.**

And, conversely, learning that honestly naming love is eventually less controlling than indefinitely structuring an unlabeled relationship around it.

---

# Revised series thesis through Volume 6

The progression is now clearer.

The high-school arc used:

> **payment → command → exceptional intimacy**

to make desire survivable.

Volume 5 removed the payment and exposed the danger:

> **desire without a shared grammar can become destabilizing and ethically messy.**

Volume 6 begins constructing a replacement vocabulary:

> **promise → request → trust → return → choice.**

But the old grammar keeps leaking through.

They still bargain.

They still create exchanges.

They still turn boundaries into games sometimes.

They still want marks.

They still manufacture reasons.

They still use labels to contain what those labels plainly cannot describe.

The important thing is that a new possibility has appeared.

Sendai kisses Miyagi because:

> she wanted to.

Miyagi wants Sendai at breakfast because:

> two is better than one.

Sendai runs to the supermarket because:

> she wants to make a grocery trip last longer.

None of those things needs ¥5,000.

None needs a broken rule.

None needs punishment.

None needs sex.

None even really needs “roommate.”

That changes my broader reading of the series.

I would now formulate *Shuukura*'s central developmental movement as:

> **The story is gradually stripping away every external explanation Miyagi and Sendai have constructed for choosing one another, until they are forced to confront the terrifying possibility that choice itself is the explanation.**

Volume 6 is where that process becomes unmistakable.

Sendai already knows the answer:

> I love Miyagi.

Miyagi is approaching it from the opposite direction:

> I want Sendai here.
> I want Sendai looking at me.
> I don't want Maika taking Sendai.
> I want to know Sendai.
> I want to touch Sendai.
> I don't want others using the name I cannot bring myself to claim.
> I want our relationship to be special.

She simply refuses the final inference because the inference would destroy her safest remaining defense:

> **four years.**

And the two supplemental stories sharpen exactly the right sides of that conflict.

The BOOK☆WALKER story says:

> when you genuinely want someone's company, even the route to the supermarket is better if the goal is farther away.

Ami's story says:

> when you genuinely trust someone, distance can hurt without meaning the relationship is dying.

Those are almost complementary lessons.

**Sendai is learning how to prolong chosen closeness.**

**Miyagi still has to learn how to survive chosen distance.**

Once those two capacities coexist, they may finally have something more durable than a contract: not guaranteed proximity, but the confidence that two independent people can leave, return, and keep choosing one another without needing an excuse. Shuukura - LN06 Release SS - 不平…

The next major work in the established sequence is **Volume 7**, with its associated supplemental material best read alongside it rather than imported prematurely into this volume's conclusions.
