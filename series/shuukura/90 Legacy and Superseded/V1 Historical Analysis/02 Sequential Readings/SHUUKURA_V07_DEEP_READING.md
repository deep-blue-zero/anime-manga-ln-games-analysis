# SHUUKURA V07 Deep Reading — Transcript Extraction

I agree with that distinction. Sexual or violent content primarily affects **content intensity and age appropriateness**; thematic maturity depends much more on what a work understands about people and what it does with consequences, interiority, contradiction, responsibility, and change. *Sound! Euphonium* can therefore be substantially more mature than a sexually explicit but psychologically thin romance.

Volume 7 is actually a strong example of that distinction. Its most interesting material is not the sexual escalation. It is about **different relational speeds, the right to refuse without producing a justification, the danger of losing oneself inside attachment, the reconstruction of childhood memory, and learning that another person's independence is compatible with their continued return**.

# 『週に一度クラスメイトを買う話』Volume 7

## \+ Volume 7 Voice-Drama Supplement

### No reason required: desire, refusal, pace, and the difference between being close to someone and disappearing into them

### Scope

This pass follows the agreed sequence: **Volume 7 → BOOK☆WALKER「私たちが大学へ向かう朝」→ 「宇都宮舞香の夏休み」前後編 → Volume 7 audio material**. Your archive manifest confirms both the Japanese Volume 7 EPUB and the associated BOOK☆WALKER bonus as distinct holdings. MANIFEST

I am keeping the spoiler boundary at the end of Volume 7.

The central argument I would make after reading the full volume is:

> **Volume 7 takes the principle “desire does not need an excuse” and forces Miyagi and Sendai to confront its ethical complement: refusal does not need an excuse either.**

That sounds simple. It isn't simple for these two.

Their relationship began by making every action contingent upon a reason:

¥5,000.

A command.

A rule.

Tutoring.

A punishment.

A broken promise.

A bargain.

The gradual disappearance of those reasons has been one of the series' main developmental movements.

Volume 6 finally gave Sendai:

> 「したかったから。それだけ」

*Because I wanted to. That's all.*

Volume 7 asks what happens when Miyagi takes that logic seriously.

She wants to kiss Sendai without a reason.

She wants Sendai's hand without a reason.

She becomes jealous without being able to construct a respectable reason.

She wants Sendai nearby simply because Sendai's presence matters.

But once “I want to” becomes sufficient justification for initiating a request, its moral mirror must also become true:

> **“I don't want to” is sufficient justification for stopping one.**

Sendai has a much harder time accepting the second proposition than the first.

That conflict produces both the volume's ugliest relational failure and its most important breakthrough.

---

# I. Summer vacation changes the meaning of “once a week” more completely than graduation did

Volume 5 made Miyagi and Sendai roommates.

Volume 6 made their desire increasingly voluntary.

Volume 7 gives them something the high-school relationship could never possess:

**unstructured time.**

Miyagi notices the difference explicitly.

Last summer Sendai visited three times per week because tutoring gave them an excuse.

This summer Sendai is simply there.

Every day.

There is:

-   「おはよう」

-   「おやすみ」

-   breakfast;

-   dinner;

-   aimless television;

-   casual touch;

-   boredom;

-   errands;

-   mornings when neither person has anything especially important to say.


That is the real destruction of the original premise.

The opposite of “buying a classmate once a week” is not:

> having sex with her.

It is:

> **seeing her every morning without requiring a reason.**

And Volume 7 understands the significance of that transition remarkably well.

What they are learning now is not intensity.

It is **duration without occasion**.

---

# II. Miyagi begins wanting things without manufacturing a cause

Early in the volume, Sendai is in Miyagi's visual field for so much of the day that her presence has become part of the background architecture of summer.

Then something significant happens.

Miyagi wants her hand.

She struggles absurdly to say even that much, eventually producing little more than:

> 「……手」

The difficulty matters because commands have historically been much easier for her than requests.

A command says:

> You must do this because the structure authorizes me.

A request says:

> I want something from you, and whether I receive it depends upon your answer.

That is naked dependence.

Miyagi is still terrible at it.

But she does it.

And when Sendai's attention shifts toward the calico cat, Miyagi reacts with characteristic jealousy and turns Sendai's own doctrine against her:

> 「理由いらないんでしょ」

You said we don't need a reason.

She kisses, licks, and bites Sendai because she wants to.

The emotional logic is childish and possessive.

The development underneath it is not.

Miyagi is finally accepting that:

> **her own desire can be causal.**

She does not need ¥5,000 to prove why the action happened.

---

# III. Then Miyagi asks the dangerous question

Miyagi asks whether Sendai wants a boyfriend.

Even the grammar of the question gives them protective distance.

“Boyfriend” keeps the conversation inside a conventional heterosexual frame.

Sendai tells her, essentially, that popularity or having options is irrelevant if the person she actually likes does not return her feelings.

Miyagi asks:

> 「好きな人は？」

Does she have someone she likes?

Sendai answers:

> 「いるよ」

Yes.

For perhaps half a second, Miyagi gets extremely close to the answer she has spent the series circling.

And then she doesn't want it.

That is classic Miyagi.

She wants to know enough to reduce uncertainty.

She does **not** want information capable of changing the structure she depends upon.

Sendai eventually defuses the danger through “Mike-chan,” the cat, and retreats into a socially safe account.

The episode clarifies that Miyagi's problem is no longer ignorance.

She knows the relevant question.

She understands that Sendai could love someone.

She can even ask.

What she cannot yet tolerate is discovering that the answer might require her to act.

---

# IV. The aquarium is their first truly successful ordinary date

Volume 7's aquarium material is one of the strongest demonstrations yet of how *Shuukura* differentiates **ordinary intimacy** from erotic intensity.

Sendai chooses the aquarium partly by thinking about Miyagi:

-   the crocodile;

-   the platypus;

-   indoor summer comfort;

-   what Miyagi might actually enjoy.


Once there, Sendai spends almost as much attention watching Miyagi as watching the animals.

This isn't unusual romantic behavior.

That is exactly why it matters.

Miyagi laughs.

She talks.

She visibly enjoys herself.

And then she gives Sendai something surprisingly direct:

> 「今日はありがと。水族館、楽しかった」

Thank you for today. The aquarium was fun.

Sendai asks her to tell her things like this in the future.

That request is more developmentally important than it appears.

Sendai wants **feedback about Miyagi's happiness**.

Not merely bodily reaction.

Not acquiescence.

Not the absence of refusal.

She wants Miyagi to say:

> this made me happy.

That is a much healthier source of relational knowledge.

---

# V. The aquarium also revises Sendai's family memory

The newly written interlude **「遠い記憶、宮城との約束」** gives the aquarium a deeper genealogy.

Young Hazuki once visited an aquarium with her older sister.

The sister she remembers there is warm:

-   knowledgeable;

-   protective;

-   holding her hand;

-   making sure she won't get lost.


It belongs to a period before the family relationship became what Sendai now experiences as emotionally depleted and fractured.

Adult Sendai does not simply erase that childhood.

Instead, the aquarium becomes capable of carrying another meaning.

The interlude gives us the striking formulation:

> 「家族とのぼやけた過去よりも、宮城がいる未来に心が向かう。」

Her heart turns more toward **a future containing Miyagi** than toward the blurred past with her family.

That distinction matters.

This isn't:

> Miyagi replaces Sendai's family and therefore the past ceases to exist.

It is:

> **a location once encoded through childhood family intimacy can acquire another layer through chosen adult intimacy.**

Volume 7 repeatedly does this with memory.

The past isn't deleted.

Its monopoly over meaning is broken.

---

# VI. Miyagi protects the aquarium memory from sex

The scene after they return home is particularly revealing.

Miyagi initiates a kiss.

Sendai wants more.

Miyagi doesn't.

And one of Miyagi's reasons is fascinating:

if they go much further, what happened at the aquarium may become mentally tangled with the more intense sexual experience.

She wants the aquarium day to remain what it was.

That reverses an earlier hierarchy.

In the first volumes, extraordinary physical intimacy seemed like the “important” thing, while going somewhere together was frightening because it resembled a date.

Now Miyagi values:

> **we went to the aquarium together and I had fun**

enough to protect that memory from being absorbed into erotic intensity.

That's real relational maturation.

It indicates that Sendai is no longer valuable chiefly as the person with whom forbidden or exceptional things occur.

She is becoming someone with whom Miyagi wants **good days**.

And good days deserve to remain legible as good days.

---

# VII. The series' most important distinction may now be intensity versus continuity

This has become clearer across Volumes 5–7.

Miyagi can tolerate enormous intensity if it remains exceptional.

What frightens her is recurrence.

A dramatic encounter can be classified as:

> something that happened once.

An aquarium visit produces:

> maybe we could go to the zoo later.

A birthday produces:

> what about next year?

Breakfast produces:

> what about tomorrow morning?

A shared commute produces:

> what about tomorrow too?

Those questions move the relationship into time.

And time is where Miyagi's deepest wound lives.

That is why Volume 7's most important development is not that Miyagi becomes physically more permissive.

It is that she increasingly starts imagining a **next instance**.

---

# VIII. “Roommate” finally becomes openly exclusive

Volume 7 also clarifies how thoroughly the roommate label has stopped being a neutral housing category.

Sendai asks:

> 「ルームメイトは私だけにしといてよ」

Keep me as your only roommate.

Miyagi answers, in effect:

> she could not live like this with anybody else anyway.

Then they make a pinky promise.

That scene is almost funny because the word “roommate” is doing impossible semantic labor.

Ordinarily:

> roommate = person who shares housing.

For Miyagi and Sendai:

> roommate = a four-year exclusive relational office containing shared domesticity, emotional priority, bodily intimacy, jealousy, promises, and future expectation while carefully avoiding the words that would normally describe any of that.

“Roommate” has become an **exclusive title**.

In practical terms, there can be only one.

The supposed euphemism now has its own monogamy.

---

# IX. Sendai's birthday moves intimacy into Miyagi's private room

The birthday section is one of the volume's major spatial milestones.

Miyagi claims to have plans with a friend.

Sendai becomes jealous.

The supposed friend does not exist.

Miyagi has gone out to arrange Sendai's birthday.

The deception is revealingly inverted from early *Shuukura*.

Once, Miyagi fabricated reasons to conceal why she was spending time **with** Sendai.

Now she fabricates another relationship to conceal the fact that she is preparing something **for** Sendai.

The excuse survives.

Its direction has reversed.

Then Sendai enters Miyagi's bedroom.

That matters enormously.

They have lived under the same roof for months, yet Miyagi's new private room has remained protected territory.

The old high-school architecture was:

> Miyagi's room = space Sendai enters because Miyagi's contract permits it.

The new apartment created equal rooms.

Allowing Sendai into Miyagi's is therefore no longer exercising ownership.

It is granting access.

That makes the birthday scene a real spatial act of trust.

---

# X. The rare cheesecake reveals how preferences are created relationally

Miyagi remembers that Sendai likes rare cheesecake.

Sendai remembers something more complicated:

she originally chose it partly because she thought **Miyagi** liked it.

Now Miyagi has stored:

> rare cheesecake = something Sendai likes.

And because Miyagi remembers it and acts upon that memory, Sendai increasingly does like it.

This is a beautiful minor example of how relationships create the people participating in them.

Preference is not always:

> I possessed taste X before meeting you.

Sometimes it becomes:

> you noticed X about me; your noticing made X meaningful; now X genuinely belongs to my self-understanding.

That is one reason the series' domestic details matter so much.

Miyagi and Sendai aren't merely discovering fixed selves.

They are **co-authoring habits and preferences**.

---

# XI. Sendai asks for an extraordinary birthday present: ordinary information

Sendai's birthday request contains two components.

One is future continuity:

> celebrate my birthday next year too.

And beyond that, again.

The other is almost comically mundane:

> tell me ordinary things about yourself.

Favorite foods.

Disliked foods.

Colors.

Preferences.

The sort of information normal friends or partners acquire through ordinary conversation.

That is brilliant because Miyagi and Sendai know things about one another that would be wildly intimate by conventional standards.

Yet their ordinary biographical database is incomplete.

They know bodies better than biographies.

They know jealousy better than favorite colors.

They know how one another sounds during extraordinary moments better than they know mundane preferences.

Sendai is now asking to fill in that gap.

This is another reason I would call the storytelling adult in the thematic sense you described.

The novel understands that intimacy isn't measured solely by **depth of secret access**.

It also consists of a huge accumulation of banal facts.

---

# XII. The cat chopstick rests are Miyagi's answer

Miyagi still cannot simply hand Sendai a birthday present and watch her receive it.

So she leaves it on the doorknob.

The gift is five cat-shaped chopstick rests.

Again:

ordinary domestic object.

Not expensive jewelry.

Not a dramatic romantic symbol.

Something you place beside dinner.

Sendai immediately changes breakfast so she can use one.

That's the meaningful response.

The gift enters **routine**.

Early objects in *Shuukura* often functioned as evidence of possession.

The university arc increasingly produces objects that function as evidence of **shared household life**.

---

# XIII. The center of Volume 7 is a fight about pace

The most important chapter, for me, is **「仙台さんはほかの誰とも違う」**.

It is also the chapter that requires the most ethical care.

The setup is relatively mundane.

Miyagi has spent time with Maika.

Sendai is jealous.

Sendai proposes a sleepover in Miyagi's room.

She promises, with the piercing as guarantee, not to go beyond kissing until Miyagi gives permission.

Then the conflict begins.

Sendai's frustration is understandable.

She feels Miyagi continually receives her desire while refusing to define what Sendai is allowed to hope for.

She feels hurt when Miyagi rejects the idea of working together.

She wants greater clarity.

None of that makes what follows acceptable.

Sendai begins pushing beyond the boundary.

When Miyagi objects, Sendai demands a **strong enough reason** for not wanting more.

That is the problem.

---

# XIV. Miyagi produces perhaps the strongest consent principle in the series

Sendai tells Miyagi that if she doesn't want something, she should give a reason substantial enough to make Sendai incapable of proceeding.

Miyagi cannot produce one.

Then she realizes that the premise is wrong.

She says:

> 「……仙台さん、キスする理由なんてなくてもいいって言ってたじゃん。だったら、したくない理由だってなくていいでしょ」

The logic is excellent.

Approximately:

> You said we don't need a reason to kiss. Then I shouldn't need a reason for not wanting to do something either.

That is the ethical culmination of the “reason” motif.

For six volumes, the series has gradually established:

> **desire does not require an externally respectable justification.**

Volume 7 adds:

> **refusal does not require an externally respectable justification either.**

These principles are not opposites.

They are necessary companions.

You may want something simply because you want it.

The other person may decline simply because she doesn't want it.

No tribunal is needed to determine whose reason is sufficiently persuasive.

That is one of the most thematically mature propositions *Shuukura* has reached.

---

# XV. And Sendai fails that test

The text should not be sanitized here.

Miyagi says:

> 「やめて」

Stop.

Later:

> 「……やだ」

No / I don't want this.

Sendai asks:

> 「理由は？」

Why?

And continues pushing the interaction.

She tries to solve Miyagi's discomfort through alternatives:

If being watched is the issue, Miyagi can cover Sendai's eyes.

If asymmetry is the issue, Miyagi can touch Sendai too.

But those solutions miss Miyagi's actual statement.

She doesn't owe Sendai a version of the encounter redesigned until the answer becomes yes.

The ethical problem is straightforward:

> **Sendai treats refusal as a problem to troubleshoot.**

That is not valid consent practice.

The fact that Miyagi is also attracted to her does not fix it.

The fact that Miyagi later participates actively does not retroactively make the earlier pressure appropriate.

And Volume 7 is unusually useful because the text itself gives us evidence that this is not merely an external moral framework imposed upon it.

Sendai's following chapter reflects on what she did.

---

# XVI. Sendai knows she did not really keep the promise

The next morning, Sendai thinks that saying she “kept” the promise would be difficult.

Technically, the encounter never reached the precise outcome she had promised to avoid.

But she recognizes that:

> **looking only at the result ignores what she did on the way there.**

That is morally important.

She even thinks explicitly about what would have happened had she continued touching Miyagi **without consent** in the bright room.

She understands that Miyagi might have fled entirely.

So Sendai has gained some ethical self-knowledge.

But we should not overstate her progress.

She also thinks:

> if Miyagi really wants to stop me, she should use stronger words.

That remains wrong.

It continues to shift responsibility onto Miyagi to produce a refusal Sendai finds psychologically convincing.

So Volume 7 does not resolve Sendai's consent problem.

It makes Sendai more capable of **seeing** it.

Those are different things.

---

# XVII. The breakthrough is not sex; it is 「まだ」

The encounter eventually reaches the actual relationship question.

Sendai asks whether Miyagi wants them to remain nothing but roommates.

Miyagi finally says:

> **「……まだルームメイトでいてよ」**

Stay roommates **for now / still**.

The crucial word is:

> **まだ**

This is completely different from:

> We are roommates and will never be anything else.

Miyagi's internal narration confirms it.

She describes herself as unable to walk at Sendai's speed.

She moves slowly.

Sometimes she stops.

Sometimes she looks backward.

If Sendai goes too fast, Miyagi worries that the distance will become large enough that she will simply give up pursuing her.

So what Miyagi is requesting is not emotional stasis.

It is:

> **pace accommodation.**

That may be the most adult relational insight in Volume 7.

Two people can want movement in the same general direction and still be moving at different speeds.

The faster partner's desire does not create an entitlement to pull.

The slower partner's need for time does not necessarily mean rejection.

Miyagi's request is essentially:

> Don't make me become ready at your speed. Stay close enough that I can keep moving toward you.

That is far more precise than “tsundere won't admit her feelings.”

---

# XVIII. Sendai finally stops—and Miyagi asks her to stay

Sendai answers:

> 「わかった。今はルームメイトでいいよ。」

For now, roommate is enough.

She stops.

Then she does something almost painfully Sendai-like:

> she asks Miyagi to praise her for stopping.

Miyagi strokes her hair and says:

> 「……ありがと」

Thank you.

This moment is awkward because stopping after someone says no should not normally require congratulations.

But narratively it matters because **both girls are trying to create a positive association between boundary recognition and continued intimacy**.

Then comes the much more important piece.

Sendai decides she should leave Miyagi's room.

Miyagi prevents her.

She insists that the sleepover should still happen.

Why?

Because if Sendai leaves immediately after the boundary, Miyagi fears she may never return.

That fear has haunted almost every boundary negotiation in the series:

> If I reject this act, am I rejecting the person?

Volume 7 finally begins separating them.

Miyagi can say:

> stop doing this

and simultaneously:

> **don't leave me.**

That's tremendous progress.

A boundary is becoming something that can occur **inside an intact relationship** rather than a miniature breakup.

---

# XIX. 「明日も今日が続きますように」 reverses four volumes of defensive temporal logic

That night, Miyagi holds Sendai's hand and thinks:

> **「──明日も今日が続きますように。」**

May today continue tomorrow.

This is almost the direct inversion of early Miyagi.

Early Miyagi continually thought:

> how do I make the inevitable ending hurt less?

Volume 4 Miyagi could tolerate:

> four years.

Now Miyagi privately wishes:

> **tomorrow, again.**

She still cannot imagine “forever” securely.

But the psychological direction has reversed.

Earlier, the future was something to contain.

Now the future is something she sometimes wants **more of**.

That is much bigger than a confession would be if the confession merely supplied the genre's expected vocabulary.

---

# XX. Volume 7's birthday structure explains why “tomorrow” is so hard for Miyagi

The LN-original bonus **「遠い記憶、仙台さんがいる今日」** gives us perhaps the strongest account yet of Miyagi's birthday wound.

Nine-year-old Shiori is already living after her mother's departure.

Her father promises to return with a cake.

Work intervenes.

A housekeeper is present and kind enough.

But the text adds an important qualification to Miyagi's loneliness:

> **being accompanied by just anybody does not solve it.**

Miyagi dislikes being alone.

She also finds it uncomfortable having someone she knows but is not particularly close to occupying the house.

That sharpens our domestic thesis considerably.

Miyagi does not need generic human occupancy.

She needs **chosen, trusted presence**.

That is why Sendai cannot be replaced by “someone.”

---

# XXI. The leftover cake becomes a temporal trap

Young Miyagi eats the birthday cake alone.

Then she has a disturbing thought.

As long as cake remains in the refrigerator, it feels as though the birthday has not ended.

And as long as the birthday has not ended:

> her father still has not come home.

The leftovers extend not celebration but **waiting**.

She wants the cake gone:

> 「早くなくなって」

Disappear quickly.

This is an extraordinary little piece of psychological writing.

It explains why Volume 7's adult birthday ritual is not trivial romance fluff.

The problem isn't “Miyagi has sad birthdays.”

The problem is:

> **birthday ritual has become structurally linked to an unreliable promise of return.**

A cake can become proof that someone is still absent.

---

# XXII. Sendai rewrites the birthday by finishing it

On Miyagi's university birthday, Sendai does exactly what her father once failed to do.

She comes home.

She has protected the date.

The cake actually arrives.

They eat it together.

Crucially:

> **they eat the whole cake.**

No leftover remains to prolong waiting.

The birthday ends properly because the person who promised to be present was actually present.

Miyagi can say that this birthday was happier than the ones before.

This is not trauma being magically cured by romance.

It is something much more credible:

> **a new experience competes with the old memory.**

The past remains true.

But it is no longer the only birthday script available to Miyagi.

---

# XXIII. Sendai's childhood and Miyagi's childhood form a revealing pair

The two newly written childhood pieces are cleverly complementary.

Sendai's childhood aquarium memory contains someone **present and protective** who later becomes emotionally distant.

Miyagi's childhood birthday contains someone **promised and absent**.

So their adult fears differ again.

Sendai knows:

> closeness can change into emotional distance even while the person continues existing.

Miyagi knows:

> someone can simply fail to come back.

Volume 7 lets each woman rework those memories through the other.

Sendai takes Miyagi to an aquarium.

Miyagi celebrates Sendai's birthday.

Sendai fulfills Miyagi's birthday promise.

Neither woman restores the childhood family.

They construct new rituals.

---

# XXIV. The piercing completes one of the longest-running motifs in the series

Back in Volume 3, Miyagi wanted to pierce Sendai's ear.

Sendai refused.

The reason was clear:

temporary marks disappear.

A piercing persists.

At that point, permanence crossed the line.

Volume 5 reversed the direction: Miyagi asked Sendai to pierce **her**.

Volume 7 finally completes the circuit.

Sendai gives Miyagi a piercer as a birthday present and grants her permission to pierce **Sendai**.

This is not a minor fashion milestone.

It is the resolution of a motif stretching across four volumes.

Earlier Miyagi wanted:

> permanent evidence that I acted upon your body.

Now Sendai voluntarily says:

> **you may leave that permanent evidence.**

The power structure has changed fundamentally.

The mark is no longer commanded.

It is offered.

---

# XXV. The piercing becomes a promise rather than a brand

Sendai explains that she wants the promise of next year's birthday left somewhere Miyagi can always see.

That turns the body into a memory device again.

But the meaning differs from early marking.

Early Miyagi's marks often said:

> you were mine for this period of time.

This piercing says:

> **I intend to return next year.**

That's more future-oriented and more reciprocal.

Miyagi is still cautious.

She will accept:

> next year.

She will not comfortably accept:

> every birthday forever.

This continues her temporal development in believable increments.

Graduation → four years → next year → tomorrow.

Miyagi does not leap to eternity.

She extends the trusted future **one unit at a time**.

---

# XXVI. The piercing scene also gives us a valuable consent contrast

This matters because of the earlier chapter.

Before piercing Sendai, Miyagi asks:

> 「いい？」

Is it okay?

Sendai answers:

> 「いいよ」

Yes.

Then Miyagi proceeds.

The scene's simplicity is conspicuous after the earlier struggle.

No elaborate justification.

No need to infer what the other's body “really means.”

No demand for stronger reasons.

A potentially painful and permanent bodily alteration is ethically simpler than some of their sexual interactions because:

> **the permission is explicit.**

This is another reason sexual explicitness itself is not where the mature thematic material lies.

The interesting issue is what the text understands about **agency**.

---

# XXVII. Miyagi's first uncomplicated compliment is tiny and important

Afterward Miyagi looks at Sendai and says:

> 「……ピアス、似合ってる」

The piercings suit her.

That's almost absurdly mild compared with what they have done together.

But direct positive appraisal remains one of Miyagi's weakest capacities.

She cannot easily say:

> you look good.

Because praise reveals orientation.

It says:

> I looked at you because looking at you mattered to me.

So this ordinary compliment belongs to the same developmental family as:

> the aquarium was fun.

The series is slowly teaching Miyagi to verbalize **positive evaluation** rather than communicating primarily through demand, irritation, or material trace.

---

# XXVIII. Sendai's second birthday gift exposes another form of care: adaptation

Sendai gives Miyagi a lip product chosen specifically around Miyagi's dislike of makeup.

She researched something Miyagi might actually tolerate.

Again, care appears as:

> I know what you dislike, so I won't simply give you what I would prefer you to become.

That is a useful contrast with Sendai's less healthy moments.

The same Sendai who sometimes pushes bodily boundaries can also be extraordinarily attentive to preference when she is not being overwhelmed by her own desire.

This is why reducing her to either “healthy pursuer” or “coercive partner” fails.

She is both:

-   perceptive enough to accommodate Miyagi beautifully;

-   and confident enough in that perception to sometimes convince herself she knows better than Miyagi's words.


Her greatest relational strength and greatest ethical vulnerability come from the same source:

> **Sendai is very good at reading people.**

The adult task is learning that reading someone does not give you jurisdiction over them.

---

# XXIX. Sendai reaches the volume's other major adult insight: love can threaten the self

Chapter 9 contains a passage I would now rank among the most important in the university arc.

Sendai looks at the piercings Miyagi gave her.

She realizes how much she wants Miyagi's attention.

She briefly thinks that if offering more of herself could pull Miyagi closer, she would give almost anything.

Then she recognizes the thought as unhealthy.

And finally:

> 「私は宮城に侵食されすぎている。」

Miyagi has invaded/eroded too much of her.

Sendai wonders what happens if Miyagi comes to occupy **all of her**.

Where would Sendai herself remain?

This is extremely important.

Until now, Sendai has often responded to dependency by wanting **more dependency**.

If she is too attached to Miyagi, she wants Miyagi equally attached to her.

Volume 7 finally introduces another possibility:

> **mutual engulfment might not be the same thing as intimacy.**

That is a substantially adult relationship question.

Love does not mature simply by increasing in magnitude.

There has to remain:

> a Miyagi who is Miyagi;

and:

> a Sendai who is Sendai.

Otherwise there are not two people left to choose one another.

---

# XXX. This gives new meaning to Sendai's work

The short-term job issue at the end of the volume is therefore not merely another jealousy scenario.

Sendai wants income.

She wants savings.

She wants to be able to remain in the area after university.

Most importantly, she does not want economic necessity to force her back into her family home.

So work represents **adult autonomy**.

Miyagi dislikes it.

Any external job creates:

-   time Miyagi does not control;

-   colleagues she does not know;

-   experiences from which she is absent.


That is exactly what makes it necessary.

If Sendai becomes financially incapable of leaving Miyagi, Miyagi may gain security.

But the relationship would lose something more important:

> **the meaning of Sendai choosing to return.**

Sendai's independence preserves the voluntariness of the relationship.

---

# XXXI. Their negotiation over the job is messy but meaningfully adult

Sendai does not simply obey Miyagi.

She also does not ignore her.

She asks what Miyagi thinks.

Miyagi essentially says:

> don't take the job.

Sendai refuses to grant Miyagi a veto.

That's healthy.

But Sendai still wants Miyagi to say that taking the job is okay.

That's psychologically revealing.

She wants:

> **autonomy plus relational blessing.**

Miyagi then does what Miyagi always does and converts the disagreement into exchange: if Sendai answers highly embarrassing personal questions, Miyagi will stop asking her to reject the job.

So their old contractual grammar survives.

But compare this with Volume 1.

Then:

> money bought obedience.

Now:

> uncomfortable disclosure helps Miyagi tolerate Sendai making an independent adult decision.

Still weird.

Still somewhat manipulative.

Much more equal.

And, crucially:

> Sendai works anyway.

Miyagi is slowly having to learn that loving someone includes living with choices she would not personally make for them.

---

# XXXII. The relationship's “secret” has changed categories

The Kakuyomu two-part **「宇都宮舞香の夏休み」** is particularly useful here.

Maika is preparing to return to her family home for summer.

Miyagi refuses to return to hers.

Maika presses a little on Ami's behalf, but Miyagi remains firm. She would rather stay where she is. She even starts giving Maika practical household reminders—locks, garbage—the same sort of things Maika's mother has just told her. Shuukura - LN07 Release SS - 宇都…

That is quietly striking.

Miyagi is acting like someone who has acquired a **household consciousness**.

And the place she intends to remain is not the apartment associated with her father's absence.

It is the home she shares with Sendai.

The story no longer has to announce:

> this new apartment has become home.

Miyagi's holiday behavior says it.

---

# XXXIII. Maika and Ami now know the impossible fact

When Maika returns home and meets Ami, the old trio's comic rhythm resumes immediately.

Then they discuss Miyagi.

Ami knows:

> Miyagi and Sendai live together.

This is explicitly no longer a secret because Miyagi herself told her. Shuukura - LN07 Release SS - 宇都…

Ami still finds it almost impossible to reconcile with the social model she remembers from school.

That is a lovely endpoint to the outside-observer arc we've been following since Volume 1.

Originally:

> Miyagi and Sendai having any meaningful connection seemed implausible.

Then:

> perhaps they secretly know each other.

Then:

> perhaps Miyagi has a secret romance.

Then:

> perhaps Miyagi and Sendai are secretly friends.

Now:

> **they openly live together, and that still sounds unbelievable.**

The secret has narrowed.

It is no longer:

> Are Miyagi and Sendai connected?

It is:

> **What, exactly, does their connection mean?**

---

# XXXIV. Maika has become an authorized witness rather than a detective

Maika tells Ami:

> 「志緒理、仙台さんとすごく仲良くて、二人を見たら驚くよ」

Shiori and Sendai are extremely close; seeing them together would surprise you. Shuukura - LN07 Release SS - 宇都…

That sentence marks a major evolution in Maika's narrative function.

She spent the high-school arc outside the relationship, carefully resisting conclusions.

Now she has entered their home.

She has interacted with Sendai.

She has seen how they behave.

She can testify that the relationship is real.

She still does not know its full nature.

But she is no longer reconstructing it from suspicious fragments.

She has become a **social witness to the household**.

That means Miyagi and Sendai's relationship is slowly entering the ordinary world.

Not through confession.

Through exposure.

---

# XXXV. Miyagi not returning home is more important than the SS makes it sound

Ami and Maika understandably want the old trio together again.

Miyagi chooses not to go.

This shouldn't be read as her abandoning her friends.

The SS makes clear that their friendship persists.

What has changed is the location around which Miyagi organizes **home**.

Maika goes home to her family.

Ami waits there.

Miyagi stays with Sendai.

That is a subtle adult transition.

Childhood and high-school friendships remain important.

But holiday return no longer automatically outranks the household Miyagi is building in the present.

Her life has acquired another center of gravity.

---

# XXXVI. The BOOK☆WALKER SS reduces romance to walking speed

**「私たちが大学へ向かう朝」** may be even better than the Volume 6 supermarket bonus as a miniature representation of the university arc.

Miyagi has the lip product Sendai gave her in her bag.

Naturally, she insists this means nothing.

Sendai says they should leave for university together.

Miyagi says no.

She walks outside.

Then she stops five steps away.

She has no obligation to wait.

She manufactures multiple reasons why she should continue walking.

Her feet don't cooperate.

Sendai emerges.

Miyagi says:

> she wasn't waiting.

Of course.

But she was.

The contract once needed ¥5,000 to guarantee that one girl would wait for another.

Now Miyagi waits **five steps outside the front door because Sendai said she was coming**.

That's a tiny but profound transformation.

---

# XXXVII. Waiting has changed meaning

For young Miyagi, waiting meant:

> the promised person hasn't returned yet.

That is the birthday cake.

For high-school Miyagi, waiting meant:

> eventual abandonment may be approaching.

For university Miyagi, waiting can increasingly mean:

> **she said she's coming, so I'll remain here for a few minutes.**

Waiting becomes possible when return becomes credible.

That is a far more psychologically important development than most dramatic romantic milestones.

---

# XXXVIII. Sendai then deliberately makes the journey inefficient

On their walk, Sendai proposes searching for Mike.

She knows the cat is unlikely to be there.

The cat is an excuse.

Finally she admits what she actually means:

> 「もう少しゆっくり歩こうってこと」

Let's walk a little more slowly.

This continues the Volume 6 BOOK☆WALKER bonus beautifully.

There:

> the checkout should be farther away so grocery shopping lasts longer.

Here:

> the station should take longer to reach.

Efficient movement is becoming romantically undesirable.

They no longer purchase time.

They **waste time on purpose**.

That's probably the cleanest possible symbol of how far they've moved from the ¥5,000 contract.

---

# XXXIX. Miyagi does not slow down—then notices when Sendai does

Sendai asks whether they can commute together again.

Miyagi refuses.

Sendai slows her pace.

Miyagi, now slightly ahead, turns around:

> 「仙台さん、遅刻する」

You'll be late.

This is pure *Shuukura*.

Miyagi says:

> I am not waiting for you.

Then:

> why are you falling behind?

She does not yet possess the direct grammar:

> walk beside me.

Her attention says it anyway.

The SS therefore takes one of the series' largest themes—continuity—and reduces it to two women trying to match walking speed.

Which is also exactly what Miyagi's major Volume 7 confession is about:

> **Sendai is walking too fast.**

The metaphor in the climax and the literal action in the bonus reinforce one another extremely well.

---

# XL. Volume 7's governing metaphor is pace, not possession

This is the major revision I would now make to the series thesis.

Possession remains important.

Marks remain important.

Jealousy remains intense.

But Volume 7 increasingly conceptualizes the relationship through **movement**.

Miyagi's problem is not simply:

> I refuse to change.

It is:

> **I cannot change at your speed.**

Sendai's problem is not simply:

> Miyagi won't give me what I want.

It is:

> **I am struggling to slow myself enough that Miyagi can keep walking beside me.**

And Sendai has her own opposite fear:

> if I orient everything toward Miyagi, do I cease walking as myself?

Those are more sophisticated questions than the earlier buyer/bought dynamic.

The relationship has developed enough that the next danger isn't only separation.

It is **misaligned movement**.

---

# XLI. The most mature theme in Volume 7 is individuation

This connects directly to your point about “maturity.”

Volume 7 has substantial sexual content.

I don't think that is what makes it thematically adult.

The adult theme is something more like:

> **How do two people become increasingly important to each other without collapsing the distinction between love and self-erasure?**

Sendai's fear that Miyagi is occupying too much of her internal world is crucial.

Miyagi has the inverse temptation:

> if Sendai's outside life is frightening, reduce Sendai's outside life.

The healthier path requires a difficult double movement.

Miyagi must learn:

> Sendai can have work, friends, university, memories and parts of herself I cannot access—and still come home.

Sendai must learn:

> Miyagi can move more slowly, withhold information, maintain boundaries and remain partly unknowable—and still love me.

In that sense, Volume 7 moves beyond:

> How do we get closer?

toward:

> **How do we remain two people while close?**

That is the more adult question.

# XLIV. Lynn's Miyagi should not become “dere”; she should become less armored

Haneda's comment that Miyagi is somewhat softer is almost the ideal vocal direction for this stage.

If Lynn suddenly played Miyagi as openly affectionate, the performance would outrun the prose.

Volume 7 Miyagi still cannot say:

> Sendai is special to me.

She still cannot call her Hazuki.

She still hides presents.

She still insists she wasn't waiting.

But compare the emotional function of her resistance.

Early Miyagi's harshness often creates distance.

Volume 7 Miyagi's harshness often occurs **while maintaining proximity**.

> Don't do that. Stay here.

> I'm not waiting. Hurry up.

> We're still roommates. Don't go away.

The same rough surface increasingly encloses attachment rather than exclusion.

A slightly softened delivery is therefore not merely “cute acting.”

It is a vocal representation of a defense mechanism becoming less necessary.

# XLV. Ichinose's sleepy Sendai represents an equally important development

Sendai was originally introduced through an image of social competence:

A highly groggy Sendai lying around in Miyagi's bed is almost the inverse of that public persona.

Not:

Cohabitation gives Miyagi access to a version of Sendai who is:

-   sleepy;

-   lazy;

-   not socially optimized;

-   occupying Miyagi's bed;

-   embedded in ordinary morning routine.


That is perfectly consistent with Miyagi's long-standing desire for the Sendai other people do not receive.

Except now the exclusivity isn't manufactured through humiliating commands.

It emerges naturally from living together.

# XLIX. Updated Miyagi after Volume 7

Miyagi has changed more than she is capable of admitting.

She can now:

-   ask for Sendai's hand;

-   initiate physical affection without a transaction;

-   tell Sendai an outing was fun;

-   prepare a birthday;

-   allow Sendai into her private bedroom;

-   tell Ami about their cohabitation;

-   actively choose the shared apartment over returning to her childhood home;

-   say that she wants Sendai to remain beside her even after setting a boundary;

-   participate in constructing another person's birthday memories;

-   accept promises extending into next year;

-   permanently alter Sendai's body with explicit permission.


But her deepest development is temporal.

She no longer primarily wants:

> a controlled ending.

She increasingly wants:

> **another tomorrow.**

Her remaining defense is “roommate.”

Yet even that has changed.

Earlier:

> roommate means this is all we are.

Now:

> **まだルームメイト**

means:

> this is what I can manage **right now**.

That “right now” contains an enormous amount of future possibility.

# L. Updated Sendai after Volume 7

Sendai is further ahead in emotional self-recognition and still behind in an important area of relational ethics.

She knows she loves Miyagi.

She wants the relationship to change.

She wants a future beyond the four-year lease.

She wants next birthdays, future jewelry, shared outings and continued cohabitation after university.

She is also becoming more adult in concrete ways:

-   earning money;

-   planning post-university independence;

-   consulting Miyagi about choices without granting Miyagi total control;

-   thinking about how her childhood family history affects her;

-   recognizing that she may be orienting too much of herself around Miyagi.


That last development is crucial.

For perhaps the first time, Sendai does not respond to excessive dependency simply by demanding matching dependency.

She becomes afraid of **losing Sendai Hazuki inside her love for Miyagi Shiori**.

But her central ethical weakness remains:

> she still sometimes treats her superior understanding of Miyagi's attraction as more trustworthy than Miyagi's immediate verbal boundary.

She recognizes more of the problem in Volume 7.

She has not solved it.

Her next developmental step needs to be not merely:

> wait longer.

It has to be:

> **accept that Miyagi does not owe me a persuasive case for stopping.**

# LI. The volume's three great movements fit together unusually well

Volume 7 can be understood through three ideas.

First:

> **No reason.**

Miyagi and Sendai increasingly allow desire to exist without artificial cause.

Second:

> **Not yet.**

Miyagi learns that refusing the current step does not require refusing the relationship.

Third:

> **Next time.**

Birthdays, aquariums, walks, promises and mornings all begin generating future recurrence.

Taken together, they produce a much more sophisticated relational grammar than the ¥5,000 arrangement:

> I want this because I want it.
> You may say no because you don't want it.
> Your no does not mean you are leaving me.
> My desire does not mean I am entitled to move you faster.
> We can remain here today and still imagine another tomorrow.

They don't consistently live up to that grammar yet.

But they can finally articulate parts of it.

# LII. Revised series thesis through Volume 7

After Volume 6, I described *Shuukura* as gradually removing every external explanation for why Miyagi and Sendai choose one another.

Volume 7 sharpens that considerably.

The series is not merely moving:

> **contract → free choice.**

It is moving toward something harder:

> **choice without possession.**

Free desire requires two autonomous persons.

That means Sendai has to remain capable of leaving the apartment for work, university and relationships outside Miyagi.

Miyagi has to remain capable of saying no.

Sendai has to remain Sendai rather than become a personality organized entirely around Miyagi.

Miyagi has to accept that her slower pace need not be an accusation against Sendai.

And both have to learn that continued closeness is meaningful **because separation remains possible**.

This is where Volume 7 becomes considerably more thematically adult than its sexual content alone would suggest.

Its most mature problem isn't:

> two women are having increasingly explicit sex.

It is:

> **Two young adults love each other enough that each is tempted to turn intimacy into certainty, and must learn that a durable relationship requires preserving precisely the autonomy that makes certainty impossible.**

Miyagi wants proof Sendai cannot disappear.

Sendai wants proof Miyagi cannot reject her.

Neither can ethically obtain that proof.

What they can obtain instead is:

> Sendai goes out and comes home.

> Miyagi says stop and still asks Sendai to stay.

> Sendai makes a promise and fulfills it next year.

> Miyagi waits outside the door.

> Sendai slows her walking pace.

That is a much more adult model of attachment than “complete access.”

It is trust built from **repeatable acts under conditions of continued freedom**.

And that is why I think the decisive line of Volume 7 is not a sexual declaration and not even Sendai's internal acknowledgment that she loves Miyagi.

It is Miyagi's:

> **「……まだルームメイトでいてよ」**

Because contained inside that **まだ** is something Miyagi has almost never been able to grant another person:

not a permanent answer,

but the possibility that today's answer may change without today's relationship having to die first.

That is where I would leave the series before Volume 8.
