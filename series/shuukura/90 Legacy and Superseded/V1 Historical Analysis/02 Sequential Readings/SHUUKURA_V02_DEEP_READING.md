# SHUUKURA V02 Deep Reading — Transcript Extraction

# 『週に一度クラスメイトを買う話』Volume 2

## Summer Vacation, Failed Categories, and the Increasing Weight of ¥5,000

### Scope

I am using the main-series Japanese Volume 2 EPUB rather than the redundant alternate packaging. Your manifest identifies the June 20, 2023 Volume 2 as the main-series copy and preserves the alternate separately. MANIFEST

This volume is especially useful as an LN text because Haneda states in the afterword that the commercial edition again contains **加筆修正** plus newly written material. Both **幕間「雨の日の宮城が私にしたこと」** and **番外編「仙台さんの放課後が五千円札になるまで」** were written specifically for the book. Haneda explicitly describes the interlude as Sendai's perspective on Chapter 3's rain sequence and recommends comparing the two viewpoints.

I am keeping the analysis bounded to Volume 2 and what we learned from Volume 1 plus 「宇都宮舞香は思考する」. I am **not yet incorporating** the Volume 2 commemorative SS 「宇都宮舞香の放課後」; that remains the next text in the sequence. Shuukura - LN02 Release SS - 宇都…

---

# I. Central Thesis: Volume 1 Created the Contract; Volume 2 Shows the Contract Becoming Inadequate

Volume 1 asked how two girls could become intimate without admitting they were voluntarily choosing intimacy.

The answer was ¥5,000.

Volume 2 asks what happens after the relationship has grown too large to fit inside that explanation.

The answer is:

> **They invent more explanations.**

Summer vacation is the perfect setting for this development because it removes the institutional machinery that had sustained their original arrangement.

During school:

> Miyagi calls after school.
> Sendai comes.
> Miyagi gives her ¥5,000.
> Miyagi gives orders.
> Sendai leaves.

But their original rules say that they do **not** meet during holidays.

That should make summer easy.

They should simply stop seeing each other.

Instead, Sendai creates another contractual fiction:

> She will become Miyagi's private tutor.
> Three days a week.
> ¥5,000.
> Studying rather than commands.

This is one of the most revealing developments yet.

By this point the contract is no longer primarily something imposed by Miyagi upon Sendai.

**Sendai herself now invents contracts to ensure that she can continue seeing Miyagi.**

And Miyagi accepts.

Volume 2 therefore moves the relationship through another sequence:

**purchased intimacy → voluntarily renewed intimacy → “friendship” experiment → explicitly physical attraction → intimacy without payment → retreat into a new time-limited contract.**

The ¥5,000 does not disappear.

Its meaning changes.

In Volume 1, money gave them permission to approach one another.

In Volume 2, money increasingly gives them **permission not to admit that permission is no longer necessary**.

---

# II. The Most Important Sentence About the Money Changes

Sendai eventually thinks:

> 「宮城と親しくなればなるほど、五千円の重みが増していく。」

The closer she becomes to Miyagi, **the heavier the ¥5,000 becomes**.

That is almost a thesis statement for Volume 2.

Originally, ¥5,000 simplifies the relationship.

It tells Sendai:

> I am here because I was paid.

It tells Miyagi:

> She is here because I bought her time.

But intimacy contaminates the simplicity of exchange.

If Miyagi asks Sendai to read manga for ¥5,000, the transaction can remain absurd but conceptually clean.

If Miyagi pays Sendai ¥5,000 immediately before kissing her, the money suddenly produces an uncomfortable question:

> **What exactly has been purchased?**

Sendai increasingly resists that implication.

She does not want a kiss to acquire a monetary price because that makes something she increasingly wants voluntarily look transactional.

This culminates near the end of summer. Miyagi tries to reintroduce payment after moving toward another kiss. Sendai refuses the logic:

> It's already too late.

And Sendai kisses her **before** any additional ¥5,000 can authorize the act.

That moment is more structurally significant than the kiss itself.

For the first time, the central mechanism named in the title becomes visibly irrelevant to what is happening.

Miyagi herself recognizes something astonishing:

**a kiss without ¥5,000 feels natural.**

That is the contract's deepest defeat so far.

---

# III. Summer Vacation Functions as a Liminal Zone

Summer is not merely a convenient romantic setting.

It temporarily suspends the ordinary social order.

No daily classroom.

No routine “after school.”

No requirement that Miyagi and Sendai exist in adjacent but separate school worlds.

Most importantly:

> **the original arrangement does not technically cover this time.**

Instead of accepting that limit, they begin creating exceptions.

First:

**family tutoring.**

Then:

**commands included in tutoring because Miyagi doesn't need much help anymore.**

Then:

**special permission.**

Then:

**“summer is different.”**

Then:

**friend-play.**

Then:

**¥5,000 as payment for physical closeness.**

Then eventually:

**no payment at all.**

Every boundary is preserved rhetorically slightly longer than it survives behaviorally.

That makes summer an experiment:

> What happens to Miyagi and Sendai if the external rules organizing their relationship disappear?

The answer is almost disastrous.

They become considerably closer.

---

# IV. Sendai's Tutoring Proposal Is One of Her Clearest Acts of Pursuit

This deserves emphasis because it strongly confirms our earlier interpretation of Sendai.

At the end of the rain sequence, she essentially tells Miyagi:

> **Hire me.**

The proposed arrangement makes practical sense. Miyagi needs to study. Sendai is academically capable.

But Sendai's later narration strips away the innocence.

She has become too accustomed to seeing Miyagi.

The idea of spending summer without her creates discomfort.

So Sendai constructs a rational, productive, socially comprehensible reason to continue entering Miyagi's apartment.

This is classic Sendai:

> **desire → reasonable institutional explanation → action**

Miyagi generally hides desire behind commands.

Sendai hides desire behind **logistics**.

The important parallel is becoming clearer:

**Miyagi:** “I'm not asking you to come because I miss you. I paid you.”

**Sendai:** “I'm not arranging three meetings every week because I want to see you. I'm tutoring you.”

They are doing precisely the same emotional maneuver in different dialects.

---

# V. Volume 2 Sharply Deepens Our Understanding of Sendai's Home

Volume 1 established that Sendai's polished social competence involves labor.

Volume 2 gives us a much clearer reason why.

When Miyagi finally enters Sendai's home, the environment she discovers does not resemble the comfortable family situation she had assumed.

Sendai's mother offers little warmth or interest.

There is an older, high-achieving sister.

Sendai's later internal narration connects her own intense fixation on Miyagi to an earlier period when her parents began visibly privileging that sister and Sendai became preoccupied with her.

That does **not** mean Miyagi is merely a substitute sister; that would flatten a specifically romantic and increasingly physical attachment into a single childhood mechanism.

But the connection matters.

Sendai learned early that:

> attention can migrate elsewhere;
> approval can be conditional;
> a family role can be occupied less securely than it appears.

That makes her public adaptability more legible.

She has become extremely good at knowing:

-   what people expect;

-   how to remain pleasant;

-   how to preserve social position;

-   how not to make herself difficult.


And it helps explain why Miyagi's room is such a relief.

---

# VI. The Two Homes Now Form an Elegant Symmetry

The LN-original rain interlude contains one of the best contrasts in the series so far.

Sendai implicitly compares:

> **the home where nobody says おかえり**

with

> **the home where an unfriendly Miyagi nevertheless gives her barley tea.**

Miyagi's house is literally empty.

Sendai's house is emotionally empty in a different way.

### Miyagi's domestic wound

People are absent.

Her mother left.

Her father materially provides but is usually physically unavailable.

### Sendai's domestic wound

People exist but do not reliably produce belonging.

Her home contains family structure without much emotional welcome.

That distinction helps explain why they satisfy different needs in one another.

Miyagi wants somebody who **returns**.

Sendai wants somewhere she can **arrive**.

This is perhaps the cleanest formulation of their domestic compatibility so far:

> **Miyagi needs someone to come home to her.
> Sendai needs somewhere that feels like coming home.**

Neither consciously understands the relationship that way.

Their routines increasingly do.

---

# VII. The Rain Interlude Reveals Just How Voluntary Sendai's Presence Already Is

Chronologically, the interlude supplements Chapter 3, but Haneda places it much later in the book.

That placement works extremely well.

From Miyagi's perspective, Sendai appearing during terrible rain reinforces the increasingly comforting idea:

> no matter what I do, Sendai comes.

The later interlude tells us what was happening on the other side.

Sendai seriously considers not going.

She has an umbrella, but the weather is miserable.

She can simply return home.

Instead she walks through the rain to Miyagi.

She admits she does not understand why she is doing this.

And the choice is framed against the relative emotional comfort of the two homes.

This retroactively changes the earlier scene:

> Miyagi thinks the contract guarantees Sendai's arrival.
> The reader learns that **Sendai chose Miyagi over home.**

The money has considerably less causal force than Miyagi believes.

---

# VIII. “Correct” and “Incorrect” Become a Failed Moral Vocabulary

The rain sequence repeatedly finds Sendai trying to classify behavior as **正しい** or not.

Wet clothes should be changed.

That is reasonable.

Miyagi helping her change clothes can therefore be rationalized as reasonable.

Then physical contact goes beyond what is necessary.

The rationale collapses.

Sendai knows it.

Miyagi knows it.

Neither immediately stops.

This is representative of the whole volume.

The girls want binary categories:

-   correct / incorrect;

-   command / voluntary;

-   friend / not friend;

-   paid / unpaid;

-   rule-following / rule-breaking;

-   ordinary / special.


But their relationship repeatedly produces states that cannot be cleanly classified.

Sendai eventually articulates exactly this in Chapter 8: some things cannot be sorted neatly into the correct box, and attempting to force their relationship into a category feels as though it might destroy it.

This is the philosophical development of Volume 2.

Volume 1 asked:

> What are we?

Volume 2 increasingly answers:

> **The available categories are inadequate, so don't ask.**

That solves nothing.

It lets them continue.

---

# IX. The “Friendship Experiment” Is a Genuine Failure

Volume 1 and 「宇都宮舞香は思考する」 established something important:

Miyagi is perfectly capable of ordinary friendship.

She has Maika and Ami.

Volume 2 therefore conducts a remarkably useful experiment:

> Can Miyagi and Sendai simply become friends?

They deliberately try.

They go out to see a movie.

This should be the least complicated possible solution to their relationship.

It fails.

And the reason is not that they dislike spending ordinary time together.

They enjoy:

-   studying;

-   eating;

-   talking;

-   reading;

-   walking;

-   watching films;

-   being in the same room.


The problem is **how Sendai performs friendship**.

---

# X. Miyagi Hates “Friend-Sendai”

During their outing, Sendai activates the social mode that makes her successful everywhere else.

She becomes:

-   accommodating;

-   pleasant;

-   smiling;

-   attentive to Miyagi's preferences;

-   conflict-averse.


In other words:

**the public Sendai.**

Miyagi despises it.

She presses Sendai until Sendai finally gives an unpolished opinion of the movie rather than politely matching Miyagi.

Only then does Miyagi regain some sense that she is talking to **her Sendai**.

That formulation is possessive for a reason.

Miyagi doesn't want the version available to everyone.

She wants:

-   irritation;

-   criticism;

-   teasing;

-   impatience;

-   honest disagreement;

-   unfiltered reactions.


Volume 1 showed her wanting expressions no one else gets.

Volume 2 clarifies that she wants **a relational self that exists specifically with her**.

---

# XI. But We Should Not Simply Call Public Sendai “Fake”

There is an important counter-reading here.

Miyagi interprets the adaptive Sendai as false.

Sendai herself experiences public sociality as effortful and curated, which partly validates Miyagi.

But it would be too simple to conclude:

> irritated private Sendai = real Sendai
> pleasant public Sendai = fake Sendai.

People legitimately contain multiple context-dependent selves.

Sendai's kindness toward classmates is not necessarily fraudulent simply because it is managed.

Her ability to accommodate others is genuinely part of her personality.

Miyagi is therefore not discovering some objective “true Sendai.”

She is demanding **exclusive access to a less regulated Sendai**.

That's a slightly different claim.

And it reveals something potentially unhealthy in Miyagi's ideal of authenticity:

> She can treat ordinary social consideration almost as contamination because it makes her feel insufficiently special.

That possessiveness deserves scrutiny rather than romanticization.

---

# XII. Something Important Changes When Sendai Actually Gets Angry

Earlier Miyagi often wanted to provoke irritation because anger proved she could affect Sendai in a way others could not.

During the failed friendship experiment, that strategy becomes less satisfying.

Miyagi finally recovers the familiar, pricklier Sendai—

and discovers that genuine disapproval can **hurt**.

That is real emotional development.

Before:

> I want to make you angry because your anger belongs to me.

Now:

> I still want the unfiltered you, but I care whether that person likes me.

The second statement contains far greater vulnerability.

Miyagi's desired exclusivity is transitioning from:

**exclusive access to Sendai's emotions**

toward:

**a desire for positive emotional standing within Sendai.**

That is a subtle but major change.

---

# XIII. “Friend” Becomes Both Too Small and Too Large

Sendai's Chapter 8 narration contains an excellent formulation of their categorical problem.

Friendship feels simultaneously:

-   too close;

-   too distant;

-   too small;

-   too large.


That sounds contradictory because their relationship is contradictory.

“Friend” is too **large** because openly accepting friendship would bring voluntary relationship obligations that the contract was designed to avoid.

But “friend” is also too **small** because they are already:

-   unusually possessive;

-   physically attracted;

-   jealous;

-   domestically enmeshed;

-   emotionally dependent.


The word cannot comfortably contain both.

Hence **友だちごっこ**—“playing at being friends.”

It is not friendship that is artificial.

It is the attempt to reduce what they are becoming to friendship.

---

# XIV. Sendai's Sexual Attraction Becomes Explicit Rather Than Merely Inferable

Volume 1 gave us strong behavioral evidence.

Volume 2 removes most ambiguity.

Sendai consciously recognizes that her physical desire is directed toward Miyagi in a way she has not experienced with other people.

That is textually important.

But it still does **not** require us to assign her an abstract sexual identity the text has not assigned.

The strongest conclusion remains:

> **Sendai discovers the person before she discovers—or perhaps before she needs—a category for the attraction.**

Her problem is no longer:

> Do I want physical intimacy with Miyagi?

By the middle of Volume 2, she knows that she does.

Her problem becomes:

> What happens to the relationship if I act fully on that desire?

That is a much more sophisticated fear.

---

# XV. Sendai Is Still the More Natural Initiator—but Volume 2 Complicates the Formula

Our Volume 1 shorthand was:

> **Miyagi controls permission; Sendai controls momentum.**

That remains useful, but the division is breaking down.

Sendai continues to create forward motion:

-   inventing summer tutoring;

-   initiating uncommanded physical contact;

-   engineering situations that force Miyagi to respond;

-   using Miyagi's own command structure against her;

-   trying to deepen kisses;

-   and eventually refusing money before initiating intimacy.


But Miyagi is also changing.

There are now moments when **Miyagi herself initiates without being protected by a formal command**.

One of the most important occurs after Sendai has been hurt during a kiss. Miyagi approaches again and effectively asserts:

> **I will do it.**

That is a small linguistic revolution.

Miyagi does not merely command Sendai to act.

Miyagi **acts**.

So the emerging pattern is less asymmetrical:

> Miyagi increasingly creates voluntary openings.
> Sendai remains more willing to push those openings forward.

They are becoming mutually active.

That is precisely why their existing safety structure becomes more unstable.

---

# XVI. Commands Serve Sendai Too

One of Volume 2's cleverest developments is Sendai realizing that she is often **saved by being commanded**.

That sounds paradoxical.

Commands ostensibly reduce her agency.

Psychologically, they can relieve her of agency.

If Miyagi says:

> Don't come closer.

Sendai can stop without admitting that she was frightened to continue.

If Miyagi says:

> Stay there.

Sendai can obey instead of deciding whether she has the courage to close the distance.

If Miyagi orders some form of intimacy, Sendai can participate without being solely responsible for initiating it.

Sendai eventually recognizes the asymmetry:

> she repeatedly forces Miyagi into choices, yet happily uses Miyagi's commands as excuses to avoid choices herself.

This substantially improves the contract as a literary device.

It is not merely Miyagi's instrument of domination.

It becomes a **shared technology for distributing responsibility**.

Miyagi can say:

> You did it because I ordered you.

Sendai can say:

> I did it because you ordered me.

Both are protected from the more frightening statement:

> **We both chose this.**

---

# XVII. Consent Becomes More Explicit Precisely Because the Rules Are Breaking Down

Volume 2 contains several scenes where the ethical limits of their improvised arrangement become clearer.

The important point is not that everything they do is exemplary—it emphatically is not.

Both girls:

-   manipulate technicalities;

-   escalate impulsively;

-   provoke;

-   sometimes press past hesitation;

-   and use the strange contract to avoid direct negotiation.


Yet Volume 2 repeatedly distinguishes **continued participation from unlimited permission**.

During the rainy-day interaction, Sendai accepts part of Miyagi's behavior under a “special case” rationale but retains a boundary.

During a later restrained/eyes-covered encounter, Sendai eventually explicitly tells Miyagi to stop and characterizes further escalation as crossing the remaining rule.

Miyagi stops.

Elsewhere Miyagi similarly rejects particular escalation from Sendai.

The operative principle becoming visible is:

> **Permission for one act does not automatically authorize the next one.**

Their ethical vocabulary is still messy and immature, but the story itself recognizes specificity.

That matters because the original “you must obey” premise could otherwise imply blanket consent.

Volume 2 increasingly demonstrates that it does not.

---

# XVIII. The Blindfold/Restraint Sequence Is More Psychologically Interesting Than Erotically Important

The surface action makes it look like an escalation of the series' kinky premise.

Structurally, something else is happening.

When Sendai's vision is removed, she becomes acutely aware of Miyagi's presence and absence.

For a moment, darkness makes her feel as though she is alone.

Then Miyagi touches her, and that physical evidence becomes reassuring:

> Miyagi is still there.

That is a small enactment of the entire relationship.

Sendai's deeper problem is not really loss of sight.

It is uncertainty about **presence**.

Miyagi's contact reassures her, then becomes too intense, at which point Sendai imposes a limit.

So the sequence moves through:

**uncertainty → reassurance → heightened awareness → boundary**

rather than simply “Miyagi gains more control.”

U35's decision to illustrate this material reinforces its importance, but its psychological structure matters more than the sensational surface.

---

# XIX. The Visit to Sendai's Home Reverses the Direction of Vulnerability

For almost the entire series to this point, Miyagi's apartment has been the principal intimate territory.

Sendai knows:

-   what Miyagi eats;

-   where Miyagi sleeps;

-   what her room smells like;

-   what books she owns;

-   how empty the house is;

-   what she is like without Maika and Ami.


Miyagi knows much less about Sendai's private world.

When she demands to visit Sendai's house, that imbalance changes.

Sendai agrees.

That decision matters more than the command.

Miyagi sees:

-   the emotionally cold mother;

-   the existence and importance of the older sister;

-   Sendai's more serious room;

-   a home that does not match the polished school image.


Sendai even hides the piggy bank containing Miyagi's accumulated ¥5,000 before letting her enter.

That is an extraordinary little detail.

The most materially obvious record of their relationship is the thing Sendai cannot let Miyagi see.

So Sendai allows Miyagi into her house—

while concealing the archive proving just how much time they have already shared.

---

# XX. The Piggy Bank Has Become an Emotional Archive

Volume 1 established that Sendai does not spend Miyagi's ¥5,000 notes.

Volume 2 makes the implications stronger.

Money has ceased to be useful money.

It accumulates.

Each note corresponds to another meeting.

Therefore the piggy bank functions almost like a private calendar:

> ¥5,000
> ¥10,000
> ¥15,000
> …

Not simply increasing wealth.

Increasing **shared time**.

This becomes especially poignant because Miyagi hates marking emotional time.

She distrusts anniversaries and memorial labels.

Sendai has accidentally created a physical chronology anyway.

Miyagi refuses to put their relationship on a calendar.

Sendai stores it in cash.

---

# XXI. Miyagi's Fear of Commemorating Time Is One of Volume 2's Best Insights

Miyagi explicitly resists assigning special meaning to memories.

Why?

Because future loss can poison them retroactively.

If you tell yourself:

> This was an important day.

and the person eventually leaves, the date remains.

It can hurt every year.

This is clearly connected to her mother.

So Miyagi prefers unlabeled time.

No anniversaries.

No commemorative marks on the internal calendar.

No declaration that something is special.

This gives enormous thematic weight to **August 31** later in the volume.

---

# XXII. August 31 Is the Moment Miyagi Knowingly Risks Creating a Memory

The final day of summer carries an obvious temporal marker.

Miyagi knows it.

She explicitly worries that what happens could attach itself permanently to:

> **August 31**

and become almost like an anniversary.

This is precisely the kind of thing she earlier wanted to avoid.

Yet she continues.

That is significant growth.

Not complete growth—she still rationalizes it through accident, impulse, and circumstance—but she permits an experience while knowing it may become emotionally permanent.

The Miyagi who fears memories because they can later hurt her is beginning to accept:

> **something can matter even though it might someday become painful.**

That is perhaps the most important movement in her character so far.

---

# XXIII. The Near-Crossing of Their Final Physical Boundary Matters for Reasons Beyond Sexual Escalation

At the end of summer, the remaining prohibition nearly collapses.

The most analytically important fact is not how far they physically go.

It is that this happens **after the payment structure has fallen away**.

Sendai has explicitly rejected Miyagi's attempt to use money as authorization.

Their closeness becomes:

-   mutual;

-   voluntary;

-   reciprocal;

-   unscripted.


Miyagi even notices that the absence of ¥5,000 does not make the interaction feel wrong.

It makes it feel strangely natural.

That means the relationship has reached a point where its most consequential intimacy occurs **outside the title premise**.

The fiction has become unnecessary.

And that terrifies them.

---

# XXIV. The Intercom Is a Perfectly Timed External Alibi

Then the intercom rings.

From a purely mechanical plotting standpoint, this is an interruption that prevents the characters from crossing the final line.

But thematically it works because neither girl wants to make the decisive choice.

Sendai effectively gives Miyagi the decision:

> continue, or answer the door.

Miyagi recognizes what Sendai is doing and calls her a coward.

But Miyagi answers the door.

Which makes **both of them cowards** by the same standard.

The mundane outside world therefore provides another excuse:

> We stopped because somebody rang the intercom.

Not:

> I chose to stop.

Not:

> I wanted to continue.

Once again an external structure absorbs responsibility that neither girl wants.

The ¥5,000 has merely been replaced temporarily by a doorbell.

---

# XXV. Chapter 10 Explains Why They Needed to Stop

Sendai's post-summer reflection is unusually perceptive.

She does **not** take a moralistic position that physical intimacy is only permissible inside an officially defined romantic relationship.

She explicitly rejects that kind of rigid purity logic.

Her concern is relational.

She thinks that if they had gone further, they might no longer be sitting together doing homework afterward.

And she concludes, or at least strongly wants to believe:

> **this continuing ordinary time is worth more than a single completed physical encounter.**

This is one of the most mature ideas the novel has articulated.

The ultimate value in *Shuukura* is not consummation.

It is **continuity**.

Eating.

Studying.

Reading.

Fighting.

Cooking.

Returning.

Being there again next week.

This reframes their hesitation.

They are not merely frightened of sex.

They are frightened that one irreversible act might destroy the ordinary life that has quietly become more valuable than either intended.

---

# XXVI. Domesticity Again Outweighs the Sensational Premise

Volume 2 doubles down on something already evident in Volume 1.

Its emotional development happens through mundane household behavior.

Sendai cooks:

-   tamagoyaki;

-   French toast;

-   omurice.


She tutors Miyagi.

Miyagi changes the room temperature for Sendai.

Miyagi continues serving barley tea because she remembers Sendai dislikes carbonation.

Sendai spends time on Miyagi's bed.

They develop a larger study space.

They know each other's habits.

They organize their week around shared time.

The physical escalation attracts attention.

But the real transformation is:

> **Sendai is increasingly helping Miyagi live.**

Food is particularly important because Miyagi's baseline relationship with nourishment is almost purely functional.

Sendai introduces:

-   preparation;

-   preference;

-   experimentation;

-   shared taste.


She doesn't simply give Miyagi calories.

She expands Miyagi's sensory world.

---

# XXVII. French Toast Becomes Memory

The French-toast motif is especially elegant.

Sendai teaches Miyagi that something Miyagi assumed she wouldn't like can actually be delicious.

Later, while spending an ordinary day with Maika, Miyagi orders French toast herself.

Maika notices the change.

Miyagi cannot eat it without thinking about:

-   Sendai;

-   their cooking;

-   their kisses;

-   the physical aftermath of that afternoon.


The significance is broader than food.

Sendai has begun modifying Miyagi's **preferences**.

That means Sendai now persists even when physically absent.

Volume 1's marks were bodily evidence that intimacy survives separation.

Volume 2 develops a softer version:

> **taste itself becomes evidence.**

Miyagi can go out with Maika and still encounter Sendai in what she orders.

The secret relationship is colonizing ordinary life.

---

# XXVIII. Maika Can Now Detect Effects Without Detecting the Cause

This is where 「宇都宮舞香は思考する」 becomes even more useful in retrospect.

Maika knows Miyagi well.

By Volume 2 she notices:

-   changed food preferences;

-   unusually completed homework;

-   reduced availability;

-   vagueness about how Miyagi has spent summer.


Miyagi's summer is almost entirely shaped by Sendai, yet she describes it as:

> “various things at home.”

That is technically close to true while concealing almost everything important.

Their relationship is therefore increasingly impossible to isolate.

Maika doesn't know Sendai is the cause.

But she sees:

> **Shiori has changed.**

The same is true on Sendai's side. Hamina notices that Sendai is less available.

So both ordinary friend groups begin experiencing the negative space created by the secret relationship.

---

# XXIX. Sendai's Jealousy of Maika Is More Interesting Than Simple Romantic Rivalry

Sendai becomes irritated by Maika in ways she does not fully understand.

But the key isn't that she thinks Maika will romantically “steal” Miyagi.

She envies what Maika possesses structurally:

> **uncomplicated legitimacy.**

Maika can:

-   call Miyagi Shiori;

-   ask her to go somewhere;

-   touch her casually;

-   spend time publicly with her;

-   occupy the recognized category of close friend.


None of this requires money.

None of it requires secrecy.

None of it requires rationalization.

Sendai can do things with Miyagi that are much more intimate physically.

Yet she lacks something Maika has effortlessly:

> an acknowledged place in Miyagi's ordinary life.

That is why Maika matters even without becoming a conventional love rival.

She represents a mode of belonging Sendai cannot buy, obey, or negotiate herself into.

---

# XXX. The Name Problem Therefore Becomes Even More Important

Sendai continues pushing toward **志緒理**.

Miyagi resists.

Miyagi retaliates at one point by experimentally calling Sendai **葉月**, then retreats.

Their first names function almost like contraband.

That is striking because their physical relationship is already considerably more advanced than their naming relationship.

The reason remains the same but becomes sharper in Volume 2:

> bodily intimacy can remain a private exception;
> first-name intimacy would resemble an ordinary recognized bond.

Names threaten to normalize what both girls depend upon keeping exceptional.

So even during their most intense encounters Miyagi rejects the suggestion:

> call me Shiori / call me Hazuki.

The body gets closer faster than language.

---

# XXXI. Sendai's Shirt Is Another Excellent Residual Object

Sendai still has Miyagi's shirt.

It has been washed.

Therefore Miyagi's smell is gone.

Sendai cannot comfortably:

-   throw it away;

-   wear it;

-   return it without significance.


The object has lost its immediate sensory trace and yet retained relational meaning.

That makes it an almost perfect counterpart to the bodily marks that fade.

The shirt is physically ordinary.

Its meaning is not.

Again the relationship creates residues that cannot be returned to neutral.

---

# XXXII. The Eraser Continues to Evolve

The eraser from Volume 1 remains in circulation.

By Chapter 10 they are literally passing it back and forth across the table while maintaining deliberate physical distance after the summer climax.

That is a lovely visual metaphor.

Previously, the eraser became Sendai's excuse to prevent Miyagi from erasing their relationship.

Now it travels:

> Miyagi → Sendai → Miyagi

across a space neither is willing to cross physically.

The relationship is still operating.

The object can traverse the distance before they can.

---

# XXXIII. Hair-Braiding Is the Perfect Final Intimate Act

After the summer escalation, Volume 2 does not end with another major kiss.

It ends with Sendai doing Miyagi's hair.

This is an excellent choice.

Hair-braiding is:

-   tactile;

-   prolonged;

-   intimate;

-   nonsexual;

-   practical;

-   gentle.


Sendai even selects a braid partly because it lets her continue touching Miyagi's hair longer.

That tells us where the relationship's center of gravity actually lies.

They have come dangerously close to crossing their final physical boundary.

What restores stability is not total retreat.

It is **a safer kind of closeness**.

U35 also gives this scene a full illustration near the end of the volume. Visually, the book deliberately closes its dramatic movement on sustained everyday touch rather than the earlier escalation.

That's important.

---

# XXXIV. The Ending Changes the Meaning of Graduation

Volume 1 introduced graduation largely through Sendai's defensive reasoning.

The relationship can be tolerated because it is:

> temporary.

There is a predetermined end.

That protects Sendai from having to imagine a future.

Volume 2 ends by making the deadline explicit.

Miyagi tells Sendai, essentially:

> Until graduation, when I call, come here as before.
> Until then, part of your after-school belongs to me.

Sendai calculates:

> about six months.

Then:

> 「半年か。……短いね」

**Half a year. That's short.**

Miyagi agrees.

This is a major emotional reversal.

The same deadline that made the relationship feel safe has begun to feel threatening.

### Earlier

Graduation means:

> I don't have to decide what comes afterward.

### Now

Graduation means:

> **There isn't enough time left.**

The wall protecting them from permanence has become a countdown to loss.

---

# XXXV. Miyagi's Final “Possession” Is Actually a Request for Continuity

Miyagi phrases the ending possessively:

> part of Sendai's after-school time is hers.

That sounds like the original buyer/master dynamic.

But the emotional content has changed almost completely.

At this point Miyagi does not primarily need confirmation that Sendai will obey strange commands.

She needs confirmation that:

> **Sendai will keep coming.**

The relationship has inverted the function of possession.

“Mine” increasingly means:

> don't disappear.

The command structure remains because Miyagi still cannot comfortably ask:

> Will you keep choosing me?

So she says:

> Your time belongs to me until graduation.

It is coercive language carrying the emotional content of a plea.

---

# XXXVI. The LN-Original Bonus Makes the ¥5,000 Motif Much Richer

The newly written **「仙台さんの放課後が五千円札になるまで」** initially sounds like comic trivia:

> Miyagi goes to the bank to get exact ¥5,000 notes.

It is much more valuable than that.

We learn that Miyagi specifically insists upon paying Sendai with:

> **one ¥5,000 note.**

Not:

-   five ¥1,000 notes;

-   a ¥10,000 note plus change;

-   whatever happens to be in her wallet.


Once her accidental supply runs out, Miyagi researches how to obtain more.

She learns about exchange machines.

She plans around banking hours.

She gives up time she could spend shopping with Maika and Ami.

She lies about meeting her father so nobody asks why she is going to a bank.

She performs actual administrative labor to preserve the ritual.

That completely undermines any claim that the money is emotionally neutral.

---

# XXXVII. The Form of the Money Matters More Than Its Economic Value

Ami jokes that ¥5,000 is an oddly intermediate amount.

Not cheap.

Not huge.

A somewhat awkward denomination.

Miyagi nevertheless thinks it is **exactly right** for purchasing Sendai's after-school.

That creates an interesting symbolic inference. I would label this an interpretation rather than an explicit author statement:

> **The ¥5,000 note is itself an intermediate object for an intermediate relationship.**

Not friendship.

Not lovers.

Not employment.

Not sex work.

Not merely classmates.

Not strangers.

Something suspended between available categories.

And the denomination cannot be broken apart without changing the ritual.

The fact that Miyagi needs specifically a **五千円札**, rather than merely ¥5,000 of value, confirms that we are dealing with a symbolic token.

---

# XXXVIII. The Bonus Also Creates Excellent Dramatic Irony

At the end of the bonus, Miyagi watches Sendai put the freshly exchanged note into her wallet and briefly wonders where it goes afterward.

We know.

Sendai saves the notes.

Miyagi doesn't.

So the money carries two private meanings neither girl has disclosed to the other.

### For Miyagi

It is painstakingly prepared proof that:

> I have the right to call you again.

### For Sendai

It becomes accumulated proof that:

> I have been here again and again.

The exact same object therefore encodes their characteristic defenses:

**Miyagi uses money prospectively—to guarantee return.**

**Sendai preserves money retrospectively—to preserve shared time.**

That is exceptionally neat characterization.

---

# XXXIX. Volume 2's Most Important Shift in Power

If we compare the relationship at the end of Volume 1 with the end of Volume 2, the original hierarchy has deteriorated dramatically.

The relationship is becoming more symmetrical.

That is not automatically healthier.

Symmetry means they can now hurt each other more effectively.

It also means they are increasingly relating as **two choosing subjects**, rather than buyer and purchased classmate.

---

# XL. Chapter-by-Chapter Emotional Architecture

The volume's structure is unusually coherent when viewed as a progression of failed containment.

**Chapter 1 — post-kiss restoration.**
Miyagi tries to restore hierarchy through commands and intensified bodily control. She experimentally uses “Hazuki,” then retreats. School-world leakage increases.

**Chapter 2 — Sendai's voluntary obedience.**
Restraint makes the formal power imbalance obvious, but Sendai internally insists that she participates specifically because it is Miyagi. The chapter then pivots into cooking, emphasizing domestic intimacy immediately after power play.

**Chapter 3 — rain and exception.**
Bad weather produces a “special case.” Miyagi and Sendai stretch rules under practical excuses. The exact ¥5,000 ritual becomes visible, and Sendai invents the summer tutoring arrangement.

**Chapter 4 — summer and Sendai's home.**
The relationship expands to three days a week. Miyagi enters Sendai's private domestic world. Sendai begins reversing the initiator/control structure.

**Chapter 5 — reciprocal vulnerability.**
They tacitly recognize one another's family wounds. Physical boundaries loosen further, while Sendai actively engineers escalation. Miyagi rearranges ordinary plans around Sendai.

**Chapter 6 — desire named internally.**
Sendai can no longer pretend Miyagi is merely interesting. Jealousy toward Maika emerges. French toast and kissing turn domestic cooperation into physical intimacy.

**Chapter 7 — friendship tested and rejected.**
The movie outing proves ordinary “friend-mode” cannot describe their relationship. Miyagi rejects public Sendai, then—crucially—begins asking for closeness without relying entirely on formal commands.

**Interlude — Sendai chooses Miyagi in the rain.**
Placed late but chronologically tied to Chapter 3, it confirms how voluntary Sendai's attachment already was and reframes Miyagi's apartment as refuge.

**Chapter 8 — ritualized summer intimacy.**
Money is increasingly uncomfortable but remains an alibi. Restraint/eyes-covered contact forces clearer boundaries. Both understand this cannot continue indefinitely.

**Chapter 9 — the contract briefly disappears.**
August 31 becomes a deliberately memorable date. Sendai refuses money as prerequisite, both act voluntarily, and they nearly cross their final boundary before an outside interruption gives them an exit.

**Chapter 10 — repair through distance and ordinary touch.**
They return to homework, sit apart, admit mutual distrust, then gradually close the distance through hair care. Miyagi formalizes the relationship through graduation; both discover six months feels short.

**Bonus — the machinery behind the excuse.**
Miyagi's deliberate currency exchange reveals how much effort goes into maintaining something she continues to call merely transactional.

The volume therefore starts with Miyagi trying to **restore** the original system and ends with both girls maintaining only its shell.

---

# XLI. Updated Miyagi Profile After Volume 2

Miyagi's central abandonment defense remains intact, but it is becoming easier for the reader—and occasionally Miyagi herself—to see through it.

She is now clearly:

**more dependent on Sendai's routine.** Summer without Sendai is sufficiently undesirable that she accepts a manufactured tutoring loophole.

**more jealous.** Her private knowledge of Sendai makes Sendai's public relationships increasingly irritating.

**more conscious of physical desire.** She can no longer plausibly describe every action as curiosity or control.

**more capable of voluntary initiative.** Some important acts now originate from Miyagi herself rather than commands directed outward.

**more vulnerable to Sendai's opinion.** Anger no longer merely proves exclusive access; it can hurt her.

**still terrified of memorializing attachment.** Yet August 31 shows that she can knowingly allow something to become meaningful.

Her core contradiction now looks like:

> **Miyagi wants Sendai to choose her freely, but still needs a structure that allows her to pretend the choice is compulsory.**

---

# XLII. Updated Sendai Profile After Volume 2

Sendai receives even more development.

She is now:

**the architect of continuation.** The tutoring arrangement exists because she creates it.

**more conscious of desire than Miyagi.** She explicitly recognizes attraction to Miyagi as unusually specific and powerful.

**increasingly jealous of legitimate intimacy.** Maika possesses ordinary access to Miyagi that Sendai lacks.

**more willing to challenge the financial structure.** She increasingly refuses to let ¥5,000 define kissing and other intimate acts.

**revealed as domestically vulnerable.** Her public social competence exists alongside an emotionally unrewarding home environment and a history of parental comparison.

**dependent upon commands in a way she understands better.** They can serve as emotional exits when direct choice becomes frightening.

**surprisingly conservative about continuity, though not morality.** She does not insist physical intimacy requires official dating; she worries that going too far could destroy the ordinary relationship she values.

Sendai's central contradiction has become:

> **She is the more natural pursuer, yet repeatedly wants Miyagi to make the final decision so that Sendai does not have to bear responsibility for changing the relationship.**

Her momentum is real.

So is her cowardice.

---

# XLIII. What Volume 2 Changes About Our Earlier Interpretation

The first volume allowed us to say:

> Miyagi uses money to possess without asking Sendai to choose her.
> Sendai accepts money to choose Miyagi without admitting she is choosing her.

Volume 2 strengthens that formulation, then begins breaking it.

By August:

**Miyagi knows the money is not enough.**

**Sendai knows the money is increasingly inappropriate.**

**Both know physical attraction exists.**

**Both know the other returns voluntarily.**

**Both know friendship is an inadequate category.**

**Both know the relationship could end at graduation.**

And yet they continue passing the ¥5,000.

Why?

Because by now the payment no longer primarily explains *why Sendai obeys*.

It explains something much simpler:

> **why they are allowed to keep seeing each other.**

The contract has evolved from a power structure into a continuity ritual.

---

# XLIV. Final Assessment

Volume 1 is about the accidental creation of a relationship.

Volume 2 is about the frightening realization that the relationship has acquired **independent momentum**.

Summer vacation removes the external schedule and should dissolve the arrangement.

Instead, Miyagi and Sendai reconstruct their lives so that it can continue.

They invent tutoring.

They rearrange plans.

They enter each other's homes.

They cook.

They learn each other's family wounds.

They try friendship and discover it is inadequate.

They become consciously physically attracted.

They briefly abandon the money entirely.

They nearly cross a boundary both have spent more than a year preserving.

Then, rather than separate, they sit down and do homework again.

That last fact may be more important than everything else.

The deepest desire of Volume 2 is not:

> I want to kiss you.

It is:

> **I want there to be a next time.**

And that changes the meaning of the final contract.

When Miyagi says part of Sendai's after-school belongs to her until graduation, “possession” is becoming another word for:

> **Please keep coming back.**

Sendai's answer—half a year is short—reveals that she now hears it that way too.

The ¥5,000 excuse survives.

Its innocence does not.
