# SHUUKURA V01 Deep Reading — Transcript Extraction

# 『週に一度クラスメイトを買う話』Volume 1

## The Five Thousand Yen as Contract, Alibi, and Substitute for Attachment

### Scope

I am treating the **Japanese light-novel Volume 1 as the primary text**, including the LN-original **幕間「まだ宮城が存在していなかった私のこと」** and **番外編「仙台さんはきっと私の名前を知らない」**. Haneda’s afterword explicitly says that the book revises and expands the web serialization, and that both the interlude and bonus chapter were newly written for the commercial edition. That makes them especially important for an LN-centered analysis rather than disposable appendices.

I will also keep the interpretation **spoiler-bounded to Volume 1**. I am not importing what we already know from later volumes or the web novel into conclusions the first volume has not yet earned.

Per our agreed sequence, after this the next text will be the Volume 1 Kakuyomu commemorative SS, **「宇都宮舞香は思考する」**, before we move to Volume 2. ShuukuraReadingOrder

---

# I. Central Thesis: The Novel Is Not Really About Buying a Person

The provocative premise suggests a story about economic dominance:

> Miyagi pays Sendai ¥5,000.
> Miyagi gets to give orders.
> Sendai obeys.

Volume 1 steadily dismantles that description.

By its conclusion, the ¥5,000 has ceased to function primarily as **payment**. It is a ritual that permits two girls who are extraordinarily bad at asking for emotional intimacy to spend time together without admitting that spending time together is what either one wants.

The subtitle is therefore almost diagnostic:

**言い訳の五千円 — the five thousand yen as an excuse.**

Miyagi's excuse is:

> I'm not asking Sendai to stay because I want her here.
> I bought her time.

Sendai's excuse is:

> I'm not coming because I want to see Miyagi.
> She paid me.

Both explanations become transparently false long before either girl is prepared to abandon them.

Volume 1 is consequently about **the failure of a contractual fiction to contain a voluntary relationship**.

And the transformation is remarkably clean:

**replaceable classmate → purchased classmate → habitual companion → private confidante → desired person.**

The opening Miyagi insists that there is no particular reason it has to be Sendai.

By the end, Sendai has skipped cram school, appeared at Miyagi's classroom, dragged her into an unused music room, forced her to stop running away, gone back to her apartment, and kissed her because she has finally admitted—at least to herself—that she specifically likes **Miyagi**.

Neither girl's original model survives the volume.

---

# II. The ¥5,000 Has a Deeper Symbolic Origin Than the Premise Initially Suggests

The LN-exclusive final story radically improves the symbolism of the money.

Chronologically, Miyagi does not originally decide:

> I want to purchase Sendai.

She notices Sendai at a bookstore, sees the usually polished girl unexpectedly flustered because she has forgotten her wallet, and impulsively pays for her purchase.

Sendai refuses to accept the favor.

Miyagi refuses to accept repayment.

Only after both girls prove incapable of tolerating an ordinary gift-debt relationship does Miyagi propose:

> Then work for the ¥5,000.

That matters enormously.

**The transaction is born because both girls are uncomfortable with ordinary reciprocity.**

A normal relationship would permit:

-   I helped you.

-   Thank you.

-   I'll pay you back sometime.

-   Don't worry about it.


They instead create a formal economy because a formal economy feels safer.

Even more interesting is Miyagi's reasoning in the LN-original chapter. She thinks about money as compensation for labor and connects the money she possesses to her father's work—to the enormous amount of time he spends away from home earning it.

That creates an extraordinary symbolic circuit:

**father's absent time → money → Miyagi's allowance → ¥5,000 → Sendai's purchased presence**

Miyagi's emotionally absent father provides her materially. She then uses a fragment of the money generated by that absence to obtain something money normally cannot buy cleanly:

**someone being there.**

This becomes still more elegant from Sendai's side. Sendai does not spend Miyagi's money. She puts the ¥5,000 notes into a piggy bank and eventually thinks of the accumulated money almost as the accumulated quantity of **time she has spent with Miyagi**.

So the symbolic circuit completes itself:

**time becomes money → money purchases time → money becomes an archive of shared time.**

I think this is one of Volume 1's most sophisticated motifs.

Calling the ¥5,000 simply “payment” misses what Haneda is doing with it.

---

# III. The Power Relationship: Miyagi Has Authority, Sendai Has Agency

Volume 1 strongly confirms something we were discussing before beginning the close read:

> **Miyagi possesses formal power.
> Sendai increasingly possesses practical initiative.**

That distinction is there almost immediately.

Miyagi controls:

-   when Sendai is invited;

-   access to her apartment;

-   the ¥5,000;

-   the formal command structure;

-   the initial rules;

-   and theoretically what happens during purchased time.


But Sendai's narration reveals something Miyagi does not fully understand:

**Sendai can simply refuse.**

She explicitly recognizes that she could disobey whenever she wanted. Her compliance is not produced by financial necessity. She does not particularly want the money. She keeps returning because she likes the room, likes the respite it gives her, and increasingly likes Miyagi herself.

This makes the title's “buying” deliberately unstable.

Miyagi believes the money is a chain.

Sendai knows the chain has no lock.

And Sendai eventually starts pulling on it herself.

### The inversion becomes explicit in the final chapters

By Chapters 8–10, Sendai is doing things that completely exceed the original paid-subordinate position:

-   calling Miyagi **志緒理** without permission;

-   licking Miyagi's fingers without being ordered;

-   becoming irritated when Hamina handles Miyagi's textbook;

-   wanting Miyagi to contact her;

-   appearing at Miyagi's apartment uninvited;

-   calling Miyagi for the first time;

-   skipping prep school;

-   showing up publicly at Miyagi's classroom;

-   physically taking Miyagi somewhere private to force a conversation;

-   demanding that Miyagi summon her;

-   encouraging Miyagi to formulate the kiss as a command;

-   initiating the actual kiss;

-   wanting a second kiss;

-   and attempting to deepen it.


By the ending, the nominally “bought” girl is propelling the relationship forward.

So I would refine our earlier formulation:

> **Miyagi controls permission. Sendai controls momentum.**

Miyagi is more naturally possessive.

Sendai is more naturally pursuing.

That is already firmly established by Volume 1.

---

# IV. The Most Dangerous Word in Volume 1 Is Not “Kiss.” It Is “Friend.”

One of the novel's most revealing inversions is that **ordinary friendship repeatedly frightens them more than eroticized behavior does**.

They can:

-   lick each other's skin;

-   bite each other;

-   put fingers into mouths;

-   exchange blood through licking;

-   leave marks;

-   search each other's bodies;

-   touch necks and lips;

-   sleep beside one another;

-   and eventually kiss.


Yet Miyagi fiercely resists:

> We're friends.

And she resists Sendai calling her **志緒理**.

Why?

Because all of the strange physical behavior can be quarantined inside the contract.

> It was an order.
> She paid me.
> That's what our arrangement permits.

But friendship cannot be explained by ¥5,000.

Neither can voluntary care.

This is why apparently innocuous acts repeatedly destabilize them.

### Chocolate

Sendai gives Miyagi Valentine's chocolate because she made some for friends.

Miyagi reacts badly to the implied category:

**friend.**

She would almost rather transform the chocolate into a command ritual—being fed, biting Sendai's fingers, licking them—than simply accept:

> Sendai voluntarily thought of me.

### Cooking

Sendai voluntarily buys ingredients and cooks karaage because Miyagi eats poorly.

Miyagi dislikes it.

Not because cooking itself is invasive, but because Sendai doing something kind **without being ordered** destroys the logic of the arrangement.

### Names

Sendai can permit Miyagi intimate access to her body before Miyagi permits Sendai intimate access to her **name**.

That sounds paradoxical until we distinguish physical intimacy from social recognition.

In Japanese address practice, **宮城** and **仙台さん** preserve a useful distance. **志緒理** would place Sendai alongside people such as Maika and Ami who occupy acknowledged interpersonal positions in Miyagi's life.

Miyagi cannot tolerate that classification.

Physicality can be denied afterward.

A name establishes a relationship.

### Food

By Chapter 10, Miyagi notices that Sendai missed lunch because she publicly chased Miyagi down. She gives her castella while insisting it is merely leftover food that would otherwise go to waste.

This is quintessential Miyagi:

> care, followed immediately by an alibi for caring.

The ¥5,000 arrangement operates the same way at larger scale.

---

# V. Miyagi Shiori: Control as a Defense Against Being Left

Miyagi initially seems like the stranger and more aggressive of the two. Volume 1 gradually reveals the emotional architecture beneath that behavior.

Her defining fear is not domination.

It is **abandonment**.

Her mother left.

Her father remains materially present but functionally absent. The apartment is full of evidence of provision and very little evidence of shared life. Miyagi eats alone. She cannot readily remember the last ordinary meal she had with her father.

Her response to abandonment is anticipatory:

> **Leave first.
> Create the rupture yourself.
> Never wait for someone who may not come.**

This makes the spring-break conflict one of the volume's most important sections.

Miyagi humiliates Sendai with the soda/popcorn incident. Superficially it is another escalation of the command game.

Her later narration reveals something much uglier and sadder:

**she is trying to make Sendai stop coming.**

The school year is ending. Their classes may change. Graduation is eventually coming.

Rather than discover that Sendai has voluntarily stopped choosing her, Miyagi tries to manufacture a reason why Sendai *cannot* come back.

That way the absence has an explanation.

And, crucially, Miyagi authored it.

Her logic resembles:

> If I make her hate me, then I won't have to wonder whether she simply stopped wanting me.

That is the emotional engine behind much of her cruelty.

### Miyagi's possession is a demand for certainty

Her desire to see expressions nobody else sees is especially important.

She resents the polished, socially successful “Sendai-san” everyone receives. She wants:

-   anger;

-   irritation;

-   discomfort;

-   surprise;

-   embarrassment;

-   anything genuine and specifically directed at **her**.


This begins even before the contract.

In the LN-exclusive origin story, Miyagi is fascinated by seeing Sendai flustered at the bookstore because it contradicts her school persona.

That directly anticipates Miyagi's later pleasure when she can make Sendai show a face “nobody else knows.”

What Miyagi wants is not merely sexual access.

She wants **exclusive access to the unperformed Sendai**.

Possession, for Miyagi, means:

> Show me something that belongs only to me.

---

# VI. Miyagi's Own Self-Conception Is Frequently Wrong

The alternating POV makes this easy to see.

Miyagi believes the social distance between them is enormous. In the LN-exclusive origin chapter, she assumes Sendai probably does not even know her name.

Then Miyagi impulsively gives her the ¥5,000.

Sendai responds by identifying her:

**宮城.**

That tiny moment matters.

Miyagi's internal social map says:

> Sendai exists in another world.
> I am invisible to someone like her.

The text immediately proves that map imperfect.

Similarly:

-   Miyagi says Sendai is replaceable.

-   She later wants specifically Sendai's voice.

-   She says they are not friends.

-   She gets jealous about Sendai's confession from another student.

-   She says ordinary acts mean nothing.

-   She automatically begins preparing two servings of dinner when Sendai is absent.

-   She says she can stop calling whenever she wants.

-   When Sendai might actually disappear, Miyagi becomes emotionally destabilized.


Her body and habits are usually several chapters ahead of her vocabulary.

---

# VII. Sendai Hazuki: Social Competence as Exhausting Performance

Sendai initially looks like Miyagi's opposite.

She is:

-   attractive;

-   academically capable;

-   socially successful;

-   teacher-approved;

-   surrounded by friends;

-   adept at reading rooms.


The interlude dismantles the idea that this means she is socially carefree.

Sendai sees her classroom almost as an **aquarium**.

She knows where everyone sits in the social ecosystem.

She recognizes that proximity to Hamina provides advantages.

She intentionally avoids behavior that will damage teachers' impressions of her.

She understands what she must watch, wear, say, and tolerate to maintain her position.

And she knows people call her **八方美人**—someone who adapts herself pleasantly toward everyone.

This isn't simply hypocrisy.

It's social labor.

Sendai's public success depends upon continuous calibration.

Miyagi's room becomes valuable because she can stop doing much of it there.

Miyagi is rude.

Miyagi is demanding.

Miyagi frequently annoys her.

But Sendai does not have to wonder:

> Am I performing correctly for Miyagi?

The contract paradoxically creates freedom.

Her role is predetermined:

> Come here. Take the ¥5,000. Obey the stated command.

Once those rules exist, Sendai can be irritated, teasing, argumentative, lazy, hungry, bored, or provocative.

She can become less **仙台葉月, socially optimized classmate**, and more simply herself.

---

# VIII. The Interlude Is a Crucial Control Experiment

The LN-original **「まだ宮城が存在していなかった私のこと」** is one of the most analytically useful pieces in the book.

It gives us Sendai immediately before Miyagi becomes part of her life.

Her conclusion is essentially:

> Different things aren't necessarily good.
> Sameness has value.
> I don't want a different everyday life.
> Probably. Surely. I think.

That final uncertainty is perfect Sendai.

She tells herself she wants:

**stable grayness.**

Not ecstasy.

Not catastrophe.

Not deep attachment.

Just a manageable life.

Then Miyagi appears.

By Chapter 10, the same Sendai who believes stability is preferable is:

-   breaking her own routines;

-   skipping prep school;

-   violating the public/private partition of the relationship;

-   forcing a confrontation;

-   and kissing Miyagi twice.


The interlude therefore makes Sendai's transformation measurable.

More importantly, it shows that **Sendai is every bit as threatened by attachment as Miyagi**, but her defensive strategy is different.

Miyagi says:

> I'll push you away before you leave.

Sendai says:

> This is temporary anyway. It ends at graduation.

Those are variations of the same defense.

Both girls attempt to control future loss by deciding in advance what the relationship is allowed to become.

---

# IX. “Until Graduation” Is Sendai's Version of the ¥5,000 Excuse

Sendai repeatedly calls the relationship **期間限定**—limited-time.

She tells herself that graduation supplies a natural ending.

This appears reasonable.

They're high-school classmates. University will change everything. They may move away.

But its psychological function is much more important.

If the relationship *necessarily* ends at graduation, Sendai never has to ask:

> Do I want Miyagi after graduation?

And she never has to risk asking Miyagi:

> Do you want me after graduation?

This is the Sendai equivalent of Miyagi's money.

Both are external structures used to avoid voluntary commitment:

**Miyagi:**
“I'm with you because I paid.”

**Sendai:**
“I'm with you only until graduation.”

Both statements let them enjoy attachment while pretending not to choose it.

Volume 1 already subjects both explanations to severe strain.

---

# X. The Contract Produces a Fascinating Consent Problem

I would resist describing this as either simply “healthy consensual power play” or simply “coercive.”

The text is more complicated than either.

Their initial rules establish real limits:

-   no violence;

-   no sex;

-   commands have defined spatial/temporal scope;

-   Sendai retains the practical ability to leave.


Yet neither girl has the emotional maturity or vocabulary of people engaged in a carefully negotiated adult D/s relationship.

They improvise.

And they frequently test loopholes.

### Miyagi's sleeping contact is treated differently by the text

When Sendai falls asleep in Miyagi's room, Miyagi touches her face, lips, neck, and eventually presses her lips to Sendai's neck.

Miyagi herself recognizes the moral distinction:

If Sendai were awake, Miyagi could issue a command and Sendai could react.

While asleep, **Sendai cannot stop her**.

That is significant. The narrative does not treat the contract as blanket consent.

Miyagi knows she crossed a different kind of boundary.

### Sendai also pushes boundaries

Sendai is hardly passive.

She repeatedly:

-   bites beyond what was ordered;

-   licks when not instructed;

-   refuses to stop immediately in some teasing encounters;

-   manipulates Miyagi toward giving particular commands.


And during the second kiss, Sendai escalates toward tongue contact. Miyagi stops her and explicitly objects that she did not consent to **that much**.

Sendai's first defense is essentially:

> A kiss is a kiss.

Miyagi rejects that.

Sendai stops.

That exchange is important because it shows how **specific consent** begins to supersede the simplistic command framework.

### The relationship is ethically messy

Neither girl is consistently exemplary.

They sometimes hurt one another.

They sometimes exploit technicalities.

They sometimes use irritation or bodily reaction as entertainment.

That's not a flaw in the characterization.

It is part of what Volume 1 is examining:

> Two lonely seventeen-year-olds have invented a contractual vocabulary for intimacy without actually understanding intimacy.

The rules protect them.

The rules also enable avoidance.

And when desire becomes more complicated than the rules, they repeatedly discover the contract cannot answer the question for them.

---

# XI. Why Sendai Is the Natural Physical Initiator

Volume 1 gives us far stronger textual evidence for our earlier hypothesis than I expected.

Miyagi often originates the *situation*:

-   issuing a provocative command;

-   touching;

-   leaving a mark;

-   inventing the eraser search;

-   commanding Sendai to close her eyes.


But Miyagi repeatedly approaches a threshold and then panics.

Sendai tends to do something different:

**once a threshold exists, she explores it.**

This pattern reaches its clearest form in Chapters 9–10.

### Chapter 9: Miyagi creates the possibility and retreats

Sendai confronts Miyagi about the sleeping kiss.

She asks whether Miyagi wants to touch her now.

Miyagi touches Sendai's lips.

Sendai licks the finger.

Sendai tells her to give an order.

Miyagi commands Sendai to close her eyes and nearly kisses her.

Then Miyagi thinks:

> What if kissing her makes her stop coming?

And throws Sendai out.

That is almost Miyagi's psychology in miniature:

**desire → possibility → fear of dependency → flight.**

### Chapter 10: Sendai comes back and finishes what Miyagi abandoned

Sendai refuses to accept the disappearance.

She goes to Miyagi's apartment.

Miyagi ignores her.

Sendai then crosses the boundary Miyagi most wanted preserved:

**school.**

She appears at Miyagi's classroom, publicly creates evidence that they know each other, pulls Miyagi into the music preparation room, demands an explanation, and forces another meeting.

Back at Miyagi's apartment, Sendai finally drops some of her own rationalization:

> She wants to kiss Miyagi and wants to know what will happen if she does.

Then she makes Miyagi put that desire into their familiar language:

> give the command.

Miyagi asks **Sendai** to do it.

And Sendai does.

Then Sendai wants to do it again.

Then she tries to deepen the second kiss.

This does not make Sendai “dominant” in a simple sexual-role sense.

It makes her **better able to convert desire into forward action**.

That's a more interesting trait.

---

# XII. The First Kiss Is Really the Collapse of the Original Contract

The kiss is obviously the volume's romantic culmination, and U35's illustration chooses it as one of the book's major visual moments.

But the deeper structural event occurs slightly earlier:

**Sendai refuses to let Miyagi end the relationship unilaterally.**

That is the real turning point.

Miyagi's original arrangement gives her extraordinary control over time:

> I call.
> You come.
> I pay.
> You leave.

When Miyagi stops calling, Sendai is theoretically supposed to disappear.

Instead:

> Sendai comes anyway.

That changes the ontology of the relationship.

She is no longer merely something Miyagi summons.

She is now an independent subject who can demand continuation.

The fascinating spatial line in Chapter 10 is Sendai's recognition that Miyagi's power has jurisdiction:

> Miyagi gets to command her **in Miyagi's home** after paying ¥5,000.

At school, Miyagi does not possess that authority.

Sendai exploits that fact to turn the power relation inside out.

The room is Miyagi's sovereign territory.

The school is not.

By invading Miyagi's school world, Sendai breaks the architecture that allowed Miyagi to compartmentalize her.

---

# XIII. Public World and Private World

The series' social setting is not decorative.

Miyagi and Sendai initially exist on opposite sides of perceived school hierarchy.

Miyagi sees students almost as color-coded groups separated by invisible borders.

Sendai's interlude gives us an eerily compatible model: the classroom as a **水槽**, an aquarium whose fish occupy predictable strata.

The irony is that both girls understand hierarchy while occupying intermediate positions.

Sendai realizes this explicitly near the end:

-   she appears to be high-status, but she is not the unquestioned center;

-   she works to avoid falling;

-   Miyagi isn't truly at the bottom either;

-   Miyagi similarly occupies an intermediate location.


They are socially different, but psychologically less different than either assumes.

### Miyagi's room becomes a counter-society

Inside it:

-   school rank loses significance;

-   polished Sendai can become irritated and disobedient;

-   quiet Miyagi becomes forceful;

-   they can create rules no classmate knows;

-   bodily intimacy exists beside mundane homework and dinner.


But the room is not simply a realm of “authenticity.”

It has its own performance:

**buyer and purchased classmate.**

They escape one social script by inventing another.

Volume 1 is about that second script gradually becoming just as inadequate as the first.

---

# XIV. The Novel's Domesticity Is More Important Than Its Eroticism

The strange commands supply the hook.

The domestic habits supply the emotional substance.

Over the course of Volume 1:

-   Sendai reads Miyagi's books.

-   They share drinks.

-   They eat dinner.

-   Sendai buys groceries.

-   Sendai cooks.

-   They play games.

-   They do homework.

-   Sendai explains math.

-   They nap.

-   Miyagi changes what she serves because Sendai dislikes carbonation.

-   Sendai brings snacks.

-   Miyagi gives Sendai food when she has missed lunch.


None of these acts needs the premise.

That is precisely why they matter.

The contract is most threatened not when they touch sexually, but when they start **living small pieces of ordinary life together**.

One of the most revealing moments occurs after Miyagi has sent Sendai home.

Miyagi begins preparing two cup noodles.

Then she realizes:

> Sendai isn't here.

Her conscious mind still refuses the relationship.

Her body has already incorporated Sendai into its routines.

That is excellent understated characterization.

---

# XV. Food Becomes a Language of Care Neither Girl Wants to Speak

Food repeatedly shifts function.

### Early

Food is incidental or poor:

-   instant noodles;

-   Miyagi's inadequate diet.


### Valentine's

Chocolate becomes a problem of category and intimacy.

### Karaage

Sendai cooks voluntarily.

Miyagi reacts defensively because voluntary nourishment resembles care.

### Snacks

Sendai begins bringing things into the room herself.

She is no longer merely consuming Miyagi's purchased time; she is investing in the environment.

### Castella

By Chapter 10, Miyagi has noticed Sendai did not eat lunch and feeds her.

But she must explain:

> leftovers.
> waste would be bad.

The pattern is almost comic:

**Every act of care requires an excuse.**

That is why “言い訳” belongs in the subtitle.

---

# XVI. The Cider-to-Barley-Tea Change Is Tiny and Extremely Important

Sendai eventually reveals that she dislikes carbonated drinks.

Miyagi verbally reacts in characteristic fashion, suggesting she may give her cider anyway.

Then she quietly starts providing **麦茶**.

This is Miyagi's emotional grammar:

> Her speech refuses care.
> Her behavior performs it.

The mismatch between what Miyagi says and what Miyagi *does* is often more reliable than her internal monologue.

That pattern should remain one of our key longitudinal markers.

---

# XVII. The Eraser Is One of the Best Objects in the Volume

The humble **消しゴム** becomes a near-perfect symbol in Chapters 8–10.

First it becomes part of Miyagi's body-search game.

Then Sendai accidentally keeps it.

Miyagi says she can simply have it.

This reflects Miyagi's broader tendency:

> Money? Take it.
> Shirt? Keep it.
> Eraser? Have it.

Objects are disposable because insisting on their return would create continuity.

Sendai refuses that logic.

She wants to return things.

And after Miyagi attempts to disappear again, Sendai deliberately turns the eraser into an excuse to approach her at school.

Her internal metaphor becomes wonderfully explicit: Miyagi is attempting to use an eraser to wipe their relationship back to a blank page.

Sendai uses the **eraser itself** to prevent the erasure.

That is almost too neat, but it works because the object had already circulated organically through their interactions.

The eraser becomes:

**an object of separation transformed into a mechanism of return.**

---

# XVIII. Marks: The Anxiety of Leaving Evidence

The reciprocal marks on their bodies are another major motif.

A temporary bodily mark does several things:

-   confirms physical contact occurred;

-   makes the other person persist after separation;

-   creates private evidence;

-   potentially risks public discovery.


Sendai's response is especially revealing.

She is disturbed by marks because they make the supposedly temporary relationship feel **permanent**.

A mark remains after she leaves Miyagi's room.

It violates compartmentalization.

This is the same problem as the first-name issue and the school encounter.

Their ideal contract says:

> Miyagi's room contains the relationship.

But intimacy produces residues that cross the boundary:

-   marks;

-   habits;

-   memories;

-   objects;

-   jealousy;

-   names;

-   changes in food;

-   involuntary thoughts;

-   eventually public contact.


The story is therefore increasingly about **leakage**.

Neither girl can keep the relationship where she put it.

---

# XIX. Bodily Exchange: Bite, Saliva, Blood, Heat

Haneda repeatedly uses bodily contact to make psychological boundaries material.

The recurring acts are oddly specific:

-   licking;

-   biting;

-   swallowing blood;

-   leaving marks;

-   touching necks;

-   sharing heat;

-   kissing.


Sendai's reaction to licking Miyagi's cut finger is especially striking: the idea that Miyagi's blood has entered her body makes her uneasy.

Why?

Because something of Miyagi has literally crossed into Sendai.

That recurs metaphorically everywhere.

Sendai wants Miyagi as a comfortable limited relationship.

But Miyagi keeps entering:

-   her schedule;

-   her thoughts;

-   her food choices;

-   her school attention;

-   her body;

-   her sense of the future.


Likewise, Sendai keeps entering Miyagi's routines.

Their anxiety about bodily permeability mirrors their anxiety about emotional permeability.

---

# XX. Voice: A Beautiful Repeated Motif

One of my favorite formal transformations in the book involves **reading aloud**.

At the beginning of their arrangement, Miyagi orders Sendai to read an embarrassing romantic/erotic manga aloud.

The purpose is humiliation.

Miyagi wants to hear the polished girl say things she normally would not say.

Later, Miyagi asks Sendai to read a school text aloud while Miyagi lies down.

Why?

Because she wants to hear **Sendai's voice**.

And it helps her sleep.

The same act evolves:

**forced performance → private comfort**

The command survives.

Its emotional meaning completely changes.

This is almost a miniature version of the entire ¥5,000 arrangement.

---

# XXI. First Names Are More Intimate Than Mouths

Sendai's attempt to call her **志緒理** deserves special attention.

At this stage:

-   Sendai has licked Miyagi.

-   Miyagi has licked Sendai.

-   They have exchanged bites.

-   They have left bodily marks.

-   Miyagi has touched Sendai while she slept.


Still:

> “志緒理” is too much.

This makes perfect sense psychologically.

First-name address cannot be dismissed as one of Miyagi's weird commands.

It asserts:

> **I have a recognized place in your ordinary social world.**

Miyagi can tolerate exceptional intimacy.

She cannot tolerate ordinary legitimacy.

That distinction may become one of the most important relational patterns in the series.

---

# XXII. Both Girls Are Unreliable, but Not in the Same Way

This is where the alternating first-person structure becomes extraordinarily effective.

Neither narrator is primarily lying to the reader.

They are **rationalizing themselves**.

### Miyagi's characteristic error

She transforms desire into hostile explanations:

-   curiosity;

-   revenge;

-   irritation;

-   command;

-   boredom;

-   fairness;

-   possession.


Her pattern is:

**feel → act → invent explanation afterward.**

### Sendai's characteristic error

She constructs reasonable conceptual frameworks:

-   the room is comfortable;

-   Miyagi is interesting;

-   this is basically friendship;

-   the relationship is temporary;

-   she's merely curious;

-   graduation will end it.


Her rationalizations are more coherent.

That makes them, in some ways, harder to detect.

Her pattern is:

**feel → construct theory explaining why it isn't dangerous → act far beyond what theory predicts.**

By the end of Volume 1, Sendai's actions have become devastating evidence against her own account.

Someone who merely enjoys a convenient room does not:

-   skip prep school;

-   repeatedly ring the intercom;

-   call for the first time;

-   publicly enter the other girl's classroom;

-   drag her away for confrontation;

-   insist on another meeting;

-   maneuver her into asking for a kiss.


Sendai is more self-aware than Miyagi.

She is not yet particularly honest with herself.

---

# XXIII. The Two LN-Original Chapters Make the Volume Much Better Structured

Haneda's afterword explains that the **interlude** and **番外編** were specifically created for the light-novel edition.

They are not filler.

They create an elegant pair of prehistory studies.

### Sendai before Miyagi

The interlude tells us:

> I prefer sameness.
> I know how to function in this system.
> Strange disruptions are usually unpleasant.
> I don't need my life to change.

Then Miyagi changes it.

### Miyagi before Sendai

The final extra tells us:

> Sendai belongs to another social world.
> She probably doesn't even know my name.
> I should keep my distance.

Then Sendai knows her name.

And Miyagi invites her home.

Both girls begin with a theory of social reality.

Their first interaction disproves both theories.

There is also a lovely reversal between the conclusions.

Pre-Miyagi Sendai says, effectively:

> Different things are better avoided.

Pre-Sendai Miyagi ends up deciding:

> Maybe it's okay if something unusual happens once in a while.

Yet by the end of the main volume:

-   **Sendai** is the one actively fighting to preserve the unusual relationship;

-   **Miyagi** is the one repeatedly trying to run back into safety.


That reversal gives the LN edition a much stronger literary architecture than the basic premise alone suggests.

# XXIV. Chapter-by-Chapter Emotional Movement

A useful compressed map is:

The plot is sparse.

The **relationship changes dramatically**.

That's the point.

---

# XXV. What Volume 1 Says About Love Without Using the Word

Neither girl needs to be consciously “in love” by the end of Volume 1 for the novel's direction to be clear.

What it establishes is **pre-verbal attachment**.

Miyagi has already reached the point where:

-   Sendai's absence changes how food tastes;

-   her presence has altered Miyagi's household routines;

-   Sendai's voice regulates Miyagi emotionally;

-   other people's access to Sendai bothers her;

-   the prospect of losing Sendai activates childhood abandonment defenses;

-   she wants bodily closeness but fears its consequences.


Sendai has reached the point where:

-   Miyagi occupies her thoughts outside the apartment;

-   she recognizes Miyagi's belongings at a glance;

-   she resents other people's contact with those belongings;

-   she wants greater naming intimacy;

-   she rearranges school and prep-school behavior around Miyagi;

-   she pursues Miyagi when Miyagi withdraws;

-   and she explicitly wants to kiss her.


Calling them merely “friends” would now be descriptively inadequate.

Calling them a couple would still exceed what they themselves have constructed.

That gap is where the story lives.

---

# XXVI. A Note on Sexuality

Volume 1 also gives us reason to be careful with identity labels.

Early Sendai says she has no particular sexual interest in Miyagi or in girls and establishes **sex is prohibited**.

By the end she:

-   wants to kiss Miyagi;

-   enjoys the kiss;

-   asks for another;

-   experiences stronger bodily desire during it;

-   tries to deepen it;

-   and recognizes how much she likes Miyagi.


The correct conclusion at this stage is therefore not:

> Sendai was “secretly lesbian all along.”

The text does not give us that identity claim.

The stronger reading is:

> **Sendai's abstract account of what she wants is being contradicted by her concrete attraction to Miyagi.**

The same distinction applies to Miyagi.

This is a story about discovering the specific person before either girl has established a comfortable categorical language for what the discovery means.

---

# XXVII. The Illustration Program Supports This Reading

U35's illustrations are not merely decorative character portraits.

The climactic Chapter 10 illustration selects the kiss as the visual culmination of the volume. Importantly, it does not visually render Miyagi as a commanding purchaser and Sendai as a passive commodity.

The bodies are close and reciprocal.

That complements the prose's larger trajectory:

**the original hierarchy no longer explains what we are seeing.**

Other illustrations similarly emphasize bodily proximity, touching, feeding, domestic co-presence, and the girls observing one another—not the exchange of money.

The franchise's visual language is already quietly telling us what the prose eventually makes explicit:

> the ¥5,000 is the mechanism; **the relationship is the subject.**

---

# XXVIII. What Is Actually “Toxic” About Volume 1?

There is a tempting fandom shorthand for a relationship like this: toxic yuri.

There are certainly unhealthy elements.

Miyagi:

-   weaponizes humiliation;

-   tests limits;

-   attempts to control attachment;

-   invades Sendai's body while she sleeps;

-   pushes Sendai away instead of communicating.


Sendai:

-   deliberately provokes Miyagi;

-   manipulates her toward particular commands;

-   occasionally ignores immediate objections;

-   invades the public/private boundary Miyagi established;

-   presses physical escalation beyond what Miyagi intended during the kiss.


But “toxic” by itself is analytically impoverished.

Neither is trying to destroy the other.

Most of the harmful behavior comes from a shared inability to say extraordinarily ordinary things:

> I want you here.
> I missed you.
> I was jealous.
> I don't want this to end.
> I want to touch you.
> I want you to touch me.
> I care what happens to you.

The bizarre contract is what happens when those statements are replaced with machinery.

Their task is not simply to become “less toxic.”

It is to develop a language in which **wanting another person does not have to masquerade as power, obligation, temporariness, or accident.**

That gives the relationship considerably more thematic substance than the premise initially promises.

---

# XXIX. Volume 1's Most Important Symmetry

The deepest similarity between Miyagi and Sendai is not social status.

It is that **both want controlled attachment because uncontrolled attachment has failed them at home.**

Miyagi's home teaches:

> people leave.

Sendai's home teaches:

> belonging is conditional upon performing the right version of yourself.

So Miyagi creates a relationship that supposedly cannot leave without her permission.

Sendai accepts a relationship in which she supposedly does not need to perform anything except obedience.

Both think they have found a technical solution to intimacy.

They have not.

They have found each other.

And that's much more dangerous.

# XXX. Provisional Character Profiles After Volume 1

## 宮城志緒理 — Miyagi Shiori

**Public presentation:** quiet, relatively ordinary, low-key, socially situated below Sendai but not completely isolated.

**Private temperament:** stubborn, possessive, irritable, curious, surprisingly bold under the protection of rules.

**Central vulnerability:** abandonment.

**Primary defense:** control and preemptive rejection.

**Relationship strategy:** turn desire into commands.

**Characteristic contradiction:** insists things mean nothing while structuring her life around them.

**Form of care:** behavior that contradicts her speech.

**Form of desire:** possession, exclusivity, sensory fixation, wanting access to the Sendai nobody else sees.

**Greatest fear as of V1:** discovering that she needs Sendai and cannot make Sendai remain.

---

## 仙台葉月 — Sendai Hazuki

**Public presentation:** attractive, socially graceful, capable, helpful, adaptable.

**Private temperament:** analytical, teasing, stubborn, curious, more confrontational than her public persona suggests.

**Central vulnerability:** conditional belonging and exhaustion from social performance.

**Primary defense:** rationalization and emotional limitation.

**Relationship strategy:** call the relationship temporary while continuously deepening it.

**Characteristic contradiction:** claims to want gray, low-intensity attachment while repeatedly initiating greater intimacy.

**Form of care:** practical action—food, study help, presence, returning.

**Form of desire:** pursuit, experimentation, touch, curiosity that becomes increasingly difficult to distinguish from wanting.

**Greatest fear as of V1:** becoming attached strongly enough that the predetermined graduation ending will hurt.

# XXXI. Final Assessment of Volume 1

Volume 1 is considerably stronger than its gimmicky elevator pitch suggests.

Its greatest strength is **micropsychological accumulation**.

Almost nothing happens on a conventional plot scale.

No grand confession.

No dramatic external antagonist.

No enormous social event.

Instead:

a drink changes.

a textbook acquires a crease.

an extra cup noodle is prepared.

a name is spoken.

a bruise becomes a mark.

an eraser is kept.

a phone is called for the first time.

a girl appears at the wrong classroom.

a kiss happens twice.

Each small change destroys one more part of the original fiction.

The result is a remarkably coherent study of **two people trying to obtain intimacy while preserving deniability**.

And I think the single best formulation of Volume 1 is:

> **Miyagi invents the ¥5,000 contract because it lets her possess Sendai without asking Sendai to choose her. Sendai accepts the contract because it lets her choose Miyagi without admitting that she is choosing her.**

By Chapter 10, both halves of that arrangement are failing.

That is exactly why the story can continue.
