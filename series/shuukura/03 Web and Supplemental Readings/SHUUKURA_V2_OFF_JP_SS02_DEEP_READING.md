## Enough ingredients for an absent cook

The opening inventory is exact and modest: a whole cabbage, two potatoes and eggs, with neither pork nor chicken. Miyagi imagines that Sendai could make something from them while complaining, but she has not summoned her today. The conditional gives Sendai an active place in Miyagi's expectation without placing her physically in the room or revealing a present private thought. For Miyagi, the same ingredients pose a choice she must make herself. (O002;L001–004.)

Her question about stir-fry meets the silence of an open refrigerator. This small personification places the missing answer inside the ordinary kitchen scene: the appliance cannot tell her whether this is the right meal to make. She shuts it, boils electric-pot water, puts cup ramen on the counter and sits. She has considered cooking and abandoned it before consulting a recipe. That sequence gives a local decision and self-appraisal, rather than a demonstrated permanent inability. (O002;L006–017.)

Sendai's good food has briefly made Miyagi think that being able to cook might be worthwhile. Miyagi casts the thought as a consequence she would rather avoid, because wanting more from meals is difficult in this house. 「昔の私のほうが良かった」receives its immediate explanation in that circumstance: the earlier self who needed only satiety and accepted solitary eating could live here more easily. This is a defensive evaluation of what desire costs her, not proof that her former life was happier or that suppressing every preference is objectively best. (O002;L017–023.)

The coming end of school extends that local problem into an imagined future. Next month will be December, so the current month is November; approaching high-school graduation supplies the historical final-school-year context. Miyagi explicitly does not know whether she will enter university, yet speaks as if Sendai's visits and their shared meals will stop. The juxtaposition is revealing: uncertainty about one future outcome coexists with a firm projection of another loss. That projection belongs to Miyagi now. The later admitted shared home and continued meals give the reader counterevidence to its eventual fulfillment without becoming information this narrator already possesses. Exact day and calendar year remain unprovided. (O002;L025.)

## When a meal recalls the people who ate it

Cup ramen does not fail as a matter of taste. Miyagi says she does not dislike it; there are many kinds and the flavor is acceptable. Its difficulty is historical association. She scarcely ate it while her mother was present and also did not eat it for a while after her absence. The second qualification prevents a neat instant transition from mother's cooking to cup noodles. The story gives a remembered sequence with an unspecified interval, not a complete chronology of every childhood meal. (O002;L027–031.)

Miyagi tells herself not to follow memory, then describes sinking into it. Fragments entangle her as she tries to rise, and the repeated「どんどん」「深く」makes the descent sustained rather than an isolated recollection. This is the narration's representation of the force memory acquires. It does not require a diagnosis or an independently observed episode of bodily paralysis. A thought that food should merely fill her stomach has encountered what the food recalls. (O002;L033–040.)

The remembered family is initially a place where meals with her mother were ordinary. Her father was sometimes away for work; her mother was someone she ate with. Even that remembered pleasure retains「ように思う」. What seemed too normal to disappear changed: the mother-made meals became father-made meals, then sharing food in this home became close enough to zero for Miyagi to describe it that way. The source neither supplies either parent's complete motives nor specifies the exact timing of each stage. Its force lies in how a daily arrangement she had not expected to lose becomes something she can no longer reconstruct through a final remembered menu. (O002/O003;L038–044.)

She cannot remember the last meal made by either parent. That limit is particular. The narration has just remembered family eating and an impression of its pleasure; it does not present a blank childhood or an inability to remember any food. Becoming tired of rehearsing the memories and accustomed to eating alone offers another kind of continuity, one built around adapting to absence rather than restoring the former table. (O003;L042–044.)

## An easier way to live, with a recurring cost

「期待は落胆を倍にして、人を絶望させる」compresses Miyagi's defensive reasoning into a general statement. The analysis can identify why she uses it without adopting it as a universal law or treating “double” as a psychological measurement. In her account, losing enjoyable shared meals brought her close to disliking eating itself. 「嫌いになりそう」keeps that state near the threshold; it is stronger than mild dissatisfaction but narrower than an established total aversion. (O003;L046–048.)

Reducing eating to satiety made everything easier, she says, and let her sink family-meal memories below the bottom of her heart. The recurrence of the sinking image changes its direction of agency. Earlier she was drawn down into memory; here she recalls managing life by putting memory down. The strategy has a recognizable use in her circumstances even though today's scene shows that it cannot ensure permanent control over what returns. Calling the strategy a simple failure would discard the relief she actually represents. Calling it a settled cure would disregard the rest of the story. (O002/O003;L035–036/048.)

The claim that the child who marked the calendar is gone is immediately qualified by the return of memory today. At this point the supplement has not explained the marks or the dates. The image still matters as Miyagi's attempt to distance the current self from an earlier way of marking time, without identifying what she marked. Her next sentence keeps that distance incomplete. It is not necessary to turn the thought into a lie: a person can have changed her habitual way of living and still encounter a past response she hoped to leave behind. (O003;L051–052.)

Sendai's food gives that response a present trigger. Miyagi says Sendai has made her remember that meals are something good, so she now thinks about things she considers unnecessary. The source does not reduce this renewed desire to either a benevolent cure or a harmful intrusion. Enjoyment has become available and harder to lose. The cost is audible in the qualified regret that perhaps she should have learned to cook from her mother. Its 「かもしれない」keeps the regret tentative rather than establishing a settled identity or vocational wish. (O003;L056–058.)

## A possible cook, an unfinished Sendai

The remembered constraints and imagined requests must stay apart. That is a particular attributed age condition concerning the knife. It does not establish every lesson her father would have refused, his complete motives, or Miyagi's exact age. The two following「もしも」sentences imagine asking her mother before she left, and asking her father despite his age objection. The alternative self is rendered through concrete domestic possibilities: a refrigerator full of ingredients, cooking as a hobby and feeding someone. 「誰か」remains unspecified. The imagined life might have spared her current dejection in front of the fridge and regret over losing future meals with Sendai, but the source's conditional force leaves these benefits untested.
