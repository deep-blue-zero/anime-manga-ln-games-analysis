## The errand's object and the time it makes

The missing cider and orange juice create a task with a clear practical endpoint: replenish the refrigerator. Miyagi would prefer those drinks to barley tea, yet not enough to leave home late at night. Sendai proposes making replenishment a joint outing and adding sweets. The disagreement is therefore about the time, effort and shape of an activity, rather than simply whether the drinks are wanted. Miyagi's no does not erase her taste; her undertaking to buy them tomorrow supplies an alternative way of satisfying it. The story gives the proposed errand two objects: the things to obtain and the time that two people might spend obtaining them. (O002; p-001p8–43,64–65.)

Alternating focalization makes that second object progressively legible. Miyagi knows from previous trips that Sendai likes to look around; her own account measures this as additional time. Sendai later describes Miyagi's efficient route to checkout as an absence of shopping enjoyment and privately prescribes detours. These are competing standards for the same activity. Neither narrator's desired pace becomes an objective measure of how shopping ought to work. Miyagi's earlier account of Sendai prolonging errands gains concrete support, but its approximate doubled duration is never timed. Sendai's account reveals an affirmative wish, rather than proving that Miyagi's preference is deficient. (O002–003; p29,103–114.)

The ending makes the contrast explicit: Sendai does not especially need stew; she wants the goal farther away. A food proposal thus becomes a way of extending the route and the encounter. That does not make every practical reason false. Carrying large bottles together may help, and she can still intend to cook something good. The private narration demonstrates that those publicly available reasons do not exhaust the purpose. Miyagi hears an offer of help and a meal, while the reader also receives Sendai's preferred arrangement of their time. The text need not name love, loneliness or abandonment to make that difference consequential. Browsing may also have value for Sendai in its own right. The scene does not separately rank enjoyment of shopping, Miyagi's company and practical help as exhaustive causes; its sharper evidence concerns the route she chooses to prolong. (O003; p100–103,122–132.)

## A refusal can hold locally and remain contested later

The first section allows Miyagi's refusal to have an effect. Sendai repeats the shopping bid with an attractive nearby-shop-and-sweets version; Miyagi still says she does not want it today. The outing then drops out of the represented evening. Sendai asks for access to the refrigerator, and Miyagi is free to move toward her room. A different proposal—tea at home—receives a small yes. Accepting tea does not establish that she secretly wanted the rejected trip, and a new invitation need not be interpreted as identical to the previous one. The setting and cost have changed. (O002; p34–55.)

The following represented shopping day has a different pattern. Sendai catches up with Miyagi, joins the trip, then pulls her arm to divert the cart. Miyagi says the necessary things are already obtained, suggests that Sendai get the extra item tomorrow, and eventually asks what it is after apparently abandoning an unproductive argument. Her frown and 「勝手すぎる」 remain in the scene. Eventual movement and a benefactive promise do not prove that the detour has become a comfortable mutual compromise. The narration also does not report a formal promise that Sendai would stay away: 「任せる」 delegated the purchase without explicitly accepting a no-accompaniment condition. The analysis must preserve the local pressure without inventing either a broken contract or a general approval of it. (O002–003; p64–65,98–132.)

This unevenness is a useful account of relational agency. Sendai can accommodate a specific refusal and later press another preference; Miyagi can refuse, accept an alternative and still dislike a subsequent accommodation. Neither a count of yeses nor a declaration that they like each other's company resolves the quality of every decision. The source represents initiative as a process with differently priced alternatives, repeated bids and an expressed complaint that remains unanswered by explanation.

## Five minutes, silence and the difference between belief and observation

Sendai's five-minute message sets the pace of the second section. She suspects that a safer request for ten minutes would not work, says five despite the walking distance, and then runs. Her imagined time limit motivates actual effort. The short repeated 「五分」「速く」 and the inconvenience of a skirt make time a bodily and stylistic constraint, rather than merely dialogue about scheduling. She tries to make her own arrival fit the smaller window she has offered. (O003; p71–97.)

Her theory of Miyagi's response remains a theory. She infers that silence means Miyagi will wait, imagines a timer, and considers the possibility that Miyagi will leave or choose another supermarket. On arrival Miyagi is there. That observation supports the narrow fact of presence, not every assumption about deadlines or unspoken agreement. The exact elapsed time is unreported; Miyagi's reason for remaining is not narrated in Sendai's section. This is precisely where alternating first person needs limits: an earlier Miyagi narration does not grant Sendai later interior access, and Sendai's confidence is not an omniscient correction. (O003; p80–99.)

The cheerful face she makes while proposing help is likewise partial evidence. It can serve a sincere desire to share the trip and a strategy for overcoming reluctance. Miyagi's cold answer does not make the smile empty, while the smile does not nullify her objection. The two expressions participate in negotiation; neither supplies a complete emotional explanation. (O003; p100–103,120–129.)

## Ordinary competence and an unfinished promise

Dinner and tea prevent a simple division between a competent caretaker and a helpless recipient. Miyagi has grown used to cooking and chooses instant noodles when it feels bothersome. She declines to defend that convenience at length. Her comparison of Sendai's admonition to a television mother is a narrative simile through which she experiences the exchange, rather than an actual parent-child status. Sendai ate before tutoring; the scene contains no shared dinner. The new tea invitation begins preparation but does not show the completed drinking. (O002; p51–63.)

Similar distinctions govern the shopping ending. Groceries, bread and the drinks go into a basket; checkout, payment, carrying them home and cooking stew remain unshown. An offer to make good stew therefore cannot discharge the existing novel corpus's unfulfilled stew responsibility. The relative placement or duplication of the two offers is not established by the bonus's volume number. What this witness independently establishes is the use of a meal proposal to prolong a particular errand, with Miyagi's reservations still visible. Its domestic details carry competing wishes without turning every meal into a single fixed symbol or every accommodation into development toward an agreed endpoint. (O003–004; p104–132.)
