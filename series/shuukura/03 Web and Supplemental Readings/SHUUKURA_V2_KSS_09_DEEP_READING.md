## An affection law that revises its own history

The platypus introduces itself through the repeated absence of a name, then supplies a detailed account of its grievance. The formal「である」voice makes its small domestic problem sound weighty. A nameless-animal opening can resonate with a familiar mock-literary formula, but that possible echo does not establish an author-confirmed allusion. More directly, the narration gives the tissue cover a history: Shiori selected it, Sendai brought it to the register, and it now regards Sendai as「ご主人様」. Selection becomes rescue in the object account. (O002;04p1–3.)

The grievance initially appears to describe a fall from privilege. Cat-shaped chopstick rests have arrived as Shiori's gift, receive repeated attention and are called cute. The platypus says they stole its place and Sendai's love, then immediately qualifies that story: perhaps it did not receive much love before either. The correction matters more than a simple label of jealousy. Its own narration supplies counterevidence against an uncomplicated history of displacement. The earlier novel also shows Sendai praising and stroking the cover before the cat gift; those actions qualify an absolute neglect claim without measuring her inner affection. (O002/O005;04p5–13;LN05p005292–293;LN06p011135.)

Quantity becomes another way to organize the grievance. Three cats can claim permission to receive three times as much affection, but the platypus refuses their proposed law. The Japanese「可愛がられてもいい」concerns an asserted allowance to be cherished; it does not establish that the cats possess a measurable tripled cuteness. Their argument and its rejection are represented object dialogue, not a rule accepted by the human owner. (O002;04p15–25.)

The platypus offers its own exception instead: only Shiori may receive Sendai's affection here, because she saved it and introduced it to its owner. Gratitude supplies a private hierarchy whose place for its own desire remains uneasy. Its boast of a heart as wide as Sendai's bed coexists with its refusal to tolerate the newcomers. These comic selfclaims make a perspective legible; they do not create human exclusivity, a romantic title or a legal property rule. (O002;04p23–29.)

## The attention that changes the feeling

A short「可愛いね」brings the grievance into the present. The platypus watches Sendai's feet from under the table, notices that she does not look at it, and infers that she is probably speaking to the cats above. The「どうやら」「らしい」qualification keeps the praise target an inference. A restricted position in the room shapes what the narrator can observe and how it interprets an otherwise unaddressed line. (O003;04p31–37.)

Distress takes the form of the commodity body: the tissue box seems to dent, and tissues feel ready to spill. The text marks these sensations with qualifying language rather than certifying an actual collapse or an emptied box. The platypus calls with a voice it strongly believes humans cannot hear, yet its hand is pulled almost at once. That timing invites a response-like reading while leaving the mechanism unexplained. The human hearing limit is the narrator's explicit expectation; the sequence does not prove that Sendai understood the call or learned about its private rivalry. (O003;04p39.)

Sendai drags it closer, pats its head and strokes its beak. Its belly slides over the floor as though doing the cleaning, giving the reassurance an undignified material route. What changes is the platypus's feeling of being forgotten: attention to the cats need not mean that no place remains for it. Its happiness is directly narrated, while Sendai's complete motive is not. The care is real within this represented scene, but it is not a universal certificate of her conduct in other relationships. (O003;04p41–45.)

## Light objects with a heavy consequence

The reassurance generates a hypothetical confidence that even more residents would not make Sendai forget it. The platypus calls such an addition impossible, so this is neither a factual forecast of a newcomer nor an analyst's registered test. The immediate complication comes from the cats already present: two are placed on its head and one on its beak. Their weight can be light while the burden feels heavy. Reassurance has not abolished rivalry. (O003;04p47–51.)

Two constraints then prevent the desired act of shaking them off. The platypus cannot bring itself to dislodge things Sendai values, and it also cannot move by itself. The conjunction keeps loyalty and material incapacity distinct. Reading the result as entirely chosen virtue would erase the second constraint; treating it as nothing but helplessness would erase the first. Neither conclusion establishes a human consent policy. (O003;04p53.)

The objects nevertheless exchange greetings. The cats use「こんばんにゃー」and receive a formal「こんばんは」before Sendai's final request that they get along. This order prevents the greeting from becoming an accomplished response to the later line. Sendai strokes the cover, and it quietly makes a catlike「にゃあ」that it intends the cats not to hear. The shift from formal argument to concealed imitation allows pleasure and a concern for status to coexist. The narrative gives the intention to conceal; it does not provide outside confirmation that the cats heard nothing. Cordial conduct is achieved without a declaration that the grievance has disappeared. (O003;04p55–60.)

## Different witnesses to the same household objects

The original novels qualify the object's origin story. Shiori selects the cover, Sendai praises it and takes over the purchase using money she calls shared, and Shiori later asks that the cover stay in Sendai's room. Shiori's frustration with the purchase is part of the human account, alongside her actual choices. The grateful object's rescue narrative selects a different meaning from those events without proving an exact agreement about the shop display or the money.「ご主人様」cannot settle the ownership dispute for the human participants. (O005;LN05p005260–340;p007350–385.)

The cat count also has a material explanation. The original gift contains five cats; Sendai takes two into the common space and plans to keep three as room decorations. A room with three therefore need not contradict a five-cat gift. The novel makes the paired use Sendai's repurposing rather than Shiori's deliberate matching-set intention. This comparison grounds the setting while leaving the supplement's exact evening and its individual cat speakers unidentified. The source adds current placement and object conversation, not another purchase, gift or transfer. (O002–003/O005;04p15–21,49–60;LN07p009310–365.)

The result is a small account of belonging whose terms remain contested. Attention can be shared, care can reassure without relieving every burden, and polite coexistence can preserve a private reserve. The object voice makes those distinctions through a tissue box, a beak, numerical rights and a hidden meow. Its independent formal work should survive synthesis without turning it into an omniscient explanation of Miyagi and Sendai.
