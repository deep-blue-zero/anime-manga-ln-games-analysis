## A game can be a shared activity and an exit strategy

The word game begins with different purposes before it has agreed rules. Sendai offers a shoebill word; Miyagi initially hears a topic, then recognizes shiritori and refuses. Sendai treats the final ん of that refusal as a losing move. Miyagi objects that the game never began. The joke is intelligible, but its procedural premise is disputed: refusal has been absorbed into a game the refusing person did not accept. No loss penalty follows simply because Sendai declares one. (O002; p8–23.)

Sendai then says shiritori itself is optional: staying awake and doing something together is what matters. Oil and console games are alternatives, not completed activities. Miyagi's eventual flying-things game is conditional participation with another purpose: she wants a quick result. She chooses the first unfamiliar bird before Sendai answers and later hurries her specifically to prevent effective thought. A compromise permits real interaction while remaining strategically unequal. It cannot be described simply as mutual enjoyment or as continued total nonparticipation. (O002; p24–33,51–60.)

Knowledge also changes inside the scene. Sendai corrects her own bird classification of a platypus, searches the toucan and recognizes its image. Her eventual シジミ is an invalid answer under the flying rule; the analyst must not silently supply the missing butterfly name. Yet a pressured wrong word does not establish general stupidity. Miyagi's console/crane-game memories support a specific experience gap, while her inference about Sendai's earlier nongaming life remains an inference. She is curious about that person and chooses not to ask tonight. Her interest and wish for sleep coexist. Even her denial that flying things are her specialty distinguishes knowing this bird from possessing a comprehensive domain. (O002–003; p28–60,72.)

## The narration identifies the convenience of an inference

The bodily sequence tests any easy equation between retained contact and permission. Miyagi removes the first held hand. Sendai subsequently grips more strongly for the stated purpose of preventing another removal. Later nonremoval therefore occurs in a materially different force context. Sendai reasons that their hands are not inseparable magnets, so keeping them joined must express Miyagi's will. She extends that reading to permission to remain, then explicitly admits wanting to believe it even if it is 「都合のいい解釈」. The qualification belongs to the narrator herself. Reading it as objective consent would erase one of the source's clearest epistemic signals. (O003; p74–85.)

Miyagi also refuses the proposed kiss-for-departure exchange and again tells Sendai to return to her room. Sendai initiates closeness before an objection; limited nonresistance is represented through her account, followed by an actual shoulder push when contact escalates. Neither observation can swallow the other. The push and explicit refusals weigh against a blanket permission reading; the earlier limited nonresistance prevents certainty that every moment had the same subjective status. Miyagi's later private account is unavailable. The final time compromise does not retroactively authorize all preceding conduct. (O003; p81–97.)

A playful, affectionate reading has evidence: changing games, familiarity, welcomed extra company as a possibility, and an ending with a promise and a gift trace. It becomes insufficient if it removes the stronger grip, repeated dismissal or expressly convenient belief. A pressure-centered reading has those concrete supports, but also exceeds the source if it makes Miyagi wholly incapable of limits or turns every retained contact into proven distress. The text represents both action and resistance, then a new accommodation. Those events warrant specified judgments about conduct without settling the entire relationship's moral or clinical identity.

## Ordinary time is a desire, not an unlimited claim

Asked what is different today, Sendai answers that nothing is special. Her interior then distinguishes commemorative days from the much larger number of ordinary ones. She cannot make every day memorable; she wants more time passing ordinary days with Miyagi. The sequence gives the title its force. The initial activity need not matter for its own sake because it creates a reason to remain together. This can be a modest domestic desire without being trivial evidence: Sendai values continuity outside marked occasions. (O003; p98–100.)

That desire does not determine the other person's schedule. Sendai frames eleven as early for vacation; Miyagi frames it as late for sleep. Both universities being on break explains the disagreement without proving Miyagi's bedtime false. Her worry about a possible bedsharing request remains anticipated, not an actual request or overnight event. The distinction from BW07's school-bound morning matters: a next-day class deadline is absent here, but a personal preference still exists. (O002–003; p15–19,64–75.)

Miyagi's midnight condition and Sendai's promise are the concrete result.
