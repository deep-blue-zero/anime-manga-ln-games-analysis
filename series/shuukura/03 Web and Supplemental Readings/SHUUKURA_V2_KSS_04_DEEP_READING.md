## The question Maika withholds

Maika's uncertainty about Miyagi and Sendai becomes a problem of responsibility for something Maika herself has said. She cannot establish the connection she suspects, yet she can identify an earlier disclosure she regrets. The movement between those two kinds of knowledge gives the story its force. An outsider has a private agenda and a difficult conversation to postpone, alongside her wish to understand another person's secrecy.

The lunch question stages that difficulty before the university disclosure becomes the explicit issue. Maika wants to ask about Sendai, hesitates, then asks what Shiori is eating and says she wants a share. Miyagi notices that this was probably not the intended question and presses her. Maika imagines a quieter conversation might produce a different answer, but finds that prospect too serious. She has an alternative and does not take it. Ordinary classroom talk provides a way to continue speaking while withholding what matters. No lunch is actually shared, and Miyagi's sensitivity to the evasion does not give her access to its exact contents. (O002;11p14–35,49.)

This small exchange complicates any division between transparent friends and an opaque central pair. Maika's withheld question has different stakes from Miyagi's guarded relationship, but the narrative places both within the same conversation. Each person can notice that something is missing without knowing precisely what it is. Maika's own interior makes her hesitation intelligible to the reader while leaving it partly inaccessible to Miyagi.

## Suspicion and the discipline of uncertainty

Maika remembers two separate encounters: Miyagi's collision with Sendai, and her own later conversation with Sendai on the way to the school shop. Sendai's reaction when Miyagi was mentioned seemed excessive to her. Repeated denials and the absence of an obvious connection have not erased that impression; she has been trying to trust Miyagi and now wavers. The narration distinguishes an effort to believe from settled belief. (O002;11p1–18.)

Her warning that assumptions produce「冤罪」is a correction to an interpretive method. Seeing the two as friends might make disconnected lines appear connected. The legal metaphor gives that danger weight without turning curiosity into a literal accusation before a court. Her caution also cannot establish that the suspected bond is absent. The original novel gives Sendai a private sense of a close relationship while she outwardly denies friendship. Maika can perceive a meaningful connection without having the category its participants would accept. The reader's advantage concerns access to those positions, rather than a license to treat Maika's uncertainty as foolishness. (O003/O005;12p32–39;LN03p009203–230.)

The original encounter further qualifies the responsibility Maika assigns herself. Sendai initiates the university topic, and Maika's report about Miyagi is tentative: she says Miyagi seems to intend the same university and has said she wants to apply. Sendai privately recognizes that the plan is not certain. These details matter against a simple account of malicious exposure or authoritative announcement. Maika nevertheless later judges her disclosure wrong. Her regret is explicit; a prior formal secrecy instruction is not. (O005;LN03p009179–220.)

## Who was told and who knows

The classroom conversation exposes a precise difference between those questions. Miyagi confirms the same-university plan, says she has no need to discuss it with others, and says she has told only Maika and Ami. Ami calls it a secret known only to them. Maika already knows that Sendai received her earlier report. Miyagi's statement about her own recipients can therefore coexist with a wider distribution of information. Ami's framing exceeds what the reader can accept as a global fact, without establishing that Ami knowingly misrepresents anything. (O003;12p41–65.)

The original novel prevents a further overstatement. Miyagi already knows that Maika and Sendai spoke: on the same November afternoon she asks Sendai about it and says Maika gave her a similar account. What remains omitted is the university content. The anticipated difficult confession at this supplement's ending therefore requires a fuller account of the conversation; it need not begin by revealing the bare existence of contact. Narrative omission must preserve what the original participants already experienced. (O005;LN03p009323–332.)

These distinctions leave several people holding different parts of the event. Maika knows her own disclosure and regret. Miyagi knows the contact and the accounts she received, while missing the relevant contents. Ami hears the classroom confirmation and offers the secret interpretation. Sendai retains the earlier qualified report and her own reaction, but does not automatically receive the new classroom confirmation or Maika's private remorse. Combining their positions would remove the very difficulty the story constructs.

## Care, amusement and a topic Miyagi wants retired

Ami helps shape this difficulty through her own contributions. Her loud bread answer interrupts Maika's hesitation, and her nutrition warning becomes unusually serious. Miyagi corrects the volume and the suggestion that bread is all she eats. Later, when Maika intends to retreat from the Sendai question, Ami insists that the collision was odd, recruits Maika's agreement and acknowledges the amusement when Miyagi calls it out. Care, comic performance and curiosity have observable places in her behavior. The source does not reduce them to a single hidden motive. (O002–003;11p37–49/12p1–30.)

Miyagi also has initiative throughout. She pursues Maika's intended question, challenges the claim of oddness, offers an explanation and asks the friends to grow tired of the topic. Maika's subsequent description of the two playing around supplies no exact bodily sequence or complete account of comfort. Ami's comic acknowledgment does not make every concern counterfeit, and continued play does not certify unlimited permission to press Miyagi. The exchange supports a particular topic limit and an incompletely described response to it. (O003;12p10–30.)

## The repair that belongs to a later scene

At the native endpoint, Maika has privately decided that she should apologize. Doing so will expose more of the conversation, and anticipating that occasion makes her slightly gloomy. No apology, forgiveness or full disclosure occurs here. The ending makes repair a difficult future action instead of converting regret into completed responsibility. Possible embarrassment, conflict avoidance and concern for Miyagi remain compatible with the account; it does not select a complete motive for delay. (O003;12p61–70.)

The already-read LN04 scene supplies a later outcome. Maika apologizes and discloses the university discussion during winter break. Miyagi says it is not worth apologizing over, while privately denying indifference and feeling disturbed. She also appreciates Maika's restraint when further questions stop. That combination qualifies both a wholly intrusive friend and a repair that erases every cost. It belongs to the later original scene, rather than an improved version of this supplement's prospective ending. (O005;LN04p005100–168.)

The later Miyagi–Sendai conversation gives the information another consequence. Sendai says she wanted Miyagi's own answer and anticipated that naming Maika as her source would provoke rejection. Miyagi privately endorses that conditional account, while insisting that her university choice has reasons of her own. Those are attributed later understandings, not evidence that Maika knew the pair's motives or that every earlier decision followed one strategy. The supplement's contribution remains distinct: it gives interior weight to the friend whose ordinary conversation moved information between them, and whose own withholding makes the effort to repair it difficult. (O005;LN04p005250–310.)
