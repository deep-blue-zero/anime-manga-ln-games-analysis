## Central argument

Paid routine resumes, but routine does not make the relationship static. Sendai receives the usual money, sees Miyagi talking more, takes part in a new shared activity, discloses a practical constraint on visiting, and accepts a local adjustment. These ordinary exchanges make the room feel more familiar while leaving knowledge and authority uneven. Sendai enjoys Miyagi's speech yet translates it privately into a taming metaphor. Miyagi teaches and competes, objects to intrusive contact and later expressly invokes a command. Sendai can interrupt the game and redirect attention, but the resulting proposal is not under her control.

The unit places Sendai's private family ambivalence inside that apparently ordinary visit. Prep school is audibly explained as an entrance-exam obligation; its imagined role as a route back into the family, and Sendai's rejection of that hope, are not disclosed to Miyagi. The contrast makes familiar domestic space meaningful without proving that Miyagi's home replaces Sendai's family or that either girl recognizes such a function. ## Compensation, objects and the resumption of an ordinary visit

The opening distinguishes an exception from a return to the normal exchange rather than announcing a new standing bargain. In p1-3 Sendai recalls taking clothes home and explains why they remain stored. Her own preference would have been to return them, but her classification of them as command compensation puts them beside the money she does not intend to spend. Material possession and private use-intention are separate facts. A report that the clothes were taken home supplies later evidence about that earlier visit; it does not rewrite what was still unshown at the earlier admitted cutoff.

Today, several days afterward, Sendai says she received the usual payment (p6). This is stronger than seeing a note offered or placed nearby; the verb here expressly reports receipt. It remains one narrated current exchange. There is no second fee in the episode, no new price for the game or experiment, and no authority to infer an unlimited command entitlement from that payment alone. Nor does the absence of a future-use plan establish that Sendai will never wear the clothes.

Miyagi's barley tea is another small adjustment (p9-11). Sendai says the reason is known to her, unlike the increased conversation. The reader can connect that understanding to already admitted speech about carbonation, but the current text does not stage a fresh explanation or show Sendai asking for the change. Later Miyagi drinks cider (p165); that is a separately attributed drink, not proof that Sendai's barley tea was replaced or that Miyagi shares Sendai's aversion.

## Enjoyed speech and Sendai's proprietary metaphor

Miyagi's remarks are mundane and sometimes irritating. She dismisses the romance novel because it lacks a happy ending, although Sendai has only begun it. Their small disagreement matters because Sendai simultaneously enjoys being addressed. The stray-cat comparison (p23-26) is her private interpretive language: she feels as if a formerly wary animal now allows closeness, and thinks of the time since early summer. It does not give Miyagi an actual declaration of trust, affection or changed relationship status. The metaphor also carries an asymmetry: Sendai casts herself as the one who has tamed another person while the ensuing game shows Miyagi setting an activity and refusing to accommodate her.

Sendai then takes the manga Miyagi was reading (p30). The absence of a complaint permits her to continue in her own account, but silence is not an explicit grant of general permission. This distinction becomes especially useful when Miyagi later physically restores her skirt and brushes a hand away. The episode contains different responses to different acts; it does not offer a single global state of permissiveness.

## Invitation, withdrawal and a negotiated practical constraint

Miyagi's game invitation is initially an ordinary request, with an expressed reason that solo play is boring (p34-40). Sendai does not know the game well and tentatively interprets the case. Her thought that the pictured character may be the attraction remains an alternative, not a settled characterization of Miyagi. The remembered report that she does not play games in which attractive men court her (p44) likewise cannot stand in for a complete account of her tastes.

The online-opponent question prompts a small rupture. Miyagi says Sendai need not play if she dislikes it and starts returning the case; Sendai hurries to clarify willingness and inexperience (p57-67). The sequence creates pressure to repair an apparent rejection, but it never explicitly calls game participation a paid command. Miyagi's spoken alternative and Sendai's expressed interest should remain present alongside the displeasure. Her teaching actually begins, although Sendai cannot remember all the controls. Sendai's guess that Miyagi has become tired of explaining is not access to Miyagi's interior.

The prep-school exchange interrupts the lesson. Sendai says there may be days when she cannot come (p72), then explains that visiting after the late finish could delay her own return until near midnight. ## A family-return possibility that Sendai both imagines and rejects

Sendai connects the desired university to a possible restoration of childhood life and describes entrance exams as a last chance to return within the family (p76-77). Almost immediately, she also thinks family no longer matters, believes she cannot enter the university others want, and says she would want to refuse even if she could (p79). Yet she signed the prepared application (p80). The contradiction is part of the evidence; one side should not be selected as the hidden authentic wish while the other is treated as mere cover.

Neither her pessimistic prediction nor her last-chance formulation is an independently verified account of what the family will do. The text names no current family speaker, specific university or present confrontation. It supports ambivalence, compliance with a prepared route and an unresolved desire for belonging. It does not establish the precise cause of exclusion, the identity or intentions of every family member, or a diagnosis of the household.

Her perception that Miyagi's wallpaper now looks familiar (p84-85) is placed against that troubled interior.
