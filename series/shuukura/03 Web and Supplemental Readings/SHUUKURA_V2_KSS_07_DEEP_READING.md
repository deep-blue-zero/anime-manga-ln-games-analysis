## Friendship that survives and company that is missed

Ami begins by separating a friendship's durability from the ease of enjoying it. Distance does not cancel the category, but it changes ordinary access. The first distinction prevents her repeated boredom from becoming evidence that university has failed her: she explicitly enjoys college and has new friends. What she misses is the familiar trio's ready availability, especially when a holiday invites thoughts of the old group. Her dissatisfaction is selective and situated. The story gives an independent cost to the move without making Ami a person whose entire social life has disappeared. (O002;07p1–5,26–39.)

The telephone exchange lets that cost become a performance. The friends address each other with playful「さん」formality, and Ami supplies a heavy voice and a deliberately serious announcement. Maika then reminds her that she already knew there would be no Golden Week return. The surprise is staged; the wish beneath it is real. Ami's insistence that her words contain no false feeling belongs beside her candid admission of the staging. This is a useful limit on a broad reliability judgment: the narration makes the performance legible rather than deceiving the reader about it. It also prevents sincerity from proving that the performance exerts no pressure. (O002;07p7–35.)

Ami similarly distinguishes her stated respect for friends' chosen paths from her recurring complaints. She says she does not intend to interfere or accuse anyone of betrayal, yet remembers complaining several times because Shiori had earlier said she would stay locally. A changed plan can disappoint a friend without establishing a promise to remain for that friend's sake. Ami's declaration and her behavior therefore remain in tension. Respect for another's agency is partly articulated, while the cost of that agency continues to be voiced. The text does not require either a perfectly selfless friend or a total attempt to control another's future. (O002;07p37–45.)

The actual apology makes the tension consequential. When Ami says she alone is far from the others, Maika apologizes. Ami immediately counterapologizes and explains that she wanted to gripe rather than receive an apology. These are observable conversational moves, even though Maika's private emotional state is unavailable. The clarification tries to change how the complaint is received; it cannot retrospectively prove that the complaint had no effect. Their friendship includes a small negotiation over what a listener is being asked to bear. (O002;07p47–50.)

## Curiosity with an attractive imagined outcome

Room sharing is attractive to Ami partly because she still lives at her family home. That is her own explanation of an emerging longing, not an established plan to move or a complete causal theory. Shiori's household offers an interesting contrast to Ami's present life, while its reported kinship arrangement remains unfamiliar enough to invite speculation. Not having heard stories about a relative gives Ami a reason for curiosity; it does not identify the actual roommate. (O002;07p50–65.)

Her imagined handsome boyfriend makes the pleasure of speculation explicit. The thought moves from something that might be happening toward something that would be amusing. That narrowing matters: a preferred interesting story is different from a settled belief. Ami admits wanting to keep teasing Shiori for her own enjoyment, as an extension of high-school play. The modest word「慎ましやか」comically reduces the apparent size of that wish. It can read as affectionate self-mockery or as a minimizing account of interference; neither reading eliminates the later evidence of restraint. (O003;08p1–12.)

Maika shares the suspicion and supplies her own history of frustrated access. She reports that Shiori refuses visits and photographs; Ami adds that questions are diverted in both two-person and trio conversations. The roommate becomes a mosaic of missing detail. The absence concerns household identity and access, not whether the friends have ever met Sendai as a schoolmate. Nor does it prove that Sendai agreed to a blanket ban on guests. The novel's private account instead identifies Shiori's external no-friends-at-home story as a makeshift cover, while the actual household guest terms have their own evidence. (O003/O005;08p14–30;LN05p005451–458;R034/R037.)

The hated-friend joke and fake crying turn that exclusion into shared play. Ami explicitly calls the remark a joke, and both laugh. Those cues prevent the words from becoming a certified belief that Shiori hates Maika. They do not establish that every cost of withheld information is imaginary. The reader's later access to Maika's hurt belongs to a different situation, after a specific new disclosure. A comic register cannot settle all emotional consequences across those contexts. (O003/O005;08p21–26;later LN06p00825–110.)

## A limit actually followed

The strongest evidence for restraint is an action. Maika says that forcing an inquiry would feel wrong. Ami agrees, privately places Shiori's feelings ahead of curiosity, and then changes the topic. The desire to tease and the decision to stop are both hers. This makes Ami more complex than an outside account of intrusive banter alone could show. It does not establish a permanent rule against questions or prove that college has simply made her morally better. The new setting and newly available interior narration are competing explanations for the difference from earlier high-school scenes. (O003;08p32–40.)

The next topic also preserves her unmet want. Ami returns to the holiday question, calls Maika's summer plan a promise, and asks that Shiori come back with her. Maika can reassure Ami about her own intention; she cannot bind the absent Shiori. The story ends with waiting and an insistent wish for high-school-like play, rather than a performed reunion. Privacy restraint and pressure for company concern different domains, so the first need not erase the second. One two-part call contains repeated requests and reassurances, not several independent promises or completed returns. (O002–003;07p30–32;08p40–52.)

Maika likewise has responsibilities independent of being Ami's audience. Her short break constrains travel. She joins the entertaining speculation, apologizes when the distance complaint turns personal, proposes the local limit and offers a future period of availability. Her own motives remain partially inferred because this source gives her voice through Ami's first person. The distinction matters when the later novel gives Maika a private account: more interior access is an additional source position, not proof that Ami understood it during this call.

The reader already knows who lives with Shiori, but neither kinship doubt nor a boyfriend fantasy gives that identity to the friends at this endpoint. Later Sendai actually tells Maika, and later Maika privately considers hurt, questions and reasons for waiting. LN06p008is Maika's first person, explicitly named by Shiori; it is not Ami's. This division is essential to the story's social argument. Shiori's concealed household affects people who value her without granting those people every private fact as a right. Ami's wish for company, Maika's constraints, their curiosity and their actual local withdrawal all contribute independent evidence. The source makes possible a substantial individual Ami study because it connects desire, self-explanation, speech, choice and consequence across a life transition.
