## A festival shared with friends, not with each other

The title, 「私たちの文化祭は始まらない」, distinguishes the school festival that is already happening from a festival Miyagi and Sendai might experience together. Both have company, work or entertainment, and places to go. Their private relation does not supply a shared public occasion. Miyagi looks toward the classroom entrance and finds people other than Sendai; Sendai looks through a window and sees Miyagi only at a distance. The spatial arrangement gives the reader two forms of attention while denying the characters an actual encounter. Their separate friend groups are not empty substitutes for the central pair. They are part of why a shared festival requires an initiative neither makes in the represented story. (O002–003; p-001p10–21,57–74,121–139,164–185.)

Miyagi begins by treating Sendai's qualified remark about coming to see her as a joke. By the end of her section she is certain Sendai has forgotten it. Her repeated attention to arrivals complicates her insistence that she is not taking the remark seriously. Sendai's later section supplies counterevidence to the forgetting claim: she remembers the words, regrets having spoken them, and feels bound by them. That does not turn the remark into a mutual promise. 「見に行こうかな」 remains a tentative utterance, and Miyagi is not shown extracting or receiving an unconditional commitment. The consequence arises partly from each character's relation to words that neither gives a shared interpretation. (O002–003; p9–10,63–68,94–95,178–180.)

The two self-descriptions should not be reduced to an identical denial. Miyagi says the words merely remain in her head and predicts that Sendai has no time to think of her. Sendai says she did not search for Miyagi, then keeps looking toward the courtyard and thinks of Miyagi's classroom when asked where to go. One makes an unsupported claim about another's forgetting; the other reports being caught by her own earlier speech while withholding a preference from friends. Both attention patterns exceed their dismissive explanations, but the narrated evidence and their available knowledge differ.

## A required smile and an actual laugh

The café provides a precise distinction between doing a task and performing its expected feeling. Miyagi delivers food and drinks, navigates crowded desks and takes part in an organized shift. She does not simply fail to function. What she dislikes is the additional expectation that correct service should come with a convincing smile. Her childhood shop-play recollection frames assigned roles as something one is expected to enact even without wanting them. The language of hell and imprisonment is hyperbolic criticism of that expectation, not a report of literal institutional punishment. Her resulting claim that she will never choose customer-service work is a character's prospective self-description, not an independently tested universal limit. (O002; p12–35,49–61,73–74.)

Ami and Maika have a credible alternative experience: each says the café is enjoyable. Ami's shop-play comparison works for her even though it fails for Miyagi. Their teasing can put pressure on Miyagi's expression, yet the scene also contains ordinary friendship and actual amusement. Miyagi laughs when Ami theatrically protests being ignored, and the three negotiate where to go next. The same person who cannot supply a service smile can respond spontaneously to friends. The difference is situational evidence, not proof that one expression is her only authentic face. (O002–003; p24–48,75–92.)

The proposed video of Miyagi's smile remains a proposal that Miyagi rejects. No recording is represented. Likewise, the maid café is a hypothetical alternative that Miyagi says she would only help backstage; it is not the café they actually operate. These limits matter when comparing the story retrospectively with the novels' later disputes about appearance, photographs and performed enjoyment. The supplement supplies an earlier context for a problem, without proving that later Miyagi consciously derives every judgment about Sendai's smile from this festival.

## Sendai's privacy does not mean she has no preference

Umina and Mariko want fortune-telling as entertainment. Its predictive accuracy is not the standard that makes it worth doing for them. Sendai's hesitation concerns the teller's status as a barely known fellow student and the classroom's poor privacy. A topic acceptable in that setting must be one that other people can overhear. This is a concrete social constraint on disclosure, not proof of an absence of questions or private interest. (O003; p100–120,159–162.)

When Sendai sees Miyagi, the apparent smile is qualified by distance: she cannot inspect details and does not use a phone to zoom in. The reader has already seen Miyagi laugh with friends, but Sendai has not seen the whole café sequence or heard Miyagi's private thoughts about arrivals. Sendai's impression of enjoyment can therefore be plausible and incomplete at once. Neither Miyagi's earlier awkward smile nor Sendai's later distant observation supplies a total emotional record. The focalization changes what the same public activity can reveal. (O002–003; p89,125–139.)

Sendai redirects Umina's attention from the window to the choice of a fortune topic. She invents an important preparatory-school test as a respectable reason to ask about next week. The narration expressly identifies that test as nonexistent. Its candid disclosure permits the reader to distinguish false dialogue from a narrator who is concealing the falsehood from the reader. Her subsequent admission that she would like to go to Miyagi's classroom also qualifies the public answer that she has no destination. Whether there remains a useful opportunity to see Miyagi there is a separate problem: Sendai infers from the courtyard sighting that Miyagi will not be serving. That is a limited inference from earlier observation, not knowledge of Miyagi's current location. (O003; p133–175.)

Sendai accepts the gymnasium plan and does not try to overturn Umina's decision. She still has a concealed preferred alternative and a reason for regarding it as unavailable. Compliance in this case does not demonstrate a person without wishes. Nor does the concealed preference establish that her friend group has no value. The story records a particular accommodation and an unshared interest, leaving the private importance of that interest partly unnamed.

## Supporting lives and the limits of the pairing

Ami's enthusiasm for the band and her boyfriend, Maika's enjoyment of service and destination banter, Umina's plans, and Mariko's interest in romance fortune-telling give both groups independently motivated activity. 杉川君 is the already reported boyfriend from the novels; this story does not identify the admired guitarist as him or provide a new given name or confirmed reading of the surname. Ami's historical boyfriend reference also must not overwrite the later breakup report at the adult novel boundary. (O002–004; p77–90,102–108,147–150.)

The ending shows Sendai following her friends toward the gym, not an actual second-day concert performance or a private reunion afterward. Miyagi's first-day attendance is already reported; Ami's courtyard-food and magic-show itinerary is proposed, with Sendai later independently observing Miyagi in the courtyard. Keeping proposed, observed and completed actions distinct prevents an attractive shared-scene reconstruction from replacing the text.

Placed after the nine independent novel readings, this supplement adds historical evidence for guarded attention, audience-sensitive speech and the difference between a performed smile and situational enjoyment. The later university festival is a different event with an actual joint occasion; its existence cannot be written into the earlier prospective record. The supplement does not establish a consciously named romantic state at this school festival, a fixed age or exact day, a complete failure of ordinary friendship, or a promise that one character objectively broke. Its stronger contribution is narrower: the reader can see that words and attention persist where each character's available account leaves them unshared.
