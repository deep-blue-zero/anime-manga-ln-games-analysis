## Consequences and a chosen later call

Miyagi places the completed midterms beside the earlier incident with Sendai. She reports opening her textbooks and attempting the familiar work of memorizing formulas or dates, but failing to settle into retention; her previously middling grades have fallen. The distinction between attempted study and no study matters. It gives the experience a practical consequence in Miyagi’s narrated life without establishing a measured cognitive impairment or an independently verified single cause. The attribution to Sendai is Miyagi’s own explanation. No numerical result is shown. (Native1–8; PROP-KAK-JP-E0035-001–004.)

The later summons adds another concrete consequence. Miyagi identifies this as her first call since the kiss. Today has brought no particular good or unpleasant event, yet she called because she did not want Sendai to think the kiss had made her withdraw. She expected to appear unaffected and now regrets that expectation. Her concern therefore includes Sendai’s possible interpretation of an absence. The text does not say Sendai actually formed that interpretation, or that avoiding it exhausts Miyagi’s reasons for wanting company. (Native12–27; PROP-KAK-JP-E0035-007–012.)

Calling and regretting the call are both represented. Miyagi has acted to continue the encounter while finding its changed stakes difficult to manage. The current book change, cider and bed-backrest position provide ordinary room conduct around that difficulty. Sendai’s apparently unawkward face is something Miyagi sees; it cannot supply Sendai’s complete interior. Her beside position is an actual local spatial fact, while Miyagi’s comparison with the former bed position supplies the prior expectation. Familiar conduct can continue while its significance has changed. (Native10,18,22,29–31.)

## Valued difference and an undisclosed magazine motive

The magazine conversation makes two kinds of knowledge available. Sendai actually says she does not much like the magazine and confirms deliberately buying it. Miyagi’s narration explicitly leaves the purchase purpose unrevealed. A voiced preference is therefore available without motive transparency. Miyagi’s idea that the cover language would suit Ibaraki, and her account of the effort involved in being agreeable to others, remain her social explanation of Sendai. Neither Ibaraki’s recommendation nor Sendai’s reason for this purchase is established. (Native33–52; PROP-KAK-JP-E0035-014–020.)

Miyagi also supplies a discriminating counterfactual. An accommodating Sendai might make her calmer, but Miyagi thinks she would not have kept calling that version of Sendai to this room for so long. This gives positive evidence of particular-person value: greater ease is not the only thing Miyagi values in their company. Sendai’s independent, sometimes unwelcome responses have a place in that preference. The counterfactual remains imagined; it does not reveal an authentic essence beneath a wholly false public persona, or prove that every difficult act is desired. Miyagi’s wish for a particular response and possible privilege remains a live rival to an uncomplicated account of valuing independence. (Native53; PROP-KAK-JP-E0035-021.)

The source keeps ordinary use and irritation visible. Sendai turns pages, asks questions and closes the magazine. Miyagi interprets a light voice and raised mouth corner as teasing, but her denial of looking is a spoken response rather than an objective attention census. Her later impression that the reading was not absorbed does not mean the book was never read. The same object subsequently becomes a means of redirecting the interaction and proposing a hairstyle. These practical uses require no total symbolic ownership explanation.

## Grades as a managed information exchange

(Native55–65; PROP-KAK-JP-E0035-022–027.)

The two uses of ordinary also have unequal evaluative contexts. Miyagi regards a clever person’s ordinary as good by her own standard; Sendai expressly distinguishes her result from good.
