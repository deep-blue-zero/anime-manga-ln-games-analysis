Miyagi Shiori and Sendai Hazuki reach graduation and agree to become roommates for four years. Their route includes actual study, ordinary care, changed tastes, preemptive separation, resisted and ignored limits, unnecessary bargaining, and a unilateral housing arrangement. The resulting agreement has meaningful Miyagi endorsement while preserving questions about how the choice was structured. The postgraduation bonus ends before either move or university entry. Neither reliable consent practices nor comprehensive mutual knowledge has been established by sharing a future address.

## Retrospective argument through V04

### A deadline changes how an ordinary day can be used

Volume 4 makes graduation an event the characters actually reach. Its important movement is not simply from separation anxiety to a decision to live together. Miyagi Shiori and Sendai Hazuki repeatedly try to make the remaining interval serve incompatible purposes: a familiar routine, preparation for independent futures, a place to obtain reassurance, and an occasion to preserve memories before ending the relationship. The same room can support successful study and obstruct it. An apparently welcome kiss can make Sendai fear departure. A kept promise can fail to make Miyagi believe the next one. Those conflicts make the final housing agreement intelligible without making it an uncomplicated resolution.

The opening disappointment is pointedly small. Miyagi has done better than expected in her finals, but Sendai does not ask. Miyagi could volunteer the result and does not. Later Sendai reveals that withholding a winter-study invitation was intentional: this time she wanted Miyagi to ask. These are distinct omissions, not proof that Sendai strategically designed every disappointing silence. The alternation gives the reader an explanation of one omission while preserving uncertainty about the other. Miyagi's wish that Sendai should take responsibility for her growing desire also remains Miyagi's allocation of responsibility, rather than the narrative's objective assignment (O002–006/O014).

The calendar gives this indirectness a cost. A holiday is approaching, another year will end, examinations will restrict visits, and graduation will remove the institutional proximity that has made encounters possible even without a summons. Miyagi understands before the final decision that attending the same or nearby universities would not reproduce their present relationship. The problem is not only geographical distance. A future with intermittent meetings would expose her to a Sendai whose other life she cannot inspect. The explicit form of that concern arrives at graduation; its earlier pressure is visible in the difficulty of asking for a single winter visit (O004/O111–112).

### A bargain need not have been necessary to become consequential

On December 23 Miyagi initiates a brief kiss and then asks Sendai to teach her during winter break. She uses a want Sendai has expressed as a means of making her own request possible. Sendai expected a request, but not this particular offer. That distinction rules out an account in which the whole exchange, including Miyagi's initiative, had already been choreographed by Sendai. It also rules out treating Miyagi as merely a passive recipient of a bargain someone else proposed. Her chosen means nevertheless gives Sendai leverage over a need she finds difficult to state plainly (O011–014).

Sendai's private narration is unusually damaging to a neutral-price interpretation of the ensuing negotiation. She knows the requested kiss has already been sufficient, calls her additional objections unreasonable, and anticipates disregarding a refusal. She avoids looking at Miyagi's face because that might make her stop. These are source-specific descriptions of choices, not psychological diagnoses. By an action-specific agency criterion, knowing that an exchange has been satisfied does not authorize extending its bodily scope; a prior reciprocal condition does not make a new intrusion permissible. Miyagi's mixed pleasure, later initiative, and continued willingness to admit Sendai remain material evidence without retrospectively authorizing each overridden limit (O015–019).

The delayed interlude adds a crucial counterfactual stated by Sendai herself: if Miyagi had not raised winter study, Sendai would have offered it. The companionship was not available only at the price Miyagi imagined she had to pay. Miyagi does not receive this knowledge. The reader therefore has grounds to question the necessity of the bargain while still recognizing that it becomes a real constraint in Miyagi's understanding. This is a strong example of how reciprocal desire can coexist with an informational advantage and a harmful use of it. Calling the desire mutual cannot dissolve that advantage; calling the interaction coercive in particular respects cannot erase Sendai's genuine wish to spend time studying together (O022/O069).

The black-cat present also has more than one function. Sendai prepared it before the encounter, then chooses a moment when it might improve the atmosphere. She selected something she thought Miyagi would be less likely to reject, rather than demonstrating assured knowledge of Miyagi's favorite animal. Miyagi accepts it and quietly thanks her. This change in atmosphere is real. It supplies no evidence that the preceding excesses have been repaired. The story lets gratitude and unresolved grievance occupy the same evening (O020–024).

### Trust is not the sum of visible signs

Miyagi's explanation of mistrust becomes more specific when she connects Sendai's broken promises with remembered failures by her parents. Her father did not reliably return or share meals when promised; her mother's promise to remain was not fulfilled. Sendai's actual breaches give that learning a present object. It would therefore be reductive to call every doubt a projection of an irrelevant past. It would be equally reductive to treat the past as a complete causal key that proves the motive of every act. The represented history supports a particular account of expectation and vulnerability, with parental intentions and much of the original history still unavailable (O034–037).

The pendant is a test of one promise with a visible answer. During the sleepover Miyagi asks to see it; Sendai first insists that she ask rather than silently inspect, then allows it. It is being worn. Sendai has temporarily removed it for bathing and put it back, so neither continuous physical wear nor a deliberate betrayal through the bathing exception should be invented. Miyagi later admits that seeing it did not achieve the reassurance she wanted. The failure is hers as a character trying to use a sign to secure a future, not a preregistered analyst forecast (O043–049/O056).

Sendai's refusal to guarantee every possible promise has a credible ethical rationale. An unlimited assurance may be impossible to honor, and refusing to offer it can be more responsible than saying whatever will soothe the other person. That argument cannot excuse choosing to violate a clear, reasonable limit moments later. The scene needs both propositions. Otherwise criticism of Sendai's behavior turns into a demand for dishonesty, or her reasonable refusal becomes an all-purpose defense of unrelated conduct. Miyagi has not told her the full family history, so Sendai's failure to identify its immediate significance also cannot be equated with knowingly disregarding a disclosed explanation (O049–053).

A later visit supplies a small but consequential counterexample to an inevitable-inspection model. Miyagi considers asking to see the pendant and decides not to, remembering that Sendai has been wearing it. Sendai displays it anyway because she expects to be asked. Here Miyagi's prediction of her own next action and Sendai's prediction of Miyagi diverge; Sendai's confident familiarity is locally wrong. Neither the earlier repeated checking nor this single restraint warrants a permanent trait verdict. Their sequence demonstrates development that remains provisional and easy for the other person to miss (O091).

### Domestic knowledge has practical content

The overnight visit is conditional: actual teaching and no unwelcome escalation. Sendai reports contacting home; the source does not provide the conversation or verify every aspect of parental approval. The meal is cooked, Miyagi volunteers to peel and cut vegetables, and no new injury occurs. Her earlier difficulty with a knife must not become a permanent incapacity or evidence of intentional self-injury. Sendai is not a faultless domestic expert either. Their available ingredients require adaptation, and she initially wants distance as well as a manageable task (O037–040).

The disagreement about stew over rice is useful because both people state preferences. Sendai tries Miyagi's combination and finds it acceptable. At breakfast Miyagi tries butter and jam together after initially preferring them separately and enjoys the result. These are actual low-stakes accommodations with room for a preference to be expressed and revised. Their significance does not depend on claiming that both people are equally free in every other interaction. Small successes show what a more ordinary negotiation can look like; they do not compensate for a failed refusal elsewhere (O041/O060–062).

Miyagi's morning care is also materially specific. She prepares two breakfasts, has a toothbrush ready, checks whether the jam is still usable, and washes dishes while Sendai changes clothes. She says she almost never skips breakfast. The evidence therefore does not support a blanket description of chronic self-neglect. What changes is not her discovery that food must be eaten, but the value of sharing it. She explicitly associates Sendai with meals becoming enjoyable and with being less willing to return to eating alone. Taste is a register of a changed everyday life, not only an elaborate symbol attached to it (O059–062).

The later shopping and nikujaga episode extends this material basis. Miyagi buys tea instead of her own soda when she chooses only one heavy bottle, obtains ingredients for a meal, and wishes Sendai could share the load. When Sendai independently proposes shopping together, Miyagi refuses. The matching wishes have not become shared knowledge or an agreed practice. Sendai researches a recipe she does not know, declines to guarantee its quality, and makes a meal Miyagi likes. Useful effort and skill limits coexist. Their domestic future is being rehearsed in partial acts while one participant still rejects naming it (O085/O088–090).

### Closeness can be sought as reassurance and still fail

The sleepover alternates between successful limits, pressured contact, and new invitations. Sendai leaves Miyagi's bed when told it is Miyagi's territory. Miyagi later enters the guest futon, asserts her rights as host, and eventually insists on sharing it instead of accepting Sendai's proposed swap. The resulting proximity is not something Sendai simply imposes on Miyagi. It is also not a scene in which the host's ownership settles every question about the guest's space. Their competing territorial claims expose how readily a rule can be extended to preserve one's preferred outcome (O045–047/O054–055).

Miyagi later identifies an aim of her earlier physical approach: she hoped contact would make Sendai's words believable and remove anxiety. It increased anxiety instead. Wanting closeness, pleasure, retaliation, and a wish for reassurance can overlap without being the same motive. This explicit account should revise an interpretation that sees only retaliation, but it cannot prove that Miyagi consciously understood the same mechanism at every earlier stage. Nor does a failed attempt to obtain reassurance make Sendai responsible for furnishing unlimited contact or comprehensive knowledge of her life (O056).

Naming remains another specific limit. Miyagi says that being called Shiori by Sendai feels special in a way it does not with Maika or Ami. She attaches a consequence to its use during the winter arrangement: loss of the visit-kiss condition. The text does not test that new consequence while it is in force. Miyagi's quiet use of Hazuki toward a sleeping Sendai also does not establish reciprocal waking permission. At graduation Sendai asks for permission to use Shiori and is refused. The housing agreement therefore changes one major category while leaving the naming boundary intact (O057/O059/O111/O119).

### The wish to end first becomes legible without becoming inevitable

Miyagi's request that Sendai say she hates her initially reaches the reader through Sendai's bewilderment. Sendai refuses to give the phrase its demanded force, tries to see Miyagi's face, and resists returning the pendant. Miyagi uses the blindfold and pendant to control access and causes actual pain by pulling the chain. The source explicitly distinguishes Sendai's fear of possible choking from actual strangulation: the latter has not occurred. Maintaining that distinction strengthens the criticism of the evidenced force rather than requiring an exaggerated event (O081–083).

The next Miyagi chapter gives an explanation Sendai does not possess. Miyagi thinks she did worse than expected on the common examination and lied when she called it so-so. Failure could prevent her from attending the geographically relevant university. Rather than have an external result force separation, she wants an ending she can initiate. The word hate is imagined as an eraser for an increasingly marked calendar and altered tastes. This directly supports a recurrence of preemptive ending, while the specific examination uncertainty supplies a new trigger. It does not make an actual failed admission or a completed breakup part of the earlier scene (O084–087).

Miyagi's subsequent pause in visits also needs its own cause. She says she decided the previous day to study alone until her examinations were over. She acknowledges that studying with Sendai is useful and more enjoyable; she still wants to do what she can independently. Sendai protests, then agrees, and their next meeting occurs after the required examinations. This is meaningful agency and a real practical limit. Reducing it to retaliation for a painful encounter would discard Miyagi's chronology and her stated competing priority (O094/O097).

Sendai's withdrawal from a refused kiss in that same conversation matters as well. She lets go and stops pressing; Miyagi subsequently offers a bounded permission. Without Sendai's own account of that moment, her silence cannot be certified as a consciously engineered trick. The later offer is not permission that existed throughout the preceding refusal. Its temporal position makes a difference. The reading must also retain Sendai's interference with Miyagi's books, rather than using the successful retreat to make the whole encounter considerate (O095–096).

### Supporting people offer knowledge the central pair lacks

Maika is a friend with a legitimate concern about what she has disclosed. She apologizes for telling Sendai about Miyagi's university possibility, explains why she thought the two were close, and stops pursuing a difficult question. Miyagi can be grateful for that restraint while privately distracted by Sendai. At graduation Maika notices Miyagi's tears and offers practical care without knowing the paid relationship. Accurate attention does not require omniscience. Her proposed outing with Miyagi and Ami also gives their friendship an explicit future beyond school (O026–030/O109).

Ami's decision to stay locally and her tears at the prospect of the others leaving make separation a broader social fact. Miyagi comforts her while thinking about Sendai. That divided attention is not the same as having ceased to value Ami. The scene gives both ordinary care and Miyagi's own recognition that she is preoccupied. It also prevents a literal reading of Miyagi's fear that losing Sendai would mean having nobody (O084/O109).

Umina has concerns that do not originate in the central relationship: a boyfriend, holiday plans, and a dispute with her parents about taking a part-time job before entrance examinations. Mariko challenges the timing and later intervenes when Sendai hesitates over an outing, drawing Umina away and leaving Sendai room to choose. The reason for Mariko's intervention is not disclosed. Its effect is observable without inventing a secret understanding of Miyagi. Sendai later identifies a specific cinema habit she shares with Miyagi more easily than with these friends, while also saying that her friends match her in more other respects. Particular compatibility need not discredit all the other relationships (O010/O072/O079/O104).

### Farewell preparations can look like the desired relationship

After examinations, Miyagi initiates a chocolate gift and becomes more talkative and receptive over several visits. Sendai is pleased and uneasy. She associates unusually agreeable behavior with an unfavorable ending and partly recognizes that being suspicious of ordinary pleasantness is a problem in her own response. The later Miyagi narration confirms a farewell component: she allowed herself extra memories because the relationship was about to end, then found the memories heavier and more persistent than expected. Sendai's fear is therefore partly well founded, but it is not proof that Miyagi experienced no genuine desire or pleasure (O097–102/O110).

The chocolate exchange preserves unequal knowledge. Sendai made her current batch only for Miyagi, but agrees with Miyagi's assumption that she also made some for her friends. This is an actual unnecessary lie by Sendai's own account. Miyagi's purchased chocolates happen to equal the number Sendai gave her the previous year; Sendai does not establish that Miyagi deliberately selected the count. These small distinctions prevent a confident retrospective reconstruction from converting every material correspondence into a conscious reciprocal message (O098).

Both admissions become known through reports. Sendai has already told Miyagi that she passed and received congratulations. Miyagi then telephones to report admission to Maika's university, fulfilling the result-report undertaking, and redirects the conversation toward a movie. Admission, choosing to attend, finding housing, and actually relocating are separate states. Miyagi's direct decision to attend is not spoken to Sendai until after the outing. A reported plan for a dormitory also does not establish residence there (O101–103/O107).

The movie repeats the summer's meeting place and route, yet it is not simply a replay. Both like the action film; they remain through the credits; Miyagi initiates a gentle public handhold and says impending graduation makes being seen less important. Sendai accepts the hand, but cannot enjoy the favorable day without fearing its purpose. When Miyagi initiates kisses at home, Sendai herself stops to ask what they mean. She wants the familiar Miyagi to choose continuation, rather than obtain a compliant performance that looks like everything she has requested. That recognition is consequential even though it cannot undo other occasions when she has pushed for compliance (O103–107).

### A new category emerges through a pressured choice

At graduation Miyagi believes the relationship should end because she cannot tolerate a Sendai whose unknown life will continue elsewhere. She recognizes that confining Sendai is not realistic; her later admission that she has no right to dictate Sendai’s other relationships supplies a separate limit on her claim. Ending the relationship appears to her to be the available alternative. Her demand to recover the pendant follows this intention, yet she acknowledges that they never agreed she could reclaim it. Her recollection of its original command also compresses the earlier refusal, withdrawal, and voluntary concession. The accepted V03 sequence must survive that later shorthand (O111–113).

Sendai removes the pendant, then presents an apartment floor plan and proposes living together. She reports having already arranged the room, informed her parents that Miyagi would live there, and used saved payments for Miyagi's portion. Miyagi explicitly says she did not authorize the search or agree to live together. Her earlier failure to answer generic messages cannot supply that authorization: she says she would have answered and stopped the arrangement if she had known its purpose. The text supports criticism of unilateral planning and a misrepresentation to Sendai's parents; it does not show a complete legal contract or disclose every financial detail (O114–116).

The jar disclosure also changes Miyagi's available explanation of Sendai's visits. The money was retained rather than needed for consumption. Miyagi had treated an unpaid version of herself as having no reason to receive Sendai's time. The disclosure gives that self-assessment a powerful counterexample, though it need not make every paid interaction secretly altruistic. Miyagi herself recognizes that money she gave Sendai was hers to use freely. The central issue is therefore not simply theft of Miyagi's money, but the use of those funds within an arrangement Miyagi had not yet authorized (O087/O115).

Sendai offers two alternatives: take the pendant and end contact, or take the envelope and become roommates. She says she will follow Miyagi's choice. That statement could protect a genuine veto; the unchosen outcome does not test whether she would honor it. The formulation also excludes intermediate possibilities such as occasional meetings, and Sendai's suggestion that she will find another roommate activates Miyagi's fear of an unknown person occupying Sendai's life. Miyagi experiences the options as directed toward a predetermined answer. Her experience, the actual narrowing of options, and her subsequent choice all need to remain visible (O116–117).

Miyagi takes the pendant but refastens it on Sendai and agrees to become roommates for four years. She changes the proposed object-choice grammar: taking the pendant does not perform the prescribed separation. Roommate is a category available without first agreeing to call each other friends. Neither a romantic confession nor a validated domestic arrangement follows automatically. Her later statement that she chose for herself and does not regret it supplies affirmative agency and ongoing endorsement. It does not retroactively authorize Sendai's unilateral preparations or settle the fairness of the original offer (O118–119/O124).

### Packing preserves a future that cannot be guaranteed

The internal bonus advances beyond graduation but stops before the move. Miyagi's father offers packing help; a mover is reserved; the old room will remain. These facts qualify a total account of familial material abandonment. They do not establish that her father knows every detail of the roommate arrangement. Miyagi refuses help because sorting the room resembles sorting memories. Her privacy is directed at a specific task, not evidence that she can never accept assistance (O121–123).

The album makes absence materially legible: repeated maternal appearances give way to absence, then printing and eventually photography cease. The narration does not specify every date or photographer. The books, retained blouse, crocodile, and cat present different practical and mnemonic choices.
