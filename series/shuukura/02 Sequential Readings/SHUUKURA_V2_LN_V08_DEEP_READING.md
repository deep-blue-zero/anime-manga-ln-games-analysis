## Possession becomes an explicit problem

Volume 8 makes the desire to possess another person more articulate while repeatedly showing that its available signs cannot satisfy it. Miyagi Shiori acquires more of what she has wanted: Sendai Hazuki accepts marks, wears earrings Miyagi selects, gives her time, returns home, and provides unmistakably personal gifts. These acquisitions do not produce a stable sense that Sendai is securely hers. Miyagi states that satisfaction lasts only briefly before becoming insufficient again. This is her present self-description, supported by the successive failures of different kinds of reassurance; it is not a diagnosis or a claim that every future interaction must follow the same course (p-012p23–42,151–169; O042–044).

The café makes the problem concrete. Miyagi knows that Sendai will return, and Sendai has invited her to visit. The unfamiliar part of Sendai is publicly accessible. What Miyagi cannot obtain there is the exclusive availability she associates with their home. A competent employee must attend to other people. Miyagi consequently experiences the café's Sendai as someone who is not hers, despite having described Sendai as entirely hers during the preceding night. The opposition concerns the limits of a claim across contexts. It does not establish two different Sendais, or an objectively false public personality masking a uniquely authentic private one (p-004p85–100,162–183; p-005p337; p-006p5–40; O003–004/O011/O013).

Miyagi's proposed solutions are themselves divided. Hidden marks give her private knowledge that customers and colleagues lack. Visible earrings make her presence portable beyond the home. Yet making the earrings conspicuous before Maika would invite questions, expose the gift's origin, and intensify Miyagi's own desire to make her claim more unmistakable. She therefore asks Sendai to make a visible sign less noticeable. Control of Sendai's appearance becomes a means of managing a feeling Miyagi admits she cannot manage within herself. The earrings' insufficiency does not mean that the gift is meaningless. Miyagi's concern that her selection should not diminish Sendai's beauty is real recipient-specific care. Her wish that Sendai remain recognizably herself also conflicts with her wish to restrict what that independent person may do (p-006p195–270; p-010p27–39; p-012p23–54; O015–016/O036/O042).

Sendai finds pleasure in being called Miyagi's possession, sometimes hearing it as if she were being told she is loved. The comparison remains Sendai's. Miyagi has not supplied the missing declaration or accepted an equivalent reciprocal status. Sendai recognizes this asymmetry and still wants more of the words. Her willing attachment gives the language substantial affective force, without converting it into a general right over her body, work or time. Indeed, Sendai continues to choose employment and asks Miyagi to authorize a decision whose outcome she says is already settled. The claim of belonging can enable a request for approval while leaving the independent decision intact (p-009p187–211; p-012p98–149; O031/O043).

The novel's central problem is consequently larger than whether either woman possesses enough evidence of affection. They acquire convincing evidence and still struggle over what it permits them to expect. A sign can reassure, solicit a response, conceal a motive or become a demand. Its force changes with the person using it, the audience and the next action.

## The history of a rule and the enforcement of a boundary

The distinction between a rule and its application is especially important in the employment scenes. At the entering boundary, Sendai has promised to contact Miyagi when she will be late. On the first October workday, Miyagi tries to add a penalty for returning too late even with contact. Sendai identifies that addition as new. Neither supplies a precise clock threshold, and Miyagi later waives that day's penalty. The later Saturday is different: Sendai expressly acknowledges returning late without being able to contact Miyagi. Work pressure explains the failure but is not an agreed exception. The following Sunday is different again: both recognize that Sendai has not returned late enough to incur a penalty (p-004p85–100,228–237; p-005p143,212; p-006p200–203,269–270; O003/O005/O008/O010/O015–016).

These differences prevent the penalty scenes from becoming an undifferentiated succession of legitimate punishments. Miyagi proposes canceling a shift even while acknowledging that Sendai will not do it. Bodily marking then expands beyond the missed notification. Sendai reports a later practice attached specifically to café workdays, which she says she never agreed to as a rule. After the café's first term actually ends, marks continue in other circumstances. The recurrence is real, but recurrence does not establish an original mutual agreement, an unchanged scope or a standing permission for the next act (p-005p168–178,285–340; p-007p132–154,174–202; p-009p39–41; O009–011/O018–019/O029).

The photograph rule provides an unusually clear test. After Sendai takes and refuses to delete a private photograph, Miyagi accepts its retention while obtaining an explicit agreement that future photographs require her permission. Sendai confirms the requirement and swears to it through Miyagi's earring. Later Sendai asks for a joint photograph, receives an explicit refusal, and takes it before Miyagi can get away. At the Christmas Eve party, Sendai also describes using Maika's presence because Miyagi will not openly complain. The distinction between the private explicit refusal and the party's ordinary group posing should be preserved. In both cases the required fresh grant from Miyagi is absent; the private case establishes a particularly unambiguous failure of the new undertaking (p-008p585–606; p-011p147–152; p-013p103–114; O028/O040/O046).

A reader can plausibly understand some photographs as ordinary friendly spontaneity. Maika's pleasure in taking them is sincere, and Miyagi sometimes poses rather than openly stopping the event. That context does not erase the later agreement or Sendai's stated effort to get ahead of Miyagi's objection. Social normality supplies Sendai with an effective means of acting; it does not by itself answer the question of permission. Conversely, the record contains actual accommodations: a refused taste of pasta and dessert are accepted, Sendai changes the lighting after Miyagi asks her to wait, and a proposed further intimate step is withdrawn without Miyagi managing a complete verbal answer. These actions keep an account of agency specific rather than turning either character into a single rule of conduct (p-014p221–229,448–454,691–711; O050/O053/O056).

The same discipline applies to the older reciprocal bargain. Reinspection of V06 confirms that Miyagi knowingly accepted a future implication when initiating an encounter with Sendai. V08 does not simply invent that past. But the earlier exchange did not fix Christmas, every later action or an irrevocable present permission. Miyagi disputes Sendai's interpretation, Sendai acknowledges that she has not received a present affirmative reply, and Miyagi eventually gives a conditional answer about the atmosphere. The existence of an earlier bargain and the validity of a particular current action are separate questions (V06 p-014p219–238; V08 p-009p509–527; p-012p233–240; p-014p340–416; O035/O044/O052/O063).

## Miyagi's new word does not settle her self-understanding

At the festival, Miyagi explicitly names her feeling as possessiveness. The sequence matters. She begins by wondering why she reacts as she does, recognizes that she already knows, supplies the word, and then tries to disown it because she and Sendai are only roommates. She asks where the feeling comes from and stops the inquiry. This is more than an analyst's retrospective category. It is an evidenced development in Miyagi's conscious vocabulary. It is also less than a completed acceptance of the category or an explicit recognition of romantic love (p-008p369–398; O025).

The roommate designation works in two directions. It protects a relationship Miyagi fears changing too quickly, and it becomes an argument for treating her own feelings as things she should not have. Later she also uses the designation to allow conduct without accepting that it has changed their relationship. The contradiction is productive: the word supplies continuity while the experiences it is asked to contain become harder to dismiss. Miyagi's difficulty is not simply that she lacks the relevant vocabulary. She can find a word and then resist what following it might require (p-008p380–390,517–528; p-014p353–380,537–542; O025/O027/O052/O054).

Miyagi also recognizes limits to her authority. She immediately corrects the thought that only she should be allowed to ask Sendai for something. She knows she has no right to stop Maika's ordinary request or control Sendai's employment. These acknowledgments coexist with pressure, anger and unwanted conduct. Ethical recognition does not immediately reorganize desire. It can instead become another source of self-dislike, making an admission or a decision harder. The reading should preserve this partial self-awareness rather than choosing between an entirely innocent Miyagi who does not understand consequences and an entirely calculating Miyagi without conflict (p-006p201–237; p-008p54–65; p-012p130–149; O015/O021/O043).

During the Christmas encounter, Miyagi's private account supplies a further complication. She wants to confine Sendai so that she cannot leave, then considers letting Sendai go before she can escape. She questions whether that would really be easier. The opposing responses arise within the same interval and do not become a settled plan. They support a specific interpretation of anticipated loss and self-protection. They do not establish a clinical attachment classification, a permanent strategy or a conscious explanation that can be placed in Miyagi's earlier states (p-014p633–670; O055–056).

The retrospective bonus strengthens the case that Miyagi actively works on her own interpretations. Before the first café visit, she says her admission about disliking the empty house was not a lie, while wishing she had swallowed it. She fears that speaking a feeling makes it harder to stop recognizing it. Later, in the mainline account of the same Sunday, she tries to convince herself that she had not meant the admission. The bonus does not make the earlier reading prescient: it arrives after the mainline freeze and strengthens a previously qualified interpretation of self-rationalization (p-006p208–209; p-016p7–41; O015/O059).

Her recollections of birthdays and Christmas also require temporal and social precision. The lonely household memories are substantial evidence. They cannot erase the earlier mother who sang and photographed a birthday, or the celebrations with Maika and Ami already reported in V07. The bonus foregrounds the father-absent household and a period when gifts become money left on a table. It does not establish that nobody ever celebrated Miyagi, that she never had friends, or that the unknown reason for her mother's absence has now been revealed (p-016p34–40; V07 p-010p65, p-012p7–12; O059/O063).

## Sendai's confidence and her knowledge have different limits

Sendai offers an unusually revealing account of her own reasoning early in the volume. Faced with Miyagi's denial that she was waiting, Sendai assembles the available facts toward the conclusion she wants. In this case, the reader knows that Miyagi was waiting. The correct conclusion does not validate the method as a general means of knowing Miyagi. Sendai can interpret a local cue accurately while remaining mistaken about the meaning of another refusal, a facial expression or a gift delay (p-004p193–243; p-005p23–33; O005/O007).

The earrings expose this limitation. Sendai wants Miyagi to select something, then interprets delay and silence as troubling possibilities. Miyagi supplies a different account: Sendai is beautiful, so Miyagi feels she cannot choose the wrong thing. The later Miyagi chapter shows how the visible gift's possible effect on Sendai's appearance makes the decision difficult. When Miyagi finally has the earrings, Sendai again infers from the familiar marking practice that she has not obtained them. The inference is wrong. A recurring outward pattern does not have one reliable meaning (p-009p65–78,280–300,478–496; p-010p5–39; p-011p29–58; O029/O032/O035–036/O039).

Sendai's private honesty is also more complicated than complete transparency. She truthfully discloses some embarrassing information, and Miyagi begins to feel that Sendai would not lie to her. Sendai continues to keep her named love private. Her public account of the earrings combines a false purchase origin with true stone vocabulary and a genuinely acquired liking for blue. These are different kinds of statement. Neither the truth of a stone word nor the sincerity of a compliment makes the complete explanation candid. Equally, an explanation can protect a promise about Miyagi's privacy while deceiving a third party about its circumstances (p-011p171–208; p-013p19–30,50–67; p-014p482–495; O040/O045/O053).

Employment offers a counterweight to a portrait of Sendai as existing only for Miyagi. She enjoys teaching, responds to Kikyō's ordinary academic needs, refuses an additional tutoring offer, considers the relative value of different jobs, and retains a plan to finance adult independence. Miyagi's company matters to these decisions without exhausting them. Sendai's forecast that abandoning university would jeopardize family support and require more work also makes the permanent-stay fantasy practically self-defeating. That is Sendai's projected consequence, not a newly issued parental ultimatum (p-005p89–130; p-007p40–46; p-009p247–270; O008/O017/O032).

Her growing desire nevertheless alters what she can tolerate. She says that she once could accept Miyagi not loving her provided Miyagi did not love someone else, but now finds even that first possibility harder to accept. She uses the roommate form while wanting to widen it. Her later admission that she is nervous and fears Miyagi leaving is especially important: the pressure does not come from an entirely untroubled, omniscient manager of Miyagi's feelings. Recognizing Sendai's vulnerability helps explain her actions; it does not justify ignoring Miyagi's limits (p-009p160–172,204–208; p-014p448–457; O031/O053).

## A record can preserve the wrong thing for its subject

The first joint photographs give Sendai a new way to retain Miyagi's time. She regrets not having photographed the aquarium or birthdays. Miyagi experiences the same instrument as an unwelcome means of exposing and reviving feelings she wants to put away. She explicitly expects the festival photographs to bring back her possessiveness. The disagreement is therefore not fully explained by whether a particular picture is flattering (p-007p328–374; p-008p481–500; O020/O027).

Miyagi calls photographs more certain than memory. That is her theory of the record, not proof that a photograph contains the event's complete truth. Maika sees a face and asks Sendai what she did; Sendai explains that Miyagi's expression does not map reliably onto mood. The general caution about expressions is useful, but Sendai's claim that she did nothing omits her decision to take the photograph after a refusal. A visible image, an observer's inference and the depicted person's actual circumstances remain distinct evidence objects (p-011p246–273; O040).

Sendai's refusal to delete the photographs is followed by bargains about explanation and smiling. Miyagi is invited to disclose why she wants possession, or to smile beside Sendai as she does beside Maika. The photograph becomes a means of obtaining a response. Miyagi eventually chooses to limit future recording while allowing an existing image to remain. That is a consequential decision within perceived constraints, not proof that she originally wanted the image taken or is now content with every retained photograph (p-008p504–606; O027–028).

The material gifts make a related distinction between duration and value. Sendai gives Maika and Miyagi hand cream in different scents because the gift will be used up; she dislikes the idea of durable traces of that public exchange remaining in both rooms. Yet she has separately prepared a lasting blue scarf for Miyagi. Its later disclosure qualifies the reader's earlier impression that Sendai had nothing else to give. The first-person narration delays that plan rather than describing all of Sendai's possessions and intentions when the public gifts are opened (p-013p177–207,261–291; O046–047).

Miyagi's black-cat pencil case is also meant to travel into settings she does not control: university and tutoring. Sendai welcomes the request to use it there. The scarf warms Miyagi while preserving the memory of a particular night; Miyagi openly says she is glad to receive it. Functional care and an attempt to remain present in another person's life coexist. The volume does not require us to choose one as the only meaning of these objects (p-013p267–307; O047).

## The social world supplies information neither narrator owns

Maika's importance extends beyond making the central pair jealous. She organizes an occasion, states practical gift preferences, chooses warm mittens that Miyagi actually wants, and sees Miyagi smile. She is also candid about not having witnessed the domestic work Miyagi says she does. Sendai confirms that work, while adding her own untested prediction about how Miyagi would live alone. Maika's question exposes a gap in her access; it does not make her account of Miyagi authoritative over the household (p-013p148–174,244–253; O046–047).

Maika's gift also reveals a difference in disclosure. Miyagi has told Sendai she does not particularly want anything, yet can be pleased by the mittens. Sendai's hurt concerns being excluded from this ordinary knowledge, not simply wanting the same object herself. The proposed alternative of a scarf becomes consequential when Sendai has one prepared privately. Miyagi's relationships allow different requests, expectations and forms of gratitude; a single inventory of her tastes would not reproduce those interactions (p-012p81–85; p-013p128–174,274–300; p-014p5–8; O043/O046–048).

Asakura belongs to Miyagi and Maika's university life. Her nervousness around Sendai, borrowed-book connection and choice to work on Christmas Eve give her interests and obligations beyond observing the pair. Noto's gaze is similarly useful without becoming omniscient: she notices Sendai repeatedly looking toward the café door, guesses which visitor is the roommate and probes boundaries Sendai chooses not to disclose. Noto's observation unsettles Sendai's practiced presentation; her guess does not disclose the entire relationship to her (p-008p321–348; p-010p40–65,90–108; p-016p113–166,184–208; O025/O036–037/O060–061).

Mio's winter café offer follows a real staffing problem caused by other workers returning home. Kikyō wants to pass her entrance examination and is visibly encouraged by a modest promised reward. Sendai's teaching attention returns to the work after briefly drifting toward Miyagi. Neither the employer's need nor the pupil's independent goal should be reduced to a pretext for jealousy or an imagined romantic threat. At this boundary these supporting figures merit substantial differentiated ensemble treatment; the volume alone does not supply equal grounds for a full individual monograph for each (p-009p247–270; p-011p274–288; O032/O040).

## Christmas and the changing weight of an ordinary word

Christmas concentrates the volume's concern with meaningful dates and unwanted records. Miyagi can enjoy the Eve party and still be tired, jealous and uneasy. Sendai can be glad of Maika's friendship while wishing for private time. Their two days are not a simple progression from a false social occasion to an authentic private one: the party provides knowledge and pleasure neither could produce alone, while the private day contains its own pressures and failures (p-013p250–307; p-014p5–20; O047–048).

Miyagi hears the Christmas greeting as special after insisting that named days are ordinary. She later tries to make the following day ordinary again. Her choice of public places is partly a way to defer an anticipated private encounter. At the bookstore and lunch table, some preferences and refusals are straightforwardly respected. At the crane game, the expected distribution of competence changes: Sendai cannot get the toy she wanted to win for Miyagi; Miyagi obtains it herself, offers it in thanks for the scarf and laughs at Sendai's uncharacteristic sulking. Miyagi can give a gift, solve a practical problem and show spontaneous pleasure beside Sendai. This episode materially challenges both an incapable Miyagi and a uniformly composed Sendai (p-014p136–308; O049–051).

The later intimate scene gives particular weight to words that once defined an exclusion. Miyagi remembers that explicit sexual vocabulary could be used casually when naming what the school arrangement would not include. The same vocabulary now names an experience and a possible change she finds difficult to contain. Her concern is partly the commemorative frame: Christmas will return, making the event easy to find again. It is not accurately represented as either simple absence of desire or a fully settled decision about what the relationship has become (p-014p340–380; O052).

The scene contains pressure to answer, a current conditional grant, renewed refusals, pleasure, active participation and actual accommodations. Its sequence is essential. Miyagi's private statements that she wants contact stopped rule out explaining every protest as an empty convention. Her chosen responses and desires also rule out treating her as absent from her own decisions. Sendai's withdrawn further request is a genuine stopping point; it does not repair all preceding overridden limits. The final requests to stop likewise remain meaningful even after the event has concluded. Miyagi rejects the inference that saying no more today promises another occasion (p-014p409–711,755–793; O052–057).

One strong reading emphasizes chosen attachment, practical care, expanding self-disclosure and the reciprocal wish to be specially known. Another emphasizes how possession, debt, photographs and the demand for a positive answer make refusal costly. Both readings have substantial evidence. The first becomes inadequate if it treats bodily response or later care as permission for every earlier action. The second becomes inadequate if it erases ordinary competence, other friendships, particular respected limits or the characters' represented desires. An adequate account specifies the action, its meaning for each person and the remaining disagreement instead of awarding the entire relationship a single explanatory label.

Through December 25, Miyagi and Sendai have more ways to carry each other's presence and more explicit reasons to fear what that presence means. Sendai has not confessed her private love; Miyagi has not named her own feeling in the same way; the four-year household term has not been mutually extended. The new winter café work and the zoo outing remain prospective. The novel ends with another mark whose concealability is offered as reassurance, continuing the problem of substituting a sign for a securely recognized person.
