University plans make departure concrete while a pendant attempts to bind a finite present. Miyagi Shiori and Sendai Hazuki acquire new information, conceal other information, and increasingly seek ordinary company beyond the categories they can agree to use. ## Retrospective argument through V03

### A future can become relevant before it becomes a shared plan

Volume 3 turns geographical separation from an abstract deadline into an ordinary conversational fact. Miyagi has already heard and acknowledged the graduation horizon. Yet when Sendai finally speaks about a university outside the prefecture, Miyagi discovers a difference between having known that school ends and understanding that Sendai will leave this town. The narration explicitly locates the new difficulty in that difference. Her request for the usual conversation therefore produces something that cannot be absorbed into the usual room: an account of a future in which the room will cease to organize their days (O006–009).

The sequence does not establish a final application decision at its first mention. Sendai names an extremely demanding university, retracts that as an old aspiration, then supplies a second, plausible target. Miyagi hears several assertions with different statuses, not one verified admissions trajectory. Sendai asks her to keep the conversation secret and proposes the same university; Miyagi refuses. The subsequent command to read is both an actual task and a way to terminate a subject she does not want to pursue. Her attention then fails to settle into the reading. The task controls what Sendai says without controlling what the already disclosed information means to Miyagi.

Miyagi's continued studying complicates her refusal. The actual midterm results improve. They remain insufficient for Sendai's target in both narrators' assessments, but Maika's university, geographically nearby, has become a possibility. Miyagi privately reviews both brochures. These facts support a development in effort and options; they do not prove an application, acceptance or settled commitment. Her spoken claim that her local target is now easy enough sits beside the reader's knowledge of the other option. It is a concealment of current consideration, rather than proof that every previous local-university statement was always false (O059–060/O074/O090).

Sendai learns about that possibility indirectly. Maika reports that Miyagi has said she wants to try the same university, while qualifying whether the intention is final. Sendai initially converts this into a decision, corrects herself, then infers that its purpose must be Maika because Miyagi has withheld it from her. This inference is understandable but insecure. Later Miyagi narrates a different concern: disclosing the nearby option would make it appear that she is following Sendai. The second account undermines Sendai's certainty about an exclusively Maika-centered motive without proving an exclusively Sendai-centered motive. Maika can be an independently valued friend, a source of a viable academic option, and part of the information route without becoming a romantic rival or a disposable pretext (O078–080/O084).

Both characters also defend the other's decision-making autonomy selectively. Miyagi initially thinks Sendai should choose her own future, then actually asks her to stay. Sendai says she has no right to force Miyagi's target, then persistently presses the subject and physically retains her during another attempt to discuss it. These are consequential discrepancies between principle, desire and means. They need not imply that the principle is wholly insincere; they show that recognizing a right does not ensure respecting it under emotional pressure. Miyagi's explicit rebuttal is particularly clear: the fact that she cannot purchase Sendai's conduct at school does not give Sendai a corresponding right to act on her there (O013/O047/O052/O080–082).

The last main chapter supplies a limited positive development. Miyagi asks what Sendai would actually do if they attended the same or nearby universities. Sendai repeats the prospect of eating together and admits privately that she has no elaborate plan beyond familiar activity. This is neither an empty answer nor a comprehensive proposal. Shared food is an evidenced good, while accommodation, schedules, relationship category and the conditions of continuing have not been negotiated. Miyagi's hypothetical question is a new willingness to examine the prospect; it is not her admission of the already concealed target. The volume ends with an actual returned handgrip, not a completed joint educational decision (O096–099).

A strong alternative reading treats university talk primarily as competition over who will adapt first. Miyagi resents the failure to consider staying; Sendai resents the possibility that Maika is being chosen. Commands and indirect questions can make disclosure into a test of precedence. That interpretation explains significant evidence. It becomes reductive if it erases real studying, realistic differences in academic options, Sendai's need to leave her family home, or Miyagi's recognition that a house full of shared memories will become difficult to inhabit. The future is a practical constraint as well as an interpersonal contest. Its uncertainty cannot responsibly be resolved at this volume's boundary.

### An object extends the arrangement after a command fails

The pendant is the clearest new material expression of Miyagi's wish to mark Sendai, but its formation resists a single origin story. Aesthetic recognition comes first in disclosure chronology: Miyagi sees a moon-shaped necklace in a shopping mall and thinks it would suit Sendai. She initially leaves without buying it. The internal bonus, read after the mainline, then supplies the intervening purchase. Remembering the object disrupts ordinary pauses; Miyagi goes back under the rationale that finding it sold out will let her stop thinking about it. She takes a wallet, discovers it is still available, and constructs reasons why one purchase would be permissible (O038/O101–103).

Her account has the structure of argument with herself. She alternately treats the object as unnecessary, a possible personal possession, a way to remove an intrusive memory, and an instrument for restoring who gives orders. The claim that design does not matter follows the particular design's attraction and her confidence that Sendai would make almost anything look good. The analysis should preserve the sequence rather than choose a pure hidden motive that renders every other sentence false. The control explanation is explicit and substantial. It does not make the earlier aesthetic response fictitious, or prove a fully developed pendant plan at the earlier piercing request.

The birthday label comes after preparation. At purchase Miyagi does not know Sendai's birthday; at giving she first asks, learns that Hazuki was born in August, then produces the wrapped box. She conditionally accepts the birthday interpretation rather than volunteering it unambiguously. This sequence makes birthday an available occasion for an already chosen act. It does not prove that the present has no affection, nor that the birthday explanation is its entire cause. Her strong refusal of a return gift matters: she wants the flow of objects to preserve an asymmetry. She has already accepted and used desired gifts from ordinary friends, so a theory that she is universally unable to receive gifts would contradict the source (O053–055/O061/O103).

Sendai receives the pendant under a threat that Miyagi will discard it if rejected. She intervenes and accepts, then opens it and offers reciprocation. Acceptance is therefore an action with curiosity, reluctance and concern, rather than an uncomplicated confession. Miyagi's subsequent demand to wear it everywhere until graduation meets an actual jurisdictional refusal: school is outside the command frame. Sendai sets a condition that Miyagi should ask. Miyagi refuses to plead and withdraws the command, allowing her to choose. Only then does Sendai voluntarily concede the extended wearing (O054–055).

This sequence is load-bearing counterevidence against absolute command power. A command has failed, its author has retreated, and its recipient chooses something similar under a different subjective description. It is also counterevidence against the claim that only money or force keeps Sendai present. Her own narration identifies softness toward Miyagi as part of accepting. Neither inference gives Miyagi unrestricted rights over the person wearing the object. The separate demand that no one else see it is contested as practically difficult; the text does not establish a freely ratified absolute secrecy promise simply because the pendant is worn.

Sendai's collar reading develops with rhetorical force. Her movement from qualified guesses to emphatic certainty about ownership is itself narrated. Miyagi's later private account of marking and command hierarchy substantially supports it, but does not turn Sendai into an omniscient observer or make that account shared between them. Nor does it settle the pendant's whole value. During illness Sendai touches it for comfort, seeking the particular presence associated with Miyagi. Later she finds its restraint burdensome and the absence of Miyagi's checks unsettling. Burden, comfort, desire and habit can coexist in the same object without one canceling the others (O057/O061/O068/O091).

The pendant also changes apparently identical gestures. A usually open button and a button opened on demand to display a gift have similar visible results but different psychological meanings in Sendai's account. A third button permitted on one occasion is not automatically a permanent norm. Miyagi later reads Sendai's aborted intervention as evidence that permission has become general; that is Miyagi's inference. Close reading must preserve the difference between explicit local assent, a habitual practice, and one person's assumption about what silence now permits (O056/O060–061).

Finally, the pendant crosses the public boundary. Umina sees and touches the chain while changing for physical education; Mariko recognizes that its length and school use depart from Sendai's preferences. Their observation is real. Sendai's exam-luck explanation is invented and unpersuasive, while her denial of having a boyfriend is not thereby false. The discovery does not reveal Miyagi as giver or disclose the paid arrangement. The secrecy has acquired an observable trace without becoming complete public knowledge. Miyagi is not shown learning of it. This limited exposure is more analytically useful than declaring the secret either perfectly intact or wholly out (O095).

### A room becomes comfortable through a person, without making that person responsible for curing it

The illness sequence is both an expansion of care and a concentrated example of its misuse. Miyagi goes to Sendai's house without being asked. She buys useful food, drink and cooling sheets, risks an awkward encounter with Sendai's mother, and nearly leaves the supplies at the door rather than claim a visit. Her interior account admits concern while her dialogue denies the conventional label. This is substantial independent initiative. It should not be reduced to a command technique simply because other scenes contain control, or treated as a miraculous transformation that eliminates her reluctance and harmful behavior (O062–064).

Her knowledge is limited. She has corrected her earlier idealization of Sendai's home, but does not know its full history. She cannot determine whether solitude or an uncomfortable family presence makes illness worse. She does not know the mother's working arrangements and cannot infer the absence of care merely from an unapplied sheet. The mother is actually home and admits the visitor. Her unemotional manner is observable through Miyagi; a complete account of her motives is unavailable.

The interlude then returns to the same visit from Sendai's perspective. Its first scene is later: she wakes after Miyagi has left, disappointed despite expecting the absence. It then rewinds to the period before Miyagi arrives. That structure lets material leftovers—a yogurt container and a used sheet—summon the preceding need. The narration supplies the fever's course, the family's minimum practical care, comparison with a more attended sister, and the difficulty of being alone with an unwelcome line of thought. These are new disclosures to the reader, not facts retrospectively available to Miyagi (O067–073).

The cooling sheets qualify a blanket neglect account. Sendai's mother placed them outside the room the previous day. Sendai did not bring them in or use them; they later disappeared. As a child she had had such a sheet applied by her mother. What has changed is not simply the existence of supplies, but their interpersonal meaning. Miyagi's similar material object enters the room and becomes a vehicle for requesting attention. This supports an interpretation of care as desired embodied company rather than mere provision. It does not supply the mother's private reasons or justify a diagnosis of either family member.

Sendai feels that Miyagi's presence cuts the room away from the rest of the house and makes it comfortable in a way the summer visit did not. That is a specific change in context and state: three days ill at home, reduced ability to read or study, and a visitor she had not expected. It does not prove that Miyagi is an indispensable treatment in every situation or that Sendai has no other resources. The text retains actual school friendships, academic work and family financing. The serious claim is narrower: this person changes the experienced meaning of this room at this time.

That vulnerability coexists with pressure. Sendai asks Miyagi to apply the sheet, throws the box, strengthens the instruction to sit, and then diverts a practical care movement into an unrequested kiss. The interlude makes clear that she knows Miyagi was reaching to clear her hair for the sheet. Her later excuse that Miyagi came close therefore omits her own role in arranging the proximity. Miyagi's perspective shows why she feels unable to use her usual physical means of refusal against an ill person, even while she experiences pleasure. Neither nonresistance nor pleasure proves that the care request authorized the contact (O064/O071–072).

The scene nonetheless contains an actual negotiated limit. Miyagi refuses the proposed kiss condition for rest, applies the sheet, and finally accepts holding a hand as a milder alternative. Sendai lies down and closes her eyes while holding it. Handholding arrives after more intense contact rather than as its conventional prelude. The reversal matters formally and developmentally: Sendai had earlier distinguished wanting to touch from wanting to hold hands, then recognized the latter, then asks for it. It is a new specific desire and local agreement, not evidence that she had always secretly wanted the same thing at every earlier scene (O051/O066).

The strongest benign interpretation emphasizes care, loneliness and an unusually direct request to remain. The strongest critical interpretation emphasizes leveraging illness and converting care into access. Both have evidence; neither is an adequate total account alone. The source depicts genuine need without making the other person responsible for meeting every form it takes. It depicts meaningful chosen comfort without turning that comfort into permission for the earlier intrusion.

### Permissions have histories, and failures have consequences

The volume repeatedly distinguishes an agreement to meet from permission for what happens after meeting. Miyagi voluntarily answers school summonses, objects to the school rule breach and refuses particular contact. Sendai's claim that coming must mean the rule no longer matters does not logically settle later consent. After the first festival meeting she recognizes this herself: she first tries to call their nonflight equal culpability, then explicitly calls that rationale false and identifies her own initiation of pressure. The self-correction is evidence of ethical awareness, while her continued wish to repeat and her renewed blame show that awareness has not reliably regulated action (O041–050).

The blouse bluff supplies a specific epistemic and practical example. In Miyagi's chapter, Sendai claims to have brought the blouse and offers inspection. Miyagi does not inspect the bag and believes the confidence. Only Sendai's next chapter discloses that the blouse was not there. The threat of an immediate exchange is therefore based on a false premise. Its function as leverage cannot be erased by later mixed willingness, and its falsity cannot be placed into the earlier reader freeze. The point is not that Miyagi should have been infallible, but that trusting a performance of preparedness is different from checking the underlying fact (O042/O047).

The later school exchange repeats the problem in another form. Miyagi chooses a condition because she believes Sendai will decline it. Sendai initially objects but ultimately accepts, confounding that expectation. Miyagi's private aim to avoid further contact is not equivalent to stating a refusal; she has made an offer and then accepts performing her part. Yet that agreement still does not authorize every subsequent escalation. The explicit stops and changes of scope matter independently of whether the original exchange was wise (O083–086).

Consequential Japanese ambiguity appears in the dispute about 足. The term can permit a leg-versus-foot disagreement, but lexical scope cannot invalidate an explicit request to end. Sendai's continuation after stop requests is an action-specific failure even if one portion of her literal description can be defended. Likewise, touching is not the same as permission to undo clothing, and a no-kiss condition still applies to an ear kiss. Miyagi's explicit restrictions are not nullified because they were added after an earlier broad proposal; the text can register the dispute about changing terms without making the recipient's present limit meaningless.

The first hug is particularly double. Miyagi has allowed bounded touch and experiences a new closeness. At the same time she cannot see Sendai's face, hears a flat voice, doubts the promised future, and eventually asks to end. The hug's intimacy does not automatically convey another person's intentions. Sendai then uses the refused first name and crosses the no-kiss condition. The scene's development is therefore neither pure successful negotiation nor undifferentiated force. It contains a permission, a meaningful new experience, a proposed future, a failed ending and a further breach, each with a different evidentiary status (O086).

A later consequence prevents the analysis from treating Miyagi's limits as inevitably empty. One week before the final chapter, she actually declines another school summons and informs Sendai within ten minutes. Sendai admits that this is not really late even while complaining. The refusal qualifies her earlier expectation that Miyagi will come anyway. In the present scene Sendai also restrains a further approach because she recognizes how little trust remains and fears losing even ordinary contact. This is a limited behavioral consequence, not proof of full repair or of a new universally successful boundary regime (O087/O098).

There are also smaller counterexamples to total command. Sendai refuses piercing; Miyagi has no instrument prepared and no hole is made. Miyagi rejects a requested exchange while Sendai is ill, despite having things she wants to command. Sendai opens the top button after Miyagi has buttoned everything and explicitly said it is not a command. Requests sometimes work, fail, change form or are withdrawn. These ordinary variations matter alongside the serious failures because they identify the conditions under which the formal hierarchy does and does not organize action (O023–027/O058/O065).

An action-specific agency criterion remains appropriate: a clearly expressed stop has weight without a special contractual word; permission for one scope is not automatically permission for the next; another person's prior misconduct does not create a right to reciprocal access. This is an explicit analytical evaluation, not a claim that the characters already share that ethical theory or a legal conclusion. The evidence does not support equalizing all power or all harm, diagnosing either narrator, or declaring the entire relationship's moral identity with one label.

### Other people notice different things and want lives of their own

Maika's contribution cannot be replaced by a generic jealous-third-party function. She undertakes ordinary shopping and task coordination, notices Miyagi's distraction, offers help when Miyagi invents a forgotten item, and preserves the possibility of a later meal with friends. Her independent university target becomes a practical route Miyagi can consider. When she meets Sendai, she asks directly about their relation after noticing particular public behaviors. Her information does not come from privileged narration; it comes from attention to a friend and publicly observable actions (O036–040/O078).

Sendai treats this as perceptiveness and then conceals the actual reason for the earlier school call. The resulting scene gives Maika grounds for uncertainty, not proof that she has solved the paid arrangement. She can ask a materially important question without being omniscient. Her own awkward conversational pacing and qualified report about Miyagi's target likewise resist treating her as a perfect analyst standing outside the central narrators' errors.

Umina has seen the summer outing from a station. This is disclosed only in Volume 3, after Volume 2's prospective record correctly had no established encounter. Sendai first denies the identification and then invents a relative-related coincidence. Umina's own outing with her boyfriend, interest in spending more time with Sendai and observations about other people's privacy have separate substance. Her sighting expands what an outsider knows without showing knowledge of the home arrangement. It also makes Sendai's management of the story active deception rather than a mere unreliable inference (O017).

Mariko contributes a different kind of knowledge. She notices an accessory's departure from Sendai's preferences and does not provide the rescue Sendai expects from her during Umina's questioning. Their teasing relies on a boyfriend interpretation that Sendai finds constraining. The source allows the reader to see that the friends can know Sendai's taste accurately while misframing the relationship behind the object. Accurate observation and inadequate social explanation are not opposites (O095).

Sendai's friendships also retain positive value. She enjoys some fashion and shopping information, agrees to small errands, has previously found lunch conversation pleasant, and anticipates missing it. Particular bad days interrupt that enjoyment. Calling all public sociability a mask would erase these explicit distinctions and reproduce Miyagi's tendency to read a managed expression as the whole person. Conversely, acknowledging real friendship does not prove that every polite answer is sincere: Sendai demonstrably lies about a relative, a teacher's instruction, university conversation and the pendant's origin. The appropriate unit of interpretation is the specific utterance and context (O017–018/O048/O077–080/O094–095).

At this stage Maika and Umina warrant substantial independent dossiers and continued role-gap attention; Mariko's evidentiary role has grown; Ami remains a bounded but distinguishable friend. None has a focalized corpus sufficient for a general behavioral simulator. A later full-corpus audit must decide individual monographs from their own sustained questions and evidence, rather than from symmetry with the central pair.

### Alternation changes what an event means without distributing all its knowledge

The chapter breaks frequently separate a constrained choice from the other actor's account of it. Miyagi's first festival chapter ends after Sendai releases her and offers an urgent choice. The actual kiss belongs to the next chapter, narrated by Sendai. To write the earlier chapter as an already completed willing kiss would destroy the prospective boundary. To treat Sendai's next claim that staying meant assent as objective proof would destroy the narrator distinction. The same principle governs objects and intentions. Miyagi believes the blouse is in the bag; the next narrator reveals the bluff. Sendai infers the pendant's meaning; Miyagi's later interiority partly supports the inference. Sendai attributes the nearby-college possibility to Maika; Miyagi later reveals concern about appearing to follow Sendai. These reversals do not establish that every narrator statement is false. The interlude adds a second telling of an event already known through Miyagi. It is not a second illness visit or a second kiss. Shared dialogue and physical sequence identify the overlap, while the differing temporal frame supplies an aftermath and an antecedent. The author's afterword identifies it as newly written Sendai perspective and says the book gives a little more family context than the web had generally explored. ### The ordinary task is a place where individuality becomes visible

Cooking becomes a recurring practice after Miyagi buys incomplete ingredients and orders dinner. Sendai admits she is not a skilled cook and cannot simply make anything from a random set. Miyagi permits reheating, but Sendai chooses a workable curry and uses frozen rice. Practical limitation, conscientiousness and an independent choice are all visible. The task does not require assuming a domestic identity already agreed by both. It does give Miyagi something she explicitly values: watching this particular person make food in a house that would otherwise be empty (O088–090).

The timing of payment matters. On this day they enter and put groceries away before Miyagi gives the note. Sendai asks what would happen if she declined it; she ultimately accepts. The hypothetical is not an actual abolition of the arrangement. Miyagi privately wants payment to keep cooking classifiable as a command, separate from Sendai's postgraduation meal wish. Yet the very need for that distinction shows that the acts have become comparable. Her insistence is evidence of how she organizes their meaning, not an analytical guarantee that no wish for continuation influences her choice.

Clothing, homework, food, erasers, messages, coins and tissues hold consequences that do not require a grand announcement. Exam sheets are shown under a condition, then a revised permission to speak is given because silence feels worse. A tie can hold a concealed memory even while looking identical to every other school tie; exchanging the ties back does not return the participants to the earlier state. A crocodile-covered tissue box interrupts touch, substitutes for a hand and becomes an object through which Sendai imagines being valued. The motifs work through differing actions and contexts rather than a fixed symbolic dictionary (O009/O032–035/O052/O060/O097).

Temperature and season bind those ordinary acts to the finite school calendar. Rain initiates practical care and later illness; a cold room makes proximity felt; an empty side becomes cold in Miyagi's thought. Those connections support a formal argument about embodiment and absence. They are not grounds for diagnosing attachment style from climate imagery. The passage from summer uniforms through autumn layers to winter clothes also prevents a timeless reconstruction from treating every similarly worded request as the same situation.

The volume's strongest developmental claim is therefore modest but substantial. Both characters increasingly want an ordinary future that their present language and rules do not secure. They learn particular facts, sometimes correct themselves, make new requests and sometimes honor limits. They also conceal consequential information, misread care, use obligations as leverage and cross clear refusals. The final returned grip supplies real reciprocity after an earlier escape in the same scene. It cannot bear the unsupported weight of a solved relationship, but it does prevent describing the terminal state as merely unchanged or wholly unilateral.
