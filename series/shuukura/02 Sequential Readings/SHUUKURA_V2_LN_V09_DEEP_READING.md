## Wanting an answer and recognizing it

Volume 9 makes the desire to know a person a problem within the relationship, rather than a benefit that automatically follows from intimacy. Miyagi asks what Sendai likes, then what she dislikes, and eventually says that she is interested in Sendai herself. Those are consequential departures from treating the other person's presence as sufficient. Yet Miyagi repeatedly rejects answers because they lead back to Miyagi. Sendai, meanwhile, really does want to watch Miyagi enjoy herself, really has acquired an interest in the shoebill, and really finds choosing something for Miyagi enjoyable. The reader has evidence for the sincerity of answers that Miyagi experiences as evasion. Sincerity, relevance and completeness therefore require separate judgments. A truthful answer can fail to supply the kind of self-disclosure that the questioner seeks. An inadequate answer need not be a lie. (O023–041; p-008p24–42,358–388; p-009p228–269; p-010p181–251.)

The distinction protects both characters from a reductive interpretation. Sendai's relational wants are still her wants. Calling her a program, as Miyagi does, does not establish an objective absence of agency. Her university attendance, employment, friend ties and actual refusals supply counterevidence. Conversely, Miyagi need not accept being watched or photographed simply because doing so fulfills a sincere Sendai preference. Miyagi's question concerns whether she can encounter a Sendai who is more than a compliant reflection, a question made reasonable by the history of paid commands and by Sendai's repeated redirection. What makes the exchange difficult is that Miyagi sometimes makes a predetermined kind of answer the test of authenticity. The novel gives the reader more information than either participant, without giving the reader permission to collapse these different problems into one correct emotional vocabulary.

## December 26: conscience, permission and practical care

The opening follows the previous volume's Christmas encounter with an ordinary domestic difficulty. Miyagi stays in her room and does not join the expected meal routine. Sendai wants access and explicitly recognizes the problem of entering without permission. Miyagi says that she has not allowed entry and says no; Sendai later treats the absence of a further complaint as probable permission and enters. The juxtaposition matters: Sendai's recognition of a norm does not reliably control her next act. Describing this as simple ignorance would miss the narration's own conscience and self-persuasion. Calling it evidence that Sendai respects no boundary would miss later local withdrawals and honored refusals. (O002–003; p-004p25–41.)

A similarly small dispute separates a permission from an obligation. Miyagi waives that day's work-related mark. Sendai insists that Miyagi should do what she proposed and offers a substitute question as the condition for not doing it. An arrangement ostensibly giving Miyagi control also supplies Sendai with a means of maintaining contact. The point is not that every command secretly reverses power. Here the exact exchange shows the recipient pressing to preserve an obligation the nominal beneficiary has waived. The distinction becomes central when Miyagi later finds that ordering a willing recipient does not produce the independent response she wants. (O003; p-004p81–88.)

Care is nevertheless actual. Sendai prepares food before work, returns from the first winter café shift, cooks the requested evening meal, and receives Miyagi's thanks and welcome. Miyagi's questions about dinner and her presence waiting in the common space are evidence of wanting this particular person, not proof that Miyagi cannot feed herself. By December 29 the pair have cooked and eaten dinner together. Miyagi reports studying during Sendai's absences; Sendai cannot verify it and recognizes that company would make even a lie acceptable to her. The scene therefore supports competence and attachment while exposing a limit of Sendai's preferred evidentiary standard. (O003–005; p-004p130–195,250–263.)

The nail-oil decision also resists a universal possession explanation. Sendai privately prefers to finish the old bottle, yet opens and uses Maika's gift because she wants to send Maika feedback. The actual pre-outcome forecast of old-first fails. Miyagi later objects to a trace from someone else on what she calls her own; Sendai is pleased by the apparent jealousy. Neither reaction cancels the independent friendship motive behind the original choice. January's later use of the old oil is a different occasion and cannot repair the failed December forecast. (O005/O020; p-004p239–296; p-007p223–225.)

## A café visit exposes different kinds of unwanted knowledge

Miyagi goes alone to the café on New Year's Eve despite previously saying that visiting alone would be boring. Her later explanations vary between boredom and wanting cake. The text permits mixed motives rather than obliging the analyst to select one false explanation and one complete hidden truth. The encounter also gives an unusually explicit address negotiation. 小松澪 introduces her full name; Miyagi tries the surname Komatsu-san and settles on Mio-san while resisting greater familiarity. Mio's preferred friendliness is not Miyagi's preferred distance. Sendai's later jealousy about names must be distinguished from Miyagi's own voiced objections. Neither supplies a universal right to regulate all future friendships. (O007–009; p-005p18–52,98–105.)

Mio and Noto have limited, distinct access. Noto calls the household cohabitation; Miyagi corrects the category to room-sharing. Mio says she has heard about a roommate and describes Sendai's school popularity. Miyagi turns that into certainty about current university popularity and imagines particular possibilities that are not established events. Noto's teasing and Mio's attempts to make conversation reveal social worlds outside the household, but do not make either observer an omniscient authority on its private meaning. Nor does the narration prove that their interest is romantic. Sendai's own private account distinguishes their interest from hers; Miyagi separately expects neither to do anything. Those remain focalized assessments. (O008–009/O021/O051; p-005p58–81; p-006p26–31; p-007p294.)

Sendai promises that neither friend will approach while Miyagi waits, but Noto does. Sendai stops Mio and fails to stop Noto, then apologizes. Miyagi stays partly because leaving during conversation is difficult. This is a broken promise and a social constraint, with no need to invent a locked exit or to deny Miyagi any choice. The scarf supplies real warmth on the return journey even though the promise failed. Material care cannot retroactively turn a failed undertaking into a kept one. (O009; p-005p120–174.)

Miyagi privately would have joined next year's evening even with other plans, if Sendai asked. Sendai does not hear that readiness. The scene supplies chosen company without making an unspoken wish a mutual calendar commitment. (O010–011; p-005p216–296.)

The photo-permission history then develops through sharply different events. Miyagi refuses a photograph before midnight and Sendai moves on. At midnight Sendai photographs without asking, invokes the occasion, and takes additional images after Miyagi allows only one. Miyagi later photographs sleeping Sendai and refuses a deletion request by appealing to Sendai's behavior. The formal earlier prohibition concerns photographs of Miyagi, so the two events do not simply instantiate an identical contract. Both nevertheless involve a particular person's expressed limit. Retaliation, a special date and an already retained image are different justifications; none supplies general permission. (O011–012/O017–018; p-005p351–356; p-006p65–74; p-007p40–50.)

## January's ordinary work does not make every favor an open grant

The January 1 grooming sequence makes small differences consequential. Miyagi dislikes a large quantity of broccoli but can tolerate a little. Sendai offers a substitute while also pressing nutrition and childishness; Miyagi eats half. Sendai's dislike of carbonation remains real even when she drinks the requested soda. Consuming something, liking it, accepting its presence and wanting another person to approve are not equivalent observations. The scene does not justify a static list in which each person either always loves or always refuses a food. (O019; p-007p119–158.)

The deferred command promise has an actual revision history. Sendai initially treats the morning offer as expired after Miyagi leaves for food. Miyagi insists on using it later; Sendai accepts the deferral. January 2 explicitly separates a request to stand and turn around from exercising that right. A later thought that Miyagi lacks general command authority does not cancel the one-off promise: the cake scene explicitly reconfirms it, then Miyagi describes it as consumed. The apparent contradiction is narrowed by the distinction between an ordinary imperative and a particular invoked right. No indefinite command entitlement should be manufactured from either passage. (O019–022/O034/O036–038; p-007p150–161,327–334; p-010p165; p-011p115–118,290.)

Care has scope too. Miyagi conditionally accepts hand grooming, declines a future repeat, and wants the procedure finished before Sendai does. Subsequent cooperation with another grooming task is its own situation. A mark-location grant does not settle restraint, intensity or a later request to undo a towel. The relationship is changing through these ordinary negotiations, but change cannot be inferred merely from recurrence, nor can compliance establish comfort with every component. (O020–022; p-007p190–395.)

## The zoo: the object of attention can be missed while being photographed

The zoo actually occurs on January 4. Before leaving, Miyagi asks which animals Sendai wants to see. Miyagi's tiger choice defeats Sendai's expectation of okapi or tapir, a character's failed prediction rather than another analyst-model test. Sendai learns rather than already knows everything about Miyagi. She genuinely finds the shoebill interesting and genuinely makes Miyagi's enjoyment her primary goal. She withholds the latter at first because she expects it to be unacceptable as an answer. (O023–027; p-008p24–48,358–388.)

Photography changes within the outing. Miyagi allows animal photographs with people incidentally included, later objects to the concentration on her, and directs Sendai toward the tiger. Sendai can photograph the person she cares about while missing why that person chose this animal. Later Miyagi deliberately poses with her ear visible, allows a photograph, and offers to photograph Sendai herself. At the penguins she separately agrees to a joint image after bargaining for bird photographs. These specific grants complicate any claim that every zoo image is simply unwanted. They also do not excuse images taken beyond a particular limit or supply an automatic right to share them with someone else. Sendai later shows zoo photographs to Mio; the text does not narrate Miyagi's separate distribution permission, while no explicit universal sharing ban should be invented. (O024–033; p-008p140–156,193–226,298–360; p-009p63–80; p-010p40–43.)

Miyagi distrusts performed smiles because she wants access to an unarranged Sendai. Yet an arranged expression can coexist with genuine pleasure, just as Miyagi's unhelpful expression can coexist with an enjoyable outing. Mio later notices both apparently unhappy and apparently happy images. Her questions unsettle Sendai's remembered confidence without certifying either the gallery or the friend as a complete account of Miyagi's feelings. The novel makes the record an interpretation problem. The photograph preserves visible form, not a complete emotional state. (O028–033; p-009p20–60; p-010p44–69.)

Miyagi does make a meaningful adjustment. She decides that using the ear-vow to compel an answer would be wrong, changes a demanded top favorite into an animal Sendai wants to see again, and later holds Sendai's hand on her own initiative. She also grips painfully while pressing for the answer. Foregoing one coercive means is not evidence that all pressure has ended. When Sendai answers that she wants to see Miyagi watching the shoebill, the reader can recognize sincerity that Miyagi still regards as evasion. Their agreement to future aquarium and zoo outings remains undated, within the stated university horizon. Captured bird photographs and the promise to give them to Miyagi are recorded separately; their actual transfer remains unshown. (O029–031; p-009p174–296.)

## The demand for a refusal can itself become a demand to comply

After university resumes, Sendai's outing with Mio and advance message are actual. Miyagi has eaten and is doing dishes when she returns. Sendai likes her work and preserves friendship while concealing the household through false explanations. She also wants to stop Miyagi's increasingly unfamiliar questions and uses contact to silence them. Her private motive is stated, not inferred solely from an awkward result. Miyagi's announcement before touching Sendai does not create the general rule Sendai invokes to justify announcing her own next act. Each participant's response must remain attached to its own occasion. (O032–034; p-010p105–188.)

The cake excursion with Maika supplies an alternative conversational experience. Maika answers with a squirrel and a past pet; Miyagi turns that ease into a standard that anyone ought to meet. Maika's recommendation to ask Sendai directly is sensible but incomplete, since Miyagi has been asking. Miyagi wants the cakes to taste good and to thank Sendai for the lip balm she actually bought, while also considering whether a disliked cake could force disclosure. Care and an instrumental experiment coexist. Asakura's recommendation and Mio's independent endorsement of the same shop show that the wider network has material consequences beyond jealousy. (O035–036; p-011p5–119.)

Miyagi then escalates the deferred command to seek an answer Sendai would resist, recognizes it as excessive, and blames Sendai for the escalation. The narration makes that blame available for scrutiny: Miyagi sets a hidden last-chance question and treats an unsatisfying answer as removing her own choice. Sendai's demand that Miyagi specify the intrusive order and take responsibility reverses Miyagi's feeling of directing the scene. Sendai expressly states reluctance while not flatly refusing, proposes an alternative, and Miyagi ends the command before its requested performance. Miyagi does not actually want the literal outcome she has demanded. The right is consumed; a later proposal does not become owed. Both participants subsequently shift responsibility for further pressure. Their own accusations cannot substitute for the observable sequence. (O037–038; p-011p161–353.)

Miyagi's eventual statement is a real advance: she tells Sendai that the zoo was enjoyable, cites the earlier promises of enjoyable and ordinary talk, asks her to state genuine dislikes, and says she is interested in her. Sendai hears that interest. Yet Miyagi also tells Sendai to make likes and dislikes and prohibits answers shared with Miyagi. Sendai's genuine answer that she dislikes being told to move away is again called a lie. Recognizing another person's agency is more difficult here than requesting its appearance. This is development with a continuing failure condition, not a completed ethical repair. (O038; p-011p378–410.)

## Valentine's Day and the information ordinary talk supplies

Sendai's next focalization establishes a cost to the new request. She fears giving the wrong answer, privately names not wanting Miyagi's dislike, and withholds a direct love declaration because it could irreversibly change the relationship. She also recognizes that she arranges to miss the supposed right timing. Miyagi's incomplete knowledge is therefore not solely Miyagi's willful refusal to see. Sendai contributes to it by concealing an answer she genuinely wants understood. The narration discloses this concealment rather than simply deceiving its reader. (O039–041; p-012p5–54.)

The supporting network then produces an actual negotiation. Miyagi will meet Mio in March if Sendai joins the three-person clothing outing with Maika. Miyagi declines a cheaply offered new command and selects a reciprocal social undertaking. No visit or outing has occurred at the endpoint. The tutoring exam has been taken and the result is awaited; further lessons after admission are requested, not yet settled. The new spring café agreement expressly includes marks on workdays, without automatically renewing every winter condition. These distinctions keep institutional and social time from disappearing inside the central pair's emotional time. (O040; p-012p100–193.)

February 14 exposes another scope error. Sendai treats a lack of objection to keeping the date free as confirmation, then pressures Miyagi to help make chocolate or go shopping. Miyagi had not agreed to either task. Her eventual truffle-making participation is particular and becomes inventive: she adds ears, Sendai accepts the variation and adds eyes, and they finish the work. Miyagi's irregular result becomes something Sendai specifically wants to eat. Miyagi likewise wants Sendai's product. Their preferences are relational on both sides, despite Miyagi's difficulty accepting that form of answer from Sendai. (O041–043; p-012p193–260; p-013p12–50,111–194.)

The most concrete new knowledge arrives through a comparatively ordinary question. Miyagi assumes Sendai likes cooking; Sendai says that is not particularly so and explains making her own meals since high school, asking her mother not to cook for her, avoiding family meals, and preparing to live alone regardless of university admission. Miyagi gains a clearer outline of Sendai Hazuki and cannot find an appropriate response. Her silence is the result of considered difficulty, not evidence of indifference. This successful disclosure is an important counterexample to the claim that Sendai never answers. It supplies a specific history without settling every cause of the family's estrangement. (O044; p-013p200–220.)

The late-February ending similarly leaves development bounded. Miyagi names both possessiveness and jealousy, sees her own anticipatory loss in the accumulation of labels, and imagines confinement without enacting it. Sendai supplies a practical reason for arranging the clothing outing; Miyagi dislikes being told the date after it is set. A prior agreement to go does not settle who should choose the date. Miyagi ultimately gives limited permission for a little hair styling and makeup, refuses other proposed marks, and actively draws Sendai closer at the end. Tomorrow's actions remain unknown. Neither this moment nor the newly heard promise to make something together next Valentine's Day establishes a mutual romance label, an indefinite household, or permission for a future intimate act. (O043–045; p-013p127–158,282–405.)

## The late-read childhood stories explain a difficulty without settling its cause

The bonus follows the sealed mainline endpoint. Middle-school Sendai sees a mother who addresses her sister about dinner while withholding similar attention from her. She connects diminished family attention with falling short of her sister's marks; a ninety-one is inadequate against a remembered hundred. These are concrete observed differences and a child's strong interpretation of them, not independent access to both parents' entire motives. Mutsuki asks for the fried shrimp Sendai thinks she likes, but Sendai is unsure whether this is indirect care or her sister's genuine preference. Her former favorite becomes hard to identify through repeated substitution. At school she cannot answer a personality quiz without imagining her sister's answers, yet she genuinely enjoys learning. The bonus provides a history relevant to adult questions of sameness without proving an empty self or a fixed inability to form preferences. (O047–048; p-015p7–157.)

Middle-school Miyagi's broccoli story is equally resistant to a single causal diagnosis. She remembers telling her mother that it was not delicious and imagines that a different answer might have produced a happy ending. The mother is absent; the story does not establish that the child's honesty caused that absence or specify the mother's fate. Miyagi enjoys a friend's visit and then feels the empty space more acutely after the friend returns for a family meal. She tries to recreate eggs associated with her mother, discovers that she cannot remember the taste, eats the unsatisfactory result and washes up. Competence, loss and failed recovery are all represented. (O048–049; p-015p159–288.)

Her reading supplies another interpretive position. She can recognize the cat picture book's love and happiness while still wanting an ending in which nothing disappears. That difference from her mother's reading is a substantive disagreement about what counts as happiness, not an inability to comprehend the plot. She stores rather than destroys the book and chooses a brighter story. The adult fear that joyful records will deepen later loss now has a stronger retrospective context. It does not follow that adult Miyagi consciously understands every connection, that Sendai knows this history, or that every future choice is determined by it. The bonus changes the reader's available explanations at its actual disclosure point; the earlier freezes remain valid records of what could then be known.
