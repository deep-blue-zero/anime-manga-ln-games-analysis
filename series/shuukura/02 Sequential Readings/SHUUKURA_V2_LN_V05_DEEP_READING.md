## Living together changes the problem of distance

Volume 5 gives Miyagi Shiori and Sendai Hazuki the shared address chosen at graduation, then denies that an address by itself solves their problem. Sendai can expect Miyagi to return to the same home, but cannot enter her bedroom, choose her reading there, or assume the bodily permissions associated with their former visits. Miyagi no longer needs to pay for Sendai to appear, but cannot explain why Sendai wants to remain or comfortably inhabit that apparently favorable situation. Their vocabulary has advanced to “roommates” before their practical understanding has caught up. This is development through a changed constraint, rather than a return to the initial arrangement with a new setting. (O002–007/O013–018; p-004p5–194, p-005p5–258.)

The distinction is already spatial. Miyagi's old room combined territory, books, money and a bounded visit; the new household separates common space from two private rooms. A door is now something Sendai can physically open but should not open without permission. She repeatedly thinks about crossing it, once imagines an undetected breach as almost no breach at all, and also wants discovery to produce a penalty resembling an old command. Nevertheless she stops herself, and later abandons an explicit request after Miyagi refuses. Her prediction that unauthorized entry would make Miyagi leave is not tested. Actual abstention matters even though it is neither pure disinterest nor proof of a universally respected boundary. Miyagi's loan of selected books onto the common table provides a workable alternative: access to a shared activity need not entail access to a private room. (O007–008/O027–029; p-004p193–196, p-006p103–219.)

The reverse direction is different. Miyagi enters Sendai's room after asking and receiving an invitation, initially offering common space as an alternative. Later book and dictionary deliveries become occasions to stay. Sendai deliberately borrows a dictionary she already owns to test whether Miyagi will visit. Their unequal access therefore develops through actions by both occupants; it is not evidence that Sendai has regained a comprehensive right over Miyagi's space. At the mainline ending she still has not entered Miyagi's new bedroom. The late-placed move-day bonus adds a positive reason for Miyagi's privacy: she wants to build and arrange her room herself. Concealment and embarrassment matter, but privacy is also authorship of an ordinary place. (O036/O043/O074/O108; p-007p98–119, p-008p5–18, p-011p173–195, p-016p67–79.)

Their first rule discussion makes this new arrangement unusually explicit. Miyagi's initial “you decide” is not accepted as unlimited delegation: Sendai's hypothetical daily-kiss rule brings her back into the discussion. The actual agreements concern unpermitted room entry, visitors without overnight guests, notice of their own overnight absences, and eating together when their schedules align with notice of late returns. Miyagi wants more shared meals than Sendai initially proposes. Joint dinner preparation and the breakfast cook/wash convention are subsequently reported as established practice, not falsely inserted as a fully ratified cooking rota in the first conversation. Their relationship can create functioning agreements; the problem is which agreements they honor and what they attempt to derive from them. (O007–010/O027; p-004p171–236, p-006p68–102.)

## Money, work and an independent future

The end of the payment ritual does not end disagreement about money. Miyagi says the saved notes belong to Sendai and should be spent as her compensation. Sendai privately admits they were originally her money, yet calls the fund joint and uses that designation for household purchases. Before the move Miyagi's parent repaid half the contract expense, replenishing the money; it is incorrect to say all saved notes were irretrievably consumed by the apartment. It is equally incorrect to treat Sendai's designation as an agreed transfer into joint ownership. Miyagi resents the preemption when Sendai pays for the platypus tissue cover. These are conflicts over the meaning of compensation, indebtedness and shared life, with practical transactions attached. Neither the exact sums nor a legal property arrangement is established. (O011/O019; p-004p239–263, p-005p279–306.)

The interlude complicates any claim that leaving her family has already made Sendai materially independent. Parental support pays their living costs while she attends university. The bed bought with her savings matters because she wants at least the place where she sleeps to be hers. Its symbolic autonomy depends on acknowledging the larger dependence, not denying it. The opened savings can itself has been discarded rather than moved into the new bedroom; a fund and its old physical container are different objects. These details give Sendai's desire for earnings a future horizon. She wants reserves against uncertain employment and housing after graduation, even though she can presently live without working. (O030/O067; p-006p243–266, p-010p46–65.)

Tutoring extends a real capacity into a new social context. Sendai remembers enjoying teaching Miyagi but does not take sole credit for her improvement. Her university friend 澪 offers an introduction, Sendai authorizes sharing contact details, she hears the senior's proposal, and only then accepts the job. At her first lesson she is nervous about ordinary conversational distance and the teacher role. 花巻桔梗 is already capable, attentive and courteous; her good initial performance is not an outcome that can be credited to the new tutor. Sendai completes the lesson and intends to review material for the next one. This is useful evidence of responsibility and unfamiliar-interlocutor limits, not a demonstrated general simulation model. (O031/O043/O070–074; p-006p253–291, p-008p26–34, p-011p5–48/157–188.)

Miyagi's objection is more specific than an inability to accept every separate activity. By chapter 8 she can partly imagine and accommodate Sendai's different university life using her own experience. Tutoring is harder precisely because it is easier to imagine through their shared summer: an ordinary occupational word has become the name of a personally privileged past. She knows professional lessons will not reproduce their private encounters, yet the substitution troubles her. Her fantasies are evidence of jealous comparison, not evidence that Sendai behaves improperly with a minor pupil. She also knows a demand to quit is unlikely to succeed; Sendai actually refuses it and continues working. The refusal preserves a consequential independent commitment despite considerable accommodation elsewhere. (O079–083; p-011p338–354, p-012p30–73.)

The supporting cast prevents this development from becoming a world containing only the pair. Maika changes her appearance through her own choices, takes Miyagi shopping without requiring a piercing, offers a place to stay, and advises early reconciliation while allowing her to remain. She can tease, question and care without knowing the actual roommate's identity. Miyagi's canceled appointment and false “relative” and visitor-rule explanations impose real costs on that friendship. 澪 has her own manner and initiative; Sendai's frank “you do not look like a tutor” reply also qualifies the editorial suggestion that only Miyagi can ever receive an unguarded Sendai. Umina's and Mariko's continuing messages show that previous social connections persist even when Sendai assigns them less priority. No supporting person's unknown motives become available merely because the central narrators make judgments about them. (O024–025/O031/O035/O066/O101–104.)

## Rules can become a reason to seek their own breach

The penalty is not a stable mechanism that simply makes rules effective. Sendai contemplates being caught breaking a rule so that Miyagi will again tell her what to do. Miyagi treats the rarity of breaches as scarcity of a desired entitlement. Both therefore have incentives that can work against deterrence. Sendai eventually warns that a penalty might be enjoyable, but then calls the exchange a joke; Miyagi receives an unsettling possibility, not the complete private explanation already available to the reader. A rule's verbal acceptance, its enforcement and its emotional payoff require separate records. (O028/O034/O046/O055; p-006p148–155, p-007p8–24, p-008p167–179, p-009p52–78.)

The postponed doria meal demonstrates the distinction particularly clearly. Sendai accepts an outing, then a work introduction becomes available for the same day. She apologizes and asks to postpone; Miyagi finally permits the delay while demanding the penalty. Sendai explicitly disputes the equation of any ordinary promise with the household rules. Her concession is “this time,” not an agreed permanent expansion. Miyagi privately admits that her entitlement was obtained through an illegitimate expansive interpretation. The doria outing subsequently happens, so the episode cannot be described as a promise simply forgotten. Its emotional consequence nevertheless outlasts its practical repair. (O032–036; p-006p292–377, p-007p5–44/90–92.)

Miyagi chooses a bodily memory device to make that consequence endure. She does not repeat the failed demand to pierce Sendai's ears; she expects that refusal to stand. Instead she asks Sendai to pierce her own ears. The omitted recipient in ピアス開けてよ initially permits Sendai to misunderstand whose body is at issue. Clarification resolves the referent but not the separate question of requiring Sendai to participate. Miyagi deliberately exaggerates her reluctance and later pain so that Sendai will remember having hurt her and feel guilty when considering promises. That mechanism is explicit in her narration and partly spoken aloud. It warrants analysis as emotional leverage without requiring a clinical diagnosis or an assertion that she simply enjoys pain. (O035/O037–039; p-007p62–85/129–253.)

The same studs then acquire competing meanings. For Miyagi they initially bind Sendai to remembering her requests. For Sendai they suggest having changed Miyagi's shape and, although she recognizes that they are not actual ownership, feel like a personal sign. When Sendai uses them to solemnize a promise she proposed, Miyagi notices the reversal in direction. Later Miyagi recognizes that the studs cannot compel anything and cannot guarantee even a promise. This is genuine recognition of the limits of the device, not evidence that its emotional influence disappears. Sendai's final solemn promise that she will never again make such an approach if refused has force partly because of that already developed material vocabulary. (O045–046/O064/O082/O092/O099; p-008p86–178, p-009p384–396, p-012p91–92, p-013p186–207, p-014p39–40.)

## Agency is specific, revocable and incompletely recognized

Volume 5 supplies strong counterevidence to both a universal-consent reading and an account in which neither character ever exercises meaningful agency. The ordinary privacy rule is actually respected. Sendai eventually leaves her own bedroom so Miyagi can change privately, contrasting with an earlier manipulated clothes-or-makeup choice. Miyagi chooses to allow a handhold during the second film; both enjoy that film through its credits. Yet when she asks for release afterward, Sendai tightens the grip and presses for an explanation before releasing her. Permission to hold a hand during a film is not permission to continue indefinitely. The same source supports both the genuine grant and the failed revocation. (O056–060/O088; p-009p81–143/193–229, p-013p13–32.)

Miyagi also crosses limits. She insists after Sendai declines the old ritual, creates visible marks despite objections about their consequences, and bites a finger hard enough to leave clear toothprints while ignoring repeated pain protests. Sendai's initial attempt to force a smile is itself invasive and rests on a claimed entitlement earned by dinner and changed plans. It does not justify the persistent bite. Likewise, Miyagi's eventual regret and restraint around the bandage are meaningful changes without undoing the earlier act. Reciprocity repeatedly becomes a justification for crossing into another domain: “you allowed makeup,” “I wore your skirt,” or “you touch me too” are not comprehensive permissions. (O049/O062–063/O075–076/O080; p-008p274–332, p-009p312–380, p-011p196–264, p-012p5–35.)

The major intimate encounter must be read through that history without reducing it to a prefabricated verdict. Sendai does actually begin to withdraw when Miyagi pushes her away. Miyagi calls her back, requires darkness and closed curtains, and later explicitly says she decided to permit the broad encounter without an exchange condition. Her pleasure and unforeseen self-discovery are also directly narrated. Those observations defeat the claim that every moment contains only passive submission or that the reader has no evidence of her desire. But Miyagi also explicitly says Sendai's promise of never trying again if refused unsettled her timing: she had imagined a later possibility, not necessarily today. Choice and pressure are both source-grounded. The promise is not an explicit threat to end their household, and should not be rewritten as one. (O092–093/O098–099; p-013p183–225, p-014p20–40.)

Within the encounter, permissions remain limited. The source records retained clothing, an absence of resulting body marks verified by Miyagi, and Sendai stopping herself from a further invasive act. It also records local refusals that she retries or ignores. Most consequentially, she hears やっぱり、やだ and treats physical response as evidence against the words, continuing while raising the reporting threshold to どうしても無理だったら. This supports an action-specific finding of failed revocation and faulty consent reasoning. Earlier permission and later pleasure do not make that inference sound. Conversely, identifying this failure does not require denying every actual choice, actual limit or positive feeling elsewhere in the scene. The analysis distinguishes observed action, each character's self-explanation and its own evaluative criterion. No legal diagnosis, total moral identity or erotic enactment follows from it. (O094–100; p-013p231–342, p-014p7–40.)

Sendai's language of melting reason is particularly important to this distinction. She repeatedly makes choices, pauses, promises, withdraws and withholds particular actions. Desire constrains her attention and supplies rationalizations; it does not establish literal inability to act otherwise. Her attraction to knowing what nobody else knows about Miyagi cannot make Miyagi's wishes transparent. The scene increases sensory knowledge while leaving the meaning of the relationship unresolved. Given-name permission still fails, and no agreed romantic label replaces “roommates.” (O091–097; p-013p130–417.)

## Alternation increases knowledge without giving either person an omniscient view

The opening dream is explicitly invented experience. Sendai knows that its Miyagi is partly assembled from convenience and fantasy, and that it does not authorize real behavior. Her later claim that the actual encounter will update dream detail is an expectation about future memory, not a second observed dream. Miyagi's later sense that an unmarked body could make the encounter seem a dream is different again: she knows the event occurred and is troubled by its persistent mental traces. The narrative juxtaposes imagined access, actual experience and the inability to erase another person's knowledge. None is interchangeable with an objective recording of mutual intention. (O026/O096/O098–100.)

Alternation also supplies smaller corrections. Sendai's eye avoidance is explicitly associated with dream days, but the later explanation does not prove that an earlier particular breakfast was such a day. Miyagi's frequent Maika outings are partly false; her bread overbuying expresses uncertainty about Sendai's tastes; the visitor prohibition she gives Maika is a cover contradicted by their actual household rule. Sendai does not know those private explanations simply because the reader now does. Similarly, the brief first decision accepting the privacy rule does not retrospectively acquire the precise motive disclosed in a later temptation scene. Sequence protects explanatory accuracy. (O014/O017/O024/O027–028.)

The interlude and internal bonus make disclosure order visibly different from event order. Both return to move-in day after the mainline has advanced. Sendai's happy empty home and then wanted returned greeting are complemented by Miyagi's unfamiliarity with a home where somebody reliably returns. Her wish to arrange her own room and place her bed near the shared wall coexist. These later disclosures enrich the interpretation of early awkwardness without turning the first reader encounter into a prescient one. They also do not narrate Miyagi returning from Maika at the ending. (O065–069/O106–111.)

The terminal absence is therefore a real unresolved consequence. Miyagi leaves before Sendai wakes, seeks temporary refuge with Maika and initially imagines weeks away. Maika's acceptance does not establish that those weeks happen. Miyagi eventually sends the minimum required notice that she will not return tonight, without revealing identity, location or the event. Her own narration combines wanting not to see Sendai with wanting to see her soon. Sendai has the absence and message, not that complete ambivalence. Four-year roommates remains a promised form of life; the stability that Miyagi associates with its name is precisely what she now fears losing. (O100–104; p-014p41–175.)

## Ordinary objects carry practical work and changing claims

The household detail is a major vehicle of development. The kettle moves from lack, to a pressured joint outing, to actual shared tea. The white color is finally Sendai's selection after Miyagi's answers change, so the purchase should not be prettified into effortless consensus. Bread variety, the still-disliked soda, preserved butter-and-jam preference, packaged meals and researched hamburg steak show both accommodation and persistent differences. Food becomes pleasurable with company without every preference merging. A carefully cooked meal can fulfill a promise while failing to settle jealousy; a cake gift can improve a moment while leaving an “undigested” conflict. (O004/O011/O014/O018–021/O073–074/O081–086.)

The platypus begins as a communal tissue cover and moves into Sendai's room by Miyagi's request. It becomes a fidget object, a literal marker of distance during a film, a soft striking implement and a supply for cleanup. Its mutable uses make it more informative than a fixed one-to-one symbolic code. The cat similarly moves between shelf and pillow according to readiness to be seen and the need for private comfort. Sendai cannot know those locations merely by cherishing the gift. The older pendant survives unworn in its case: material continuity does not mean an old wearing rule remains active. Ordinary ownership, usefulness, concealment and symbolic claim overlap without becoming identical. (O005/O013/O039–041/O043/O048/O050/O097/O100/O108.)

The strongest present conclusion is consequently limited but substantial. Shared life makes their attachment easier to enact and harder to disguise, while generating new private spaces and outside obligations that neither can simply absorb. They can negotiate meals, find a film both enjoy, respect a closed door, offer work and friendship care, and choose continued proximity. They can also use gifts, promises, physical reactions and reciprocity as inadequate substitutes for listening. Increased knowledge of the other's body does not resolve knowledge of the other's reasons; a name that preserves a future does not determine what that future permits. The last message leaves both that future and its next negotiation open through this exact source boundary.
