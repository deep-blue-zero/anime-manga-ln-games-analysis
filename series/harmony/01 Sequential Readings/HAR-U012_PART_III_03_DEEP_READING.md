## A revision of the reward model, not an observed control system

Gabriel begins with a choice between money now and a larger promised sum a year later. Tuan expects people would choose the immediate option, then offers a survival explanation for immediate valuation. Gabriel describes widespread genetically programmed tendencies and suggests there must be some reason this trait is easy to implement across vertebrates. No comparative dataset or evolutionary experiment is shown. Her account of the hyperbolic curve—value rising sharply as a benefit approaches the present and staying low across the far future—contrasts with what the dialogue calls exponential rationality. These are claims inside the scientific explanation, not an externally validated mathematical law for every actual decision. E004.

The decisive addition is temporal competition. Short-term and longer-term desire agents struggle to be selected; that survival game is called will. Gabriel says this feature was not addressed in the Russian researcher’s model and that her consortium is revising the account using its experimental results. The new source therefore advances the research route positively: a named interlocutor explains what the ongoing extension is meant to add. It supplies more than another vague assertion that brains can be modeled. It still does not provide the paper, raw results or a demonstrated device that controls a person. E004.

The animal example also complicates the opening emphasis on immediate desire. Some pigeons wait for the larger delayed quantity. Gabriel explicitly declines to say all do. The model’s category of will is extended through competing tendencies, not through an assertion that every animal chooses impatiently or that “genetic” means an organism never selects among alternatives. Her claim to have shown animal will is tied to the model’s definition of selection. Its normative extension—human will need not be uniquely exalted—belongs to her framing, rather than following as an unargued moral fact adopted by this analysis. E001, E004, E006.

Nuaza enters at the point where Tuan privately links finer prediction to the manipulation of reward. The narration names him as her father; her spoken question names Doctor Nuaza and asks about participation. Gabriel confirms initial participation, says he is no longer at the consortium and does not know whether he conducted other research using the model. ## Reward becomes salience, and choice becomes perception

The lecture changes scale when Tuan asks what the new model changes in the view of human beings. Gabriel first extends the definition of will to animals, then turns to pain. “Reward” is expressly detached from ordinary benefit or pleasure: it denotes a psychological action that attracts conscious interest and leaves an impression. This preserves and deepens the earlier correction of a simplistic pleasure account. The term’s technical meaning matters because an aversive sensation can now enter the same competition. E006.

Tuan initially objects that pain is unavoidable. Gabriel’s response concerns attention: a competing activity can keep an injury from entering consciousness, and the perceived strength of pain depends on circumstances. In this model, selection occurs among processes generating experience. It does not mean a person deliberately consents to pain, is morally responsible for feeling it or can simply decide that an injury does not exist. The difference between subpersonal competition and reflective permission is essential in a novel where bodily refusal and consent already matter. Gabriel’s explanations and illustrative cases are still testimony in an interview; no pain experiment is performed on Tuan. E006.

Vision, hearing, smell and taste then join the selected agents. Their steep present salience usually makes them difficult to ignore; Gabriel retains that frequency qualification. Tuan recognizes a possible account of reality’s constitution. Gabriel responds that consciousness and reality have much the same meaning because available reality is limited by consciousness. That is an argument about the limits of accessible experience. It does not by itself demonstrate that no external world exists, that the waiting room is false or that anyone currently manufactures the visitor’s sensations. The interview has expanded the possible stakes from changing decisions to changing experienced reality, while the actual implementation question remains unanswered. E006–E007.

## Civic courage and the last denial

Gabriel stands and extends her hand to close the discussion. Tuan accepts the handshake, then deliberately asks about the declaration. She tells the reader she likes questions in bad taste and hopes an ambiguous prompt might draw out something deeper. The plain reply that it is terrible disappoints her. “Boring” is her appraisal of that response, not evidence that Gabriel feels no fear. Her scientist interlocutor has ordinary reasons to dislike being asked for a deadline decision by an inspector who has just questioned nearby research. E007–E008.

Tuan presses the proximity between this work and the perpetrators’ claimed power. Gabriel acknowledges the proximity but denies both perpetration and an achieved capacity to manipulate people or display whatever reality the researchers choose. That denial has evidential limits in both directions: it is not independent proof of innocence or technical incapacity, yet the scene supplies no basis for converting it into a confession. The model-to-tool gap remains expressly present in the source. Tuan answers with the caster’s witnessed act as a reason the threat might be real. She does not demonstrate the consortium’s role in that event. E008.

Gabriel says she will do nothing and calls the declaration only a threat; she then invokes the public courage of the agreements and society’s refusal to submit. Her position moves from calling the event terrible to a stated civic response. It may express conviction, defensive convention or a wish to end intrusive questioning; none is conclusively exposed. Tuan calls the answer admirable, perhaps too admirable. That excess belongs to the narrator’s suspicion. The contrast with the model is nevertheless productive: an account of competing, time-sensitive desires sits beside a public ideal of unified social resolve, just as the interviewer tries to elicit an individual decision under the deadline. E004, E008.
