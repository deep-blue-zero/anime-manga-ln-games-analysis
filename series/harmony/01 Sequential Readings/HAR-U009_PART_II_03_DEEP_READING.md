## Rescue, betrayal and a corrected account of friendship

The section begins inside `〈recollection〉`, returning to the restaurant before the caprese arrives. Tuan asks why Kian became Miaha's friend. Kian first answers with something that appears to answer a different question: she told her parents about the medication. The apparent detour matters because her account of friendship includes what she failed to do and what she finally did against their shared plan (E001; HAR-CL054).

Her account gives a concrete distinction between publicly safe childhood and the first felt evidence of a changing body. She stopped taking the medication when bodily weakening made mortality real to her. Fear did not begin merely as fear of punishment: the pain of being a living organism became frightening. She says she could tell neither girl and delayed telling her parents until Miaha was beyond saving. This remains Kian's retrospective testimony, including her judgment of timing; the reader does not inspect a medical rescue sequence here. Yet it is clear new evidence about her actual disclosed conduct and motive, rather than another narrator-imposed description of a passive follower.

Tuan says Kian is her lifesaver. Kian calls herself a betrayer. Tuan rejects that allocation of blame and genuinely thanks her while privately withholding why she values survival: being alive lets her injure the body through forbidden pleasures. `噓はひとことも言ってない` insists on sincerity without claiming complete disclosure. Care and concealment coexist inside this apparently reassuring conversation. That qualification prevents the scene from becoming a simple restoration of transparent mutual understanding.

Kian then explains that she thought herself a balancer. She shared the girls' suffocation under resource-conscious kindness but did not initially share a wish to kill herself or others. She thought agreeing with Miaha might relieve her and let Kian keep her from the brink. This does not erase Kian's eventual participation or prove her strategy succeeded. It changes the meaning available for her earlier nods: assent can be an attempted act of care as well as agreement with a proposition (E002).

Tuan expressly repudiates her follower description as a serious misunderstanding. Her attribution of exceptional strength and nobility is a new evaluative response, qualified in part by `たぶん`; Kian herself still calls her conduct cowardice and necessity. The text supplies Kian's reason and Tuan's self-correction confidently while preserving their disagreement over how to name it. Kian being called an adult here is ethical praise for shouldering responsibility, distinct from the earlier institutional definition of adulthood. ## The recorded appeal and the changed meaning of courage

The log addresses Kian and says contact resumes after thirteen years. It defines goodness as willing the continuation of a value. Family, happiness and peace are examples whose maintenance requires consciousness. Natural growth, pain, aging and death are then presented as limits to moral permanence; health technology is condemned for allowing one good to cover everything. Tuan identifies both voice and thought as unmistakably Miaha's. The prose gives that recognition emphatically; analysis should not weaken it into a merely arbitrary possibility. What recognition alone does not establish is the complete bodily history since the reported death, the technical status of the voice, or the common cause of thousands of deaths. Those remain different questions (E018; HAR-CL061).

The resumed log retains the same ID and an explicit `playtime:2m52s06ms` field. Its historical argument moves from a visible sovereign who can be overthrown to discipline distributed inside people. The Three Musketeers maxim becomes a demand extended to everyone, with a protected long life held hostage. This larger-scale reciprocity recalls the earlier public-identity hostage lesson while sharpening the speaker's objection to its unlimited scope. The quoted history and universal claims remain hers.

The appeal then narrows from world history to the three girls. The caller says Kian and Tuan did not come to her side, describes hurt, and asks Kian to prove ownership and impermanence now so that they may return to their shared day. The repeated appeal for `勇気` is ethically charged beside the opening recollection. Tuan had come to recognize Kian's care and survival as courage; the recorded appeal recruits courage toward a fatal proof of allegiance. The word does not have one neutral meaning shared by both evaluations (E019).

Kian's apology follows, traced involuntarily by Tuan's lips. A phrase previously taken as a possible will or an isolated address to the dead now has a recorded solicitation immediately before it. This is direct new evidence of interpersonal pressure and of the utterance as a response within a call. It substantially changes the available causal account of this particular scene. It still does not prove that persuasion alone, molecular will control, or a specific combination explains Kian's action, and it does not establish the caller's role in every simultaneous event.
