## An ordinary father and a limited denial

The meeting's first emphasis is recognition rather than estrangement: despite thirteen years of aging, this is plainly the father Tuan remembers. Her identification connects the defeated participant in the ethics gathering, the departure after her near-fatal attempt and the man now before her. It does not make those events one causal story already explained by him. Tuan says the journey was difficult and many people died. His expression of regret provokes her direct accusation that he told Miaha to kill. He denies that accusation. The denial is clear speech from a newly encountered participant, rather than an independently verified acquittal. E001.

His answer about the thirteen years also qualifies an easy picture of stationary exile. He says he sometimes traveled, using identities supplied by the organization to reach most places without revealing who he was. The new claim supplies an institutional means of concealed mobility; it does not disclose a particular journey, an actual earlier encounter with Tuan or the identity of the note writer. Nuaza has been found, but his availability to the world was not the same thing as his availability to his daughter. E001–E002.

They walk shoulder to shoulder, and Tuan tries not to miss a word. The posture makes paternal instruction physically available again while preserving her accusation and distrust. Familiarity enables the explanation; it does not announce forgiveness. Her hands rubbing against the evening cold also keep the exposition in an ordinary vulnerable body. A jacket, falling temperature and riverbank remain material conditions around the account of controlling minds. E002–E003.

## A guarded technology waiting for a disaster

Nuaza describes the working group as uniquely capable of controlling the world, with computational resources and medical plans gathered in Dian Cecht. He then corrects Tuan's assumption that they are still principally researching the technology: most research, he says, has already finished. His description changes the alleged organization from an open-ended scientific project to a custodian preparing for a possible collapse, keeping a technology secret until a feared contingency would justify distributing its seeds. His wish that this time never come supplies a restraint in his self-account. His statement that Miaha and others seem to think differently introduces disagreement without yet explaining their full plan. E002.

This is a substantial disclosure, and it should not be reduced to a vague possibility merely because a participant gives it. It is also not a tour of a working system. No computational installation, medical plan, protected document or current distribution operation is inspected here. Most research being finished also need not mean that Gabriel's ongoing model extension has stopped; different scope and institutional knowledge remain alternatives to an automatic accusation that she lied. His “only organization” and world-control descriptions are his scope claims. They clarify his stated institutional purpose without certifying omnipotence or making his denial of ordering deaths true by implication.

The reward-system discussion extends earlier accounts through feedback. Nuaza confirms Tuan's broad paraphrase and the familiar meeting metaphor, then describes recursive interaction: choices alter the reward systems that produce later choices, so small deviations can grow and make will unstable or difficult to predict. The explanatory emphasis is on a changing relation among agents, not a single hidden miniature person issuing commands. His scientific account remains fictional, attributed exposition, rather than a real-world theorem established by this reading. E003.

The motive he gives for controlling that relation predates Tuan's birth. Elders at WHO, life governments and medical corporations feared renewed catastrophe and sought a safety net against irrationality. Tuan supplies the remembered imagery of riots, ethnic slaughter and escaped nuclear weapons; her father agrees. The response to mass violence is therefore represented as a project to control the source of choice itself. Fear of losing civilization supplies its moral language, rather than proving that no other safety arrangement could have existed. E003–E004.

Nuaza's account identifies overlapping members inside existing institutions, and Tuan narrates the group as positioned even above WHO's already exceptional power. This is a strong account of concentrated informal authority, not evidence that every person in those institutions belongs to the group. The overlap matters precisely because official organizational names do not exhaust the channels through which decisions and money can travel. Vashilov's earlier allegation now has a participant's institutional account alongside it, without becoming a displayed financial audit. E004.

## Information control, rather than an impassable membrane

The blood-brain barrier is a particularly consequential new disclosure. Nuaza says molecules that cross it were developed quickly, and that the belief that it protects brains from medical molecules was a misunderstanding his group deliberately circulated. In his explanation, the relevant papers were publicly available but buried amid other papers through information management. He describes molecular disguise using coverings similar to enzymes and proteins. Thus the asserted obstacle was not simply defeated in a hidden laboratory: its public status as an obstacle was itself managed. E004.

This materially requalifies the reader's use of the earlier technical exchange. CL042/K029 accurately preserved what the liaison and Tuan said at U007, including qualifications and diagnostic limits; CL058/K043 kept a proposed research route and missing implementation distinct. Those historical speech frontiers stay intact. Nuaza now offers a contrary insider account of technical possibility and deliberate epistemic asymmetry. One cannot continue using the earlier membrane explanation as an uncontested present guarantee. Equally, a claimed ability to enter a brain is not proof that every brain is currently monitored, that every molecule performs the same function or that the mass deaths have now received a complete causal demonstration. No cited paper is retrieved in this scene. E004.

Tuan recalls Gabriel's detailed reward model and privately joins it to molecular access as a possible route to controlling will and consciousness. Her father says their problem lay in the research's direction. The distinction is important: access and modeling may be prerequisites in his account, but their combination does not settle what the program should produce or what it actually did. It prepares the later side-effect problem rather than answering it in advance. E005, E009, E011–E012.

## When the body's remaining nature becomes a target

Tuan objects to the project of controlling will itself. Nuaza answers by comparing the suppression of harmful thought with everyday molecular suppression of bodily disease. The reasoning is an analogy, carrying an evaluative category from one practice to another. His quoted “harmful” thought is not an independently agreed diagnostic class. E005.

The literal confusion field holds Tuan's failure to supply a ready rebuttal. She almost says free will, then recounts human projects of containing nature through cities, systems, managed bodies and the suppression of unhealthy habits. If the brain is part of the body, she asks, where is the ground for exempting it? She loses her ground and sits on the sand, watching boys with a dog and asking why human souls deserve a respect not granted a dog's soul. That is a specific destabilization of her argument. It does not prove that every possible ethical defense of mental autonomy has been refuted, or that she consents to the experiments that follow. E005.

Nuaza's celebration of a society with minimal death, equality, peace and love then meets the smell of food excluded from that society. The meal list names masgouf, chicken and bread, rice with meat and kebab. The riverbank does not simply oppose a scientific proposition with an irrational appetite. It supplies the sensory residue of things ruled out by a definition of health, showing that a successful order may exclude valued experiences. The source now names the earlier masgouf as opened carp; the attribution remains this narration's meal description, rather than an external culinary correction. E006.

## The middle position is Tuan's, not an adjusted Miaha

Tuan recalls Miaha's hatred of the caring society and its members' self-binding obedience to diffuse norms. The ownership language becomes personal and bodily: Miaha's breasts, bottom and body belong to Miaha. Tuan says she shared that appeal as a schoolgirl and probably still does somewhere within herself. The present qualification matters alongside the earlier force of identification. E007.

Her professional compromise occupies the gap between Miaha and society. She found an environment near war where she could smoke and breathe more easily, while explicitly wanting to avoid participation in war itself. The role gives her a usable intermediate place, rather than an unqualified identity as either obedient citizen or uncompromising revolutionary. This explains a sustained pattern of tactical participation without proving that professional aid is insincere or that every institutional tie has been discarded. E007.

She then explicitly withdraws her own occasional doppelganger image. It was a lie: she had imagined how Miaha might have become if she had adapted to society. The passage gives unusually direct evidence against treating likeness as identity. The imagined adjusted Miaha is a construction through which Tuan understood herself, not an observed alternative life or a complete explanation of the actual woman's choices. Her attachment and resemblance remain; the equivalence does not. E008.

## Distress as the institution's limit case

Reports of increasing youth self-killing precede Nuaza's account of recruiting distressed adolescents. Tuan treats those reports, the list of violent bodily acts and the unspoken sense that something is wrong as signs of souls incompatible with the order. The wider claims about what people felt beneath consciousness remain her social interpretation; no population-wide access to private minds is shown. The account links care's public success to injuries it fails to make legible within its own value system, without establishing that everyone felt the same thing. E008.

Nuaza identifies one reason for inviting Miaha: she was a concentrated point of the stress this society produces. If the program could subdue such a strong will toward death, he says, it could control anyone. He reports taking in many young people, particularly those seeking death while watching their bodies weaken through disordered eating, and “treating” them. He names the desired system of harmonious will the Harmony Program and calls the experiments on Miaha and others its limit tests. This is now an explicit reported experimental history, rather than only a donated-body lead or Tuan's hypothesis about research. E009.

His “one reason” leaves other motives unenumerated. The account does not explain the earlier death report, the full custody timeline, informed consent, each subject's outcome or Miaha's current whereabouts. The intense living will he describes as the target of control strengthens the research connection without supplying a complete survival story. His claim of many children and Tuan's subsequent image of a dozen desperate children should not be silently turned into an audited cohort size. Anonymous participants remain a reported group; none has enough individuated evidence for a fabricated personal record. E009–E010.

## Jealousy does not cancel the objection about purpose

Tuan refuses a morally flattering explanation of her first anger. It is not initially anger that Miaha became an experimental subject. Her first inward question is why she herself was not chosen. She imagines that the adults wanted deeper despair and probably had recruitment nets at emergency ethics or therapy centers; that imagined arrangement remains conjectural. She connects her father's discovery of Miaha's despair with Kian's remembered “edge,” then interprets his departure from wife and daughter in those terms. The causal account of abandonment belongs to her uptake, not an explicit confession of that precise motive by him. E010.

Inside the anger field she admits something like jealousy, extending it through the ordinary resentment of an unchosen child. She nevertheless calls the experiments grotesque and says “最低ね” to her father. Personal injury and a defensible ethical charge coexist. Recognizing the jealousy does not refute the charge, while the charge should not erase the disturbing first wish to have occupied Miaha's selected place. This is stronger and more specific than a generalized diagnosis of Tuan as jealous or a sentimental reading of pure protective concern. E010.

Nuaza answers with a counterfactual rescue argument: without their intervention the children would have remained in danger and might eventually have died. Tuan objects that he is intentionally exchanging result and purpose. The distinction takes his therapeutic benefit claim seriously enough to locate what it cannot establish. A helpful outcome, even if granted, would not by itself establish that the experimental objective was rescue. Conversely, the experimental purpose does not prove no rescue occurred. His pained nod, qualified agreement and admission of imperfection show strain in the encounter without making him confess every accusation. E011.

## A harmonious choice and the question of “I”

Nuaza names an unforeseen grave side effect, then acknowledges that it might have been predictable by following the logic fully. Tuan reaches an answer before the reader receives a fuller description from him. She imagines reward systems in harmony, choices without conflict and actions that are self-evident, then asks what it means for “I” to be here. Her spoken conclusion is that consciousness disappeared. E011–E012.

The conceptual turn is firm: a project advertised as arranging harmonious will threatens the very conscious self whose decisions it claims to improve. Conflict is made relevant to the presence of the first-person subject, not merely to bad judgment to be removed. The title's promise of harmony therefore acquires a cost within this conversation. The last line is still Tuan's inference. Nuaza has not yet answered, confirmed which subjects experienced what, explained a residual behavior or given an operational account of disappearance.
