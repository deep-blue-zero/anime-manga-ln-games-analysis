# 『青春ブタ野郎はハツコイ少女の夢を見ない』
## Volume 7 Deep Dive
### Choosing life without moral innocence: grief, rewritten futures, recognition, and kindness taking the hand of kindness

**Primary source:** Original Japanese EPUB listed above.  
**Cumulative scope:** Volumes 1–7.  
**Spoiler boundary:** Complete spoilers through Volume 7 only. No Volume 8 or later information is used to settle the final world-state or Shoko-memory questions.  
**Provenance note:** This is the canonical v2 migration of the previously completed Volume 7 analysis. The old response survives in project context in unusually high detail, but this Markdown is still treated as an archival reconstruction rather than falsely labeled a byte-identical export.

---

# 1. Central thesis

Volume 6 forced Sakuta to say something he had been able to say for nearly everyone except himself:

> 「僕は……生きたい」

> 「僕は、生きていたい……」

Volume 7 asks the harder question that follows:

> **Can Sakuta actually live after choosing life when survival does not feel morally clean?**

The book begins by confirming the worst possible consequence of the Volume 6 ending.

Mai dies.

The doctor's language is restrained:

> 「手は尽くしましたが……残念です」

Mai's mother strikes Sakuta and screams:

> 「麻衣を返してぇ！」

Nodoka arrives still dressed for work and demands:

> 「なんで、咲太が守ってあげなかったんだよ！」

> 「なんで、お姉ちゃんを幸せにしてあげなかったんだよ！」

Sakuta's mind immediately begins constructing the familiar conclusion:

> `──僕が死ねばよかった`

But this time he stops.

That interruption is one of the most important signs that Volume 6 changed him.

Mai used her life to preserve his. If Sakuta now declares his continued existence a moral mistake, he does not merely condemn himself. He empties Mai's choice of meaning.

So the central question becomes:

> **What do I owe the dead if I remain alive?**

Volume 7's answer is unusually mature because it refuses moral purification.

After Sakuta eventually prevents the fatal collision and returns to a state in which neither he nor Mai is struck, he walks through the snow crying:

> 「正しいわけないだろ……」

> 「間違いだらけだよ……」

> 「ちっともよくないだろ……こんなの……いいわけない……」

He does **not** say:

> I found the morally correct route.

He says, in effect:

> **I chose life, and the choice still carries loss.**

The volume's first major thesis is therefore:

> **Survival is not innocence.**

Its second thesis is relational.

Volume 6 revealed that Shoko, Sakuta, and Mai are all too willing to solve love by removing themselves from the equation.

- young Shoko distances herself so Sakuta will grieve less;
- Sakuta is willing to die so Shoko can receive his heart;
- adult Shoko sacrifices the future in which she survives;
- Mai puts her body in front of the car.

The final chapter title gives the corrective:

> **やさしさとやさしさの手をつないで**

Kindness taking the hand of kindness.

The solution is not one sufficiently good person becoming the total cost.

It is **distributed care**.

Tomoe sees Sakuta when nobody else can.

Rio cares for him in grief and later helps reason through the Shoko problem.

Nodoka helps locate Mai.

Hanawa Ryoko uses professional access.

Mai waits.

Present and future Sakuta combine knowledge.

Shoko's parents and doctors continue fighting materially.

Public awareness of organ donation changes.

An eventual donor exists outside the intimate sacrificial triangle.

The broader ethical answer is therefore:

> **The opposite of helplessness is not omnipotence. It is interdependence.**

# 2. Source, structure, and narrated sequence

The EPUB contains twenty-six spine items and twenty-one image resources. Its main sections are:

1. **第一章　灰色で空っぽの風景**
2. **第二章　あの日の雪がやむ前に**
3. **第三章　初恋少女の夢を見ない**
4. **第四章　やさしさとやさしさの手をつないで**
5. **あとがき**

The architecture is highly disciplined.

## Chapter 1 — consequence

Mai's death is confirmed. Sakuta lives inside the future produced by her sacrifice. Adult Shoko reveals that in this future she received **Mai's** heart rather than Sakuta's. The future has changed again.

The wedding ring reveals something even more painful: adult Shoko has eventually married the surviving Sakuta.

She became, in effect:

> **梓川翔子.**

The future in which Mai dies gives Shoko almost everything the sick child once feared she would never have:

- survival;
- adulthood;
- university;
- Sakuta;
- marriage.

Then adult Shoko deliberately helps Sakuta erase that future.

## Chapter 2 — return to the decision point

Sakuta wakes on December 24 in a state where nobody can perceive him.

The usual witness becomes the invisible person begging to be found.

Tomoe sees him.

That recognition allows others to perceive him.

Future Sakuta contacts present Sakuta.

Future Sakuta reaches Mai and prevents her from running toward the accident.

He then physically prevents present Sakuta from enacting the self-sacrificial route.

The two Sakuta states reintegrate.

## Chapter 3 — surviving does not solve Shoko

December 25 begins with ordinary life:

- breakfast;
- handholding;
- school;
- snow;
- friends.

Rio cries because Sakuta is alive.

But young Shoko remains critically ill, and her future schedule is disappearing.

The story then identifies a deeper origin point: the Shoko anomaly begins not merely with the middle-school girl but with fourth-grade Shoko's fear of writing a future she may never reach.

Sakuta and Mai decide together to risk the history that produced their relationship in order to give Shoko another chance.

## Chapter 4 — continuity after rewriting

The narrative resumes on January 6 in a reconfigured world.

Sakuta initially remembers the older Shoko largely as a girl from a dream. Yet that dream still led him toward Minegahara and therefore toward Mai, Rio, and Kunimi.

At Shichirigahama he passes a recovering middle-school girl after surgery.

His body recognizes her before explicit memory returns.

He calls:

> 「牧之原さん！」

The memories return.

Shoko answers:

> 「はい、咲太さん！」

The volume ends with recognition rather than metaphysical explanation.

---

# 3. The Mai-death future: grief without metaphysical consolation

The first chapter is valuable because it refuses to let the Volume 6 sacrifice function as a harmless lesson.

Mai is actually dead in this state.

Her absence reorganizes:

- Sakuta's daily time;
- Nodoka's family world;
- her mother's emotional life;
- the entertainment industry's public archive;
- Shoko's body.

Sakuta enters a state of severe grief and disorganization.

He barely eats normally.

Time becomes difficult to structure.

Rio and Kunimi eventually find him, and Rio takes him home.

This is an important reversal of Volume 3.

Rio once described her family house as a place without bodily warmth.

Now she uses it to shelter a friend whose world has become emotionally uninhabitable.

The girl who once feared being alone becomes somebody who can make another person less alone.

The public Mai also persists through recorded media. Television footage of her work survives when the woman herself does not.

One interview contains the unusually direct statement:

> 「好きなのは私の方ですよ」

This matters because Volume 1 began with a split between public Mai and private Mai. By Volume 7, her public professional self had become capable of acknowledging private love without surrendering total privacy.

Her death turns that integrated public image into an archive.

Sakuta can see her and still be unable to reach the person.

It is a brutal inversion of the Volume 1 visibility problem.

---

# 4. Adult Shoko's wedding ring: fulfilled desire surrendered voluntarily

Adult Shoko places her hands near her chest and tells Sakuta:

> 「麻衣さんの心臓があるんです」

Sakuta also notices his own chest scars are gone.

The known donor history has changed.

Then the silver wedding ring changes the emotional stakes again.

Shoko says:

> 「念願の学生結婚というものをしてしまいました」

The implication is clear.

In this future, the girl who loved Sakuta eventually becomes his wife.

That means Shoko is not giving up an unrealized crush when she helps Sakuta return.

She is giving up a lived marriage.

Her philosophy is stated directly:

> 「大好きな人には幸せになってほしいんです。いつも笑っていてほしいんです。たとえ、それがわたしに向けられた笑顔でなくても」

and:

> 「咲太君が幸せになるまで、わたしはどんな未来からでも、何度だって咲太君を助けにきますよ」

Finally:

> 「だからもう諦めて幸せになってください」

This is morally beautiful.

It is also evidence that Shoko's self-erasure pattern has become extraordinarily sophisticated.

She can surrender not only a desired future, but a fully inhabited one.

The volume admires her nonpossessive love while also giving enough evidence to ask whether Shoko has ever learned to treat her own happiness as a claim that other people must take seriously.

---

# 5. Sakuta's own Adolescence Syndrome

The largest retrospective correction in Volume 7 is that Sakuta is not merely an observer of other people's anomalies.

Adult Shoko tells him:

> 「今、わたしと咲太君がいるのは、『未来』なんです」

and:

> 「そして、そうしたのは、咲太君です」

The apparent present of the Volume 6 crisis is reclassified.

Sakuta's own inability to accept the approaching choice—between Shoko's future and his future with Mai—produced a future-oriented split or refusal.

Shoko describes:

> 「咲太君の中にある、未来を拒んだ弱い自分」

The word `弱い` should not be converted into moral condemnation. The significant point is **refusal**.

Sakuta wanted the decision point not to arrive.

The phenomenon turned that refusal into an experienced future.

This is the first clear case where the series shows one person's Syndrome operating inside a larger anomaly associated with another person.

The temporal architecture has become **nested**.

---

# 6. Dreams as the interface among possible states

Adult Shoko says:

> 「人が常識を捨てられるのは、夢の中だけですから」

She attributes part of the explanatory framework to a future Futaba Rio.

Dreaming now becomes more than ordinary symbolic psychology.

By the end of the volume, dreams can carry several kinds of information:

- memory-like residue;
- future-state anticipation;
- alternate-future autobiographical experience;
- cross-state recognition;
- causal guidance after history changes.

Tomoe dreams an unseen Sakuta before she later sees him.

Nodoka dreams Mai's accident.

Rio dreams the future in which she cared for grief-destroyed Sakuta.

Young Shoko dreams her older selves.

Final-state Sakuta remembers older Shoko first as a dream.

The safe conclusion is:

> **Dreaming is becoming a privileged channel through which otherwise inaccessible state information can survive or cross causal reconfiguration.**

What remains unresolved is whether all these dream forms obey one mechanism.

---

# 7. Invisible Sakuta and the Volume 1 inversion

After Shoko sends him back toward December 24, Sakuta wakes unable to be perceived.

He cannot simply call for help.

Rio does not see him.

His reflection is unavailable.

His attempts to declare himself through writing or typed language do not automatically restore personal recognition.

Yet he can still affect objects.

He extinguishes a lamp and covers Rio.

Material causation can remain while personal identity is socially unavailable.

Sakuta eventually puts on the school's rabbit mascot costume and goes to Fujisawa Station.

He calls:

> 「僕が見える人はいませんか！」

and:

> 「僕を見つけてくれよ！」

The formal echo of Volume 1 is explicit.

Mai once wore an absurd bunny-girl costume in a library because she needed one person to prove that she still existed socially.

Sakuta now understands the desperation from inside.

The rescuer has been forced to inhabit the heroine's earlier phenomenology.

---

# 8. Tomoe sees him: recognition as stabilization

The person who finally sees Sakuta is not Mai, Rio, or Kunimi.

It is Tomoe.

> 「先輩、なにやってんの？」

Once Tomoe recognizes him, other people can perceive him as well.

This makes her one of the most causally important people in the volume.

Tomoe also tells him that she dreamed of him the previous day.

That connects directly to Volume 2, where Tomoe and Sakuta shared possible-future states.

The strongest first-pass inference is:

> **Their earlier anomalous connection makes Tomoe unusually capable of recognizing a Sakuta who is not yet stabilized in the present social state.**

The exact mechanism is unresolved.

The broader recognition thesis becomes stronger:

> **Recognition in AoButa can be more than emotional validation. Under some conditions, one person's recognition participates in whether another person becomes socially/ontologically available.**

---

# 9. Two Sakutas: moral argument made literal

Future Sakuta communicates with present Sakuta by telephone.

The device echoes Volume 3, where two Rio states communicated through a phone rather than direct bodily confrontation.

The two Sakutas then argue about what a good Sakuta should do.

Future Sakuta says:

> 「僕は麻衣さんを助けに来たんだ」

and warns present Sakuta away from the fatal route.

Present Sakuta is horrified.

> 「僕は何してんだよ！」

Future Sakuta says:

> 「僕は麻衣さんを幸せにしたい」

and:

> 「牧之原さんには何もしてあげられない」

Present Sakuta calls him:

> 「いかれてる」

Future Sakuta answers:

> 「それでもいい」

and:

> 「生きて、麻衣さんを幸せにできるならそれでいいよ、僕は」

This is one of the series' strongest formal devices.

The Volume 6 moral conflict is externalized into two versions of Sakuta who possess different amounts of lived consequence.

Present Sakuta has not experienced Mai's death.

Future Sakuta has.

Neither is counterfeit.

Neither is simply “the good one.”

The difference demonstrates that moral judgment is time-indexed by experience.

---

# 10. Mai and the grammar of shared happiness

Future Sakuta reaches Mai through the help of Nodoka and Mai's manager Hanawa Ryoko.

Mai immediately understands that he did not come simply to see her.

When she learns what her alternate-future sacrifice accomplished, she says:

> 「私は、ちゃんと咲太を守れたんだ」

and:

> 「私はやっと咲太の力になれたんだ」

This is psychologically coherent. Mai has long known that Shoko was there for Sakuta during an earlier period when Mai was not.

Her death becomes proof that she could finally protect him.

Future Sakuta refuses to romanticize the outcome:

> 「よくないよ、麻衣さん」

He can recognize her agency and still reject death as a good result.

Mai nevertheless tries to go to the accident because she believes present Sakuta will die otherwise.

Future Sakuta physically restrains her.

The intervention is likely life-preserving and still coercive.

Then Sakuta promises to make her happy.

Mai corrects him:

> 「別に、私は咲太に幸せにしてもらわなくていいわよ」

> 「ふたりで幸せになるの。私と咲太のふたりで」

This may be the most important relationship line in the first seven volumes.

The grammar changes from:

> one partner produces the other's happiness

into:

> **a plural subject participates in becoming happy.**

The relationship is moving beyond rescuer/rescued structure toward co-authorship.

---

# 11. Farewell to adult Shoko: carrying rather than repaying

Adult Shoko realizes that her intervention produced the Mai-death future and describes herself as having failed.

Sakuta does not reduce her life to that failure.

He tells her:

> 「全部、僕が持っていくから」

and:

> 「全部、未来に持っていくからさ」

This is a decisive change in his gratitude ethic.

He no longer says:

> I owe you my body.

He says:

> I will carry what you gave me.

Then they say:

> 「さよなら、翔子さん」

> 「ばいばい、咲太君」

The finality of `さよなら` matters.

Elsewhere the series has increasingly invested ordinary future-oriented expressions such as `またね`, `いってきます`, and `おかえり` with enormous weight.

Here the two allow an ending.

Sakuta does not look back.

The first love remains formative without needing to retain possession of his future.

---

# 12. Integration of the two Sakutas and moral residue

Present Sakuta still reaches the dangerous location and appears almost peaceful as he prepares to accept death.

His martyrdom has become morally aesthetic to him.

Future Sakuta pushes him away.

Sakuta experiences both:

- pushing;
- being pushed.

The two states converge.

The rabbit suit remains empty.

The narration makes the identity result explicit:

> 「咲太の意識はひとつに戻った」

The integration parallels Rio in Volume 3.

The earlier self is not destroyed as false.

The later self is not crowned as uniquely authentic.

The mature Sakuta contains both:

- the boy willing to die;
- the boy who has learned to refuse death.

Then comes the crucial ethical sequence:

> 「正しいわけないだろ……」

> 「間違いだらけだよ……」

The healthy change is not that Sakuta feels no guilt.

It is that he can feel guilt **without immediately turning guilt into another act of self-destruction**.

That is genuine development.

---

# 13. `ただいま` / `おかえり`: return rather than salvation

Sakuta eventually finds Mai where she waited.

He says:

> 「ただいま、麻衣さん」

She answers:

> 「おかえりなさい、咲太」

This domestic pair is the opposite of `さよなら`.

It does not mean the moral problem has disappeared.

It means a person returned to a relationship that expected his continued presence.

The next morning deliberately contains no metaphysical grandeur.

There is breakfast.

There is school.

Mai and Sakuta hold hands.

The sea looks more beautiful because Sakuta is alive to see it with her.

The book begins repositioning ordinary life as the endpoint the supernatural plot exists to protect.

---

# 14. Rio's tears: friendship beyond explanation

When Rio sees Sakuta alive, her composure collapses.

> 「大丈夫じゃない！」

> 「二度と、会えないんだと思ってた」

She says:

> 「絶対に、梓川は自分を犠牲にすると思ったから」

and finally:

> 「梓川が生きててよかった……」

Later, after Sakuta explains the extraordinary events, Rio's response is simply:

> 「そっか」

This is important development for the “logical witch.”

Not every crisis requires an explanatory conquest.

Sometimes the most important fact is:

> your friend is alive.

The group then builds a large snowman and takes photographs.

Ordinary adolescence becomes recovery evidence.

---

# 15. Bounded responsibility and the vending-machine revelation

Sakuta encounters fundraising and donates money.

He is self-conscious about the motive.

Part of him is altruistic.

Part of him is guilty.

Part of him is bargaining with the universe—perhaps a good act will somehow help Shoko.

The novel does not require pure motivation before action can have value.

Sakuta then confronts a broader limit.

There are too many suffering people in the world for one person to respond maximally to all of them.

A sustainable moral life requires something like:

> 「過度な期待はせずに、かと言って絶望もしないで」

Do not expect omnipotence.

Do not surrender to despair.

Help where actual capacity and relation make action possible.

This is a major correction to Sakuta's rescuer psychology.

At a vending machine, in the middle of this unresolved Shoko crisis, he suddenly realizes:

> 「僕は、もう幸せなんだ……」

This does not mean Shoko no longer matters.

It means Sakuta no longer believes misery is the proper proof that he cares about someone who is suffering.

The volume's ordinary-happiness thesis is:

> **Happiness is not proof that life is morally uncomplicated. It is the capacity to inhabit ordinary life without requiring all suffering to disappear first.**

---

# 16. The deeper Shoko origin: fourth-grade futurity

The future schedule continues to erase itself as the prior future becomes inaccessible.

Sakuta eventually realizes that middle-school Shoko is not the deepest origin point.

The original conflict begins with fourth-grade Shoko being asked to write a future she may not physically possess.

That reclassifies the entire Shoko architecture.

The older Shokos are best understood, at this boundary, as genuinely experienced future possibilities generated from the younger Shoko's unresolved relation to futurity.

This is not the same as saying they are imaginary.

They have:

- bodies;
- memories;
- causal effects;
- scars;
- relationships.

The important correction is that they are not best modeled as one adult body simply traveling backward along a fixed timeline.

---

# 17. Rio's scientific caution and the cost of rewriting

When Sakuta begins hoping that returning to the deeper origin will magically save Shoko, Rio refuses wishful science.

Changing history does not automatically create:

- a cure;
- a transplant donor;
- a new medical technology.

A heart disease remains a heart disease.

This is excellent use of Rio because it prevents the supernatural framework from trivializing medical scarcity.

The more frightening consequence is relational.

If fourth-grade Shoko resolves her Syndrome differently, Sakuta may never meet the high-school Shoko who saved him.

Then he may never choose Minegahara.

Then he may never meet:

- Rio;
- Kunimi;
- Mai.

Sakuta says:

> 「麻衣さんと出会わない人生なんて、人生じゃないっての」

The intervention now risks not one life but the causal history of Sakuta's current personhood.

---

# 18. Sakuta's largest procedural ethical improvement: telling Mai first

Earlier Sakuta often followed this structure:

> identify crisis → design solution → accept cost alone → inform others afterward.

Here he does something different.

On the bus, he tells Mai:

> 「やっぱり、僕は牧之原さんを助けたい」

He discloses the risk before acting.

The risk affects both of them, so both of them receive standing.

Mai understands that rewriting Shoko's past may erase the history of their own relationship.

She still says:

> 「過去を変えてもいいよ」

She reframes the risk:

> 「少し遠回りをするだけ」

and imagines:

> 「もう一度咲太と出会って」

> 「もう一度咲太と恋をして」

Sakuta promises:

> 「必ず、麻衣さんを見つけるから」

Mai answers:

> 「そしたら、ふたりで幸せになるわよ」

This is romantic, but its strongest meaning is procedural.

The couple is willing to risk continuity **together** rather than one partner deciding the other's future through secret sacrifice.

---

# 19. Young Shoko's final schedule and the ethics of “someday”

Young Shoko reveals that she has dreamed the older futures.

Her speech briefly shifts from the usual `咲太さん` to the older Shoko's more intimate:

> 「咲太君」

The voice shift makes continuity audible.

Yet Shoko still proposes a self-erasing solution.

She wants a future in which Sakuta never meets her, so that when she dies he will not grieve.

Sakuta finally gives her the lesson he only recently learned:

> 「自分を大事にしていいんだって！」

You are allowed to value yourself.

Shoko's final future schedule replaces guaranteed milestones with an ethical orientation.

She writes:

> 『ありがとう』

> 『がんばったね』

> 『大好き』

and:

> 「いつか、やさしい人になりたいです」

The importance of `いつか` should not be underestimated.

A child who once could not safely imagine adulthood can once again write:

> **someday.**

This does not mean she has stopped wanting university, romance, or long life.

It means the future no longer has to be guaranteed as a milestone chart before it can be meaningful.

Mai tells Sakuta to finish grading the homework.

Rio provides a red pencil.

Sakuta draws a huge `花マル`.

This is the mature rescuer in miniature.

He cannot fix Shoko's heart.

He can recognize her work.

---

# 20. Final state: history changes, disposition remains

After the deeper rewrite, January 6 arrives in an apparently stable world.

Much of Sakuta's ordinary social life remains.

He remembers older Shoko primarily as a dream girl from Shichirigahama.

That dream still influenced him enough to lead him toward Minegahara.

The relationship therefore retains causal force even when its public historical status changes.

Mai's earlier film about a girl with serious congenital heart disease and transplantation has become culturally important and increased public awareness of the issue.

This should not be overstated into a direct causal claim such as:

> Mai's film caused Shoko's donor to exist.

The stronger reading is institutional and thematic:

> **Public art can become part of the social ecology through which strangers recognize suffering and donation becomes thinkable.**

Then Sakuta and Mai pass a recovering girl with her parents at Shichirigahama.

Sakuta's body responds before conscious memory does.

He turns.

He calls:

> 「牧之原さん！」

The name unlocks the relationship.

Warm memory returns.

Shoko cries and answers:

> 「はい、咲太さん！」

The younger address form returns too.

The final proposition is therefore:

> **A relationship can survive the rewriting of explicit autobiographical history as dream, disposition, name, place, language, and embodied recognition.**

Memory matters enormously.

It is not the only carrier of continuity.

# 21. Phenomenon map through Volume 7

A cautious cumulative map now looks like this:

### Volume 1 — Mai

Perception / collective recognition / memory erasure.

### Volume 2 — Tomoe

Possible futures / repetition / anticipatory selection.

### Volume 3 — Rio

One autobiographical self → two simultaneous bodies.

### Volume 4 — Mai/Nodoka

Two autobiographical selves → crossed visible embodiment.

### Volume 5 — Kaede/Hanakaede

One body → discontinuous autobiographical/personality continuities across time.

### Volume 6 — Shoko/Sakuta

Future-state overlap / transplant future / possible organ duplication.

### Volume 7 — Shoko/Sakuta

Nested future states / Sakuta's own future refusal / observer stabilization / dream residue / identity reintegration / history rewriting.

The strongest cumulative conclusion is **not** one universal quantum law.

It is:

> **Adolescence Syndrome is a family of literally operative anomalies whose forms repeatedly organize crises of recognition, memory, identity, social possibility, and futurity.**

# 22. The scar model: strengthened, not fully proven

Volume 6 gave the hypothesis:

> future Shoko possesses Sakuta's heart while current Sakuta also possesses it; the world reacts to the duplicated heart through the chest wounds.

Volume 7 strengthens this dramatically.

In the Mai-donor future, adult Shoko carries Mai's heart instead.

Sakuta notices his own scars are gone.

This is strong convergent evidence.

The correct classification is:

- **SI:** the scars are causally associated with temporal duplication/incompatibility involving Sakuta's heart.
- **UA:** the exact physical law and why it manifests as those wounds.

The series now provides better evidence for a literal bodily connection than for Sakuta's earlier psychological-guilt interpretation.

# 23. Ensemble as care network

Volume 7 is the strongest evidence yet that *AoButa* has become an ensemble drama rather than serial heroine routing.

## Rio

Scientist, caregiver, grieving friend, skeptic of magical optimism, provider of the red pencil.

## Tomoe

The singular observer who restores Sakuta to public perception. The girl once terrified of losing her own social place becomes the person whose recognition gives someone else a place.

## Nodoka

Grief-stricken sister, operational ally, and later ordinary household participant. She also carries alternate-state residue through dreams.

## Kunimi

Continues his characteristic care through presence rather than forced interpretation.

## Hanakaede

Continues practicing ordinary school re-entry. The post-crisis life remains part of the ensemble.

## Sakuta's father

Acknowledges that too much responsibility was placed on Sakuta during the family collapse. Adult accountability finally becomes explicit.

## Shoko's parents and medical staff

Their continued presence matters because Shoko is not saved by teenage sentiment alone. Medicine and family care remain real constraints and resources.

The care network at New Year is therefore materially different from early-volume Sakuta-plus-one-girl structure.

# 24. Japanese voice, address, and future grammar

Several address and formulaic expressions carry unusual thematic weight.

## `咲太さん` versus `咲太君`

Younger Shoko usually says `咲太さん`.

Older Shoko uses `咲太君`.

When young Shoko in intensive care says:

> 「わかっていますよ、咲太君」

future-state continuity becomes audible.

At the final beach reunion:

> 「はい、咲太さん！」

the younger form returns.

## `さよなら`

Sakuta and adult Shoko choose a genuinely final farewell. The word matters because the series usually prefers forms that assume continuation.

## `いってきます / いってらっしゃい / ただいま / おかえり`

These ordinary domestic formulas now form an existential architecture.

Going is meaningful because return is assumed.

Coming home means another person maintained a place in which return mattered.

## `ふたりで幸せになる`

Mai's grammar is philosophically decisive.

The subject is plural.

Happiness becomes joint practice rather than one person's gift to another.

## `自分を大事にしていい`

The `～ていい` construction gives permission.

Sakuta is not commanding Shoko to feel differently.

He is telling her she is allowed to include herself among the people worthy of care.

# 25. Institutions, public art, and the anonymous donor

The ending's successful transplant can be criticized as dramatically convenient, but the novel does important work to prevent it from becoming a costless miracle.

A donated heart still comes from someone who died.

Another family still experienced tragedy.

The improvement is narrower and ethically meaningful:

> Sakuta and Mai are no longer deliberately converted into sacrificial means for a chosen recipient.

Organ donation returns to its legitimate institutional form: care after death rather than intentional production of the death.

Mai's film also gives public representation a more positive role than Volume 1 allowed.

Celebrity once threatened to make Mai public property.

Now authored public performance can distribute recognition outward and make serious illness more legible to strangers.

The public sphere is therefore not inherently corrupt.

The question remains:

> what kind of recognition does visibility create?

# 27. Ethics and philosophical implications

## Survival is not innocence

Sakuta survives without declaring every lost future justified.

That is ethical maturity.

## Gratitude is not existential debt

Adult Shoko releases Sakuta from the idea that being saved obligates him to surrender his own life or romantic future.

## Love without possession

Shoko can desire Sakuta and still want his happiness with Mai.

The danger is that this nonpossessiveness may become chronic self-negation.

## Partnership creates shared jurisdiction

Mai's relation to Sakuta's existential choices is neither ownership nor irrelevance.

## Bounded responsibility

No person can respond maximally to all suffering.

Moral finitude need not become cynicism.

## Witnessing can be sufficient

The flower mark is important because Sakuta finally performs care without claiming causal control over the outcome.

## Distributed care

The final survival state is produced by a network rather than a hero.

This is the volume's mature alternative to self-sacrificial exceptionalism.

# 28. Counterreadings, limitations, and unresolved tensions

### Counterreading: the final donor is a deus ex machina

There is real dramatic convenience. The strongest defense is that transplant medicine always depended on contingent donor availability and the novel has spent two volumes establishing that material fact. The ending still relieves the intimate zero-sum structure through an outcome the protagonists cannot engineer.

### Counterreading: the third path is costless

False. Another donor death remains implicit.

### Counterreading: Shoko's selflessness is wholly healthy

The book repeatedly shows her solving problems by removing her own claims. Sakuta's `自分を大事にしていい` is a necessary correction.

### Counterreading: Shoko's final schedule is merely lowered expectations

That tension exists. The stronger reading is that she changes future from guaranteed milestones to ethical direction, but material loss should not be romanticized into spiritual superiority.

### Limitation: future Sakuta remains coercive

He restrains Mai and physically overrides present Sakuta. Life-saving purpose does not remove the autonomy issue.

### Limitation: Shoko drugs Sakuta

Her loving intent does not erase bodily-autonomy violation.

### Unresolved: Tomoe as observer

The formal rhyme with Volume 2 is strong; the exact physical rule is not.

### Unresolved: final-state memory

Sakuta's Shoko memories return. Universal recovery for every character is not established.

# 29. What Volume 7 changes in the series-so-far model

1. The apparent Volume 6 present is retrospectively reclassified as a possible future state.
2. Sakuta is confirmed as an Adolescence Syndrome subject, not merely investigator/witness.
3. Syndromes can apparently nest inside one another.
4. Fourth-grade Shoko becomes the deepest identified origin point of the Shoko future architecture.
5. Dreams become major cross-state information carriers.
6. One observer—Tomoe—can apparently stabilize a possible Sakuta into public perception.
7. Multiple authentic Sakuta states can reintegrate without one being declared counterfeit.
8. The scar/duplicate-heart model is substantially strengthened.
9. Sakuta's ethics shift from unilateral sacrifice toward disclosure, consultation, and shared risk.
10. Mai/Sakuta receives its clearest co-authorship formulation: `ふたりで幸せになる`.
11. Adult Shoko's fulfilled marriage future is revealed and voluntarily surrendered.
12. Young Shoko's self-erasure tendency remains even after she understands the futures.
13. The care network becomes genuinely distributed across peers, family, professionals, medicine, media, and strangers.
14. Ordinary happiness becomes a central ethical category.
15. Relationship continuity is shown to persist through dream, disposition, place, name, and bodily recognition even after history changes.

The series' recognition thesis now extends to:

> **seeing, remembering, naming, recognizing, and caring are different operations—and under some conditions, recognition may even participate in what version of a person becomes socially available.**

# 33. Cumulative archival synthesis through Volume 7

The first seven volumes can now be described as a progressive enlargement of recognition.

Volume 1:

> see the person society is erasing.

Volume 2:

> do not confuse social adaptation with falsity, but do not let adaptation erase desire.

Volume 3:

> contradictory needs can belong to one person.

Volume 4:

> loving another person does not require becoming her.

Volume 5:

> a later self can be real even when an earlier autobiographical continuity returns.

Volume 6:

> your own life belongs among the lives worth protecting.

Volume 7:

> living afterward does not require pretending the choice was morally pure.

The Shoko arc completes this movement by transforming kindness itself.

At first, kindness is something an exceptional person gives another.

By the end, solitary kindness has become dangerous because every exceptional person tries to become the sacrifice.

The sustainable form is the chapter title:

> **やさしさとやさしさの手をつないで.**

One kindness takes another kindness by the hand.

Then another.

The supernatural problem is not defeated by one hero becoming morally pure enough.

A life continues because:

- someone sees;
- someone waits;
- someone cries;
- someone thinks;
- someone writes;
- someone grades;
- someone treats;
- someone donates;
- someone returns.

That is the mature answer of the first major movement of *AoButa*.
