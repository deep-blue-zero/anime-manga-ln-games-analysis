# 『青春ブタ野郎はプチデビル後輩の夢を見ない』
## Volume 2 Deep Dive
### Air-reading, self-fashioning, possible futures, and the cost of trying to optimize away every social risk

**Primary source:** Original Japanese EPUB listed above.  
**Cumulative scope:** Volumes 1–2.  
**Spoiler boundary:** Strictly through Volume 2. The later nature of Shoko, later temporal mechanics, and later developments in Rio's or Tomoe's lives are not imported.  
**Provenance note:** This is a migrated reconstruction of the established pre-v2 analysis. It preserves the original first-pass model and leaves exact XHTML locators pending rather than fabricating them.

---

# 1. Central thesis

Volume 1 examined what happens when a person becomes so socially unacknowledged that she turns into `空気`.

Volume 2 turns the same word around.

Koga Tomoe does not disappear because nobody reads the atmosphere. She is in danger of disappearing into **the labor of reading it too well**.

Her basic social skill is `空気を読む`: detecting what the group expects, what will look embarrassing, which response might threaten belonging, and how to modify herself so that she remains compatible with the social field around her. The novel does not mock this ability as simple shallowness. Tomoe's fear is not imaginary. Social exclusion happens. Rumor has consequences. A badly managed confession can restructure a peer group. Being alone can become socially legible as failure.

The problem is therefore not that Tomoe changes herself.

She has already done so deliberately and successfully after moving from Fukuoka. She learned makeup, fashion, hair, speech, and metropolitan peer norms. When asked about the current self she created, she says:

> 「……気に入ってる。すごく好き」

Sakuta answers:

> 「それが古賀だよ。前がどうだろうと、今のその姿が古賀」

That exchange establishes a crucial *AoButa* principle:

> **A deliberately made self can still be a real self.**

The problem begins when self-fashioning becomes compulsory friction-removal—when every desire, embarrassment, conflict, and attachment must be optimized in advance so that Tomoe never has to occupy an awkward social position.

The volume's central thesis is therefore:

> **A self can be deliberately made without being false. But self-authorship fails when making yourself becomes erasing every part that creates friction.**

The temporal/repetitive phenomenon literalizes the fantasy at the heart of that strategy. What if a person could preview the future, reject the embarrassing outcome, and reroll until social life no longer contained an intolerable risk?

Volume 2's answer is:

> **A human life cannot be calculated into a state where nothing important has to be risked.**

The final title-level formulation, `君が選んだこの世界`, matters because Tomoe's growth is not toward perfect prediction. It is toward accepting the world that follows from a choice she cannot guarantee will be painless.

# 2. Source, structure, and narrated sequence

The source contains twenty-eight spine items and twenty-two images. The main chapter sequence is:

1. **第一章　ブタ野郎には明日がない**
2. **第二章　明日は明日の風が吹く？**
3. **第三章　噓の恋人はじめました**
4. **第四章　ありったけの噓を君に**
5. **第五章　ラプラスの小悪魔**
6. **あとがき**

The chapter titles deliberately move between futurity and deception.

The opening immediately denies Sakuta and Mai the simple ordinary continuation promised by Volume 1. June 27 repeats. The first task is therefore epistemic: determine whether time itself is looping, whether Sakuta is dreaming, whether the world is being overwritten, or whether something else is happening.

Tomoe becomes the key because the repeated future is structured around a social problem she is trying to avoid. Sakuta's involvement appears secondary but real: he experiences the repeated state with her and retains memory across the resets.

The fake relationship then converts abstract social anxiety into sustained performance. Sakuta and Tomoe must act as though they are a couple to neutralize a confession and protect Tomoe's place in her group. The performance itself produces consequences neither can fully control—most importantly, Tomoe genuinely falls in love.

The late repetitions are therefore different from the early ones. Tomoe is no longer merely trying to protect a social group. She is trying to construct a future in which she can keep the emotional experience she wants without accepting the fact that Sakuta's love remains directed toward Mai.

The final resolution requires not correct prediction but truthful loss.

# 3. Causal synopsis: what changes and why

Tomoe is placed in a socially dangerous position when Maesawa confesses to her. Rejecting him risks social fallout because another girl in her group is interested in him. Tomoe attempts to avoid being cast as the person who disrupted the group.

The anomaly produces repeated future-like experiences beginning from June 27. Sakuta and Tomoe share them for reasons the volume does not fully explain.

Sakuta initially resists the plan to fake a relationship because it requires lying to a large number of people and risks instrumentalizing everyone around them. He nevertheless agrees after Tomoe's predicament activates his history with Kaede and his desire not to leave someone alone inside a social crisis.

The fake relationship succeeds instrumentally: it changes how Maesawa and the group interpret Tomoe. But its emotional cost grows.

Tomoe experiences:

- being treated as Sakuta's girlfriend;
- dates and ordinary couple-like time;
- the security of being publicly chosen;
- and a form of intimacy she cannot later reduce to “just acting.”

She eventually wants to preserve those experiences. The repeated future becomes an attempt to produce an ending in which she can keep Sakuta without destroying his relationship with Mai or humiliating herself.

That outcome does not exist.

Sakuta remains in love with Mai through repeated variations. Tomoe's feelings, meanwhile, become *more* real through repetition rather than less.

She explicitly rejects the idea that feelings simply reset:

> 「気持ちは変わるよ」

and:

> 「繰り返した分だけ、積み重なっていく……」

The final movement requires Tomoe to confess within an outcome she knows will hurt.

> 「あたしは、先輩が好き……」

> 「大好きぃ───！」

Sakuta does not return the romantic feeling. He answers the effort:

> 「よくがんばったな」

The successful future is therefore not the one in which Tomoe gets what she wants. It is the one in which she stops requiring the future to protect her from having wanted it.

# 4. The phenomenon: possible futures, repetition, and explanatory limits

The best first-pass model is **not** conventional time-loop certainty.

The volume repeatedly invokes Laplace's demon, prediction, and quantum-style language. The phenomenon behaves like an extremely accurate future possibility or anticipatory simulation centered on Tomoe's attempts to avoid an unwanted social outcome.

A cautious working model is:

> **Tomoe unconsciously generates, inhabits, or observes highly accurate possible-future states beginning from June 27 and repeatedly rejects outcomes she cannot accept, while Sakuta becomes a co-experiencer for an unresolved reason.**

This is a **strong inference**, not textual law.

## 4.1 Narrated experience versus public history

The most important bookkeeping distinction is:

> **What Sakuta and Tomoe experience is not necessarily what the final public history contains.**

The fake romance can therefore be emotionally real for the two participants even if it never occurs in the stable history perceived by everyone else.

This produces what can be called **counterfactual intimacy**.

A relationship experience can matter because it was lived, even if the final public state does not contain it.

## 4.2 What remains unresolved

At the Volume 2 boundary:

- whether the states are alternate worlds, simulations, dreams, or something else is unresolved;
- why Sakuta shares them is unresolved;
- whether Tomoe consciously controls the reset is only partially clear;
- why June 27 is the effective return point is not established as a general law;
- literal quantum entanglement is not proven;
- generalization to other people is unwarranted.

## 4.3 Laplace language

Rio's explanatory vocabulary is useful because Tomoe's social strategy resembles prediction. If one could know every relevant variable, perhaps embarrassment could be avoided.

The novel then shows the human limit of that fantasy:

> emotion itself becomes a new variable because living through a possible future changes the person doing the predicting.

The observer is not outside the system.

Tomoe accumulates experience.

That is why rerolling does not return her to emotional zero.

---

# 5. Koga Tomoe: a made self threatened by compulsory adaptation

Tomoe is easy to reduce to “a girl desperate to fit in.” That loses the strongest part of her characterization.

She has already demonstrated extraordinary adaptive agency.

After moving from Fukuoka, she studies how to present herself in a new social environment. Her standard Japanese is part of that presentation. So are clothes, hair, makeup, and knowledge of peer norms. None of this makes her false.

The book explicitly validates the present Tomoe.

The problem is the reason she cannot tolerate social solitude:

> 「ひとりは……恥ずかしい」

This is not merely fear of loneliness. It is fear that loneliness will be *seen* and socially interpreted.

Being alone becomes an exposed status.

That distinction matters because Tomoe's behavior is governed by an imagined spectator even when nobody is actively attacking her.

Her fears are nevertheless grounded. The volume demonstrates that group membership can become unstable quickly. Social atmosphere is not a hallucination.

Tomoe's growth therefore cannot be:

> “stop caring what anyone thinks.”

That would simply turn Sakuta's coping style into a universal moral command.

Instead, her arc moves toward selective authorship:

- she can retain the metropolitan self she likes;
- she can care about friends;
- she can still dislike humiliation;
- but she no longer has to erase every desire that creates social risk.

Her kindness outside status competition is important evidence of character continuity. She helps a lost child and retrieves Nana's strap without an obvious social payoff. That matters later because the final belonging she gains is not simply a reward delivered by Sakuta. Her own prior care helps create the relationship through which Yoneyama Nana reaches toward her.

The final social rescue is therefore partly self-authored.

---

# 6. Sakuta: selective disobedience and the ethics of lying for someone else

Tomoe diagnoses Sakuta accurately:

> 「わかっててあえて読まないじゃん」

That line refines the Volume 1 `空気` model.

Sakuta is not socially blind. He often knows exactly what the atmosphere demands and refuses to obey.

Through Volume 2, the series now has three different relations to `空気`:

- **Mai:** erased when the atmosphere ceases to acknowledge her.
- **Tomoe:** governed by the need to anticipate and obey it.
- **Sakuta:** aware of it but willing to violate it selectively.

The fake relationship tests whether Sakuta's refusal of social expectations makes him more ethically free or simply permits another kind of unilateral action.

He initially knows the deception is morally dubious. He admits:

> 「正しい判断をしたという自覚はない。」

He considers whether helping Tomoe functions as `罪滅ぼし`—an attempt to compensate for what he could not prevent with Kaede.

He also recognizes the scale of the lie:

> 「全校生徒約千人に、嘘をついてやる」

That is important because the novel does not present him as innocently stumbling into the deception. He chooses it while knowing that “helping” does not make the method clean.

His statement:

> 「僕はたったひとりでいいけどね。そのひとりが必要としてくれたら、生きていける」

should not be converted into the volume's universal solution. It describes Sakuta's own low-volume social need. Tomoe eventually gains a different answer involving plural friendship and social continuity.

This is one of the first places where *AoButa* resists making Sakuta's psychology the normative endpoint for the heroine.

---

# 7. Mai and the continuing central relationship

Volume 2 is critical because it proves Volume 1 did not end in a temporary heroine-resolution romance.

The June 27 repetition effectively threatens the history in which Mai accepted Sakuta's confession. The relationship must therefore be reconstructed by choice.

When Sakuta explains the fake relationship, he also admits that Tomoe's predicament activated his experience with Kaede. Mai listens rather than reducing the situation to jealousy.

Her key response is:

> 「その嘘の責任は、ちゃんと取りなさいよ」

This is not permission to romantically exploit Tomoe. It is a demand that Sakuta not treat a socially useful lie as costless just because his intention was benevolent.

Mai also develops a relationship with Kaede independent of Sakuta's immediate mediation. She helps with clothes and outside-world motivation. This matters because the series begins building female relationships that are not simply reactions to the male protagonist.

The bunny suit also changes meaning.

In Volume 1, it was a public desperate attempt to become visible.

In Volume 2, within the romantic relationship, it can become private, chosen intimacy.

The same costume therefore moves from failed public recognition to authored erotic/playful presentation.

Mai and Sakuta discuss the future, including the possibility of the same university. The relationship is beginning to acquire ordinary infrastructure rather than remaining an anomalous crisis bond.

Mai's cheek kiss and:

> 「これでわかったでしょ？」

reaffirm reciprocity after the unstable future states.

# 8. Rio, Kunimi, Kaede, Nana, and ensemble continuity

## Rio and Kunimi

Rio reveals that seeing Kunimi makes her happy. She is distressed by the feeling because Kunimi has a girlfriend and because acknowledging desire threatens a relationship she values.

Sakuta offers rational advice. Rio answers:

> 「今は正論なんて聞きたくない」

Later she says:

> 「今のところ、自分でなんとかするつもりだから」

Kunimi appears to have noticed more than he says and does not force disclosure.

This subplot is an important ethical counterpoint to Tomoe.

Volume 2 **does not** teach that every feeling becomes healthy only when confessed. Sometimes respecting a feeling includes refusing to conscript another person into resolving it.

## Kaede

Mai's involvement broadens Kaede's care network. Kaede is no longer supported only by Sakuta, which is important both for her future autonomy and for reducing the impossible pressure on the sibling bond.

## Yoneyama Nana

Nana's role in Tomoe's social future matters because it prevents the ending from saying:

> one boy replaced the need for peer community.

Tomoe can lose a desired romance and gain forms of belonging that are not consolation prizes.

## Young Shoko

The volume ends with a middle-school girl who introduces herself:

> 「わたしは、牧之原翔子と言います」

The name is the same as Sakuta's remembered first love, and the resemblance is enough to create a serious unresolved problem.

At the Volume 2 boundary, that is all.

No later temporal answer belongs in this artifact.

# 9. Japanese voice, dialect, and self-fashioning

Tomoe's voice is one of the clearest reasons to analyze the series in Japanese.

She usually uses:

- `あたし`;
- `先輩` for Sakuta;
- contemporary standard-Japanese peer speech;
- socially calibrated informal register.

Her Hakata/Fukuoka speech can leak through under emotion or surprise.

The correct interpretation is **not**:

> dialect = authentic Tomoe; standard Japanese = fake Tomoe.

That would contradict the book's explicit validation of her chosen present self.

A better model is:

> **register reveals history and effort.**

The standard register is an authored competence. The dialect leakage demonstrates that self-authorship does not erase prior linguistic embodiment.

This is a subtle but important identity point. People may have multiple genuine voices.

The volume also gives `空気を読む` unusually strong thematic weight. Tomoe's skill is not merely “social awareness.” It is predictive labor performed under the threat of embarrassment and exclusion.

# 10. Time, memory, and counterfactual experience

Volume 2 forces the project to separate four different orders earlier than most series would:

- **narrated order:** the sequence in which the reader experiences repeated futures;
- **experienced order:** Sakuta and Tomoe's accumulated memories;
- **public history:** the final world other characters appear to inhabit;
- **causal order:** the still-uncertain relation among Tomoe's anticipation, reset desire, and state transition.

This prevents a common analytical mistake:

> saying the fake relationship “did not happen,” therefore it cannot have changed Tomoe.

For Tomoe and Sakuta, it was experienced.

It therefore belongs to their psychology even if it does not belong to stable public chronology.

This produces an early *AoButa* principle:

> **an unrealized future can still become part of a person's lived history if the person remembers living it.**

# 11. Institutions, rumor, phones, and social atmosphere

The phone is the volume's most important ordinary object.

For Tomoe, phone responsiveness is bound to belonging. Messages require management. Delayed replies can become social signals. The device externalizes the constant demand to remain synchronized with the group.

This contrasts with Sakuta and Kaede, for whom networked communication is associated with cyberbullying and danger.

The same technology therefore carries opposite social meanings:

- **connection as belonging** for Tomoe;
- **connection as exposure** in Sakuta/Kaede's history.

The fake relationship also demonstrates how rumor functions as an institution without formal administration. Nobody needs to issue a rule. The school collectively updates its model of Tomoe based on visible relational cues.

Gender asymmetry appears in the sexual-rumor dimension. A girl's status can be reinterpreted rapidly through assumptions about dating and sexual availability. The book uses this socially while also indulging in fanservice, creating another paratextual tension.

---

# 12. Place, ordinary life, and illustrations

The volume's dates and summer spaces matter because possible futures are built out of ordinary adolescent activities:

- school;
- part-time work;
- dates;
- beach/outing expectations;
- phone communication;
- changing clothes;
- waiting.

The phenomenon is spectacular, but what Tomoe wants to optimize is not an apocalypse. It is ordinary social adolescence.

The illustrations continue the franchise's dual function: they preserve emotional beats while also marketing attractive female bodies. That is particularly relevant in a volume about a girl whose social value is partly mediated through appearance and peer legibility.

The private bunny-suit scene should be read as an authored shift in meaning from Volume 1, not simply repetition of a franchise icon.

---

# 13. Ethics and philosophy

Volume 2's strongest philosophical question is whether freedom consists in:

> being unaffected by other people.

The novel says no.

Tomoe's problem is not that she needs others. Sakuta also needs others, though fewer of them. Rio needs Kunimi and friendship. Mai needs both work and Sakuta.

The issue is whether dependency allows choice.

Tomoe's early social life treats the anticipated judgment of others as veto power over her own desires. Her development does not abolish dependency; it makes dependency less totalizing.

The fake relationship also produces a useful ethics-of-lying distinction.

A lie can:

- protect someone;
- redistribute social pressure;
- create genuine new feelings;
- injure third parties;
- and impose future obligations.

“Benevolent” does not mean costless.

# 14. Counterreadings and limitations

### Counterreading: Tomoe's problem is superficiality

Too weak. Her self-fashioning is deliberate, skilled, and genuinely liked. The problem is coercive adaptation, not fashion itself.

### Counterreading: the correct lesson is “stop caring about friends”

Contradicted by the ending, where plural social belonging remains important.

### Counterreading: repeated futures prove literal quantum entanglement

The volume does not establish that. The scientific language is heuristic.

### Counterreading: Tomoe's romantic confession means she should have pursued Sakuta harder

The ethical force of the ending lies in acknowledging a desire without converting it into entitlement.

### Limitation: fake-dating romantic comedy can soften deception costs

The genre makes the arrangement pleasurable to read. That does not remove the fact that Sakuta and Tomoe manipulate the interpretive environment of hundreds of peers.

### Unresolved: why Sakuta shares the future

This remains one of the most important mechanical questions carried forward.

# 15. What Volume 2 changes in the series-so-far model

1. `空気` expands from social erasure to social overcompliance.
2. Sakuta is confirmed as someone who reads the room and deliberately disobeys it.
3. Self-fashioning is not equated with falsity.
4. Possible futures can be psychologically real even when absent from public history.
5. Emotion accumulates across repeated states.
6. The anomaly appears relational: Sakuta can share another person's future-state experience.
7. Mai/Sakuta survives temporal destabilization through renewed choice.
8. Mai begins functioning as part of Kaede's care network.
9. Rio/Kunimi provides a model of feeling that need not be confessed.
10. The young Shoko cliffhanger destabilizes Sakuta's remembered biography.

The recognition model becomes:

> **being socially safe is not the same as being self-authored.**

# 19. Cumulative archival note

Volume 1 asks what happens when society stops seeing someone.

Volume 2 asks what happens when someone spends so much effort anticipating society that the imagined audience begins governing the self from inside.

Sakuta's answer—simply refusing the atmosphere—is useful but not universal.

Tomoe's more difficult achievement is to remain a socially connected person while permitting herself to make a choice that the social world cannot make painless in advance.
