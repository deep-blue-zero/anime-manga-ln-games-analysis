# 『青春ブタ野郎は迷えるシンガーの夢を見ない』
## Volume 10 Deep Reading
### Empathy without conformity, voice after social contamination, and choosing which “everyone” gets to matter

**Spoiler boundary:** This reading uses Volumes 1–10 and the already analyzed story-position bonuses *Animal Land* and *Spring Days*. It does **not** use Volume 11 or later material to identify 美東美織, authenticate 霧島透子's claims, or explain the new university-period phenomena.

---

# 1. Central thesis and volume role

The central thesis of Volume 10 is:

> **Empathy is not the same thing as conformity. Learning what other people think can deepen relationship and moral understanding; allowing every perceived social judgment to vote on who you are dissolves the boundary of the self.**

A sharper formulation is:

> **The answer to the social world is not to become immune to other people. It is to choose whose voices deserve jurisdiction.**

Volume 10 begins the university movement by returning to the oldest AoButa word: `空気`.

But `空気` has changed scale.

In Volume 1, school atmosphere could erase Mai.

In Volume 2, Tomoe survived by reading atmosphere too well and rebuilding herself around anticipated judgment.

In Volume 8, institutional normality made one educational route look like the only legitimate route.

Volume 10 asks a larger question:

> **What if “the air” is not merely a classroom mood but an enormous decentralized social system that tells people how a university student should dress, laugh, desire, speak, date, dream, and interpret the ambitions of others?**

The chosen test case is almost perfect: 広川卯月, the girl who previously **could not read the air**.

That inability caused isolation. It also protected her from many of the ordinary cruelties circulating around her. She could be laughed at without understanding the laugh. She could fail to notice that conventional peers regarded her idol career as embarrassing. She could pursue Budokan without constantly metabolizing the statistical improbability and the contempt of spectators who found the dream childish.

Volume 8 had already shown that this was not merely ignorance. Uzuki had constructed an ethic around it after dropping out of ordinary high school:

> **Her happiness is not something “everyone” gets to decide.**

That makes Volume 10's phenomenon unusually cruel. Uzuki does not simply gain social intelligence. She gains access to a field of judgments that had always existed but that she had previously lacked the equipment to perceive.

At first the gain looks almost entirely positive.

She can understand Sakuta's sarcasm.

She can synchronize with ordinary conversation.

She can resolve conflict with Nodoka through empathic language.

She can notice embarrassment, irony, hostility, praise that is not really praise, and the difference between what people say and what they mean.

Then the cost appears:

> `私も、みんなに笑われてたんだ`

And later:

> `私の頭の中に、みんながいて、みんなが色々言ってて……いちいち聞いてたらさ、何が自分なのか、わかんなくなってきた`

The problem is not that other people's judgments are unreal.

Many of them are real.

That is precisely why the crisis is harder than simple peer pressure.

Uzuki learns that:

- people did laugh at her;
- apparently supportive remarks can contain envy or condescension;
- the idol industry is brutally selective;
- Sweet Bullet can currently draw around two thousand people rather than the roughly ten thousand she associates with Budokan;
- thousands of idol groups exist while only a tiny number reach that stage for the first time in a given year;
- some people really do view persistent dream-chasing as pathetic;
- and, worst of all, **she herself is capable of the same contempt**.

Her crisis therefore becomes an ontological one:

> **If a viewpoint exists inside me, is it mine?**

> **If knowing what others think changes what I think, where does “myself” end?**

Sakuta gives the volume's strongest philosophical answer:

> `他人の気持ちを知れば、自分の気持ちだって変わるんだよ`

and:

> `『自分』なんて意外と曖昧だからな。何が自分かなんてわかるはずない`

This rejects a fantasy of an uncontaminated authentic self.

There is no pristine Uzuki who can simply be restored by deleting knowledge. Human beings are changed by encounters. Sakuta himself changed because he knew Mai's grief and Shoko's desire to live. Information is not external dirt that can always be washed away.

Yet Volume 10 does **not** conclude that individual selfhood therefore disappears into society.

Its resolution comes by distinguishing two versions of `みんな`.

The first is anonymous:

> the vague social majority; the eyes passing on campus; the imagined audience; the people who laugh at the volunteer, the busker, the struggling idol, or anyone who appears to be trying too hard.

The second is concrete:

> Nodoka, Yae, Ranko, Hotaru, the fans still clapping in the rain, and the people who have actually shared the labor of Sweet Bullet.

At Hakkeijima, Sakuta tells Uzuki:

> `今こそ空気を読めよ、づっきー`

The line deliberately reverses the whole book.

The skill that caused her crisis becomes useful once its object changes.

Do not read the **anonymous social average**.

Read the people whose actions are in front of you.

The four members know the odds. They know people laugh. They know Budokan may not happen. They are singing anyway.

Their “air” contains something no statistical model can replace: chosen commitment.

Uzuki's final declaration makes the transformation explicit:

> `私がみんなを武道館に連れていく！`

followed by the reciprocal demand that the members and fans take **her** there too.

The leader no longer bears hope alone. The group no longer passively borrows Uzuki's certainty. Ambition becomes distributed.

Volume 7 transformed kindness from unilateral rescue into a network.

Volume 10 does something similar to dreaming:

> **A durable dream is not one person's certainty imposed on everyone else. It is a future whose uncertainty can be carried collectively.**

A second major thesis follows:

> **Maturity is not the end of adolescence if adulthood still requires people to invent a socially legible self.**

Chapter 1 is titled `思春期は終わらない`.

Sakuta tells Miori that he would like to have graduated from adolescence by now.

The novel spends the rest of its pages demonstrating why that hope is premature. University gives students more freedom than high school, but it also removes ready-made identities: no uniform, no permanent classroom, no fixed class membership. Freedom creates a new anxiety—**if nobody tells me what a university student looks like, I must decide what I look like**—and that anxiety produces another form of synchronization.

The university movement therefore does not abandon the series' adolescence premise.

It radicalizes it:

> **Adolescence is not merely a biological age. It is the unstable work of becoming socially recognizable without allowing recognition to become total authorship.**

Finally, the ending changes the causal stakes. The working model developed by Sakuta and Rio treats Uzuki as having been incorporated into a broader field of social synchronization. Then, after Uzuki leaves the university, a Santa-clad woman visible apparently only to Sakuta says:

> `せっかく、空気を読めるようにしてあげたのに`

and identifies herself as:

> `霧島透子`

At the Volume-10 boundary, this is **not proof** that every earlier hypothesis was false. It is a claim by a newly embodied, selectively visible figure whose reliability is unknown.

But it is enough to force a major epistemic downgrade:

> **The collective-network model remains a powerful description of what the phenomenon expresses socially. It can no longer be treated as a sufficient causal explanation.**

The university arc begins with “Adolescence Syndrome” returning as a rumor.

It ends with somebody claiming to be able to cause it.

## 2.3 Narrated sequence

The main narrated movement begins on **September 30**, at the end of Sakuta's first university summer break / start of the second semester.

Important dated anchors include:

- September 30 — mixed-faculty seminar social gathering; Sakuta meets Miori.
- October 3 — Spanish class; Uzuki and Miori meet; Nodoka reports the Sweet Bullet dispute.
- October 4 — Uzuki's air-reading shift becomes unmistakable.
- October 6 — viral long version of the earbud commercial is already circulating; Uzuki's fame accelerates.
- October 17 — Sakuta watches Uzuki's changed quiz-show performance.
- Later October — Miura/Misakiguchi excursion, Budokan confrontation, weekend Odaiba/Hakkeijima live sequence.
- Monday after the Hakkeijima performance — Uzuki submits her university withdrawal and encounters Sakuta on campus.

The novel's time span is therefore compact—roughly one month—but the social shift is enormous.

# 3. Causal synopsis: what changes and why

## Chapter 1 — University freedom and the return of adolescence

Sakuta attends a social gathering for a mixed-faculty seminar and meets **美東美織**, a first-year international-business student who does not own a smartphone.

Miori is socially fluent but difficult to place. She can read motives quickly, attract attention without apparently seeking intimacy, and leave situations before they become too binding. She describes herself as socially “hungry” while behaving as though hunger and satiety are both equally tolerable.

The university environment is explicitly contrasted with high school. Students gain freedom from:

- uniforms;
- fixed classrooms;
- assigned seats;
- stable class membership.

But those structures had also supplied belonging automatically. University students now have to construct their own communities.

Miori casually raises the subject of Adolescence Syndrome. Sakuta realizes he has gone roughly a year and a half without encountering it.

The same chapter establishes Sakuta's current ordinary life:

- university study;
- restaurant work;
- part-time tutoring;
- Rio working at the same tutoring school;
- Hanakaede's more independent high-school/work life;
- Mai's continuing career and university attendance.

On October 3, Sakuta encounters Uzuki in Spanish class and sees the looseness of university relationships around her. Uzuki behaves as her usual socially unusual self, while a peer group shifts seamlessly from intense friendliness in her presence to a different lunch conversation after she leaves.

Nodoka then reports a conflict inside Sweet Bullet.

Two members have already departed. The group's original three-year Budokan objective has not been achieved. Individual work is expanding, particularly for Uzuki, and the agency is considering a solo path.

Nodoka is not simply jealous. She wants two goods that may no longer align easily:

1. Sweet Bullet reaching Budokan together;
2. each member's individual labor being rewarded.

Sakuta initially underestimates the crisis as a “luxurious problem” caused by success.

That judgment becomes one of the volume's useful corrections to his own confidence.

## Chapter 2 — Uzuki enters the social field

On October 4, Uzuki changes.

She now:

- dresses more like her campus peers;
- understands implied meaning;
- catches facial reactions;
- anticipates what Nodoka wants to hear;
- uses socially smoothing language;
- and produces an `愛想笑い` Sakuta has never previously seen from her.

She describes the change positively: she feels synchronized with everybody else.

The conflict with Nodoka suddenly becomes easy to repair because Uzuki can now understand what had previously remained implicit.

This is precisely what makes the phenomenon difficult to classify as harm.

Uzuki has apparently gained a capacity she lacked.

The benefits are real.

Sakuta nevertheless senses that the speed and totality of the synchronization are wrong.

Miori provides a mundane social explanation: people consume the same websites, stores, search results, trends, and judgments. Nobody needs to coordinate clothes explicitly if they are all drawing from the same informational field and avoiding the same risks—looking unfashionable, trying too hard, standing out incorrectly.

Rio then constructs the supernatural working model. If the effect is Adolescence Syndrome, perhaps Uzuki is **not** the one generating it. Perhaps ordinary air-reading university students form an unconscious, entanglement-like social network that generates averaged values such as `普通` and `みんな`, and Uzuki has become incorporated into it.

Rio explicitly treats quantum entanglement as analogy, not established real-world physics.

The same chapter supplies the market catalyst. A longer version of a wireless-earbud advertisement reveals that the previously unidentified singer covering a Kirishima Toko song is Uzuki. The video rapidly passes one million views. Public discourse suddenly discovers a different Uzuki:

- not merely the natural-airhead television personality;
- but a highly capable singer;
- visually striking;
- potentially solo-marketable.

Attention intensifies.

On the train, Uzuki is newly embarrassed by Sakuta looking at a sexy magazine cover featuring her. She hides from students discussing her instead of cheerfully introducing herself as she once might have.

Then she sees 赤城郁実 recruiting volunteers for school-refusal support. Other students pass by or privately mock the “high-consciousness” effort.

Uzuki understands the mockery.

That recognition turns inward:

> `私も、みんなに笑われてたんだ`

## Chapter 3 — social knowledge becomes self-dissolution

Ten days later, Uzuki's new social competence has entered her media performance.

She answers quiz questions correctly and understands the host's entertainment rhythm. A prior comic identity built partly on her unpredictable misunderstanding becomes harder to reproduce authentically.

Nodoka is increasingly worried because Uzuki now hides behind smooth affect rather than transparent confusion.

Tomoe provides another important social mirror. Sakuta asks how she responds to a street musician whom many people ignore. Tomoe can sincerely admire the courage of performing in public while also admitting that she might look away or laugh along with friends. If the musician later became famous, she could imagine claiming she had seen him early.

This is exactly the contradiction tormenting Uzuki:

> **People can admire aspiration and punish visible aspiration at the same time.**

Sakuta and Uzuki then leave campus for Miura/Misakiguchi in an improvised `自分探し` excursion.

Away from campus, old Uzuki's spontaneity becomes more visible, but the new social awareness remains.

She reveals that a university friend asked:

> why she was “still” doing something like being an idol.

Sakuta interprets the remark as envy mixed with self-protection. Uzuki has a socially legible thing she can call hers. People without such a thing may experience her certainty as accusation.

They eventually travel to Nippon Budokan.

There, Uzuki gives the dream numbers:

- thousands of idol groups exist;
- only a tiny number reach Budokan for the first time in a given year;
- she places Sweet Bullet around thirtieth in an informal imagined queue;
- the group can currently fill around two thousand seats;
- Budokan represents roughly ten thousand.

The gap becomes emotionally real.

Uzuki asks:

> `ここって、本当に私が来たい場所だったのかな`

Sakuta correctly refuses to answer for her.

She then explains the identity crisis directly:

> `私の頭の中に、みんながいて...何が自分なのか、わかんなくなってきた`

Sakuta answers that other people's feelings really do change one's own. The self is not perfectly sealed. What has been learned cannot simply be unlearned.

Uzuki asks whether she is experiencing Adolescence Syndrome, whether “recovery” would make her unable to read the air again, and which version Sakuta prefers.

He answers:

> `どっちもいいと思うよ`

The distinction is important. He does not say either is interchangeable. He affirms both.

But the scene ends with Uzuki giving him the most polished `愛想笑い` he has yet seen.

His recognition remains incomplete.

## Chapter 4 — the lost voice and the concrete “we”

Mai reveals that she obtained a driver's license and bought a car. Part of the reason is professional convenience; another part is explicitly relational:

> `車なら堂々と出かけやすいからね`

Her fame has made public dating difficult, so she creates infrastructure for ordinary intimacy.

She and Sakuta drive to an Odaiba live.

Sweet Bullet's performance is extremely strong. The text makes a point of separating **artistic competence** from **market reach**: they can look fully professional and still be far from Budokan-scale popularity.

Then Uzuki's body fails.

Her dance slips out of synchronization.

Her voice disappears.

The other four members immediately cover her parts and complete the performance.

At the hospital, Mai explains from professional experience that severe stress can produce temporary inability to speak and other bodily symptoms. This is significant because the text supplies an ordinary embodied mechanism rather than declaring the aphonia supernatural.

The agency confirms that a solo offer exists and had already been refused once. The group realizes Uzuki's crisis is not reducible to the solo-debut rumor.

Nodoka then reveals the deeper failure. Before the live, Uzuki had asked whether they genuinely thought Sweet Bullet could reach Budokan.

Usually the members answered uncertainty by borrowing Uzuki's confidence. This time, when **Uzuki herself** needed somebody else to say it first, nobody could.

Nodoka recognizes that the group had outsourced hope to its naturally optimistic center.

She decides:

> `今度はあたしたちが引っ張ってく`

The following day, Sweet Bullet performs outdoors at Hakkeijima without Uzuki.

Rain causes an equipment failure. The four continue a cappella.

Some casual spectators leave.

That fact is essential. The performance is not rewarded by magical universal recognition. Many people simply do not care.

Uzuki, hidden among the audience, sees this and says:

> `これが私たちの現実`

She admits her speaking voice has returned but says she has no right to rejoin the stage because she has discovered a person inside herself who laughs at Nodoka and the others for chasing an unlikely dream.

Sakuta's response is not to deny the cynicism.

He says Nodoka already knows.

The others know.

They know they are not successful enough.

They know people laugh.

They know Budokan is far away.

They sing anyway.

Then comes the book's decisive inversion:

> `今こそ空気を読めよ、づっきー`

Uzuki reads **their** intentions.

She enters during her normal solo section from inside the crowd.

The fans part into a path.

The members pull her onto the stage.

Power returns and sunlight breaks through the clouds at the climax. This is formal apotheosis, not evidence by itself of supernatural weather control.

The five finish together.

## Ending — `Congratulations`

The next morning, Uzuki has already submitted a university withdrawal form.

She accepts the solo opportunity but refuses to graduate from Sweet Bullet.

Her new promise is reciprocal:

- she will take the members and fans to Budokan;
- they must take her there too.

She finally explains why she enrolled in Statistics Science. She wanted to understand:

> `みんなってなんなのか`

because she thought understanding “everyone” would help her understand Nodoka and the others.

Her loving motive was therefore always present inside the dangerous experiment.

Sakuta congratulates her:

> `卒業、おめでとう`

The line treats her departure as completion of the question that brought her there, not as simple academic failure.

Uzuki then runs through the university crowd, perceiving the surrounding attention but no longer synchronizing to it in the same immediate way. She loudly waves goodbye to Sakuta.

The narration is careful:

> she resembles the old air-unreading Uzuki, but she has **not returned** to ignorance.

She knows people laughed.

She knows she can laugh at other people.

That knowledge remains.

Only the involuntary real-time absorption appears to have ended.

Seconds later, the ontology of the volume changes.

A woman in a Santa outfit appears beside Sakuta.

Dozens of students show no sign of seeing her.

She is surprised Sakuta can.

She says they have never met before, says he should know her, claims she made Uzuki able to read the air, and identifies herself as:

> `霧島透子`

The main story ends there.

# 4. Adolescence Syndrome / phenomenon dossier

Volume 10 contains **three different phenomena or phenomenon-like processes**, and they should not be collapsed.

## 4.1 Uzuki's air-reading transformation

### Directly observed facts

Uzuki abruptly becomes able to:

- infer implied meaning;
- recognize sarcasm and irony;
- read facial reactions;
- coordinate affect with a peer group;
- modify dress and presentation toward the campus average;
- recognize ridicule;
- use socially strategic smiles;
- and understand the gap between explicit speech and underlying intention.

The change is sudden enough that Sakuta and Nodoka regard it as anomalous.

### Rio/Sakuta working model

Rio proposes that if the event is Adolescence Syndrome, Uzuki may not be its originator.

The surrounding student population may constitute an unconscious information-sharing network producing averaged values such as `普通` and `みんな`, with Uzuki newly incorporated.

This model has three virtues:

1. it explains why Uzuki does not seem to have wished for the obvious surface effect;
2. it maps closely onto the university's belonging anxiety;
3. it captures the observed convergence without requiring explicit coordination.

But it remains **SI/TI**, not established law.

Rio herself phrases quantum entanglement as an analogy whose result can be made to look similar if interpreted conveniently.

### Sociological substrate without the supernatural

Miori supplies a mundane causal layer:

- students search the same trends;
- shop at similar stores;
- see the same model outfits;
- consume the same information;
- fear being judged unfashionable;
- and fear being judged as trying too hard.

Thus large-scale convergence does **not** require telepathy to exist at all.

The supernatural question is whether Uzuki's sudden incorporation exceeds that ordinary process.

### End-state

By the final campus goodbye, Uzuki no longer seems to perceive surrounding ridicule in real time.

But she retains knowledge acquired while she could.

This yields a crucial continuity rule:

> **Loss of anomalous access does not erase learning produced through that access.**

This parallels earlier volumes in which explicit autobiographical or world-state memory could disappear while affective, procedural, or motivational residue remained.

## 4.2 Aphonia

Uzuki's temporary inability to speak should **not** automatically be classified as a second supernatural manifestation.

Mai explicitly supplies a non-supernatural embodied pathway: severe stress can produce temporary loss of speech and other functional symptoms.

The sequence supports that reading:

- Uzuki's voice genuinely fails during the live;
- later her voice returns;
- she then intentionally conceals the recovery because she feels unqualified to rejoin the group.

The aphonia is therefore best treated as:

> **TF: real temporary bodily symptom; SI: stress-related; UA: whether the broader Syndrome state contributed indirectly.**

Thematically, however, its function is unmistakable.

A girl who cannot distinguish her voice from “everyone's” voices literally loses her voice as singer.

## 4.3 Santa-clad “Kirishima Toko”

The ending provides the strongest new phenomenon evidence.

TF:

- Sakuta sees a woman approximately college age dressed as a miniskirt Santa.
- Roughly dozens of nearby students display no sign of seeing her.
- She circles Sakuta to verify that he sees her.
- She knows his surname.
- She says this is their first meeting.
- She says he should know of her.
- she claims she made Uzuki able to read the air;
- she says `わたしはね、霧島透子って言うの`.

What is **not** established:

- that she is truthfully the same entity/person behind the uploaded songs;
- that her causal claim is accurate;
- how she became selectively invisible;
- why Sakuta sees her;
- whether she caused the entire university synchronization field;
- whether she only altered Uzuki;
- whether “Kirishima Toko” is a personal name, adopted identity, role, or something else.

At the V10 boundary, all of these remain UA.

## 4.4 Internal correction to the volume's working theory

The final scene does not justify discarding the social-network interpretation completely.

It does require downgrading it from explanatory closure.

The clean V2 statement is:

> **RC-V10-01:** The Rio/Sakuta model remains a strong description of the phenomenon's social form, but the final Toko claim means it can no longer be treated as a sufficient causal account. External agency is now a live possibility.

# 5. Hirokawa Uzuki: from protected nonconformity to chosen permeability

Uzuki is one of the cleverest title heroines in the series because her initial “deficit” was also a source of strength.

## 5.1 The pre-Volume-10 Uzuki

The V8 understanding matters.

Uzuki struggled in ordinary school because she did not intuit social atmosphere well. She could become isolated without understanding why. Her mother's response was not to train her into perfect conformity, but to help her find an educational form that fit her and to teach her that happiness could not simply be assigned by `みんな`.

That is why Uzuki could help Hanakaede.

She had already lived the proposition:

> **not fitting one social form does not mean you are defective.**

Volume 10 does not reveal that this insight was false.

It tests what happens when the person who learned to live without the majority suddenly acquires the majority's perspective.

## 5.2 Why she entered the university

The ending retroactively clarifies the entire book.

Uzuki chose Statistics Science because she wanted to understand:

> `みんなってなんなのか`

And she wanted to understand `みんな` because she wanted to understand her **members** better.

This is important morally.

Her dangerous movement toward social assimilation begins in love.

She does not seek conformity because she hates herself.

She seeks comprehension because she values Nodoka and the others.

That makes the resulting crisis a warning not against empathy but against **unbounded empathy**.

## 5.3 The gifts of air-reading

The new capacity genuinely improves parts of her life.

She can:

- understand jokes without having them explained;
- detect sarcasm;
- see why Nodoka is upset;
- repair conflicts efficiently;
- identify sexual/visual embarrassment;
- perform television banter deliberately;
- and navigate ordinary peer conversation more smoothly.

Uzuki herself says she is glad she gained it.

Any reading that simply says “conformity bad; return to old self good” therefore contradicts the text.

## 5.4 Knowledge as injury

What hurts is not social skill itself.

It is delayed reinterpretation.

Once Uzuki understands contempt, past memories change meaning.

A comment that once sounded like praise can become condescension.

A laugh that once meant nothing can become humiliation.

She realizes:

> `私も、みんなに笑われてたんだ`

This resembles retrospective trauma in a non-identical form: the event does not change, but the newly acquired interpretive framework changes what the event **was for her**.

## 5.5 The worst discovery is internal

Being mocked is painful.

Discovering the mocker inside herself is worse.

At Hakkeijima:

> `私の中にもいるんだよ。叶わない夢を一生懸命追ってるのどかたちを、笑っている私が……いるの`

This is the book's most morally sophisticated Uzuki moment.

The problem is not “society infected a pure girl with evil.”

Contempt is ordinary human possibility.

Sakuta explicitly frames it that way.

The task is not to prove oneself incapable of ugly thoughts.

It is to decide whether those thoughts receive authority.

## 5.6 Selfhood after contamination

At Budokan, Uzuki asks what is still hers when other voices occupy her head.

Sakuta refuses the fantasy that a true self remains untouched underneath all relation.

The self changes through knowledge.

This is consistent with the series' long project:

- Tomoe deliberately authors herself;
- Rio cannot expel contradictory desires;
- Nodoka must differentiate herself without denying resemblance;
- Hanakaede must inherit without substitution;
- Sakuta's choices change because other people's suffering becomes morally real.

Volume 10's new formulation is:

> **A socially affected self is not automatically a false self.**

The danger begins when social input becomes total jurisdiction.

## 5.7 The final Uzuki is neither old nor new

The ending insists on this.

She looks like the old air-unreading Uzuki because she loudly waves in a way that attracts dismissive looks.

But she has not reverted.

She knows:

- that people can ridicule her;
- what irony is;
- what envy feels like;
- what her own cynicism feels like;
- how unlikely Budokan is;
- and how the industry sees her individual market value.

Her regained freedom therefore cannot be ignorance.

It is closer to:

> **selective noncompliance after knowledge.**

That is much more mature than innocence.

# 6. Sakuta: the rescuer becomes a boundary interpreter

Volume 10 continues the ethical movement visible in V8 and the two bonuses.

Sakuta increasingly understands that caring for somebody does not entitle him to decide the answer.

## 6.1 He is not omniscient

The volume deliberately opens by letting Sakuta underestimate the Sweet Bullet problem.

He calls it a `贅沢な悩み`.

He assumes the group's shared history is too strong for environmental changes to matter seriously.

The narration later admits he had underestimated the situation.

This is healthy for the series. Sakuta's social perception is good, but not magical.

## 6.2 At Budokan he does not answer Uzuki's question

When Uzuki asks:

> `ここって、本当に私が来たい場所だったのかな`

Sakuta remains silent because:

> only Uzuki can know and decide.

This directly continues the V8 principle:

> `決めるのは咲太じゃない`

His role is becoming less “produce the correct choice” and more “help the other person hear the choice they are making.”

## 6.3 `どっちもいい`

Uzuki asks which version he prefers:

- air-reading;
- air-unreading.

He answers:

> `どっちもいいと思うよ`

and emphasizes the inclusive `も`.

This is recognition without restoration pressure.

It parallels his refusal to rank Hanakaede against Kaede.

He does not require Uzuki to return to a familiar version in order to remain recognizable.

## 6.4 But recognition remains fallible

The beautiful `愛想笑い` defeats him.

He spends the whole day with Uzuki and realizes he still has not reached her actual fear.

This is important after ten volumes of a protagonist defined by unusual observational ability.

Reading people is not mind reading.

Sakuta can notice performance without immediately knowing what lies underneath it.

## 6.5 `今こそ空気を読め`

At the final live, Sakuta does not tell Uzuki:

> “You definitely still want Budokan.”

He asks her to read the people who are visibly enacting the answer.

The intervention is provocative and characteristically Sakuta-like, but the final inference remains hers.

That is a more bounded form of intervention than many of his early-series tactics.

## 6.6 `卒業、おめでとう`

Sakuta's congratulation is one of the strongest examples of his anti-normative recognition ethic.

Dropping out of university is conventionally coded as failure.

He instead asks what university was **for Uzuki**.

If her purpose was to investigate `みんな`, if she believes the investigation is complete enough, and if she already possesses a professional path she actively chooses, leaving can be interpreted as completion.

This does not make dropping out universally wise.

It means Sakuta refuses to let the institution monopolize the definition of progress.

## 6.7 His own university motive remains unresolved

Uzuki suggests Sakuta chose Statistics Science for a reason related to her own.

He repeats the practical explanation that it was the faculty with the highest admission probability for him.

The text does not settle whether he is evading a deeper motive.

That remains **UA/FP** at the V10 boundary.

# 7. Mai: ordinary intimacy under celebrity constraint

Mai is not the title heroine, but Volume 10 continues treating her as co-protagonist of Sakuta's adult life.

## 7.1 The car as autonomy infrastructure

Mai has obtained a driver's license and purchased a car.

She says explicitly:

> `車なら堂々と出かけやすいからね`

The point is not luxury.

Her celebrity status has reduced the ordinary public space available to the couple. A car gives them a semi-private corridor through public geography.

This is a mature echo of Volume 1.

The problem is still public visibility.

The answer is no longer disappearance or dramatic confession.

It is infrastructure.

## 7.2 Professional knowledge grounds the body

When Uzuki loses her voice, Mai does not immediately invoke Adolescence Syndrome.

She has seen performers develop stress-related inability to speak, hearing problems, and other bodily dysfunctions.

This is important to the series' epistemology:

> **Supernatural experience does not license supernaturalizing every symptom.**

## 7.3 Mai's question to Nodoka

After Nodoka confesses the group's failure to reassure Uzuki, Mai asks:

> `明日はどうするの？`

She does not lecture.

She makes Nodoka articulate agency.

Nodoka answers that they will perform as four and pull Uzuki forward this time.

This is the same mature care grammar Mai has increasingly developed with Sakuta:

> prompt decision; do not monopolize it.

## 7.4 Ethical blemish: eavesdropping

At the hospital, Mai and Sakuta listen from around the corner while Sweet Bullet's manager speaks privately with the members.

The information is narratively useful.

It is also a privacy violation.

The novel largely treats it as pragmatic rather than ethically serious. The V2 ledger should preserve the boundary even when the story does not foreground it.

# 8. Ensemble and relationship-state changes

## 8.1 Nodoka: hope can no longer be outsourced to Uzuki

Nodoka's most important realization is not simply that Uzuki is struggling.

It is that the group had been relying on Uzuki to make the dream believable.

Whenever:

- a live was cancelled;
- fan numbers stagnated;
- work failed;
- members lost confidence;
- or other members left;

Uzuki was usually first to say:

> let's go to Budokan together.

When Uzuki finally asks **them** whether they believe it, they cannot answer.

Nodoka realizes they had borrowed courage from the person they assumed did not need any in return.

Her response:

> `今度はあたしたちが引っ張ってく`

is therefore a major relationship correction.

## 8.2 Sweet Bullet: friendship becomes distributed authorship

The unit's professional structure matters.

Members have separate careers:

- variety;
- drama;
- gravure;
- sports programming;
- solo-market opportunities.

The conflict is not reducible to “friendship versus fame.”

It is about how a collective survives when individual success creates asymmetry.

The final answer is not to suppress Uzuki's solo opportunity.

She accepts it **and remains in the group**.

That is a strong anti-zero-sum resolution:

> individual authorship and collective authorship need not be mutually exclusive.

Whether the workload and market pressures remain sustainable is an open question.

## 8.3 Hanakaede

The interstitial bonuses matter here.

Hanakaede now:

- uses a smartphone;
- works at the restaurant;
- attends her own school;
- travels independently;
- meets Kotomi independently;
- and attends the Hakkeijima live without Sakuta accompanying her.

At the outdoor event, Sakuta knows she is present but cannot find her in the crowd.

That is precisely the point.

She has an adolescent life he does not administer.

## 8.4 Tomoe

Tomoe's Fukuoka register still leaks under irritation.

Her earlier self-construction has not been reversed. It has become more permeable.

More importantly for Volume 10, she supplies evidence that social ridicule is often double-minded: people can admire somebody's public courage while also laughing because the surrounding group expects laughter.

## 8.5 Rio

Rio remains scientifically disciplined.

She does not accept Sakuta's preferred explanation merely because he wants a Syndrome case.

Her quantum language is explicitly provisional.

She also remains socially changed: she now wears modest makeup at university and participates in a professional tutoring role. Her post-V3 life continues rather than freezing her as “science exposition.”

## 8.6 Kunimi

Kunimi has moved into firefighting after training, another reminder that adulthood has diversified everyone's paths.

The high-school ensemble is no longer synchronized by a common institution.

That makes chosen continuity more visible.

## 8.7 Miori

Miori is the major new ensemble variable.

At the V10 boundary she is best described as:

> **a socially perceptive, smartphone-less observer who participates enough to understand the air but repeatedly controls relational distance.**

She is not an anti-social purity figure.

She uses the internet through a computer.

She follows fashion searches.

She worries about being mocked for dressing badly or trying too hard.

She therefore belongs to the same social field she describes.

Mai observes:

> `美織って、ちょっと咲太に似てるわよね`

This is a significant authorial cue, but its exact future meaning remains FP.

Miori's unusual knowledge of Adolescence Syndrome, absence of a smartphone, effortless insight into social convergence, and fast rapport with Mai should all remain prospectively indexed without later-volume assumptions.

## 8.8 Akagi Ikumi

Ikumi's volunteer recruitment scene is brief but thematically central.

She is doing socially useful work helping school-refusal students and is quietly mocked as someone who is too earnest or `意識高い`.

The scene demonstrates the mechanism of norm enforcement without formal authority.

Nobody has to prohibit her work.

A few glances and laughs are enough to make public moral seriousness feel embarrassing.

Uzuki's new sensitivity lets her finally see the cost.

# 9. Japanese voice, address, and translation-sensitive language

## 9.1 `空気を読む`

The standard translation “read the room” captures pragmatic meaning but loses the literal environmental metaphor.

In this volume, the literal `air` matters because the social field is treated as:

- everywhere;
- inhaled unconsciously;
- difficult to locate;
- produced by everyone and nobody;
- and capable of changing behavior without direct command.

The chapter title `空気の味は何の味？` plays on precisely this materiality.

## 9.2 `みんな`

This may be the single most important word in the book.

It has at least three referents:

1. **anonymous majority** — generalized social judgment;
2. **peer group** — campus friends and ordinary university students;
3. **chosen collective** — Sweet Bullet members and fans.

Uzuki's arc is partly the process of distinguishing these.

At the beginning she wants to understand `みんな` as a sociological problem.

During the crisis, `みんな` occupies her head as a diffuse tribunal.

At the end she addresses a concrete `みんな` and asks for reciprocal help reaching Budokan.

The same word moves from social abstraction to relational specificity.

## 9.3 `普通`

`普通` again functions as socially produced average rather than neutral description.

At university, clothing similarity is partly created by fear of appearing:

- unfashionable;
- too eager;
- too different.

“Normal” is thus generated by mutual anticipation.

Nobody needs to write the rule down.

## 9.4 `愛想笑い`

Uzuki's `愛想笑い` is one of the clearest markers of transformation.

This is not simply “a smile.” It is socially functional smiling: affect used to smooth interaction, conceal friction, and prevent further questioning.

Old Uzuki was difficult partly because she did not perform this layer reliably.

New Uzuki becomes socially easier to deal with and **harder to know**.

The paradox is central.

## 9.5 `声`

Voice operates at multiple levels:

- speaking voice;
- singing voice;
- figurative own voice;
- the voices of `みんな` in Uzuki's head;
- collective group vocals;
- anonymous online/public discourse.

The title heroine “loses her voice” only after the boundary between those levels becomes unstable.

## 9.6 `卒業`

`卒業` is normally institutional.

The ending repurposes it.

Uzuki leaves university without receiving a degree, yet Sakuta says:

> `卒業、おめでとう`

He treats completion as relation between person and purpose, not merely institution and credential.

That is thematically consistent with the series but ethically contestable, which the counterreading section addresses.

## 9.7 `お兄さん`

Uzuki continues calling Sakuta `お兄さん` because her relational map runs through Hanakaede.

This is important continuity. Even after becoming his university peer, she does not reclassify him primarily through campus status.

Their relationship was formed through care around Hanakaede and remains marked by that history.

# 10. Time, memory, information, worlds, and identity

Volume 10 is less about alternate histories than Volumes 6–9, but it extends the series' identity model in another direction:

> **information can permanently alter a self even when the channel through which the information arrived later disappears.**

Uzuki can apparently stop reading air in the anomalous sense.

She cannot become the person who never learned that:

- people mocked her;
- praise can conceal envy;
- she herself can mock others;
- Budokan is statistically remote.

This closely resembles earlier persistence patterns:

- artifacts surviving social erasure;
- counterfactual experiences leaving relationship effects;
- learned content surviving autobiographical discontinuity;
- alternate futures leaving motivational residue.

Volume 10 adds:

> **social knowledge survives lost access to the social channel.**

This is why the ending's “old Uzuki” appearance must not be mistaken for reset.

AoButa's increasingly stable position is that history can be rewritten, memory can fail, and anomalous states can end without restoring a metaphysically untouched prior self.

# 11. Institutions, adults, social atmosphere, and material conditions

## 11.1 University as freedom and anxiety

University removes many externally assigned identifiers.

That creates real freedom.

It also creates a new burden:

> **when the institution stops telling you what you are, you have to decide—and become legible as—the kind of person you are.**

The fashion convergence is therefore not trivial decoration.

Clothing becomes one of the new uniforms produced horizontally rather than vertically.

## 11.2 Decentralized normalization

The most politically interesting feature of Volume 10's `空気` is that it has no ruler.

Students discipline themselves through anticipated peer judgment.

Nobody orders Miori to search “autumn university student outfit.”

Nobody orders Sakuta to buy what the mannequin wears.

Nobody orders groups to converge.

Each person's desire not to look wrong helps create the average that later appears objective.

The social norm is therefore:

> **collectively authored and individually obeyed.**

## 11.3 The idol industry

Sweet Bullet's problem is institutional as well as emotional.

The agency sees asymmetric market potential in Uzuki.

The viral commercial gives empirical support to that judgment.

The market suddenly rewards the individual more efficiently than the group.

The solo-debut proposal is therefore not villainous exploitation on its face. It may genuinely be a rational professional opportunity.

The conflict arises because rational individual development can destabilize a collectively authored dream.

## 11.4 Social media and attention

The same online ecosystem can:

- reveal Uzuki's singing ability;
- create millions of views;
- generate new fans;
- amplify solo opportunities;
- fabricate disbandment rumors;
- monetize fear;
- and turn bodily distress into speculative content within hours.

Volume 10 thus treats attention as both resource and hazard.

## 11.5 Tutoring ethics

Sakuta's workplace contains a small but meaningful cautionary subplot: a male tutor misreads a female student's dependency/attention romantically and creates trouble.

The function is not merely comedy.

Volume 10 is centrally about reading social signals.

The tutoring incident reminds us that **believing you can read another person's feelings can itself become a boundary violation**.

Sakuta's more restrained behavior with Uzuki should be read against that background.

# 12. Place, season, objects, ordinary life, and formal motifs

## 12.1 Campus

The university is the physical embodiment of the social-world problem.

Students flow through it rather than remaining in a stable class.

Its freedom makes grouping more self-authored and therefore more normatively revealing.

## 12.2 Miura / Misakiguchi

The improvised excursion gives Uzuki a landscape outside campus synchronization.

Cycling through open fields, eating tuna, and moving through broad sky temporarily loosen the pressure of `みんな`.

The `p197` illustration captures her physically expansive posture beside the bicycle: arms raised, face open, hair moving in the wind.

It is one of the volume's clearest visual images of a self occupying space without shrinking to social expectation.

Yet the trip does not cure her.

Freedom from the environment gives room to speak; it does not solve the conflict.

## 12.3 Budokan

Budokan transforms an abstract dream into architecture.

The building is emotionally powerful precisely because it is silent and empty when Sakuta and Uzuki arrive.

There is no cheering audience to romanticize it.

Only scale.

The dream becomes measurable.

## 12.4 The car

Mai's car is a small adult-life technology with large thematic significance.

It creates ordinary couple privacy inside a world where public visibility restricts ordinary movement.

In earlier volumes, escape from gaze often required supernatural or extreme conditions.

Here it requires transportation planning.

## 12.5 Rain, power loss, and sunlight

The Hakkeijima climax uses almost maximal idol-drama staging:

- worsening rain;
- electrical failure;
- a cappella continuation;
- dispersing casual audience;
- Uzuki entering from the crowd;
- fans making a path;
- members lifting her onto the stage;
- returning electricity;
- sunlight breaking through cloud.

The formal symbolism is obvious and effective.

But the method must separate symbolism from mechanics.

There is no V10 evidence that Uzuki's return caused the power or weather changes.

Treat them as dramaturgical apotheosis unless later primary material explicitly says otherwise.

# 14. Ethics and philosophy

## 14.1 There is no socially uncontaminated self

Sakuta's answer to Uzuki rejects one of the most seductive versions of authenticity:

> the belief that a “real self” is whatever existed before other people affected it.

That model cannot survive human relationship.

Love changes preferences.

Knowledge changes judgments.

Grief changes priorities.

Recognition changes identity.

The ethical task is therefore not purification.

It is **authorship under influence**.

## 14.2 Understanding another viewpoint does not require obeying it

Uzuki's crisis begins when comprehension becomes internal jurisdiction.

She can understand why somebody laughs at an unlikely dream.

That does not make the laugh morally authoritative.

This is one of the book's most useful philosophical distinctions:

> **Cognitive empathy is not endorsement.**

The fact that a judgment is intelligible from inside does not mean the judgment deserves to govern action.

## 14.3 Anonymous majorities versus concrete others

The abstract `みんな` has low ethical accountability.

It can mock without relationship.

It can disappear when the rain gets heavy.

It can change fashions and judgments without remembering who was hurt.

Concrete others bear cost with you.

Nodoka and the others train, fail, cover Uzuki's missing parts, sing without electricity, and remain when the dream is embarrassing.

Volume 10's ethics therefore privilege:

> **relationship-bearing voices over disembodied social averages.**

This does not mean fans or strangers never matter. It means their judgments do not automatically possess equal jurisdiction over identity.

## 14.4 Dreams after realism

Uzuki's earlier certainty was partly protected by not perceiving ridicule.

The final dream is stronger because it survives realism.

She now knows Budokan is unlikely.

Nodoka knows too.

The others know too.

They choose the project anyway.

This is not optimism through ignorance.

It is commitment under uncertainty.

## 14.5 Distributed hope

The final Budokan promise corrects the group's emotional architecture.

Uzuki had been functioning as the generator of certainty.

That was unsustainable.

The new formula is reciprocal:

> I will take you.

> You take me.

This is the aspiration equivalent of Volume 7's distributed care.

## 14.6 The right to leave an institution

Sakuta's `卒業、おめでとう` supports a person-centered view of education.

Institutions are means with legitimate purposes, not sacred endpoints whose completion defines human worth.

But this principle requires caution.

The story can support Uzuki's specific withdrawal because she:

- has a stable professional career;
- entered with a self-defined investigative motive;
- identifies a different chosen path;
- and is not shown leaving because of immediate academic incapacity.

It should not be generalized into “university dropout is liberation.”

# 15. Counterreadings, limits, and hypothesis stress-test

## 15.1 “Uzuki was better before she could read the air.”

**Counterevidence:** She explicitly values new capacities, including understanding jokes, members, sarcasm, and interpersonal nuance.

**Judgment:** Rejected. The resolution is selective permeability, not return to innocence.

## 15.2 “Uzuki was better after she could read the air.”

**Counterevidence:** Total access produces self-dissolution, concealment, shame, paralysis, and loss of voice.

**Judgment:** Rejected. Social competence without boundaries becomes domination by the social field.

## 15.3 “The phenomenon is definitely a university-wide quantum-entanglement network.”

**Counterevidence:** Rio explicitly presents the physics language as analogy; the final Santa figure claims personal causal agency.

**Judgment:** UA. Strong thematic/social model, insufficient causal proof.

## 15.4 “Kirishima Toko definitely caused the phenomenon.”

**Counterevidence:** Only her own unverified claim establishes this.

**Judgment:** CB/UA. Important but not independently confirmed.

## 15.5 “Uzuki's aphonia is definitely Adolescence Syndrome.”

**Counterevidence:** Mai provides ordinary stress-related precedent; the text treats the symptom medically.

**Judgment:** Not established. Best current label: stress-linked physical symptom within the larger crisis.

## 15.6 “The returning sunlight and electricity prove supernatural intervention.”

**Counterevidence:** No causal statement links the events to Uzuki.

**Judgment:** Treat as formal staging, not mechanics.

## 15.7 “Sweet Bullet deserves Budokan because they are talented.”

**Counterevidence:** The novel explicitly distinguishes performance quality from market reach.

**Judgment:** Rejected. Artistic merit and audience scale are not equivalent.

## 15.8 “Nodoka resents Uzuki's solo success.”

**Counterevidence:** Nodoka explicitly wants members' effort rewarded and recognizes Uzuki's exceptional ability to make people smile.

**Judgment:** Too simple. Her conflict is collective dream versus individual opportunity, mixed with fear of losing a member.

## 15.9 “Uzuki's withdrawal from university is straightforward empowerment.”

**Counterevidence:** It is sudden and removes future educational optionality; the novel celebrates it more than it examines long-term tradeoffs.

**Judgment:** Thematically coherent, practically contestable. Record as VJ tension.

## 15.10 “University conformity is uniquely pathological.”

**Counterevidence:** Similar norm production existed in high school; university merely changes how it is generated.

**Judgment:** Rejected. University freedom reveals rather than invents the mechanism.

## 15.11 “Miori is outside the social system because she has no smartphone.”

**Counterevidence:** She uses a computer, searches fashion trends, copies online outfits, and worries about judgment.

**Judgment:** Rejected. She controls one channel of access but remains socially embedded.

## 15.12 “Sakuta has finally learned perfect noninterference.”

**Counterevidence:** He still provokes, maneuvers, eavesdrops with Mai, and inserts himself into Uzuki's crisis.

**Judgment:** Rejected. His restraint has improved, not become absolute.

## 15.13 “The final Uzuki is exactly the old Uzuki again.”

**Counterevidence:** Narration explicitly denies simple restoration; acquired knowledge remains.

**Judgment:** Strongly rejected.

## 15.14 “The crowd making a path means society finally recognizes Uzuki authentically.”

**Counterevidence:** Much of the casual audience has already left; the remaining crowd is disproportionately fans and interested listeners.

**Judgment:** The scene represents recognition by a concrete audience, not universal social reconciliation.

## 15.15 “The public is the villain.”

# 20. Retrospective-correction status

Volume 10 does not overturn the main theses of Volumes 1–9.

It substantially **extends** them.

## RC-V10-01 — the collective-network model becomes incomplete

During the volume, Sakuta and Rio develop a plausible working model in which ordinary university students form an unconscious social network and Uzuki is incorporated into it.

The final Santa figure says:

> she made Uzuki able to read the air.

Because this is unverified self-report, the correct correction is not:

> “Rio was wrong.”

It is:

> **Causal confidence must be lowered. The social-network model remains descriptively powerful but is no longer sufficient as causal closure.**

## RC-V10-02 — Uzuki's V8 strength is refined, not negated

V8 could make Uzuki's inability to read air look almost purely liberating after she finds a suitable environment.

V10 corrects that simplification.

Not reading air protected autonomy but also limited mutual comprehension.

Reading air provides real interpersonal goods.

The mature position is therefore not pro- or anti-air-reading.

It is about **bounded permeability**.

## RC-V10-03 — “ordinary life” after the high-school crisis is not the end of Syndrome

The long quiet period and university transition might suggest Sakuta had effectively graduated from the phenomenon-prone stage.

Chapter 1 explicitly raises that hope.

The title and ending reject it.

The series' next movement treats adolescence as persisting into nominal adulthood.

# 21. Volume thesis and questions carried forward

Volume 10 can be condensed into five propositions:

> **Knowing what other people think is not the same as belonging to them.**

> **A self changed by relationship is still a self; authenticity does not require immunity from influence.**

> **The anonymous social majority should not receive the same moral jurisdiction as people who share risk, labor, and responsibility with you.**

> **A dream becomes stronger when it survives realism and becomes reciprocally carried rather than dependent on one person's certainty.**

> **The end of high school does not end adolescence if selfhood is still being negotiated under social gaze.**

The most elegant lexical transformation belongs to `みんな`.

At first, Uzuki enters university to understand “everyone.”

Then “everyone” occupies her head.

Then the anonymous crowd teaches her ridicule.

Then many members of the public leave when the rain becomes inconvenient.

Finally, Uzuki stands before a **concrete** everyone—named members and actual fans—and says, in effect:

> I will carry you; you carry me.

That is the book's answer to `Social World`.

Not isolation.

Not merger.

**Reciprocal relation with boundaries.**

And then the novel breaks its own provisional explanation open.

A woman nobody else seems to see stands next to Sakuta and complains that her gift to Uzuki has been wasted.

She calls herself Kirishima Toko.

The university movement therefore ends its first volume with the most destabilizing possibility yet:

> **perhaps the social world is not merely generating phenomena. Perhaps someone has begun deliberately editing the conditions under which people experience it.**

At the Volume-10 boundary, that is only a possibility.

It is now impossible to ignore.

## Final synthesis

Volume 10's most important achievement is that it refuses both easy answers to social influence.

The first easy answer would be:

> **Be yourself and ignore what everyone thinks.**

Uzuki already lived close to that condition.

It protected her.

It also left her unable to understand people she deeply loved.

The second easy answer would be:

> **Maturity means learning to read the room.**

Uzuki tries that too.

It makes her smoother, more empathic, less awkward, and more conventionally successful.

Then it fills her head with so many voices that she cannot find her own.

The book's real answer is harder:

> **Other people are allowed to change you. They are not automatically allowed to govern you.**

Uzuki does not return to ignorance.

She learns what ridicule is.

She learns what envy sounds like.

She learns that she is capable of contempt.

She learns the numbers standing between Sweet Bullet and Budokan.

She learns that the public may want *her* more than it wants the group.

And then she chooses again.

Not because the dream is guaranteed.

Not because nobody is laughing.

Not because the cynical voice inside her has been disproven.

She chooses it because Nodoka, Yae, Ranko, Hotaru, and the remaining fans are still singing in the rain.

That is the “air” worth reading.

The self survives the social world not by becoming impermeable.

It survives by learning the difference between **hearing a voice** and **granting it authority**.
