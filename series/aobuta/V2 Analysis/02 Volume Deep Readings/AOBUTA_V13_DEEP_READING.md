# 『青春ブタ野郎はサンタクロースの夢を見ない』
## Volume 13 Deep Reading
### To become “someone”: recognition, borrowed identities, and the danger of a public self with no owner

**Spoiler boundary:** Volumes 1–13 plus the already analyzed story-position bonuses *Animal Land* and *Spring Days*. Volumes 14–15 are excluded.

# 1. Central thesis and volume role

Volume 13's central proposition is:

> **A self can be socially made without being fake, but recognition becomes destructive when the only self one can inhabit is a role that requires the previous person to disappear.**

A sharper formulation is:

> **The problem is not that Iwamizawa Nene borrowed an identity from other people. Everyone in the series is partly made through other people. The problem is that “Kirishima Toko” becomes an infinitely reproducible public role whose successful performance erases Nene's autobiographical continuity, relationships, name, and eventually her own capacity to recognize herself.**

This distinction matters because the novel explicitly refuses a naïve doctrine of authenticity. Ikumi remembers being told:

> `郁実ちゃんはいい子だね`

and recognizes that repeated social recognition helped constitute the “good girl” she became. Sakuta similarly understands part of his own moral vocabulary as received from Shoko. A socially shaped self is therefore not automatically a counterfeit self.

Nene herself exposes the weakness of easy authenticity language when Takumi tells her that he loves “Nene as Nene”:

> `私のままの私ってなに!?`

and then:

> `そんな立派な自分らしさがわかってたら苦労しない！`

The novel does not answer by revealing a pristine “real Nene” buried underneath society. It moves toward a more defensible idea:

> **A viable self is one whose history, desires, failures, relationships, and future choices can be owned without requiring the person to replace that history with somebody else's ready-made role.**

`霧島透子` is uniquely dangerous because it is already an anonymous public sign. The singer has a voice, songs, a stable Christmas aesthetic, enormous public recognition, and no securely public body. The persona therefore has high social value but unusually low biographical specificity. It can be copied, projected onto, and occupied.

Nene is not the only occupant. By the climax Sakuta sees roughly a hundred Santa-clad young people who all believe they are Kirishima Toko. The epilogue confirms that every Santa questioned by police said essentially the same thing, and that both men and women were among them.

The syndrome has expanded from:

> one person becoming socially invisible

into:

> **many concrete persons disappearing into one culturally legible identity.**

That creates the volume's deepest paradox:

> **“Kirishima Toko” becomes more socially real as the individual people claiming to be Toko become less socially real.**

The role is visible. The persons are not.

The danger is literal. At Mai's police event, invisible people still possess bodies. Ordinary spectators interpret the spaces occupied by Santas as empty. They move forward. Police see no dangerous crowd density. The physical crowd becomes more compressed than the recognized crowd. The barrier collapses. A speaker falls toward Mai.

This is one of AoButa's strongest integrations of literal mechanics and social meaning:

> **A person whom a social system cannot perceive still occupies space. Misrecognition does not abolish material consequence.**

Volume 13 is also about epistemic ownership of identity. Millions of young people have dreamed a scene in which Mai announces:

> `実は、私が霧島透子なんです`

Current-world Mai answers publicly:

> `私は霧島透子さんではありません`

Yet rumor survives.

The ethical problem is not reducible to “which statement is factually correct?” If the dreams show another possibility state, another Mai may make a different declaration without transferring that identity to the Mai standing here now.

This extends the branch-world personhood principle developed in Volumes 9 and 11:

> **Possible selves are not interchangeable, and possible-world testimony does not own the present person.**

A third proposition concerns Sakuta's care. Chapter 2 explicitly makes him Santa's helper—the reindeer. He carries the gift, transports information, preserves an archive, and connects Nene to Takumi. The decisive recognition is not his alone. Takumi remembers. Takumi calls Nene by name. Nene speaks her own humiliation. Nene begins recognizing herself.

The mature alternative to “save everybody yourself” increasingly becomes:

> **connect the person to the relationship, record, or institution that can help without making oneself the only solution.**

Even Sakuta's later physical rescue of Mai is surrounded by a distributed care network. Mai catches him when he falls. Rio accompanies him in the ambulance. Kunimi drives professionally. Ikumi stays nearby. Hanakaede comes to the hospital. Tomoe and Sara visit on a work break. Nodoka and Uzuki call. Mai arrives after fulfilling her police obligations.

The rescuer can now be rescued.

Finally, the afterword explicitly says:

> `いよいよ青ブタも最終章です。`

Volume 13 is therefore not merely another title-character case. It opens the final chapter. Its ending makes that architecture explicit: March 31, the false Toko candidates largely recovered, the public still wanting a Toko, rumor returning to Mai, and the shared dream's key date one day away:

> `明日、多くの若者がその日の夢を見たと言う、四月一日がやってくる。`

The volume solves a mass false identity. It does not solve the identity around which those false identities formed.

## Narrated chronology

### Dream / possible state

Before the waking December 25 opening, Sakuta experiences an unusually concrete April 1 scene at a Yokohama music festival. Mai appears as a secret vocalist and publicly declares herself Kirishima Toko. Dream-Sakuta owns a smartphone, calls Ikumi using a number waking Sakuta has never consciously learned, says `たぶん、時間がない`, then appears to transfer responsibility with `あとは任せたぞ`.

### December 25

Sakuta wakes in Hakone. Mai immediately receives an offer for the same April 1 music festival. After returning to Fujisawa, `#夢見る` traffic has exploded into millions of posts. Hanakaede reports dreaming that she became the other Kaede continuity again, then decides she wants to attempt university entrance. Sakuta calls the exact number remembered from the dream; Ikumi answers and reports the same call from her side.

### December 28

At the tutoring school, multiple students describe overlapping future-like dreams. Kento and Juri share Enoshima relationship material. Other dreams concern exams and future outcomes. Dream information is already altering present behavior.

### January 3

At the repaired Azusagawa family home, New Year has become ordinary family life. Mai remains conspicuous as a younger person who did not dream. Sakuta confirms the Sweet Bullet members share April 1 dream material and reveals that he is saving for driving school so he can share driving with Mai.

### January 5

Sara begins work at the family restaurant. She shows Sakuta thousands of `#夢見る` posts describing the same April 1 festival from different audience locations. Tomoe privately reveals a much longer dream spanning Christmas Eve through April 9. In her experienced state Mai is critically injured on February 4 while serving as one-day police chief. That evening Mai confirms she has received exactly that job.

### January 6–9

Media labels the phenomenon `集団未来視`. Toko appears to Sakuta and says she herself did not dream. Miori reveals that she can independently see the Santa woman. Miori and Sakuta investigate Iwamizawa Nene's old campus archive. Miori first proposes a status-loss model, then makes the more important logical observation that an actually famous original Kirishima Toko should not need to disappear merely because Mai entered the university. Rio cautions that the status theory is speculative. Ikumi's model—that the dreams may show another possibility world rather than a fixed future—becomes a serious alternative. On January 9 Mai publicly says `私は霧島透子さんではありません`, but social-media suspicion survives.

### January 16–30

Toko continues ordinary university attendance despite being unseen by most people. Nodoka discusses what it means for women accustomed to being the most visible person in a room to encounter Mai's incomparable celebrity, but ultimately says the more important thing: `咲太はお姉ちゃんの味方でいてよ`.

On January 30 Nene/Toko makes Sakuta help Santa. They buy an orange birthday scarf. Her relationship history reveals that Takumi was her long-distance boyfriend. At Haneda, Takumi cannot perceive her and cannot remember the relationship. Nene leaves the gift behind.

### February 1–3

Sakuta begins driving school. Miori discovers that Nene's viral Toko cover is only one among more than one hundred similar videos by young people whose voices sound uncannily Toko-like. Some associated media are observer-dependent. Takumi calls from Hokkaido after the funeral of a former schoolmate who had not dreamed, generating the unverified rumor that non-dreamers may not exist in the observed future.

Sakuta visits Nene's apartment on February 3. She is throwing away old clothing and her Miss Campus trophy. When he asks about the engraved name `岩見沢寧々`, she says `それは誰？` and insists `わたしは霧島透子よ`. Her apartment is saturated with Christmas imagery matching Toko videos. Sara identifies the recurring Toko-video props; Rio concludes Nene was not disappearing because she secretly was Toko—she was collecting the props `霧島透子になるため`. Sakuta flies to Hokkaido with Ikumi.

### February 3–4

At New Chitose, Sakuta gives Takumi the trophy. He traces Nene's engraved name and remembers. At the airport onsen he explains Nene's pre-Toko biography: local Hokkaido visibility, modeling, stalled Tokyo career, and an original ambition to become a television announcer. He realizes again that he loves her.

On February 4 they return. Takumi confronts Nene, who can be seen by him after his memory returns but still does not recognize him or herself. His repeated biographical testimony and renewed confession eventually crack the flat Toko persona. Nene calls herself `霧島透子のまがいもの`, asks `私のままの私ってなに!?`, and admits `何者かであるって思いたいの！`. She becomes visible to Ikumi, then warns that she is not the only Toko.

At Mai's event Sakuta sees roughly one hundred Santa-clad people whom others cannot perceive. Their invisible bodies cause crowd-density misperception. The barrier fails; a speaker falls toward Mai; Sakuta catches it and is injured. While bleeding he tells both ordinary spectators and Santas `大丈夫です`. Broader visibility returns.

### March 31

Sakuta and Miori pass their driver's-license examinations. Police follow-up confirms that the Santas all believed themselves to be Kirishima Toko and did not coordinate an attack. Most have regained their ordinary identities. With false Toko candidates gone, rumor attaches to Mai again. April 1 arrives tomorrow.

# 3. Causal synopsis: what changes and why

Volume 13 is best modeled as four interacting causal systems.

## 3.1 Dream information alters the present

The dreams are not passive observations. Sakuta learns a valid phone number. Tomoe warns of a specific event and equipment accident. Millions anticipate Mai's April 1 declaration. At least one person reacts destructively to an unwanted dream of his own future. Even if the dreams are observations of possibility rather than destiny, **information about possibility changes present choices**.

This creates a feedback problem:

> the more people see a possible future, the more their present behavior may make that possible future likely—or prevent it.

## 3.2 Public recognition supplies Nene with a replacement self

Nene's old self depended heavily on visible success: local modeling, Miss Contest recognition, university prominence, and expected adult achievement. When that trajectory destabilizes, a viral Toko cover supplies a new socially legible answer. Strangers tell her she sounds like the already-famous anonymous singer. She begins reproducing the role through voice, clothing, Christmas props, and self-description.

The answer feels like:

> **I am somebody again.**

## 3.3 Replacement becomes erasure

The new identity does not integrate with Nene. It displaces her. Takumi forgets. Nene no longer recognizes her own name. She discards the trophy. Most people cannot perceive her. The public persona survives while the biography disappears.

The literal mechanism turns an ordinary adult fear into social ontology:

> **If the world recognizes only the role and nobody recognizes the person who used to occupy the body, where does that person's history live?**

## 3.4 Relational and material recognition reopen the biography

Sakuta preserves the trophy. Takumi touches the engraved name and remembers. He can then see Nene. He tells her shared memories without demanding she instantly remember him. His confession creates pressure not toward prestige but toward particularity.

Nene's decisive move is her own: she speaks in Nene-specific first person. She names failure, overconfidence, humiliation, and the desire to be somebody. The recovery is therefore not simply “boyfriend love cures her.” It is a chain:

> **archive → remembered relation → naming → autobiographical speech → renewed self-recognition → broader social recognition.**

The case then expands beyond Nene: many other people have occupied the same role.

# 4. Adolescence Syndrome / phenomenon dossier

## 4.1 `#夢見る`

Observable facts:

- millions of younger people report unusually vivid dreams;
- multiple people can access apparently the same event from different viewpoints;
- some dream information is externally verifiable and novel to the dreamer;
- the same venues, dates, and conversations recur;
- some outcomes later begin matching W0;
- outcomes can also diverge;
- Tomoe's dream differs radically in duration.

The evidence is too heterogeneous for a single “prophetic dream” rule.

## 4.2 Possibility-world model

Ikumi proposes that the dreams may show `もうひとつの可能性の世界`. This fits prior adjacent-world evidence and explains several anomalies, especially dream-Sakuta's smartphone and the multi-viewpoint structure.

The model remains incomplete. It does not yet explain why millions gain access at once, why some people do not dream, or whether all dreamers are observing the same state.

Current status:

> **SI/B for at least part of the dream corpus; not universal law.**

## 4.3 Tomoe is a special case

Tomoe experiences months of continuous life from Christmas Eve through April 9. Sakuta explicitly compares the event to her earlier `ラプラスのプチデビル` future simulation. Her anxiety about graduation and university friendship supplies a plausible recurring social substrate.

Do not collapse her experience into the ordinary mass-dream category.

## 4.4 The future is mutable

Tomoe's dream correctly supplies the February 4 job and dangerous equipment context. It fails to become the same outcome. Mai is not left unconscious; Sakuta is injured instead.

Best V13 conclusion:

> **Dreams can carry real information about accessible future/possible states without becoming fate.**

## 4.5 No-dream/death rumor

A deceased Hokkaido youth had not dreamed. Peers infer that people absent from the future cannot dream it.

That is only **CB/UA**.

Mai and Nene both report no dream and remain alive within V13. The rule is therefore at minimum incomplete.

## 4.6 Nene/Toko substitution

The narrowest model consistent with the observed case is:

> **Nene increasingly replaces her socially and autobiographically recognized identity with the public persona “Kirishima Toko.” As Nene-specific recognition collapses, both memory and perception of Nene fail.**

What remains unexplained:

- why Sakuta can see her;
- why Miori can see her;
- why Takumi forgets;
- why some digital records become observer-dependent;
- the exact threshold for restored visibility.

## 4.7 Nene is not original Toko

The volume decisively revises the working identification. Nene calls herself a `まがいもの`; Rio states `岩見沢寧々は、霧島透子じゃない`; Nene says there are other Tokos. The mass Santa population confirms a broader substitution phenomenon.

The originating singer remains unresolved.

## 4.8 Invisible bodies and crowd mechanics

The February 4 event demonstrates that recognition failure changes physical safety:

- unseen Santas occupy real space;
- ordinary attendees perceive empty gaps;
- crowd compression increases;
- police underestimate density;
- barriers fail;
- a speaker falls.

The phenomenon therefore has infrastructural consequences, not only emotional ones.

## 4.9 Mass resolution

After Sakuta is hit, he directly addresses the Santas while visibly bleeding. Police later report that many describe the shock of that encounter as what snapped them out of the Toko state.

The correct causal statement is narrow:

> **Recovery correlates with severe shock plus direct interpersonal acknowledgment.**

Do not state that `大丈夫です` alone is a universal cure.


# 5. Iwamizawa Nene: becoming someone by becoming replaceable

## 5.1 The wound is larger than jealousy of Mai

Miori's first social theory is intuitively powerful. Nene had been a model, Miss Campus winner, and highly visible university student. Mai's return to campus could instantly turn a person accustomed to being the most conspicuous woman in the room into an ordinary second-order presence.

But the method requires restraint.

Miori is theorizing from:

- Nene's old SNS;
- the Miss Contest archive;
- campus status;
- the timing of the archive's cessation.

Rio immediately points out that this starting premise remains speculation.

Nene's later confession confirms some adjacent facts:

- she came to Tokyo with great confidence;
- her work did not expand as expected;
- she experienced herself as a failure;
- she desperately wanted to remain `何者か`.

She does **not** explicitly say:

> “Mai stole my identity.”

Mai's presence therefore remains best classified as a plausible social destabilizer rather than the single established cause.

## 5.2 Nene had a future before Toko

Takumi supplies one of the most important biographical corrections in the book:

> Nene wanted to become a television announcer.

Her Miss Contest success mattered partly because it could serve as a route toward that adult future.

This prevents an easy backward rewrite in which Nene had “always really been a singer.” The Toko role is a later substitute. It did not simply reveal her original professional self.

## 5.3 Achievement had become a theory of personhood

Nene's disappointment is not merely occupational.

Her language shows a chain:

> achievement → distinction → public value → proof of being somebody.

When the first term fails, all the downstream terms become unstable.

She had expected Tokyo to confirm:

> **I am the kind of person who becomes something.**

Instead it confronts her with:

> perhaps I am ordinary; perhaps I have failed; perhaps nothing distinguishes me.

That is why viral comparison with Toko has such force.

It does not merely offer popularity.

It offers an answer to:

> Who am I now?

## 5.4 Viral recognition becomes identity infrastructure

The Toko cover succeeds socially.

Viewers compare her to the anonymous singer.

The anonymous singer already possesses enormous cultural value.

Nene begins to reproduce the role.

This is not only cosplay.

By February her room contains a coherent Toko environment:

- tree;
- Santa figures;
- reindeer;
- snow globes;
- Christmas cards;
- Toko-video-like props.

The private apartment has become:

> **a stage set capable of supporting the sentence “I am Kirishima Toko” even when nobody else is present.**

Sara's observation is crucial because it makes the props comparative evidence rather than merely eccentric decoration.

## 5.5 Why an anonymous persona is unusually dangerous

“Kirishima Toko” has enormous public specificity at the level of style and almost none at the level of biography.

People know:

- the voice;
- the songs;
- the Christmas imagery;
- the mysterious persona.

They do not possess a stable public:

- face;
- family;
- school history;
- romantic history;
- ordinary body.

That means many people can plausibly imagine:

> maybe I am the person behind this.

The persona is culturally dense and biographically empty.

Nene can therefore adopt it more easily than she could become:

> Sakurajima Mai,

because Mai's identity is attached to an overwhelmingly particular biography and body.

This is why the mass Toko phenomenon is not arbitrary. The anonymous singer functions almost like a role with an open casting call.

That is a thematic interpretation, not a literal rule of Syndrome mechanics.

## 5.6 The trophy: public-status object becomes autobiographical archive

The Miss Contest trophy originally signifies:

> success.

Later it signifies:

> Nene.

The difference is important.

When Sakuta finds it in the trash, the object's practical prestige value no longer matters. The engraving matters:

> `岩見沢寧々`

The trophy says that a particular person once desired, entered, won, and celebrated something.

Nene's attempt to throw it away therefore participates in autobiographical deletion.

Sakuta refuses.

Narratively, this is vindicated because the trophy helps Takumi remember.

Ethically, the act remains paternalistic.

The object belongs to Nene.

She chose to discard it.

Sakuta decides that the choice does not represent a self he is willing to treat as fully authoritative.

V2 must keep both judgments:

> **effective preservation**  
> and  
> **unconsented override.**

The pattern now has a strong series history: Kaede's notebook, photographs, marriage form, mother diary, middle-school yearbook, counterpart notes, and now Nene's trophy. AoButa repeatedly gives memory a material substrate when minds become unstable.

## 5.7 `それは誰？`: the deeper horror is self-nonrecognition

The most frightening line in Nene's apartment is not:

> “I refuse to be Nene.”

It is:

> `それは誰？`

She reacts to her own name as though Sakuta has introduced a stranger.

The event has moved beyond:

- shame;
- denial;
- deliberate reinvention.

The old autobiographical continuity is no longer accessible as “me.”

That makes the Toko case a much stronger identity crisis than ordinary role performance.

## 5.8 Toko's cool `わたし` and Nene's returning `私`

The Japanese text gives this identity split a subtle orthographic register.

The Santa/Toko persona commonly uses the hiragana first person:

> `わたし`

When Nene's emotional autobiography returns in the parking structure, the prose repeatedly presents:

> `私`

The shift should not be treated as a perfect binary code. Japanese authors vary orthography for many reasons.

But here the timing is meaningful.

Toko's voice is:

- light;
- composed;
- ironic;
- teasing;
- almost frictionless.

Nene's returning first person is:

- furious;
- embarrassed;
- repetitive;
- self-accusing;
- syntactically explosive.

The page visually thickens the “I” as personal history returns.

## 5.9 Takumi remembering does not instantly restore Nene

At New Chitose, the trophy works.

Takumi remembers.

When he later faces Nene, he can see her.

But Nene keeps answering his shared memories with:

> `わからない`

This is important.

Another person's recognition can preserve a route to you.

It cannot automatically restore your own autobiographical access.

The story needs two movements:

1. **someone remembers Nene;**
2. **Nene begins speaking as Nene.**

## 5.10 `わからない` → `わかってる`

The parking-structure scene is built around a lexical transformation.

Takumi:

> Do you remember this?

Nene:

> `わからない`

Again.

Again.

Again.

Then she finally explodes:

> I came to Tokyo confident.

> Work did not grow.

> I became a model in name only.

> I thought I could become something.

> I became a counterfeit Toko.

Sakuta answers:

> `ちゃんとわかってるじゃないですか`

The object of “knowing” has changed.

Earlier:

> Do you know the facts Takumi is giving you?

Now:

> Do you know the ashamed, ambitious, disappointed person speaking them?

Sakuta's answer is:

> yes—at least enough to begin.

## 5.11 `私のままの私ってなに!?` destroys the easy authenticity cliché

Takumi says he loves:

> Nene as Nene.

Nene asks:

> what does “me as me” even mean?

She is right to ask.

She has been:

- local model;
- high-school girlfriend;
- Tokyo aspirant;
- Miss Contest winner;
- struggling professional;
- announcer hopeful;
- viral singer;
- Santa;
- Toko.

No scene reveals one of those as the metaphysical original and all others as masks.

The strongest reading is therefore:

> **authenticity in Volume 13 is not purity from social influence. It is continuity that can include its own revisions without pretending the earlier person never existed.**

## 5.12 `何者か`

Nene's central confession is:

> `何者かであるって思いたいの！`

`何者か` is stronger than a neutral “somebody.” It means a person with recognizable identity, consequence, or standing.

She wants to believe:

> her life amounts to something.

This makes her case much more ordinary than the magic initially suggests.

University and early adulthood ask everyone in this arc:

- What will you do?
- Who will you become?
- Who will choose you?
- What value will you have when school identity ends?

Nene merely possesses a social environment capable of quantifying the anxiety in:

- followers;
- likes;
- views;
- public rank.

## 5.13 The novel does not mock her desire to be special

Miori is capable of sharp schadenfreude analysis.

Nene herself calls her failure pathetic.

Yet the resolution does not say:

> learn to accept being worthless and ordinary.

Sakuta says:

> if you want to become somebody, become an announcer or something else later.

The future is not closed.

The important correction is:

> **do not make a prefabricated famous persona the condition of being allowed to exist.**

Ambition survives.

## 5.14 Sakuta's `愛されてる` argument: emotionally effective, philosophically bounded

Sakuta tells Nene she cannot claim to have nothing because:

> she has a person she values who values her.

Then:

> `愛されてるんですから`

This is effective in the scene because Nene's self-condemnation has become totalizing. She is mentally deleting all counterevidence.

The analysis should resist a weaker extrapolation:

> a woman's worth is proven because a man loves her.

The text itself leaves the future broader:

- announcer;
- other work;
- her own choices;
- relationship;
- ordinary life.

Takumi is evidence that her life contains attachment and history, not the metaphysical source of her value.

## 5.15 `今は拓海で我慢する`

Nene's teasing line after the breakdown is a much better recovery sign than a solemn “I finally know who I am.”

> `今は拓海で我慢する`

She is:

- irritated;
- affectionate;
- funny;
- slightly arrogant;
- specific.

A person has returned where a smooth persona had been.

Recovery therefore looks less like purification than renewed friction.

## 5.16 Nene does not recover the old self unchanged

The resolved woman is not necessarily:

> the exact Nene who first came from Hokkaido.

She now owns:

- failed modeling;
- Toko substitution;
- invisibility;
- Takumi forgetting;
- her own forgetting;
- humiliation.

Recovery means those events become part of one life.

It does not mean they vanish.

# 6. Sakuta: reindeer, ally, protector, and ordinary adult

## 6.1 Dream knowledge gives information, not sovereignty

Sakuta knows things other people do not:

- a phone number;
- an April 1 declaration;
- a warning that Mai is in danger;
- Tomoe's February 4 outcome.

His best development is that he increasingly treats this information as:

> evidence requiring investigation

rather than:

> unilateral authority over other people's lives.

That development is incomplete, but visible.

## 6.2 `トナカイの仕事` reframes intervention

Nene tells him he will help Santa.

The reindeer:

- carries;
- transports;
- connects;
- does not own the gift.

This is an unusually apt metaphor for Sakuta's healthier role.

He becomes useful by facilitating:

> Nene → Takumi

rather than trying to become:

> the person Nene must need most.

That is almost a direct correction to Ikumi's Volume-11 helper problem.

## 6.3 He still crosses boundaries

The chapter title should not romanticize everything Sakuta does.

He:

- forces the Takumi issue;
- uses Nene's phone to arrange an encounter;
- reveals private relationship history;
- refuses to discard her trophy;
- brings that trophy to Hokkaido;
- helps create a confrontation Nene did not request.

The result is good.

The process remains partly paternalistic.

The current urgency—especially the possibility that Mai is in danger—makes the choices understandable, not automatically consent-neutral.

## 6.4 He knows when his own words are insufficient

A major improvement is Sakuta's explicit recognition that:

> his speech cannot solve Nene.

He needs:

- Miori's contradiction;
- Rio's modeling;
- Sara's visual observation;
- Ikumi's identity experience;
- Takumi's relational memory.

This is mature distributed problem-solving.

## 6.5 `味方`: person-specific allegiance replaces abstract heroism

Nodoka tells Sakuta:

> `咲太はお姉ちゃんの味方でいてよ`

This creates a valuable lexical echo with Volume 11's:

> `正義の味方`

Ikumi wanted to be an ally of justice.

Nodoka asks Sakuta to be Mai's ally.

The second formulation is ethically narrower and therefore safer.

It creates:

- a named person;
- a relationship;
- mutual standing;
- a person capable of telling the ally when he is wrong.

Abstract justice can expand responsibility without limit. “Mai's ally” is bounded.

## 6.6 The protector line is immediately made reciprocal

Sakuta's fear produces:

> `麻衣さんのことは僕が守るから`

On its own, the line could reproduce the old unilateral rescuer pattern.

Mai answers:

> `じゃあ、咲太のことは私が守ってあげる`

The verb `守る` is returned to him.

The relationship refuses a fixed protector/protected hierarchy.

## 6.7 Catching the speaker differs from planned sacrificial substitution

At the police event, Sakuta sees an immediate falling object headed toward Mai. There is no time for deliberative consent. He intervenes.

He is injured.

But he does not deliberately choose:

> my death in exchange for hers.

That distinction matters after Volumes 6–7.

AoButa can criticize self-annihilating rescue ethics without requiring passivity during an emergency.

## 6.8 The organ-donation speech creates a deliberate-looking formal echo

Immediately before the crowd failure, Mai's police-chief speech discusses:

- traffic safety;
- accident prevention;
- the possibility of becoming a donor for somebody in need if an accident nevertheless occurs.

The sequence strongly recalls the earlier arc in which:

- fatal accidents;
- donor organs;
- Sakuta;
- Mai;
- Shoko

were linked across possible futures.

Then a new accident nearly strikes Mai.

Sakuta intervenes.

Nobody dies.

The earlier death architecture returns at a smaller, survivable scale.

This is best read as a formal callback rather than evidence of a shared supernatural mechanism.

## 6.9 `大丈夫です`: care reflex and recognition act

After taking a blow to the head, Sakuta stands and reassures everybody else:

> `大丈夫です`

The behavior is absurd enough to reveal character.

Sakuta still defaults to:

> regulate the room before acknowledging your own injury.

Yet he says it separately to the Santas whom others cannot see.

That gives the phrase a second function:

> **I am addressing you because you are here.**

Whether that recognition is mechanically necessary for recovery remains unresolved.

## 6.10 The rescuer is no longer alone

The hospital sequence matters because it refuses the old protagonist isolation.

Care arrives from multiple directions, but nobody suspends all ordinary life indefinitely.

- Rio leaves for tutoring.
- Kunimi returns to emergency duty.
- Tomoe and Sara visit during a restaurant break.
- Hanakaede goes to reassure their parents.
- Ikumi leaves when no longer needed.
- Mai arrives after police obligations.

This is a functioning care network rather than a harem tableau.

The people care and continue living.

## 6.11 The driver's license is an anti-heroic milestone

The epilogue begins with Sakuta waiting for a test result.

He passes.

The original reason for entering driving school was partly:

> to share the driving burden with Mai.

That is a remarkable late-series contrast.

Early Sakuta care:

> bleed for the person.

Adult Sakuta care:

> **learn to drive so the person does not always have to.**

The ordinary skill is arguably a more sustainable expression of partnership than spectacular sacrifice.

# 7. Mai and the continuing central relationship

## 7.1 Dream-Mai does not own current Mai

The novel deliberately makes the conflict difficult.

Dream-Mai says:

> I am Toko.

Current Mai says:

> I am not.

If the dream is another possibility world, both can be true of different continuities.

The public nevertheless tends to collapse:

> possible Mai

into:

> present Mai.

That is precisely the identity mistake the branch-world arcs have taught the reader to resist.

## 7.2 Celebrity makes misrecognition scalable

Nene suffers from insufficient recognition.

Mai suffers from excess interpretive recognition.

Millions of strangers feel entitled to decide:

> what hidden identity she must have.

The contrast reveals that the real ideal is not maximum visibility.

It is:

> **recognition bounded closely enough to the person that the public story cannot simply overwrite first-person life.**

## 7.3 The coming-of-age denial is an act of self-jurisdiction

Mai responds clearly:

> `私は霧島透子さんではありません`

She does so with:

- humor;
- professionalism;
- managerial support;
- public composure.

This demonstrates part of what Nene lacks.

Mai has a highly developed public self that does not exhaust the private person.

She can perform publicity without needing publicity to tell her who she is.

## 7.4 Denial cannot fully reverse a viral narrative

Mainstream media largely accepts the correction.

Social media does not.

The volume therefore distinguishes:

> formal public statement  
> from  
> distributed belief.

Once a compelling identity story becomes culturally useful, evidence alone may not erase it.

## 7.5 Nodoka's limit is mature sisterhood

Nodoka admits she cannot understand what it feels like to carry Mai's level of public responsibility.

She does not convert that limit into resentment.

She asks Sakuta to remain:

> Mai's ally.

This is care through epistemic humility.

## 7.6 Mutual protection becomes embodied

Before the event:

> Sakuta will protect Mai.

Mai:

> will protect Sakuta.

At the event:

- Sakuta catches the speaker;
- Mai catches Sakuta's falling body.

The symmetry is almost diagrammatic.

Protection is not ownership.

It circulates.

## 7.7 Public duty is not discarded because danger is possible

Mai still performs the police event.

The text does not treat celebrity work as an expendable hobby that Sakuta may cancel whenever he is frightened by a possibility.

That preserves her agency and professional identity.

## 7.8 Handholding closes the main crisis

After hospital treatment, police questioning, and calls from the ensemble, Sakuta and Mai simply walk together and take each other's hand.

The gesture is private.

No audience is required.

In a volume dominated by public identity, that matters.

Their relationship is real without trending.

# 8. Ensemble and relationship-state changes

## 8.1 Miori

Miori is now analytically central without being explained.

She can see the Santa woman. She can access Nene-related records. She notices a contradiction in Sakuta's model. She also produces a harsh but useful social reading of status and schadenfreude.

Her strongest contribution is epistemic:

> she is willing to say that the current premise does not fit.

Why she can perceive Toko remains unresolved. Her unusual distance from smartphone culture is context, not proof of mechanism.

## 8.2 Rio

Rio's scientific role continues to mature.

She:

- rejects overconfidence in Miori's status theory;
- takes the possibility-world hypothesis seriously without calling it proven;
- incorporates Sara's observation;
- revises the Nene model when the evidence changes.

She also reflects on her dream of Kunimi and newly recognizes that being unable to reciprocate somebody's love can itself be painful. That is a quiet long-range correction to her Volume-3 perspective.

## 8.3 Ikumi

Ikumi contributes:

- logistics;
- travel;
- insight into socially authored selfhood.

Her `いい子` recollection matters because she now understands that an identity shaped by other people's expectations can still become sincerely one's own.

She remains uncomfortable receiving thanks, preserving the Volume-11 helper residue.

## 8.4 Tomoe

Tomoe's dream strongly reactivates her earlier simulation architecture. Her likely anxiety about university friendship shows that the old social vulnerability has not vanished; the life stage has changed.

She also gives Sakuta frightening information because she trusts him to use it seriously.

## 8.5 Sara

Sara's most important contribution is entirely ordinary observation:

> she notices the recurring Christmas props in Toko videos.

Her value to the investigation no longer depends on clairvoyance.

That is an excellent post-Volume-12 recovery marker.

She also begins restaurant work, extending the university arc's emphasis on mundane participation.

## 8.6 Hanakaede

Hanakaede dreams of becoming the other Kaede continuity again.

The fear persists.

That prevents V8 from being misread as permanent psychological closure.

But her waking choice is expansive:

> university.

Kotomi is part of the scaffold, and Sakuta recognizes a deeper desire to go farther into the world.

Most importantly, this future belongs to Hanakaede. It is not Kaede's unfinished wish controlling her.

## 8.7 Nodoka

Nodoka's role is no longer to compete with Mai's brilliance.

She can state:

> I do not know her experience.

Then identify the care structure that matters.

This is a mature sisterly position.

## 8.8 Kunimi

Kunimi's firefighter career intersects directly with Sakuta's injury.

His `二度目はなしにしてくれ` places an affectionate boundary on Sakuta's risk.

He represents professionalized care:

- trained;
- scheduled;
- team-based;
- handed off to medicine.

## 8.9 Takumi

Takumi's forgetting is real harm.

After memory returns, however, he does not demand that Nene remember him immediately. He explicitly accepts that she may not know him because he failed to know her.

His promise is:

> he will remember and wait.

His decision to step in front of her moving car is the major exception: emotionally understandable, physically reckless, and not a model of proportionate care.

# 9. Japanese voice, address, and translation-sensitive language

## 9.1 `何者か`

The Chapter 3 title `Someone` points directly toward Nene's `何者か`.

`何者かになる` is not merely becoming “a person.” It implies becoming somebody with recognizable consequence or identity.

The syndrome literalizes the fear embedded in the expression:

> fail to become somebody, and perhaps you become socially nobody.

## 9.2 `私のままの私`

Takumi's reassurance sounds natural until Nene exposes its circularity:

> `私のままの私ってなに!?`

English “be yourself” can flatten the force. The Japanese makes the conceptual problem audible: what defines the self that is supposed to remain itself?

## 9.3 `まがいもの`

`まがいもの` carries stronger contempt than neutral “copy.”

Nene calls herself:

> imitation;
> counterfeit;
> not the real thing.

Her self-condemnation still uses a hierarchy in which only one authentic Toko may possess value.

The recovery requires exiting that competition.

## 9.4 `わたし` / `私`

The Toko persona frequently speaks with hiragana `わたし`. Nene's emotional return repeatedly uses kanji `私`.

This is not a mechanical ontology marker, but the change contributes to voice:

> stylized persona → autobiographical assertion.

## 9.5 `わからない` / `わかってる`

Nene repeats `わからない` to Takumi's memories.

Sakuta later answers her self-condemnation:

> `ちゃんとわかってるじゃないですか`

The verb moves from recognition of external facts to recognition of internal biography.

## 9.6 `味方`

Nodoka's:

> `咲太はお姉ちゃんの味方でいてよ`

is stronger than generic “support.”

It asks him to remain on Mai's side.

The echo with `正義の味方` creates a useful ethical contrast:

> universal justice hero  
> versus  
> accountable ally of a particular person.

## 9.7 `守る`

Sakuta and Mai repeat the same verb back to each other.

The Japanese makes reciprocity explicit rather than merely thematic.

## 9.8 `大丈夫`

Sakuta's `大丈夫です` is characteristically misdirected: he is bleeding, yet reassures everybody else. The second use, directed at invisible Santas, also functions as direct acknowledgment.

## 9.9 `ざまぁ`

Miori uses `ざまぁ` for schadenfreude at a fallen high-status person. This echoes Sakuta's small `みんなざまぁ` satisfaction in Volume 11.

The recurrence prevents moral simplification: sympathetic characters can feel pleasure at reversal without that pleasure becoming a complete moral verdict.

# 10. Time, memory, dreams, worlds, and identity

## 10.1 State notation

Continue using:

- **W0** = current primary world.
- **W1** = the adjacent possibility world previously implicated in Volumes 9 and 11.

For Volume 13, use:

- **D-state** = a coherent world/state accessed through a dream cluster whose exact relation to W1 remains unknown.

Do not silently identify all December 24 dreams with W1.

## 10.2 Multi-viewpoint dreaming suggests coherent event-space

Sakuta and Ikumi access the same phone call from opposite ends. Thousands report the same festival from different audience positions.

That is stronger evidence than merely similar symbolic dreaming.

It suggests shared access to an event-space with stable relations among observers.

Its metaphysical category remains open.

## 10.3 The smartphone is a state fingerprint, not proof

Dream-Sakuta owns a smartphone. W0 Sakuta does not.

The detail matters because another Sakuta biography already differs in technology/social history. Yet W0 Sakuta could theoretically buy a smartphone before April.

Classify as:

> strong clue, not decisive proof.

## 10.4 Exact phone data proves informational content

Waking Sakuta dials the number learned in the dream and reaches Ikumi.

At least some dream content therefore carries:

- novel;
- externally valid;
- recoverable information.

The dream cannot be reduced to psychological symbolism.

## 10.5 Dream knowledge creates recursive causality

Once millions know a possible April 1:

- the media discusses it;
- fans expect it;
- Mai's “secret” appearance stops being secret;
- rumors reorganize around the expected declaration.

The observed possibility begins causally shaping W0.

This creates a recursive problem:

> **How can one determine whether a dream was predictive if awareness of the dream changes the world being predicted?**

## 10.6 Nene/Toko continuity test

| Axis | Toko-substitution state |
|---|---|
| Body | physically continuous |
| Name | `岩見沢寧々` displaced by `霧島透子` |
| Memory | Nene-specific autobiography deteriorates |
| Relationship | Takumi becomes inaccessible/forgotten |
| Records | old archives persist selectively |
| Vocation | announcer aspiration displaced |
| Social recognition | most observers cannot perceive Nene |
| Self-recognition | Nene's own name becomes stranger |
| Material archive | trophy discarded |
| Future choice | narrowed to occupying Toko role |

The case is therefore not ordinary rebranding. Multiple continuity anchors collapse together.

## 10.7 Physical versus digital archive

The university archive persists, but access to videos can be observer-dependent. The trophy remains stubbornly physical.

This makes Volume 13 another case in which material objects carry identity across unstable recognition systems.

The novel does not establish that the trophy itself is magical. It is best treated as a powerful autobiographical cue within the phenomenon.


# 11. Institutions, adults, social atmosphere, and material conditions

## 11.1 `#夢見る` becomes a distributed epistemic institution

By Volume 13, `#夢見る` no longer functions as a small rumor network. Millions of young people are posting, comparing, and sorting future-like experiences. The resulting social system performs some of the functions normally assigned to institutions:

- collection;
- cross-checking;
- pattern recognition;
- dissemination;
- public agenda-setting.

It lacks the safeguards institutions ordinarily need:

- controlled verification;
- a stable taxonomy;
- known false-positive rates;
- causal isolation;
- protection against feedback effects.

The danger is therefore epistemic as well as supernatural.

The same report repeated thousands of times begins to feel:

> true

even when the underlying event may belong to another possibility state.

This is especially important for Mai. The public obtains what looks like overwhelming corroboration for an April 1 declaration that current Mai says is false of herself.

Volume 13 thus separates:

> **many witnesses**

from:

> **settled ownership of truth.**

## 11.2 `集団未来視` is a media label, not a demonstrated mechanism

The phrase:

> `集団未来視`

is socially powerful because it compresses a difficult phenomenon into two intuitive words:

> collective future sight.

But the novel itself supplies reasons to resist the label as literal explanation:

- possible-world evidence;
- the dream-Sakuta smartphone discrepancy;
- Tomoe's distinctive long simulation;
- mutable outcomes;
- dreams that may be ordinary/self-fulfilling.

The media label is therefore a useful object of study in its own right.

Institutions need names before mechanisms are known.

Once a name exists, the name can prematurely discipline interpretation.

## 11.3 Current-person testimony competes with network belief

Mai says publicly:

> `私は霧島透子さんではありません`

This is unusually high-quality first-person evidence concerning current identity.

Traditional news coverage largely accepts it.

Social-network belief persists.

The divergence captures a modern recognition problem:

> **formal correction and distributed conviction do not operate on the same clock.**

A public narrative can remain useful to participants after it loses institutional legitimacy.

## 11.4 Celebrity turns identity into quasi-public property

Mai has spent years in an industry where other people routinely discuss:

- her image;
- career;
- desirability;
- personality;
- future choices.

The Toko rumor intensifies an existing structure.

Strangers now possess a huge dream archive that appears to tell them who Mai “really” is or will become.

The pressure is not merely that people are wrong.

It is that people behave as though interpretive access creates jurisdiction.

Volume 13's response is consistent with the branch-world ethics established earlier:

> **another possible version of Mai does not give the public ownership over current Mai.**

## 11.5 The university archive preserves what living memory loses

The Nene archive creates a productive institutional paradox.

The university still contains:

- photographs;
- Miss Contest records;
- biographical traces.

Living recognition nevertheless fails.

The institution can therefore “remember” Nene in one sense while the social body around the institution does not.

This reinforces a recurring AoButa principle:

> **record, memory, recognition, and presence are separate systems.**

## 11.6 Digital archives are observer-dependent

Miori and Sakuta can access Nene-related visual material that another observer effectively cannot perceive.

The record has not simply been deleted.

Access to its content appears recognition-dependent.

This prevents a simplistic archive theory in which:

> documentary existence automatically guarantees social existence.

The physical trophy becomes especially important because it remains materially available when digital and interpersonal access are unstable.

## 11.7 Police cannot manage a population they cannot perceive

The February 4 event is one of the series' strongest institutional thought experiments.

Police have:

- personnel;
- barriers;
- crowd-control responsibility;
- physical proximity.

They do not have:

> perception of the entire crowd.

The invisible Santas occupy real space.

Ordinary spectators treat their positions as gaps.

People move forward.

Police underestimate density because the world available to them contains fewer bodies than the real physical space does.

The barrier eventually fails.

This is not ordinary police negligence.

It is a mismatch between:

> **the population an institution can count**

and:

> **the population physically present.**

The scene gives literal force to a broader social truth without reducing the literal mechanics to allegory:

> institutions cannot adequately govern people who are systematically absent from their field of recognition.

## 11.8 Institutions work once the crisis becomes legible

AoButa does not use the incident to declare adult systems useless.

Once the physical crisis is visible:

- police respond;
- emergency services transport Sakuta;
- Kunimi works inside professional procedure;
- a hospital performs CT imaging;
- the wound is treated and stitched;
- police interview the Santa participants afterward.

The failure is selective rather than total.

This is important for the series' adult transition.

The solution to adolescent crisis is not:

> young protagonists are better than institutions.

Increasingly, it is:

> **institutions become useful when the relevant person and problem can enter their field of legibility.**

## 11.9 Firefighting, medicine, tutoring, driving, and adult role structure

Volume 13 continues the occupational architecture of Volumes 11–12.

Kunimi works in emergency response.

Sakuta tutors.

Sara works at the restaurant.

Hanakaede plans university.

Sakuta and Miori attend driving school.

These systems are mundane precisely because they distribute responsibility through:

- training;
- time;
- procedure;
- qualification;
- scope.

This is the adult counterweight to the “hero” model.

## 11.10 The driver's license as state recognition

The epilogue gives considerable narrative space to a completely ordinary institutional result.

Sakuta waits.

His number appears.

He passes.

A license is issued.

There is no ambiguity about whether society recognizes him for this purpose.

His photograph is bad.

That is funny rather than existential.

The card does not need to make Sakuta:

- beautiful;
- famous;
- unique;
- mysterious.

It merely needs to connect:

> this legal name + this body + this qualification.

After a volume about people disappearing into a glamorous identity, that mundane form of recognition is quietly restorative.

# 12. Place, season, objects, ordinary life, and formal motifs

## 12.1 Christmas that refuses to end

The story begins on December 25, when ordinary Christmas is already ending.

Stores and streets will remove seasonal decorations.

Nene does the opposite.

By February, her apartment remains saturated with Christmas.

For everybody else:

> Santa is seasonal.

For Nene:

> Santa has become permanent.

The seasonal role cannot end because the person underneath no longer has a stable identity to return to.

## 12.2 Hakone garden and the unseen woodpecker

The front-color Hakone scene has Sakuta and Mai hearing a woodpecker without immediately seeing it.

The moment is ordinary.

It should **not** be converted into a supernatural clue.

Formally, however, it resonates with the volume's later perception problem:

> an unseen presence can still produce effects from which its presence is inferred.

The book begins quietly with that proposition before giving it catastrophic crowd-level form.

## 12.3 Motomachi: a costume that should be impossible to miss

Nene walks through a public shopping district in conspicuous Santa clothing.

Almost nobody reacts.

The setting proves the strength of the phenomenon.

If she were dressed inconspicuously, lack of attention could be explained socially.

The text instead puts her in clothing that should make ordinary non-noticing almost impossible.

## 12.4 The orange scarf

The scarf is both:

- a new birthday gift;
- a repetition of an earlier relationship object.

Takumi's old scarf survived time, failed examinations, and memory loss.

The new scarf is chosen by Nene while she is already losing access to the relationship.

The object carries a continuity the people temporarily cannot.

## 12.5 The Miss Contest trophy

The trophy passes through three meanings.

### First

> public achievement.

### Then

> worthless residue from a failed self.

### Finally

> autobiographical evidence.

Nothing about the object changes.

The person-object relation changes.

This is a recurring AoButa strategy: material things become stable enough to expose how unstable memory and identity have become.

## 12.6 Christmas props as identity technology

A Santa figure by itself is decoration.

A tree is decoration.

A snow globe is decoration.

A reindeer is decoration.

But their repeated concentration across:

- Toko videos;
- Nene's room;
- other Toko imitators

creates an environment through which the persona can be continuously rehearsed.

The props are therefore not merely symbols of Toko.

They help make:

> **a livable set for the Toko role.**

## 12.7 The speaker: amplified voice over invisible bodies

A large speaker is designed to make one public voice audible across a crowd.

At Mai's event, that object nearly crushes her because the crowd cannot see many of the bodies below the stage.

The formal opposition is unusually sharp:

> **amplified celebrity voice / unrecognized ordinary body.**

The culture can make a famous person's speech overwhelmingly audible while remaining unable to count the people physically standing beneath the apparatus.

## 12.8 The hospital

The hospital again becomes the place where supernatural crisis is translated into ordinary bodily fact.

Sakuta has:

- a head injury;
- bleeding;
- imaging;
- stitches.

The metaphysical problem continues elsewhere.

The body receives ordinary treatment.

This separation remains important.

## 12.9 March 31: `The day before`

The epilogue title is not just a cliffhanger label.

The entire volume has been organized around:

> knowledge of a date before the date arrives.

Ending on March 31 means the novel deliberately stops at the boundary between:

- possible-world knowledge;
- current-world choice;
- public expectation;
- whatever April 1 will actually become.

The temporal pause is itself part of the argument.

## 12.10 Ordinary life remains the desired substrate

Even during the final-arc escalation, the novel repeatedly returns to:

- work;
- meals;
- school;
- family visits;
- driving lessons;
- commuting;
- part-time shifts;
- teasing.

These scenes are not filler.

They provide the stable world in which the characters can be:

> particular people rather than abstract cases.

# 14. Ethics and philosophical implications

## 14.1 Social construction does not make a self counterfeit

Volume 13 provides multiple counterexamples to a simplistic authenticity ethic.

Ikumi's reliable identity was strengthened by other people's expectations.

Sakuta's moral vocabulary was shaped by Shoko.

Mai's professional self is partly learned through years of public performance.

Therefore Nene's problem cannot be:

> “other people influenced her.”

The ethical question is:

> **Can the socially shaped self still contain the person's own history, agency, contradictions, and capacity to choose?**

## 14.2 Public consensus does not own personal identity

The April 1 dream cluster gives the public astonishingly strong evidence.

Still, current Mai says:

> no.

If possibility worlds are real, the ethical principle becomes even more important:

> **a choice made by another continuity belongs to that continuity.**

The existence of a possible Mai who says “I am Toko” would not transfer the statement to current Mai.

## 14.3 `何者か` is social pressure, not merely private vanity

Nene's desire to become somebody is intensified by:

- media work;
- likes;
- views;
- modeling;
- campus rank.

But the underlying question belongs to the whole university arc:

> What will I be when the protected school identity ends?

The platforms do not create the entire need.

They provide seductive numerical answers to it.

## 14.4 Anonymous prestige identities are unusually colonizable

An identity with:

- huge cultural value;
- repeatable aesthetics;
- repeatable voice;
- minimal public biography

is structurally available for projection.

This is why Toko can become a mass identity.

The interpretation does not require the real-world claim that anonymous artists literally cause identity dissolution.

The supernatural mechanics exaggerate a recognizable social possibility:

> prefabricated prestige can feel easier to inhabit than building a disappointing adult self.

## 14.5 Recognition must be able to include failure

Toko has no room for:

- failed auditions;
- stalled career;
- being wrong;
- embarrassing romantic history;
- ordinary resentment.

Nene returns when those facts can be spoken as:

> mine.

A self capable only of displaying success is too narrow to survive ordinary life.

## 14.6 Love is evidence, not essence

Takumi's love matters because:

- it preserves history;
- motivates remembering;
- gives Nene a particular witness;
- creates a future relation she may choose.

It should not be turned into:

> love creates human value.

The stronger proposition is:

> **a totalizing claim such as “I have nothing” is false when it erases real relationships, commitments, and possibilities from the accounting.**

## 14.7 Sakuta's trophy intervention is paternalistic even though it works

Outcome does not retroactively create consent.

Sakuta saves the trophy because he believes the self throwing it away is in the process of deleting Nene.

That may be a defensible emergency judgment.

It remains an override.

The distinction is essential to the project's intervention ledger.

## 14.8 Takumi's car intervention is emotionally understandable and physically reckless

Standing before a moving car is not a proportionate model of romantic courage.

The scene communicates:

> I will force a moment of encounter because ordinary requests cannot reach you.

It also creates avoidable bodily danger.

The narrative success should not erase that judgment.

## 14.9 The Santas are not villains

They do not coordinate an attack.

They do not come with a common plan to harm Mai.

They are people trapped in a recognition crisis.

The accident emerges from:

- invisibility;
- physical crowding;
- institutional misperception.

This matters because otherwise the mass group could easily be converted into a threatening mob and morally simplified.

## 14.10 Unrecognized people remain systemically consequential

The crowd sequence generates a broad social proposition directly from literal mechanics:

> **A system that treats unseen people as absent will make incorrect decisions about real space, risk, and resources.**

The metaphorical applicability is powerful because the literal claim remains intact.

## 14.11 Mutual protection is better than sacrificial possession

The Sakuta/Mai exchange gives care a reciprocal structure.

Each has standing in the other's risk.

Neither therefore has absolute standing to decide that their own destruction is an acceptable gift to the other.

The series is still working toward this principle rather than perfectly obeying it.

## 14.12 Distributed care is the adult answer to heroic isolation

The hospital sequence shows care that is:

- real;
- plural;
- bounded.

People help and then return to their lives.

That is healthier than a model in which intimacy is measured by permanent orbit around the injured person.

# 15. Counterreadings, limitations, and hypothesis stress-test

## 15.1 “Nene disappeared because Mai stole her status”

Too strong.

Miori proposes the model, and Nene later confirms career/status humiliation. Nene never directly identifies Mai as the central cause.

Retain Mai's arrival as possible social substrate, not established sufficient cause.

## 15.2 “Nene was always fake”

Rejected.

Her previous identities were socially shaped but biographically hers. The novel's own Ikumi/Sakuta parallels show that social construction is normal.

## 15.3 “Takumi's love cures Nene”

Too reductive.

The causal chain also contains:

- trophy;
- memory restoration;
- name;
- Nene's own autobiographical speech;
- Sakuta's challenge;
- future vocational possibility;
- broader social recognition.

## 15.4 “Being loved by a man proves Nene's human worth”

Rejected as a general thesis.

Sakuta uses Takumi as evidence against the specific totalization `何もない`. The novel leaves Nene's future wider than romance.

## 15.5 “The Santas are villains attacking Mai”

Textually false.

Police find no coordinated hostile plan.

## 15.6 “Sakuta cures the Santas by saying `大丈夫です`”

Unproven.

Direct acknowledgment and the shock of seeing him bleeding both correlate with recovery. Exact causal weight remains open.

## 15.7 “No dream means future death”

Unsupported.

It is a peer rumor built from one death. Mai and Nene already complicate the rule.

## 15.8 “The dreams definitely show the fixed future”

Weakened by:

- possibility-world evidence;
- Tomoe's mutable February 4 outcome;
- state discrepancies.

## 15.9 “The dreams definitely show W1”

Not established.

Use D-state until the text identifies the relation.

## 15.10 “Toko caused every dream”

Not established.

Earlier Toko testimony denied directly giving the dreams, and V13 does not conclusively prove or falsify that claim.

## 15.11 “Nene is Kirishima Toko”

Corrected by Volume 13.

Nene is one person who became Toko.

The originating singer remains unresolved.

## 15.12 “Miori sees Toko because she does not use smartphones normally”

Interesting but unsupported.

Her media habits may have thematic relevance. No causal rule connects them to perception.

## 15.13 “The original Toko must be Mai”

Current Mai explicitly denies the identity.

The dream raises the possibility of another-state identification. It does not settle the present identity.

## 15.14 “Mai does not dream because she is no longer adolescent”

Mai jokes/speculates along these lines.

The text does not establish a maturity cutoff.

## 15.15 “The Toko phenomenon is simply social-media addiction”

Too reductive.

Social media is clearly part of the substrate. The literal case includes:

- memory loss;
- perceptual erasure;
- observer-dependent records;
- mass identity substitution.

The phenomenon cannot be collapsed into media criticism alone.

## 15.16 “Nene recovers her original self”

Too clean.

She recovers autobiographical access and social visibility. The experience itself now belongs to her history. There is no untouched pre-Toko self to restore.

## 15.17 “The hospital scene proves the ensemble still exists mainly to orbit Sakuta”

The scene argues almost the opposite.

People care while retaining:

- jobs;
- family obligations;
- emergency shifts;
- ordinary schedules.

## 15.18 “The speaker rescue proves Sakuta has learned nothing”

Too harsh.

Immediate emergency intervention differs from deliberate sacrificial substitution.

Still, his reflex to reassure others while seriously injured proves that over-responsibility has not vanished.

The mature reading preserves both.

## 16.1 Recognition becomes infrastructural

Earlier arcs primarily made recognition interpersonal.

Volume 13 shows that nonrecognition can alter:

- crowd density;
- institutional counting;
- public safety;
- information access.

Recognition has become a property of social systems.

## 16.2 Misrecognition can come from excess as well as absence

Nene disappears because too little remains attached to Nene.

Mai is threatened by a public that attaches too much interpretive identity to Mai.

The healthy target is therefore not maximum recognition.

It is:

> **accurate, bounded recognition that leaves the person authority over her own current continuity.**

## 16.3 Possible-world knowledge does not own current identity

The dream can contain one Mai.

Current reality contains another.

The existence of multiple possibilities makes person-specific consent and continuity more important, not less.

## 16.4 The hero becomes a connector

Sakuta's strongest work in Nene's case is:

- preserving;
- carrying;
- introducing;
- asking;
- witnessing.

The decisive relationship is Nene/Takumi.

That is a major intervention-ethic development.

## 16.5 Ordinary adulthood is now the positive counterweight to Syndrome

Driving school, work, university planning, emergency service, family visits, tutoring, and licensing are not merely what happens between supernatural incidents.

They are the structures through which the cast learns to inhabit identities that do not require crisis for proof.

# 21. Volume thesis and questions carried forward

Volume 13 can be reduced to seven propositions.

> **A socially constructed self is not necessarily a fake self.**

> **A role becomes annihilating when successful performance requires the person's own biography to become inaccessible.**

> **Being “someone” cannot safely mean occupying a prestige identity that anyone else can occupy just as well.**

> **Collective belief does not acquire ownership of a current person merely because millions share evidence about a possible version of that person.**

> **Unrecognized people remain materially present; systems that cannot perceive them can still be broken by their bodies.**

> **Love and recognition help restore a person, but neither substitutes for the person's own renewed ability to say “I.”**

> **Adult care increasingly looks like connection, delegation, institutional competence, ordinary skill, and shared burden rather than spectacular self-sacrifice.**

The volume's key question is therefore not yet:

> Who is Kirishima Toko?

It is:

> **Why is Kirishima Toko an identity that so many people can disappear into?**

Nene gives one answer.

She arrives in Tokyo expecting to become somebody.

She fails.

Then strangers give her a ready-made answer:

> You sound like Toko.

The answer feels like rescue.

The persona already possesses:

- attention;
- status;
- aesthetic coherence;
- mystery;
- social value.

Nene only has to enter it.

But a role with no publicly secured owner can admit more than one occupant.

Then more than one.

Then a hundred.

The result is a nightmare form of recognition:

> everybody knows “Kirishima Toko,”  
> while the actual people inside the identity become impossible to see.

Nene begins returning when the self can contain what Toko could not:

- failure;
- embarrassment;
- ambition;
- bad career results;
- a boyfriend she had forgotten;
- the fact that she still does not know what “my true self” means;
- the possibility of deciding later what to become.

Her recovery therefore does not answer:

> `私のままの私ってなに!?`

with an essence.

It answers with:

> **an owned history plus an open future.**

Takumi can remember.

Sakuta can preserve a record.

Rio can revise a theory.

Sara can notice a pattern.

Ikumi can supply a comparison.

But Nene still has to say:

> I failed.

> I wanted to be somebody.

> I became an imitation.

That ugly first-person speech is exactly what the elegant Toko persona could not contain.

The February 4 crowd then scales the problem outward.

A hundred Santas stand before Mai.

Nobody counts them.

The public walks into their bodies because, socially, those bodies occupy no recognized space.

The barrier fails.

The invisible becomes physically undeniable.

AoButa's first volume asked whether one witness could keep a person socially real.

Volume 13 asks what happens when a whole institution, crowd, media environment, and public imagination all recognize the wrong thing.

Its answer is not comforting.

> **Misrecognition can become infrastructure.**

But recognition can also become distributed care.

A boyfriend remembers.

A friend notices a logical contradiction.

A scientist refuses certainty.

A younger sister admits that she cannot fully know her famous sister's experience.

A former title heroine notices Christmas props without needing supernatural sight.

Another former title heroine flies north to help without demanding to become the savior.

A firefighter drives an ambulance.

A girlfriend catches the boyfriend who caught the falling speaker.

A sister comes to the hospital.

Coworkers visit during break.

Then everybody goes back to life.

That is the future AoButa increasingly treats as worth protecting.

Not a perfect self.

Not a perfect world.

Not the role everybody already admires.

A life populated by particular people who know enough of your history to notice when you disappear—and who can still let you decide what to become next.

## Final synthesis

Volume 13 is a book about wanting proof that one is somebody.

Nene spends years collecting proof.

Modeling work.

A university name.

A Miss Contest trophy.

Photographs.

Followers.

A viral performance.

Those proofs work until they do not.

Then the strongest possible substitute arrives:

> everybody says she sounds like Kirishima Toko.

So she becomes Toko.

She buys the props.

She wears the costume.

She adopts the voice.

She removes the old clothing.

She throws away the trophy.

She loses the boyfriend.

She loses the name.

And eventually the social world cooperates so completely with the substitution that almost nobody can see her at all.

The paradox is vicious:

> **the more successful the public identity becomes, the less person remains for the public to recognize.**

Takumi's role is not to tell Nene who she truly is.

When he tries the easy phrase—

> Nene as Nene—

she destroys it:

> `私のままの私ってなに!?`

The question deserves to win.

No lover, scientist, or narrator has a neat definition.

What Takumi can do is remember.

What Sakuta can do is preserve an object, connect people, and refuse the claim that failure means nothingness.

What Nene can finally do is speak.

Not elegantly.

Not as a perfect idol.

Not as a mysterious singer.

She says, in effect:

> I thought I could do it.

> I failed.

> I wanted to become something.

> I became an imitation.

> I still want to believe I am somebody.

That speech is not an answer to the metaphysics of essence.

It is something more useful.

It is:

> **autobiography.**

And autobiography is exactly what the Toko persona had erased.

The novel then reveals that Nene was never unique.

A hundred Santas stand before Mai.

Nobody else can see them.

The public walks into their bodies because socially those bodies occupy no space.

The barrier falls.

The invisible becomes physically undeniable.

Volume 13 therefore brings the series' oldest recognition problem into the scale of public systems.

It also gives the clearest picture yet of what the alternative might look like.

Not one perfect rescuer.

A network.

Not one perfect identity.

A biography capable of containing failure.

Not one certain future.

Possibilities that remain answerable to present choices.

And after all the dreams, disappearing identities, Santas, flights, hospitals, viral rumors, and public crises, Sakuta spends March 31 waiting for an ordinary number to appear on a licensing board.

`134`.

Passed.

The photograph is bad.

Miori's is bad too.

They laugh.

The person on the card does not need to be beautiful, famous, viral, mysterious, or somebody's ideal.

The state only needs to know:

> this is the person who passed the test.

It is one of the most mundane images of stable identity in the series.

Then the book turns forward.

Tomorrow is April 1.

Millions have already seen it.

Current Mai has already said the dreamed identity is not hers.

The false Tokos have awakened.

The original Toko is still unresolved.

And the final chapter has begun.
