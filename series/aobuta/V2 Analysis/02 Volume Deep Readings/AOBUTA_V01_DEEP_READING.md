# 『青春ブタ野郎はバニーガール先輩の夢を見ない』
## Volume 1 Deep Dive
### Hypervisibility, social erasure, and the difference between being seen and being recognized

# 1. Central thesis

The first volume of *AoButa* is not fundamentally about an invisible celebrity. It is about **recognition under two opposite forms of social violence**.

Sakurajima Mai has spent much of her life being looked at. She is a nationally recognizable actress and model whose body, face, labor, and image are consumed by strangers. Yet that extraordinary visibility has repeatedly failed to guarantee that other people recognize her as a person with authority over her own life. Her mother can override a stated boundary because the entertainment machine needs a decision. Strangers can know her name while knowing nothing about her. The public can see her everywhere while the individual Mai remains insufficiently legible as a subject.

At Minegahara, the relation flips. Mai goes from hypervisible to socially negligible. Students learn not to address her; she becomes something like `空気`—air, atmosphere, a person around whom social life routes itself without direct acknowledgment. The supernatural disappearance does not create that social structure from nothing. It literalizes and radicalizes it.

The volume's governing distinction is therefore:

> **Attention is not the same as recognition.**

A person may be watched without being known. A person may be desired without being respected. A person may be famous without possessing her own public image. Conversely, the absence of attention can become a form of erasure when nobody is willing to act as though the person in front of them is socially real.

The compact thesis is:

> **The opposite of social erasure is not celebrity; it is being known by someone who recognizes one's subjecthood.**

This is why Sakuta matters to Mai. His importance is not that he creates her existence through romantic desire. It is that he continues to treat her as a person when both celebrity culture and school atmosphere have failed to do so in different ways.

The volume also establishes the series' basic ethical problem around Sakuta. He is unusually willing to refuse social atmosphere. That is a virtue when the atmosphere is unjust. But the same disposition makes him prone to unilateral intervention, privacy violations, reckless self-sacrifice, and the assumption that if he can force a crisis toward resolution, he may be entitled to cross boundaries on the way there.

Volume 1 therefore establishes two long-running questions at once:

1. **What makes another person socially real?**
2. **When does caring enough to act become acting as though the cared-for person's boundaries are secondary?**

# 2. Source, structure, and narrated sequence

The EPUB contains twenty-nine spine items and twenty-four image resources. Its principal narrative architecture is:

1. **第一章　先輩はバニーガール**
2. **第二章　仲直りの代償**
3. **第三章　初デートに波乱は付き物だ**
4. **第四章　僕らの思い出**
5. **第五章　君だけがいない世界**
6. **あとがき**

The chapter progression is well matched to the volume's social-ontological escalation.

The opening begins from anomaly and incongruity: Mai appears in a bunny-girl costume in a public library and almost nobody reacts. The image is comic and erotic, but its function is desperation. She has chosen a conspicuous form because ordinary presence no longer guarantees perception.

The middle of the book expands outward from the oddity into biography. The disappearance cannot be understood without Mai's history in entertainment, her conflict with her mother, her withdrawal from work, and the school's preexisting social habit of treating her as someone not to be engaged. The supernatural problem is therefore causally entangled with a social and relational history.

The final movement radically raises the stakes. The problem ceases to be merely that people do not see Mai. Sleep and memory transform the entire social world into a place in which she has effectively never mattered to most people. Sakuta is left trying to preserve a relationship that no longer has public support or stable autobiographical confirmation.

The final confession functions simultaneously as romance, public disruption, and an attempt to make collective acknowledgment impossible to avoid.

The closing hook then destabilizes the apparently completed arc by returning Sakuta to June 27. The novel therefore ends with the important warning that the series' anomalies need not respect the reassuring architecture of a single resolved heroine story.

# 3. Causal synopsis: what changes and why

Mai notices that people increasingly fail to perceive or acknowledge her. She tests the problem through conspicuous clothing and travel. Sakuta becomes one of the few reliable witnesses.

Their investigation reveals several overlapping conditions:

- Mai had already become socially isolated at school before literal disappearance.
- She had once wished for a world in which nobody knew her after years of invasive public attention.
- Her withdrawal from entertainment was connected to a serious boundary violation by her mother and the industry.
- Perception loss spreads beyond the school environment.
- Sleep becomes a major transition point in which observers forget Mai.
- Physical traces do not behave exactly like ordinary memory: some artifacts remain while identifying information becomes blank, unavailable, or detached from the remembered person.

Sakuta attempts to resist forgetting through sleep deprivation. The plan is bodily reckless and ultimately unsustainable. Mai stops him by secretly inducing sleep, prioritizing his immediate physical safety over his chosen plan. This is an ethically mixed act that the novel treats with warmth but that should remain visible as a consent problem.

After Sakuta wakes, he too has forgotten Mai. Emotional and procedural residue nevertheless remains strong enough to create dissonance. Through the accumulated traces of the relationship, he recovers memory. He then publicly declares his love at school, breaking the atmosphere that had made nonacknowledgment easy. Collective attention returns to Mai.

The important causal caution is that the book does **not** prove a clean formula such as:

> confession + mass attention = cure.

The confession, memory recovery, return of public perception, Sakuta's refusal of `空気`, and Mai's own desire to remain in the world all converge. Their exact necessary and sufficient relation remains unresolved.

# 4. The phenomenon: experience, mechanics, and explanatory limits

Volume 1 provides the first Adolescence Syndrome case and should therefore be read cautiously. Later series knowledge must not be used to manufacture a universal law here.

## 4.1 Observable domains

The anomaly affects at least:

- immediate visual/social perception;
- willingness or ability to acknowledge Mai;
- autobiographical memory;
- written identifying information;
- the relation between physical artifacts and semantic identity;
- sleep-associated state transitions.

## 4.2 Sleep as a transition

The strongest observable regularity is that sleep is associated with forgetting. Sakuta fears sleep because remaining awake appears to preserve his ability to recognize Mai. This produces one of the book's most effective embodied devices: memory is not an abstract database. It becomes something the body cannot preserve indefinitely because the body must sleep.

That makes the eventual failure of the no-sleep strategy thematically apt. A relationship cannot remain real by requiring one person to destroy his body in order to remember it.

## 4.3 Physical residue versus semantic erasure

The world does not behave as though Mai were simply deleted from history. Some traces remain. Yet references that would identify her can cease to function as reliable evidence. This distinction will matter for the series' later interest in memory and personal continuity, but at the Volume 1 boundary it should remain descriptive rather than systematized.

## 4.4 Rio's science

Rio provides a speculative model using quantum observation language, but she repeatedly qualifies it. Her own wording explicitly marks the move from microphysics to human-scale experience as imaginative extrapolation:

> 「あくまで粒子の世界……ミクロの世界での話。」

and:

> 「荒唐無稽で空想科学的な拡大解釈」

She also later remarks:

> 「科学的な検証なんて当てにならないよ」

The correct first-pass position is therefore:

- **TF/CB:** Rio uses observer-related quantum language as a model.
- **SI:** the model helps the characters notice the importance of observation/recognition.
- **UA:** whether the phenomenon literally obeys quantum mechanics in any rigorous sense.

The science functions primarily as diegetic heuristic and thematic vocabulary, not as proven physical law.

## 4.5 Best current formulation

At the end of Volume 1, the strongest non-reductive formulation is:

> **Mai's Syndrome makes literal a crisis in which public attention, social acknowledgment, memory, and personal existence have come apart.**

It is not *merely* a metaphor for social exclusion, because the literal effects are independently operative. But the social structure is not decorative background either; it gives the anomaly its human meaning.

# 5. Mai Sakurajima: hypervisibility without authorship

Mai is already a mature professional when Sakuta meets her. The novel's interest lies not in teaching her that acting is fake or that public performance corrupts authentic identity. She **likes acting**. Her problem is authorship.

The clearest earlier wound concerns the swimsuit job arranged while she was still young. Mai had drawn a boundary. Her mother and the professional environment converted that boundary into something that had to yield to adult expectations. Mai later describes the social logic she was forced to inhabit:

> 「空気を読んで、利口な判断をしなければならなかった。子供なのに、大人のふりをしなければならなかった。」

This sentence is crucial because it joins `空気` to premature adulthood. Reading the atmosphere is not presented as neutral social competence. It can mean learning that the apparently rational choice is to participate in one's own boundary violation because refusing would make one difficult, childish, or professionally irresponsible.

Mai's earlier wish:

> 「誰も私のことを知らない世界に行きたい」

must therefore be read historically. She once wanted relief from being publicly possessed. By the present action, she does **not** actually want annihilation. She wants to work again, attend school, maintain relationships, and decide what her life will be.

The difference is the core of her arc:

> wanting freedom from invasive visibility is not the same as wanting not to exist.

Her agency becomes clearest when she resumes deciding for herself. The narration's formulation:

> 「麻衣は麻衣のことを自分で決めていたのだ。」

should be treated as a central Volume 1 line. Mai's development is not simply “accepting help from Sakuta.” It is resuming jurisdiction over Mai.

She protects Sakuta from Fumika's demand for a scar photograph. She confronts her mother. She chooses a new agency. She establishes conditions for the romance rather than surrendering to the intensity of Sakuta's confession.

When she says:

> 「今の、一ヵ月後にもう一度言って」

she demands temporal verification. Crisis intensity is not enough. If Sakuta means it, ordinary time should not dissolve it.

Her later:

> 「……うん、いいよ」

therefore carries more weight than an immediate climactic kiss would have. It converts a rescue bond into a chosen relationship.

# 6. Sakuta: refusing the atmosphere and learning the danger of heroic overreach

Sakuta's most distinctive early quality is often described as not caring what people think. Volume 1 makes that too simple.

He **can** perceive the atmosphere. He simply does not always accept its authority.

Early in the book he can say:

> 「空気と戦うなんてバカバカしいですって」

Later, his stance hardens into:

> 「もう、空気なんて読んでやるか、バカバカしい」

This is not social incompetence. It is selective disobedience.

That makes him unusually capable of recognizing people whom the group has tacitly agreed to overlook. His history with Kaede makes this more than adolescent contrarianism. He knows what it is like for collective social behavior to become materially destructive.

His helping ethic is stated directly:

> 「困ってるのに、誰にも頼れないのはしんどいから。」

This is one of the first durable formulations of Sakuta's moral psychology. He is especially responsive to the person who cannot ask anyone else.

But the volume also establishes the danger inside that virtue.

Sakuta:

- presses Mai for explanations she does not always want to provide;
- treats his scar as bargaining material;
- engages in boundary-pushing banter;
- becomes aggressive toward Mai's mother;
- grabs Tomoe in an early conflict;
- devises a multi-day sleep-deprivation strategy;
- and ultimately turns a private romantic truth into a public declaration without Mai's prior consent.

Some of these actions are effective. That does not automatically make them ethically clean.

The first-pass Sakuta thesis is therefore:

> **Sakuta is morally valuable because he refuses unjust social scripts, but he has not yet learned that refusing the group's authority does not grant him authority over the person he is trying to help.**

---

# 7. Mai and Sakuta: recognition before romance

The central relationship works because romance grows from an epistemic and ethical relation.

Sakuta is important to Mai first because:

> he continues to recognize her.

Mai is important to Sakuta because she treats his impossible experiences—Kaede's wounds, the scars, Shoko—not as disqualifying absurdities. Both know what it is like for socially implausible suffering to be treated as though the suffering person must be mistaken.

Their banter produces intimacy, but the novel is careful not to make banter identical with consent. The teasing often involves sexual language or boundary testing, and the power to answer back is part of why their dynamic works. The more important moments are those where each protects the other's subjecthood.

Mai's brief focalized/interstitial material is also important because it prevents her from existing only as an object in Sakuta's narration. The novel temporarily gives the reader access to her independently of what Sakuta can know.

By the end of the volume, the romance has acquired a crucial condition:

> it must survive ordinary time.

That makes the relationship structurally continuous rather than a resolved “route.”

# 8. Ensemble and relationship changes

## Rio Futaba

Rio is immediately valuable because she is both skeptical and willing to reason within Sakuta's impossible testimony. She does not require that an experience be scientifically explained before treating the witness as sincere.

Her models are tentative. Her friendship is more stable than her physics.

## Kaede

Kaede is largely off-center in this volume but foundational to Sakuta's psychology. Her cyberbullying history explains why he takes social atmosphere seriously even while mocking it. The physical wounds associated with her crisis also give the series an early warning that psychological/social violence and bodily reality will not remain cleanly separable.

## Shoko

The older Shoko belongs to Sakuta's past. She listened to him during the period when Kaede's crisis had isolated him. Sakuta says plainly:

> 「すげえ、好きでした」

Yet nobody at Minegahara knows her. At the Volume 1 boundary, this is a character mystery, not a time-mechanics problem to be solved with later information.

## Tomoe

Tomoe's early presence introduces the social anxiety that Volume 2 will later foreground, but Volume 1 only establishes enough to show how intensely peer perception can govern seemingly trivial choices.

# 9. Japanese voice, address, and translation-sensitive language

The most important lexical target in Volume 1 is `空気`.

A translation such as “read the room” captures part of `空気を読む`, but the Japanese word does more work here because the novel shifts between:

- atmosphere as unspoken social expectation;
- Mai being treated like `空気`;
- Sakuta's refusal to obey atmosphere;
- and an anomaly in which social nonrecognition becomes literal disappearance.

The word therefore moves from social metaphor toward ontology.

Mai's speech tends toward controlled, adult, precise phrasing, especially when contrasted with Sakuta's deliberately provocative casual register. That gap is part of her characterization: she has spent years learning professional composure.

Sakuta's bluntness should not be translated into simple rudeness. He frequently chooses flat or outrageous wording because it punctures ritualized social language. The effect can be humane or invasive depending on context.

The narration also repeatedly distinguishes looking from knowing. A reader should track verbs of seeing, remembering, knowing, and deciding rather than treating them as interchangeable.

# 10. Time, sleep, memory, and identity

Volume 1 already establishes that autobiographical memory is constitutive of social identity without being the whole of it.

If everyone forgets Mai, she loses:

- shared history;
- relational proof;
- public continuity.

Yet Sakuta's eventual recovery suggests that affective or procedural residue can persist beneath explicit recall.

At the first-pass boundary, the novel leaves open whether this residue is:

- neurological memory;
- emotional trace;
- anomaly-specific persistence;
- or simply narrative evidence that total deletion was incomplete.

The final return to June 27 also means narrated time itself is now unstable. The book ends before the reader can know what kind of instability this represents.

# 11. Institutions, social atmosphere, and material conditions

Volume 1's supernatural plot is deeply institutional.

## Entertainment work

Mai's earlier boundary violation is possible because a minor's refusal is filtered through:

- a manager-parent;
- production incentives;
- adult ideas about professionalism;
- and a culture that rewards “reasonable” compliance.

The issue is not that acting is inherently corrupt. The novel's stronger critique is that an institution can make exploitation look like mature cooperation.

## School

The school does not need an explicit order telling students to ignore Mai. Social atmosphere is enough. This is a recurring AoButa insight: harm can be decentralized.

## Medicine

Sakuta and Kaede's prior history also establishes medicine's epistemic limit. A clinical system may describe symptoms without possessing a complete explanation for the impossible bodily events the characters experience.

## Media

Fumika's interest in Sakuta's scars creates another recognition problem. Documentation can make suffering visible while also converting it into consumable evidence.

# 12. Place, objects, illustrations, and formal motifs

The bunny-girl costume is the most obvious visual symbol, but it should not be read only as fanservice. It is an instrument of forced conspicuousness: Mai makes herself visually impossible to overlook and discovers that visibility itself has stopped functioning normally.

The publication nevertheless profits from the erotic appeal of the same image. That creates a genuine tension between story and paratext:

> a story about a young woman's control over her public body is marketed through conspicuous presentation of that body.

This is not enough to invalidate the story's critique, but it belongs in the critical ledger.

Oogaki functions as a useful geographic contrast. Travel undertaken for work has often meant being transported as part of Mai's professional image. Travel with Sakuta can become chosen movement.

Phones, written references, sleep, photographs, and clothing all function as tests of whether identity can persist when social memory fails.

---

# 13. Ethics and philosophical implications

The volume's deepest ethical distinction is between **attention and recognition**.

Recognition requires more than sensory contact. It involves treating another person as someone whose:

- testimony matters;
- boundaries matter;
- choices matter;
- history matters;
- future is not public property.

Sakuta's public confession is therefore ethically complicated. It helps destroy the social consensus of nonrecognition. It also exposes Mai to a public act she did not preauthorize.

Likewise, Mai's decision to drug Sakuta's coffee protects his health while overriding consent.

The novel's best ethical moments are not those where someone proves purity. They are those where contradictory goods remain visible:

- protection and autonomy;
- visibility and privacy;
- care and intrusion;
- performance and authenticity;
- social belonging and refusal of social coercion.

# 14. Counterreadings, limitations, and unresolved tensions

### Counterreading: Mai disappears because she “wanted it”

This is too simple and risks blaming her for a phenomenon whose present consequences she explicitly rejects. The past wish is relevant, but current desire and social conditions matter too.

### Counterreading: Sakuta's confession proves romance cures the Syndrome

The temporal association is strong; causal sufficiency is not demonstrated. Collective attention, Sakuta's recovered memory, Mai's own commitments, and the social atmosphere change together.

### Counterreading: Rio's quantum model is the world's physics

The text itself calls the application speculative. Treating it as established law exceeds the evidence.

### Limitation: sexual comedy

The book often uses sexual boundary-testing for humor. The reciprocal banter is part of the relationship's texture, but comic framing can obscure real questions of consent and objectification.

### Unresolved: Shoko

At this point, older Shoko is an unexplained biographical mystery. No later answer belongs here.

---

# 15. What Volume 1 changes in the series-so-far model

Because this is the opening volume, its “delta” establishes the baseline.

1. Adolescence Syndrome is literally operative but psychologically/socially shaped.
2. Recognition is not equivalent to attention or fame.
3. `空気` is both social mechanism and central lexical motif.
4. Sakuta is a selective social dissenter rather than someone unable to read atmosphere.
5. Sakuta's care ethic is powerful but interventionist.
6. Mai is not a disposable heroine; the romance is constructed as continuing ordinary-life commitment.
7. Rio's science is heuristic, not confirmed cosmology.
8. Memory, sleep, physical traces, and social existence can diverge.
9. Kaede and Shoko establish unresolved histories that materially shape Sakuta.
10. The series intends ordinary institutions—school, family, work, media, medicine—to matter as much as the supernatural layer.

# 18. Open questions carried forward

1. What precisely determines who can perceive Mai?
2. Why is sleep such a powerful transition point?
3. What survives when explicit autobiographical memory disappears?
4. Are written references blank because the world changes, because observers cannot semantically resolve them, or through another mechanism?
5. Is public recognition necessary, sufficient, or merely correlated with restoration?
6. How much of the phenomenon is related to Mai's earlier wish versus her current school environment?
7. Who exactly is Makinohara Shoko, and why does nobody at Minegahara know the girl Sakuta remembers?

9. Will Sakuta learn to distinguish justified refusal of social pressure from unjustified override of individual boundaries?

# 19. Cumulative archival note

The first volume establishes the interpretive discipline that should govern the rest of the series:

> **Never collapse a literal anomaly into metaphor, but never let the literal anomaly distract from the social and relational structures that give it meaning.**

Mai is not simply invisible because she is lonely. She is literally disappearing. But the book's literary force lies in the fact that a person can be made socially absent long before the world stops seeing her.

And Sakuta's first great virtue is not that he rescues Mai.

It is that, when the atmosphere tells him there is nobody there worth addressing, he keeps talking to the person in front of him.
