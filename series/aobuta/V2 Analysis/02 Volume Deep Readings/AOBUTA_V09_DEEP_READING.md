# 『青春ブタ野郎はランドセルガールの夢を見ない』
## Volume 9 Deep Reading
### Recognition without regression, reconciliation without restoration, and the making of home after rupture

**Primary source:** Original Japanese EPUB listed in the metadata above.

**Publication position:** Ninth numbered light novel.

**Spoiler boundary:** Volumes 1–9 only. The later bonus stories **『アニマルランド』** and **『スプリングデイズ』**, although narratively adjacent to the year that follows this volume, are not used here. Volume 10 and all later material are likewise excluded.

**Analytical naming convention:** As in Volumes 5 and 8, **Hanakaede** is used in English analytical prose for the restored `花楓` autobiographical continuity, while **Kaede** refers to the post-amnesia `『かえで』` continuity. This is only an analytical disambiguation; both names are pronounced `かえで`.

---

# 1. Central thesis

Volume 9 is a novel about **returning without going backward**.

Its central proposition is:

> **A repaired family is not the old family recovered. It is a new relationship built by people who have been changed by the rupture.**

A second formulation is even more important for Sakuta:

> **Growing up is not proving that you no longer need your parents. It is becoming able to see them as finite people, accept that the independence you built without them is real, and choose relation without surrendering the self that independence produced.**

The title makes the mysterious elementary-school girl appear to be the volume's obvious heroine. Formally, she is important. She opens the novel, guides Sakuta through the adjacent possibility world, and reappears after the one-year time skip. Yet the emotional center of the book is not the girl. It is **Sakuta's relationship to his mother, to the family that broke, and to the life he built after that break**.

Volume 5 treated the family's collapse primarily through the sister whose autobiographical identity ruptured. Volume 8 then asked what Hanakaede owed Kaede after inheriting the consequences of that rupture. Volume 9 turns the camera toward Sakuta and asks what the same event did to the older brother who remained continuously conscious through it.

The answer is severe.

Sakuta tells himself that he became independent. He learned to:

- cook;
- clean;
- wake himself and Kaede;
- work;
- manage the household;
- deal with school;
- protect his sister;
- function while his mother was unavailable;
- and stop expecting his family to return to its former form.

These competencies are real. They are part of who he is. But the novel reveals the hidden price of that competence: he did not simply mature. He also **cut his mother out of the field of expectation because he had no practical alternative**.

His crisis begins when Hanakaede's successful reunion with their mother makes the impossible suddenly plausible. A restored family seems within reach. Sakuta initially interprets this as uncomplicatedly good. Only when he becomes globally unrecognizable does he discover that he is not emotionally standing where Hanakaede stands. Hanakaede can move toward their mother as a daughter seeking reconnection. Sakuta has spent two years becoming someone who no longer knows how to be a son.

That is why the novel's key emotional sentence is not the supernatural question "why can nobody see him?" It is his discovery that, during an entire reunion day, **he did not notice that his own mother never called his name**.

The horror is double.

His mother did not recognize him.

But he had become so practiced at not expecting recognition from her that he did not recognize its absence.

The volume therefore returns to AoButa's foundational concept—recognition—but moves it inward and backward toward the family.

Volume 1 asked:

> What happens when a social environment refuses to recognize a person who is physically present?

Volume 9 asks:

> What happens when the person at the origin of your life no longer recognizes you—and when you have survived by training yourself not to need that recognition?

The literal phenomenon becomes global. Once Sakuta's mother does not perceive him, nobody in his primary world perceives him. Sakuta theorizes that the world is making her recognition "correct," but this remains his interpretation rather than a proven law. What is thematically clear is that maternal recognition has become entangled with **his sense of being a person who belongs in the world**.

The new abdominal scar reinforces the connection. Unlike his older chest wounds, it runs toward the navel, the bodily site where he was once physically connected to his mother. Sakuta himself reads the symbolism. The novel makes the association too deliberate to ignore, while still leaving the exact supernatural mechanism unresolved.

The solution is also carefully structured.

Sakuta does **not** solve the problem by becoming the child he was before the family crisis.

He does **not** move into the adjacent possibility world where the family never fractured and simply accept the objectively more comfortable life.

He does **not** ask Shoko to explain or rescue him.

He does **not** let Mai replace his mother.

And he does **not** demand that his mother restore him before he acknowledges her.

Instead, he:

1. experiences the life in which the family remained intact;
2. refuses to appropriate the accomplishments and relationships of the Sakuta who lived that life;
3. returns to his own imperfect world even after being warned that everyone will still have forgotten him;
4. accepts Mai's recognition and care without treating her as the person who must solve the maternal wound;
5. names the anger and resistance hidden inside his "independence";
6. chooses to visit his mother himself;
7. recognizes that she, too, suffered because she did not run away from the impossible demand to save Hanakaede;
8. thanks her without requiring immediate reciprocity;
9. commits to returning even if she still cannot see him.

Only then does his mother say:

> `……咲太`

The first repaired unit is the **name**.

The climax does not say they "returned to being a family." It says:

> `一緒に泣いて、この日、咲太たちは家族になった。`

They **became** family that day.

That grammar is decisive. Biology preceded the crisis. Family status existed legally and genealogically. Yet the novel describes family as something that must also be relationally made.

This creates the volume's third thesis:

> **Family is both inherited and authored. Blood can create obligations and history; recognition, care, memory, and repeated return make those ties inhabitable.**

The word `ホーム` in Chapter 4 therefore reaches well beyond one address.

The Yokohama company apartment is the household from which Sakuta came. Yet when he and Hanakaede enter, their bodies register themselves as guests: `お邪魔します`.

Fujisawa, by contrast, is where Sakuta instinctively thinks:

> `帰るべき街。帰りたい家`

It is the life he built with Kaede/Hanakaede, Mai, school, work, friends, ordinary routines, and chosen commitments.

The resolution does not require him to declare one home false and the other true.

It allows the origin-family relationship to be repaired **without invalidating Fujisawa as the home his adulthood created**.

The compact V9 thesis is therefore:

> **Recognition without regression. Reconciliation without restoration.**

Or more fully:

> **Recovery does not mean returning to the life that existed before the wound. It means recognizing what the wound changed, keeping the adulthood and relationships built afterward, and still choosing whether to reopen a relation that once became impossible.**

# 3. Causal synopsis: what changes and why

## 3.1 March 1: the girl, Mai's graduation, and the first new scar

Volume 9 opens exactly where Volume 8 left the mystery.

At Shichirigahama, Sakuta meets the elementary-school girl he had previously encountered only in a dream. She resembles Mai not merely as "what Mai might have looked like as a child," but closely enough that Sakuta later recognizes mannerisms and expressions from Mai's actual child-actor drama.

The girl obeys a simple maternal safety rule:

> `知らないおじさんと話したらダメ`

Sakuta introduces himself so he is no longer, technically, an unknown man. When he asks her name, adult Mai arrives.

The girl disappears.

Her footprints stop beside Sakuta instead of leading away. Mai says that, from her perspective, Sakuta had been standing alone before she approached.

Two points are established immediately.

First, the girl's existence is not a simple shared perception.

Second, she appears to be difficult to perceive simultaneously with adult Mai.

No explanation is yet licensed.

That same day, Mai's high-school life closes. Her graduation initiates the book's larger concern with adulthood and family. Sakuta meets Mai's mother, and the novel deliberately refuses an easy reconciliation arc between mother and daughter.

Mai says:

> `仲直りなんてしてないわよ`

and:

> `嫌いよ`

This is one of the volume's most important ethical calibrations. Continuing relation does not require retrospective purification. Mai has not decided that what happened to her was acceptable. Her mother has not become a secretly ideal parent.

Mai instead explains that the dislike feels less total because her life now contains more things that matter. The negative feeling did not necessarily shrink. The field around it expanded.

This principle anticipates Sakuta's own family resolution.

The past does not have to be reclassified as good.

A life can become larger than the wound.

Mai then reveals why she has become more willing to interact with her mother. A younger child actor and her single mother made her think about family structure. The girl's mother reportedly wants her daughter to be "special" partly because the family lacks the conventional father-centered shape she imagines other children possess. Mai cannot simply condemn them because she can see mutual affection.

More importantly, Mai thinks forward:

> `自分が家族を持ったときに、家族のやり方がわからないと困るだろうから`

She is not seeking the recovery of a lost childhood. She is trying to understand what she may want to build later.

This introduces the volume's future-family counterpoint: **Mai and Sakuta are not merely surviving adolescent crises together; they are beginning to imagine adulthood as a shared project.**

At home, Sakuta discovers a new scar.

It is unlike the three chest scratches associated with prior crises. A white, fissure-like line runs from his right flank toward his navel.

The novel has not yet explained it.

## 3.2 The marriage registration as "amulet"

During an ordinary evening, Sakuta and Mai watch the first volume of Mai's debut drama `ここのえ`. The viewing confirms why the elementary-school girl disturbs him: she resembles the performed public record of child-Mai to an uncanny degree.

Mai then gives Sakuta a Fujisawa local-design marriage registration as a joke-serious `御守り`.

She writes `桜島麻衣`.

He writes `梓川咲太`.

This is not yet a proposal, engagement, or attempt to marry immediately. Indeed, the later chapter explicitly says otherwise. But the object becomes one of the novel's most important pieces of material evidence.

It is:

- bureaucratic;
- romantic;
- future-oriented;
- co-authored;
- tied to Fujisawa;
- and physically portable.

A document intended to create legal family later becomes a mnemonic anchor that helps preserve recognition now.

The object is therefore the perfect Volume 9 artifact: **chosen future family intervenes in a crisis produced by damaged origin family.**

## 3.3 Hanakaede chooses her mother

Sakuta's father calls to say their mother can receive home care and wants to see Hanakaede.

Hanakaede answers immediately:

> `会いたい`

> `お母さんに、会いに行きたい`

This matters after Volume 8.

Hanakaede's choice is not made by Sakuta.

She has already been learning to choose a school rather than inherit Kaede's dream. Now she chooses whether to reopen a family relation.

At the same time, Sakuta does something surprisingly restrained with Shoko.

He has both a new scar and a mysterious girl. Earlier Sakuta might have sought Shoko as impossible authority. Here he deliberately decides not to involve her. If she knows and did not warn him, perhaps the issue is not fatal. If she does not know, calling would burden a girl who deserves to live her own life.

His formulation is revealing:

> `牧之原さんは、自分の人生を謳歌するのに忙しいからな`

and:

> `誰よりも幸せになってもらいたいんだ`

Shoko has ceased to be "the future answer."

She is a person whose ordinary life now places a moral boundary around Sakuta's access to her.

## 3.4 Hanakaede's institutional and family future

Before the family reunion, several quieter developments complete Volume 8's educational arc.

Hanakaede personally states that she wants to attend the correspondence school:

> `私、ここに通いたい`

She applies online.

This is the practical continuation of:

> `行きたい学校は、自分で見つける`

The decision is no longer merely thematic. It becomes administrative action.

She graduates middle school. Her father attends. Mai also quietly supports her.

Shoko sends an ordinary letter and healthy photograph from Okinawa. The fact that Sakuta does **not** know exactly what is happening in Shoko's life is treated positively. Distance is no longer failure.

## 3.5 March 15: reunion succeeds too well

Hanakaede's reunion with her mother is emotionally tense but not catastrophic.

On the way, Hanakaede buys her mother's favorite beaker pudding. This immediately exposes one difference between the siblings: Sakuta realizes that Hanakaede knows a simple preference he never learned.

The meeting is warm.

Mother calls:

> `花楓`

Hanakaede calls:

> `お母さん`

She tells her mother about:

- school;
- Mai;
- the entrance examination;
- the correspondence-school choice;
- and the life that happened while the family was separated.

The meeting stretches into dinner.

Hanakaede stays overnight.

Sakuta's father gives him a key to the company apartment because he may need it in the future.

From one angle, the scene looks like family repair.

That appearance becomes the trap.

## 3.6 March 16: Sakuta is not ignored; he is unrecognized

The next morning, Sakuta goes to school and discovers that nobody perceives him.

The novel is careful about vocabulary.

Being **ignored** still requires someone to notice you and choose not to respond.

Sakuta is not being ignored.

His teacher skips his name. Tomoe, Rio, Kunimi, and the wider school move as though he is not present. Calls do not connect in the ordinary relational sense.

The condition recalls Volume 1, but the structure is different.

Mai was being erased through a school/social atmosphere from which people ceased to recognize her.

Sakuta's crisis points toward his mother.

He returns toward Yokohama.

On the way, he sees his mother and Hanakaede shopping together. Neither perceives him.

The successful reunion has produced a scene in which mother, father, and Hanakaede can visibly form a complete family tableau without Sakuta.

That possibility becomes psychologically unbearable.

## 3.7 The diary: truth gained through an ethical violation

Using the key his father gave him, Sakuta enters the apartment.

He finds his mother's private diary and reads it.

The content is load-bearing.

The mother repeatedly blamed herself during Hanakaede's bullying and breakdown:

- she could not protect her child;
- she saw herself as a failed mother;
- she wanted to become Hanakaede's mother again.

Her recent entry after the reunion says she wants to live again as **a family of three**.

Sakuta is absent.

The supernatural crisis and the diary converge.

But the scene should not be allowed to become ethically frictionless just because the information is narratively useful.

The key authorizes entry to the home.

It does not authorize reading a private diary.

Sakuta once again crosses an informational boundary in the name of solving a crisis. The result is sympathetic and causally important, but the method remains invasive.

The deeper shock comes after he reads.

He realizes his mother did not call his name the previous day.

He also realizes **he had not noticed**.

This is the book's true wound.

Sakuta thought he had "handled" the family fracture.

Instead, he had stopped expecting maternal recognition so effectively that its absence had become ordinary.

His own narration reclassifies the adaptation:

> `無意識に心から切り離していた。無自覚に切り捨てていた。`

He had to make a life without her.

Eventually "without her" became a condition he no longer examined.

## 3.8 The navel and Sakuta's causal hypothesis

Sakuta links the new abdominal scar to the navel:

> the place where he had physically been connected to his mother before birth.

This is one of the most explicit body-symbol alignments in the series.

He also forms a theory for the global erasure.

Perhaps the world is changing so that his mother's recognition is not "wrong." If she recognizes a family that does not include him, the world removes him from everyone else's recognition as well.

This is a powerful model.

It is **not a proven law**.

Rio has not established it. No objective narrator confirms it. The phenomenon's literal causality remains partly open.

What is undeniable is the narrative correspondence:

- maternal nonrecognition;
- global nonrecognition;
- umbilical scar;
- and the old family configuration.

## 3.9 "Family of three" and the problem of home

Sakuta sees his parents and Hanakaede eat together.

They look like a family.

He is not inside the frame.

He leaves and locks the apartment, internally linking the action to closing something inside himself.

But when he reaches Fujisawa, his body's orientation becomes clear.

Fujisawa is:

> the city he returns to;

> the place he should return to;

> the home he wants to return to.

That is the first major answer to Chapter 4's eventual title `ホーム`.

"Family home" and "home" are no longer synonymous.

The Yokohama apartment contains origin-family history.

Fujisawa contains the life Sakuta actually inhabits.

Repairing the former cannot ethically require annulling the latter.

## 3.10 The girl guides him to the "happy dream"

At night at Shichirigahama, Sakuta becomes psychologically close to the dark sea.

He says he wants to see Mai.

The elementary-school girl appears.

Unlike everyone in the primary world, she sees him.

She asks if he is lost:

> `おじさん、迷子？`

Sakuta answers that perhaps he is lost in life.

The girl takes his hand and says she will go home with him.

The physical warmth matters. Sakuta's body reenters the world through contact.

He boards the Enoden, expecting to return to Fujisawa.

He falls asleep.

He wakes in a different life.

# 4. The phenomenon: experience, mechanics, states, and explanatory limits

Volume 9 is one of the series' most complex phenomenon books because it contains several anomalies that may be related but should not be collapsed into one mechanism.

## 4.1 Phenomenon cluster A: the new abdominal scar

Textual facts:

- the scar is absent earlier and appears after the first waking encounter with the girl / Mai graduation day;
- it is white and fissure-like;
- it runs from the right side toward the navel;
- the area is numb;
- Sakuta connects the navel to maternal bodily origin.

The strongest interpretive reading is obvious: this scar encodes the damaged mother-son bond.

The literal mechanism remains unresolved.

It should therefore be classified as:

- **TF:** scar exists and is anatomically oriented toward the navel;
- **SI:** its narrative function concerns maternal origin/connection;
- **UA:** exact supernatural causal process.

The volume never directly states, in prose, that the scar vanishes after reconciliation. The later interlude says Sakuta experiences no further Adolescence Syndrome for the next year, which strongly suggests the crisis resolves, but the final physical state of this particular scar should not be invented.

## 4.2 Phenomenon cluster B: global nonrecognition

On March 16, Sakuta becomes unrecognizable to essentially everyone in the primary world.

This resembles Volume 1 formally but is not simply a repeat.

The exact sequence matters:

1. mother appears not to recognize Sakuta during the March 15 reunion;
2. Sakuta does not consciously notice this;
3. the next day, the school/world fails to recognize him;
4. mother and Hanakaede pass him without perceiving him;
5. the diary confirms mother imagines the restored household as a family of three.

Sakuta's own causal model is:

> maternal recognition is being universalized.

This is thematically strong and mechanically plausible within AoButa's established logic of perception, but remains an **SI/TI**, not a confirmed physical law.

## 4.3 Phenomenon cluster C: the adjacent possibility world

After following the mysterious girl, Sakuta wakes in an alternate/adjacent world.

Call the ordinary narrated world through Volume 9's opening **State A**.

Call the adjacent world **State B**.

### State A

- the Hanakaede crisis caused severe family fracture;
- mother required hospitalization / long-term treatment;
- Sakuta and Kaede/Hanakaede built an independent Fujisawa household;
- Sakuta chose Minegahara partly to create distance from middle-school social history;
- Akagi Ikumi is not part of his high-school daily life;
- by March 16, Sakuta is globally unrecognized.

### State B

- Sakuta's earlier response to Hanakaede's bullying diverged;
- the branch includes a remembered incident involving his occupation/seizure of the school broadcast room;
- the family remained together in Yokohama;
- mother, father, and Hanakaede recognize him as an ordinary family member;
- Hanakaede is headed toward Minegahara;
- Sakuta still attends Minegahara;
- Mai is still his girlfriend;
- Rio, Kunimi, Tomoe, and much of his social network still exist;
- Akagi Ikumi is a classmate in his Minegahara environment;
- the new abdominal scar remains on A-Sakuta's body.

Rio uses beakers to describe adjacent possible states and says:

> `過去や未来を含めたあらゆる可能性の世界は、いつもすぐ側にある`

The language is a useful narrative model.

As with earlier Rio explanations, it should not be mistaken for demonstrated real-world quantum physics.

## 4.4 The branch is not adequately reduced to "Sakuta's dream"

Several facts complicate a purely subjective fantasy reading.

The strongest comes after Sakuta returns to State A.

He finds handwriting from **another Sakuta**.

The note says, in effect, "get it together, other me," and leaves instructions involving Mai.

A second message is directed toward the Kirishima Toko mystery.

This strongly implies reciprocal displacement:

> while A-Sakuta inhabited State B, B-Sakuta apparently inhabited State A.

That makes the adjacent world textually stronger than an internal wish-fantasy.

The exact ontology remains open:

- branching universes?
- overlapping probability states?
- exchange between real possibilities?
- another AoButa-specific state model?

The novel does not require us to select one.

## 4.5 Reciprocity and nonappropriation

The reciprocal-Sakuta element creates an important ethical question.

If State B is real enough to have its own Sakuta, then B is not merely a reward-space offered to A-Sakuta.

It contains another person's:

- family repair;
- school history;
- friendships;
- reputation;
- obligations;
- and romantic life.

A-Sakuta repeatedly refuses to appropriate B-Sakuta's achievements.

When B-world mother praises "Sakuta" for protecting Hanakaede, A-Sakuta knows the praise belongs to the person who actually lived that history.

This is one reason his refusal of State B is ethically stronger than "I prefer my original timeline."

He recognizes that a more comfortable life is not automatically **his** life to possess.

## 4.6 The mysterious girl does not fit Rio's model

Rio explicitly notes that the girl remains unexplained.

The possibility-world model can account for alternative Sakutas and different outcomes.

It does not automatically explain:

- why the girl looks like child-Mai;
- why she first appeared in a dream;
- why adult Mai and the girl appear difficult to perceive together;
- why she alone can perceive Sakuta during global erasure;
- why she seems able to show him how to cross worlds;
- why she claims the actual ability is Sakuta's rather than hers.

This distinction must remain live.

## 4.7 The epilogue overturns Sakuta's temporary projection explanation

By the end of the main crisis, Sakuta can reasonably suspect that the girl was a manifestation connected specifically to his family Syndrome.

The epilogue weakens that explanation from inside Volume 9 itself.

One full year passes.

The text states:

> `あれ以来、思春期症候群になることはなかった。`

Yet at his university entrance ceremony, Sakuta sees the same elementary-school figure again.

Therefore:

> **The mysterious girl is not adequately explained as a temporary by-product of the March family crisis.**

Her exact identity remains **UA**.

This is not a later-volume retrospective correction. It is a Volume 9 internal correction to Sakuta's own provisional theory.

## 4.8 Kirishima Toko becomes a cross-world marker

After returning, A-Sakuta finds B-Sakuta's question:

> `霧島透子をどう思う？`

Volume 8 had already established that Kirishima Toko's current cultural presence did not exist in the futures Shoko remembered.

Volume 9 now connects the name to another Sakuta from another possible world.

The evidentiary status should remain restrained:

- **TF:** B-Sakuta asks about Kirishima Toko.
- **SI:** Kirishima's significance differs across world/history states.
- **UA:** what she is, what caused the divergence, and whether she is causally involved in world-crossing.

# 5. Sakuta: the son hidden inside the caretaker

Volume 9 may be Sakuta's most important psychological volume so far.

The series has long shown him as:

- protective older brother;
- boyfriend;
- friend;
- crisis responder;
- blunt truth-teller;
- person willing to spend his body for others.

Volume 9 asks a simpler and more destabilizing question:

> **What happened to Sakuta as a son?**

## 5.1 The competence that concealed grief

Sakuta has spent years converting crisis into function.

His mother became unavailable.

Kaede needed him.

So he learned what had to be learned.

That makes him look mature.

Mai later validates that growth, but the novel first strips away the heroic self-description.

Sakuta did not calmly choose adult independence.

He says:

> `母さんがいなくても、大丈夫になるしかなかっただけ`

He **had** to become capable.

This distinction matters.

There is a difference between:

- chosen independence;
- developmental independence;
- and forced self-sufficiency under family breakdown.

Sakuta's skills are real.

The necessity that produced them was still harmful.

## 5.2 The failure to notice his own exclusion

The mother diary scene reveals the most painful effect of forced self-sufficiency.

Sakuta can notice:

- other people's pain;
- Kaede's fear;
- Mai's loneliness;
- Rio's contradiction;
- Shoko's self-erasure.

Yet he does not notice that his mother has not said his name.

He has trained his perception away from his own need.

This is consistent with the Sakuta established across earlier volumes: he is often perceptive about others and less ethically generous toward himself.

The volume therefore continues the V6 proposition that his own life must count, but moves from physical life to **relational need**.

His right to live in V6 becomes his right to admit:

> I wanted my mother to see me.

## 5.3 Sakuta's responsibility reflex is tested

At first, he worries that Hanakaede's reunion could retraumatize her.

This is understandable.

It is also his familiar reflex to imagine himself as the person responsible for managing everyone's outcome.

Miwako subtly redirects him by asking:

> `咲太君は、お母さんに会いたい？`

The question is almost radical in Sakuta's emotional grammar.

Not:

- what does Hanakaede need?
- what should father do?
- what would be safest?
- what is the correct intervention?

But:

> **What do you want?**

Sakuta answers that he may be afraid.

This is a movement toward self-inclusion.

## 5.4 Not using Shoko is moral development

The decision not to call Shoko deserves emphasis.

Earlier, older Shoko functioned as:

- comfort;
- ethical mentor;
- future knowledge;
- impossible rescue.

Sakuta now refuses to treat her as a resource.

He wants her to be busy living.

That is one of the clearest signs that his care ethic has matured from **reciprocal debt** toward **respect for another person's independent life**.

## 5.5 The temptation of State B

State B is dangerous not because it is obviously false.

It is dangerous because it is good.

In that world:

- mother makes lunch;
- family breakfast is ordinary;
- `いってきます` receives `いってらっしゃい`;
- Hanakaede did not lose two years in the same way;
- Sakuta's family remained coherent;
- he still has Mai;
- he still has Rio and Kunimi;
- familiar social bonds remain.

It is almost an optimized version of the life he might have wanted.

Rio calls it:

> `居心地のいい可能性の世界`

That phrase, `居心地`, links directly to the volume's home argument.

A good home should be habitable.

But a place can be **too** comfortable if living there requires appropriating another self's history.

## 5.6 "Failure" and authorship

Sakuta compares himself with B-Sakuta and calls the primary-world version a possible `失敗作`.

The thought is revealing.

B-Sakuta appears to have made one crucial earlier choice "better" and prevented more damage.

The novel refuses to let this become an optimization fantasy.

Sakuta decides:

> `失敗作なら失敗作でも構わない。失敗作なりに精一杯生きればいい。`

This is not an argument that failure is intrinsically valuable.

It is an argument against **retroactive replacement of the self**.

A-Sakuta cannot undo his history by possessing B-Sakuta's.

He can only become responsible for the life actually formed through his own decisions.

The ethics are therefore:

> **authorship matters more than timeline optimization.**

## 5.7 Refusing B-Sakuta's praise

The phone call with B-world mother is one of the best demonstrations of this principle.

She thanks "Sakuta" for being a good older brother and protecting Hanakaede.

A-Sakuta understands that the praise belongs to B-Sakuta.

He does not let the shared body/name turn another self's action into his own moral capital.

This is a subtle continuation of the identity problems from Volumes 3–5:

> same-looking person, same name, or same body does not automatically transfer authorship.

## 5.8 Returning while still forgotten

The mysterious girl warns him:

> `あっちに帰っても、みんなおじさんのこと忘れてるよ`

Sakuta returns anyway.

This is the ethical center of the possibility-world movement.

He does not return because suffering proves authenticity.

He returns because:

- his Mai is there;
- his promises are there;
- his Hanakaede is there;
- his friendships are there;
- his mother's unresolved relation is there;
- and those relationships are his to face.

He says:

> `母さんのことは、自分でなんとかするよ`

That line should not be read as a return to solitary saviorism.

The rest of the volume immediately corrects that risk.

He will face the mother himself.

But he does so after receiving:

- the girl's guidance;
- Rio's analysis;
- B-Sakuta's information;
- Mai's recognition;
- Mai's care;
- and the relational confidence built across eight prior volumes.

"Doing it himself" here means **not outsourcing the decision**, not doing it without support.

## 5.9 Planting ordinary existence

When Sakuta returns and everyone except Mai still fails to perceive him, he does something intentionally unheroic.

He goes to school.

He studies.

He follows routine.

He behaves as though ordinary life deserves to be preserved even before the world confirms him.

This is a major development.

Earlier Sakuta often responds to crisis by escalating:

- staying awake;
- public confession;
- night travel;
- reckless biking;
- running toward danger.

Here he also uses **continuity itself** as resistance.

He "plants" the fact of his existence in ordinary routine.

That is a more sustainable form of self-assertion.

## 5.10 Recognizing his mother before being recognized

The hospital climax completes Sakuta's ethical movement.

He finds his mother alone.

His rehearsed speech disappears.

What emerges is:

> `母さん、がんばったんだ……`

This line joins one of the series' most important care chains.

Older Shoko taught Sakuta forms such as:

- `よくがんばりました`
- `よく言えました`

Sakuta used similar permission with young Shoko and Kaede.

Now he turns the language **upward**, toward the parent who failed him.

He recognizes that his mother broke not because she did nothing, but because she tried to carry something she could not successfully carry.

The novel's narration makes the distinction explicit:

> she suffered because she did not simply flee;
> effort itself exceeded what her mind could survive.

This does not erase the consequences.

Hanakaede still suffered.

Sakuta was still parentified.

The family still fractured.

Compassion is not exoneration.

Then Sakuta says:

> `母さん、ありがとう`

Crucially, he does this **before** receiving recognition.

He is willing to leave and come back:

> if she cannot see him today, he will return until she can.

This is relation without coercive demand.

Only then:

> `……咲太`

The mother names him.

He becomes visible first at the place where he had become invisible.

# 6. Mai and the continuing central relationship

Mai is not the "solution" to Sakuta's family crisis.

That is precisely why her role is so strong.

Volume 9 gives the romance a new function:

> **Mai becomes the person with whom Sakuta can imagine making a future family without requiring her to substitute for the origin family that failed.**

## 6.1 Mai and her mother: reconciliation without purification

Mai's own maternal relationship provides the first model.

She does not claim to have forgiven or "made up" with her mother.

She says she still dislikes her.

The emotional change is not moral reversal.

Other valued things have become more numerous.

This offers Sakuta a model he later needs:

> a damaged relation can become smaller relative to the total life without the original injury being declared acceptable.

## 6.2 Future family as a deliberate project

Mai's thought about not knowing "how to do family" if she later creates one with someone is unusually adult.

She is asking about:

- domestic norms;
- parenthood;
- inherited family models;
- and whether a person can build differently from what she received.

That question becomes the romance's new horizon.

The marriage registration then gives that horizon a physical form.

## 6.3 The marriage registration is neither joke nor proposal

The document's ambiguity is important.

Sakuta and Mai fill in their names.

Mai calls it an `御守り`.

They are clearly imagining one another inside a possible legal future.

But later, Mai explicitly refuses the idea that:

> `いつか、ふたりで家族になろう`

is already a proposal.

She tells Sakuta that a proper proposal should come when he has achieved real social independence.

This protects the romance from collapsing into teenage fusion.

Their future is serious.

It is also deferred.

Adulthood includes material and social conditions, not merely emotional intensity.

## 6.4 Mai restores recognition but does not take over

After Sakuta returns to the primary world, he leaves a handwritten letter in Mai's mailbox.

He later goes to the Minegahara field, where Volume 1's public recognition climax occurred.

Mai comes directly to him.

She sees him.

She runs.

She embraces him.

The location is a deliberate rhyme with Volume 1, but the relation has changed.

In Volume 1, Sakuta publicly asserted Mai's existence against a social world that had forgotten her.

In Volume 9, Mai becomes the first person to recover Sakuta.

The direction of recognition reverses.

The novel strongly associates this recovery with the signed marriage registration—the co-authored future-family document Mai has physically kept—and Sakuta's new handwritten letter.

The exact supernatural mechanism remains uncertain.

The relational function does not.

Mai remembers because their bond has been materially and behaviorally enacted, not only emotionally believed.

## 6.5 "Let's become family someday"

Mai says:

> `……いつか、ふたりで家族になろう`

This is one of the most important lines in the romance so far.

It is not:

> I will replace the family that failed you.

It is:

> we may build another family in the future.

That difference protects Sakuta's mother as a separate relationship he still has to confront.

Mai can offer a future home.

She cannot rewrite his childhood.

## 6.6 Mai names the hidden resentment

At home, Sakuta finally says what his independence cost.

He had to forget his mother enough to function.

The restored mother now threatens the emotional architecture of the life he created.

His thought contains an ugly but human:

> `なんで、今さら`

Why now?

Why after he already learned how not to need this?

Why should a "good" recovery destabilize a life built around surviving its absence?

Mai does not shame him.

She says:

> `咲太はそれでいいのよ`

and then:

> `そういうのをね、大人になったって言うのよ`

This is emotionally necessary, but analytically complicated.

The generous reading:

- Mai validates the adulthood Sakuta actually built;
- reconnection does not require pretending that adulthood was a mistake;
- anger toward late restoration is compatible with love.

The critical reading:

- forced parentification should not be romanticized as simply "becoming adult";
- Sakuta's competence emerged under unfair conditions.

The strongest synthesis is:

> **Mai validates the person Sakuta became without validating the conditions that forced him to become that person so quickly.**

## 6.7 "I'm waiting"

The next morning, Sakuta decides to face his mother.

He asks Mai whether she will be all right alone.

She says, in effect, that she will wait.

Not:

> "Try your best."

Not:

> "I'll come and solve it with you."

Not:

> "You shouldn't go."

But:

> `待ってるから`

This is perhaps the most mature care line in the volume.

Mai gives him:

- relational security;
- a future return point;
- confidence that leaving temporarily will not dissolve the bond.

It closely resembles the Volume 8 idea that Hanakaede's exam includes returning to the home where Sakuta waits.

The care roles now circulate.

People take turns being the one who leaves and the one who remains available.

# 7. Hanakaede and the family: sisterhood after the sister arc

Hanakaede is not the title character of Volume 9, but her choices make the crisis possible.

## 7.1 Her reunion is genuinely successful

The novel does not sabotage Hanakaede's courage to create Sakuta's drama.

Her mother sees her.

Hanakaede sees her mother.

They talk.

They eat.

They spend time together.

Hanakaede stays overnight.

This is a real good.

Sakuta's later crisis does not retroactively make her reunion a mistake.

That ethical separation is important.

## 7.2 Hanakaede knows her mother differently

The beaker pudding scene is small but sharp.

Hanakaede knows a favorite food Sakuta does not.

That is a form of relational memory.

Sakuta may have taken on practical household responsibilities, but practical caregiving did not make him omniscient about every family member.

Hanakaede's daughter-mother relation contains knowledge that is not reducible to Sakuta's protector role.

## 7.3 Hanakaede's guilt remains active

On the way to the reunion, Hanakaede worries that her mother may resent or reject her.

She knows the family crisis followed her bullying and breakdown.

Even when she rationally knows she was not morally responsible for being victimized, causal sequence can become guilt.

Volume 9 therefore continues the series' distinction:

> **causal involvement is not moral blame.**

Sakuta helps name the fear without declaring it irrational.

## 7.4 Final family scene: Hanakaede joins rather than mediates

At the hospital, the decisive mother-son repair occurs before Hanakaede returns.

This is structurally correct.

Hanakaede is not asked to fix Sakuta's relation with mother.

When she returns, she sees them crying and cries too.

The final family sentence emerges from **shared emotion**, not from one sibling serving as translator between the other and the parent.

# 8. The mother, father, and three models of motherhood

Volume 9 is unusually dense in its treatment of motherhood.

It does not offer one "correct mother."

Instead, it creates several different failures and partial successes.

## 8.1 Sakuta's mother: maternal identity overwhelmed

The diary reveals a woman whose sense of being a mother became inseparable from her inability to stop Hanakaede's suffering.

She repeatedly interprets inability as moral failure.

The danger is not lack of care.

It is care experienced as total obligation.

When she cannot solve the child's pain, she interprets herself as unfit for the role.

This is psychologically different from abandonment through indifference.

It is also not harmless.

Her collapse removes her from both children and pushes Sakuta into premature household responsibility.

The novel's mature position is therefore neither condemnation nor absolution:

> **she tried, she broke, the break harmed others, and all four facts can remain true.**

## 8.2 Mai's mother: motherhood intertwined with career and image

Mai's mother is different.

Her relationship with Mai was damaged through professional boundary violations and control.

Mai still says she dislikes her.

Yet Mai is willing to interact with her because the relationship no longer occupies the whole emotional field.

This model asks whether a mother can be recognized as a flawed adult without requiring the daughter to relabel harmful behavior as loving.

## 8.3 Rio's mother: refusal of child-centered identity

In State B, Rio introduces another model when discussing her own mother.

She describes her as a person who, in a sense, refused to make "being mother" the center of her life.

Rio does not simply claim that this means her mother hates her.

The issue is that her mother preserved:

- career;
- selfhood;
- adult priorities;
- and a life not organized around the child.

Rio still suffered loneliness from that structure.

The text thus triangulates motherhood:

- totalizing motherhood can break a parent;
- instrumentalizing/control-oriented motherhood can injure a child;
- preserving adult selfhood can leave a child insufficiently accompanied.

No simple ideology resolves the problem.

## 8.4 Father: present, caring, still delegating

Sakuta's father is active:

- he coordinates the reunion;
- researches schooling;
- returns home;
- gives Sakuta a key;
- supports the family's logistics.

Yet the broader family history still shows substantial responsibility having shifted onto Sakuta.

His line of entrusting Hanakaede to Sakuta has been a recurring structural problem.

Volume 9 improves his presence but does not erase the long-term delegation.

# 9. The mysterious girl: guide, double, or something else?

The cover and title encourage the reader to treat the knapsack girl as the central supernatural problem.

The novel consistently refuses to stabilize her.

## 9.1 What is textually established

She:

- resembles Mai during her child-actor period;
- first appeared to Sakuta in a dream;
- then appears in waking reality;
- is initially not seen by adult Mai;
- disappears without a normal trail of footsteps;
- can see Sakuta while the world cannot;
- physically touches him;
- guides him toward the adjacent possibility world;
- says the crossing ability is fundamentally Sakuta's;
- knows he may remain forgotten after returning;
- reappears a year later after no further reported Adolescence Syndrome.

## 9.2 What is not established

Volume 9 does **not** establish that she is:

- literally a younger Mai;
- Mai from another world;
- Sakuta's inner child;
- a personification of his Syndrome;
- a future child;
- a deity;
- Kirishima Toko;
- or any other later-theory identity.

All such claims remain unsupported inside the Volume 9 boundary.

## 9.3 Why "child Mai" is especially tempting and especially unsafe

The resemblance is not incidental.

The novel makes it stronger by having Sakuta compare her with recorded child-actor Mai.

The editorial profile page labels her `???` and explicitly foregrounds the resemblance.

But resemblance is evidence of relation, not identity.

The novel repeatedly preserves the difference.

That uncertainty must remain intact.

# 10. State B: the ethics of a happier possibility

The possibility-world section is not merely a mechanism puzzle. It is a moral counterfactual.

## 10.1 The branch gives Sakuta what he thought he lost

State B contains an ordinary family life.

This is not presented as obviously fake or sinister.

The mother packs lunch.

Sakuta can discuss lunch quality.

He can leave home with `いってきます`.

His mother answers.

These are intentionally mundane.

The novel understands that the deepest fantasy for a traumatized family is not luxury.

It is **forgettable normality**.

## 10.2 Ordinary dialogue is the missing family substance

Earlier, Miwako asks Sakuta what he wants from his mother.

He realizes he cannot imagine some grand statement.

Most mother-son communication was never memorable enough to become an "important scene."

That is the point.

Family is often built from exchanges too trivial to archive.

The State B phone call gives Sakuta a rehearsal in precisely that grammar:

- lunch;
- rice texture;
- fried chicken;
- croquettes;
- gratitude.

The primary-world reconciliation later succeeds without a grand scripted speech because he has rediscovered the language of ordinary relation.

## 10.3 A better outcome does not erase authorship

State B appears to have avoided much suffering.

That does not mean A-Sakuta is ethically required to stay.

Why?

Because the benefits are attached to a life another Sakuta actually lived.

A-Sakuta's choice becomes a defense of personal authorship:

> I may be the worse result, but I am responsible for this result.

This is an important anti-utilitarian pressure inside the book.

If the only metric were current comfort, State B might win.

If persons have histories that cannot be freely swapped, staying becomes more complicated.

## 10.4 The novel does not romanticize suffering as proof of reality

A weaker reading would say:

> A-Sakuta rejects the happy world because pain makes life authentic.

That is not quite the text.

He rejects it because the primary world contains:

- his actual acts;
- his actual failures;
- his actual care;
- his specific Mai;
- his specific Hanakaede;
- the relationships formed through choices he remembers making.

The issue is not that pain is morally superior.

The issue is that **another self's good life cannot be claimed merely because it would feel better**.

# 11. Japanese voice, address, and translation-sensitive language

Volume 9 is unusually dependent on ordinary Japanese family language.

## 11.1 `家族`

The word changes referents across the book.

Possible formations include:

- mother + father + Hanakaede = `家族三人`;
- the earlier four-person biological family;
- Sakuta + Mai as a possible future family;
- Sakuta's Fujisawa relational network;
- the final hospital scene in which they "become family."

The same word therefore covers:

- genealogy;
- household;
- imagined configuration;
- legal future;
- emotional recognition.

The final `家族になった` works because the novel has spent the entire book demonstrating that these dimensions do not automatically coincide.

## 11.2 `母さん` and `お母さん`

Sakuta's address is `母さん`.

Hanakaede uses `お母さん`.

The distinction is ordinary but useful. The siblings do not collapse into one shared linguistic relation with the parent.

## 11.3 `咲太`

The mother's restored use of Sakuta's given name is the climax.

In a book about nonrecognition, **naming is re-entry into relation**.

The name arrives before any supernatural exposition.

That is appropriate for a series whose Volume 1 resolution also depended upon person-specific recognition.

## 11.4 `見える`, `認識`, `無視`

The novel carefully separates:

- being visible;
- being cognitively recognized;
- being socially responded to;
- being deliberately ignored.

This continues the series' increasingly sophisticated recognition vocabulary.

## 11.5 `帰る`

`帰る` and `帰る場所` do major thematic work.

The mysterious girl promises to go home with Sakuta.

State B offers one possible home.

Sakuta says he knows where he needs to return.

Fujisawa is named as the place he wants to return to.

Mai waits there relationally even when physically elsewhere.

"Return" becomes an ethical orientation rather than a geographical reversal.

## 11.6 `居心地`

State B is `居心地のいい`.

Sakuta later says it is almost **too** comfortable.

This is not contempt for comfort.

It is suspicion toward a comfort that asks him to live inside another self's authored history.

## 11.7 `がんばった`

The care word migrates again.

The series has used "you did well / you tried hard" to give permission for vulnerable people to stop converting suffering into obligation.

Sakuta now says it to his mother.

The phrase does not mean:

> everything you did was correct.

It means:

> I recognize that you were struggling as a person.

That distinction is central.

## 11.8 `ありがとう`

Sakuta's `ありがとう` to his mother is not repayment.

It is gratitude disentangled from idealization.

He can thank her for:

- giving birth;
- raising him;
- recovering enough to exist in relation again;

without claiming she never failed him.

## 11.9 `今さら`

The emotional force of "why now?" is difficult to translate if rendered too neutrally.

It contains:

- resentment;
- exhaustion;
- disbelief;
- fear of destabilization;
- guilt for resenting a good event.

It is one of the most psychologically honest words in the volume.

## 11.10 `大人になった`

Mai tells Sakuta that his complicated adaptation counts as having grown up.

The phrase should not be flattened into simple praise.

The novel immediately shows the "adult" crying like a child.

Maturity here includes being able to admit the child need that adulthood had covered over.

## 11.11 `待ってるから`

Mai's phrase is compact.

It offers:

- continued bond;
- permission to leave;
- expectation of return;
- no demand to perform bravery for her.

It is an interdependent alternative to "I'll fix it" and "handle it alone."

## 11.12 `もうひとりの僕`

The counterpart language deliberately mirrors earlier multiple-self problems.

The other Sakuta is not called an impostor.

He is another `僕`.

The volume again uses first-person language to preserve identity ambiguity without eliminating personhood.

## 11.13 `失敗作`

Sakuta's self-description as a "failed product" is harsh.

His rejection of the implication matters more than the insult itself.

He refuses the idea that one can rank whole selves as successful/failed according only to outcome quality.

# 12. Home, place, season, objects, and formal motifs

## 12.1 Shichirigahama as threshold

Shichirigahama has accumulated unusual density across the series.

It is associated with:

- older Shoko;
- horizon imagery;
- Mai;
- crises of recognition;
- transitions between present and possible future;
- the mysterious girl.

In Volume 9 it becomes a threshold where Sakuta is:

- recognized by the girl;
- lost;
- guided;
- transferred between states;
- and later chooses his route back.

## 12.2 Fujisawa as lived home

Fujisawa's importance is not based on birth.

Sakuta made a life there.

The apartment contains:

- Kaede/Hanakaede;
- memories of crisis;
- Mai's repeated presence;
- routines;
- work;
- school commute;
- study;
- food;
- ordinary intimacy.

The novel's home ethic is therefore experiential:

> **home can be made after origin-home stops functioning.**

## 12.3 Yokohama apartment: technically family, experientially guest

When Sakuta and Hanakaede enter the parents' company housing, they feel the bodily grammar of visiting.

They say `お邪魔します`.

The smell is not their own.

A key can grant access without producing belonging.

This is an excellent material distinction between:

- legal/familial relation;
- practical access;
- embodied home.

## 12.4 Hospital: the site of failed motherhood becomes the site of mutual recognition

The mother spent years in medical space after collapse.

Sakuta's final visit brings him into the physical site of her vulnerability.

He does not confront a symbolic "mother."

He encounters a finite person in a small room.

That spatial reduction matters.

The parent becomes visible as human.

## 12.5 The key

The father's key is ambivalent.

It says:

> you may need this home again.

Yet Sakuta uses it both to enter and later to close the door.

The key permits movement between family configurations.

It does not decide where he belongs.

## 12.6 The diary

The diary is one of the strongest objects and one of the ethically most difficult.

It contains:

- maternal self-condemnation;
- longing;
- recent family imagination;
- Sakuta's omission.

Its content reveals truth.

Its acquisition violates privacy.

AoButa again refuses to make information ethically neutral.

## 12.7 The marriage registration

The local marriage registration is the counter-object to the diary.

The diary is:

- private;
- unilateral;
- backward-looking;
- discovered without consent;
- marked by Sakuta's absence.

The marriage registration is:

- co-authored;
- future-oriented;
- voluntarily shared;
- marked by both names.

The first reveals a family Sakuta is absent from.

The second materially records a family he may help create.

That formal opposition is among the volume's strongest.

## 12.8 The counterpart note and letter

B-Sakuta leaves documentary evidence across the world boundary.

A-Sakuta then rewrites the Mai letter in his own handwriting rather than simply delivering the counterpart's version.

The content is nearly the same.

The authorship is not.

This is an elegant small-scale enactment of the volume's selfhood argument:

> **another self can help you without replacing you.**

## 12.9 Lunch and croquettes

Food carries ordinary family recognition.

State B's lunch becomes a rehearsal space for maternal-son conversation.

Primary-world croquettes form the three-person tableau that excludes Sakuta.

The mother later recovers not through a dramatic meal but through hospital recognition.

Food therefore represents the ordinary domestic world Sakuta misses rather than a magical cure.

## 12.10 Spring and the one-year skip

The interlude is deliberately indifferent:

- people cry;
- summer arrives;
- people laugh;
- autumn arrives;
- people study;
- winter arrives.

Time is not a reward mechanism.

It moves.

After several volumes obsessed with futures that might disappear, Volume 9 allows one full year of ordinary temporal continuity.

Then the university epilogue proves that mystery also continues.

# 14. Institutions, social atmosphere, and material adulthood

Volume 9's supernatural story is inseparable from ordinary institutions.

## 14.1 Graduation and the end of role-based identity

Mai graduates.

Hanakaede graduates.

Sakuta is about to enter his final high-school year.

The book is full of roles ending:

- senpai;
- middle-school student;
- dependent child;
- hospitalized mother;
- protected sister;
- high-school girlfriend as purely adolescent relationship.

The institutional calendar forces psychological transition.

## 14.2 Correspondence school becomes concrete

Hanakaede applies to the school she chose.

Volume 8's autonomy becomes paperwork.

This is important to the series' growing interest in documents:

> choice is not complete when felt; it becomes socially real when translated into institutional form.

## 14.3 Marriage registration: law as future possibility

The local marriage form is legal bureaucracy used as intimate symbolism.

The series has often treated institutions as potentially oppressive social structures.

Here bureaucracy also offers a neutral form through which two people can imagine chosen family.

It is not automatically romantic because the government makes it valid.

Its meaning comes from the people who voluntarily write their names.

## 14.4 University as the next developmental institution

The epilogue moves Sakuta into university without giving him an empty reset.

He brings:

- Mai;
- Nodoka;
- family history;
- the mysterious-girl problem;
- and a middle-school relationship he had forgotten.

The university is therefore not "new life, old story over."

It is a new social institution into which unresolved history arrives.

## 14.5 Material independence and Mai's proposal standard

Mai's insistence that a real proposal should come after Sakuta becomes socially independent prevents the romance from treating love as economically weightless.

This is notable after Sakuta's earlier conversation with his father about university costs.

Adult partnership requires:

- desire;
- responsibility;
- future planning;
- material capacity.

The novel does not reduce marriage to those factors, but it refuses to erase them.

# 15. Ethics and philosophical implications

## 15.1 Recognition is morally prior to correction

The hospital climax shows Sakuta recognizing his mother before asking her to recognize him.

He sees:

- effort;
- failure;
- pain;
- recovery;
- personhood.

Only then does he say thank you.

This reverses the logic of grievance without deleting grievance.

## 15.2 Compassion does not erase responsibility

The mother's breakdown becomes understandable.

That does not make its consequences acceptable.

This distinction should remain firm:

> **explanation is not exoneration.**

Sakuta can recognize why she broke while also acknowledging that he and Hanakaede paid a price.

## 15.3 Forced growth can become real growth

One of the book's hardest questions is whether growth produced by unfair necessity is somehow false.

The answer is no.

Sakuta's independence is real.

His competence is real.

His Fujisawa life is real.

What should be rejected is the reverse claim:

> because the growth was real, the conditions that forced it were good.

They were not.

Volume 9 allows a more difficult moral sentence:

> **something valuable can grow from harm without making the harm valuable.**

## 15.4 Family relation is not ownership

Mother does not own Sakuta's adulthood because she gave birth to him.

Sakuta does not own his mother's recovery because he suffered during her absence.

Mai does not own Sakuta's family decisions because she loves him.

Hanakaede does not owe her mother a specific form of life because she returns.

The repaired family is possible because each relation becomes more voluntary, not because blood is ignored.

## 15.5 The ethics of alternative worlds

State B raises a problem rarely handled with sufficient seriousness in "better timeline" stories.

If another timeline contains another person who is meaningfully you, can you ethically take over that life?

Volume 9's answer trends toward no.

A-Sakuta accepts help from B-Sakuta.

He does not claim B-Sakuta's accomplishments.

This is a personhood-preserving multiverse ethic.

## 15.6 Privacy and rescue

Sakuta's diary violation must remain in the ledger.

He has a recurring tendency to treat access to information as justified when a relationship is at stake.

The volume's sympathetic framing does not eliminate the boundary problem.

The result was useful.

The method was still invasive.

## 15.7 Receiving care

Sakuta has often been better at giving care than receiving it.

V6–V7 made this explicit around life and sacrifice.

V9 translates the lesson into family and romance.

He allows:

- the girl to guide him;
- Rio to interpret;
- B-Sakuta to leave help;
- Mai to remember him;
- Mai to hold him;
- Mai to wait.

His final action toward mother is personal, but not solitary in origin.

# 16. Counterreadings, limitations, and hypothesis stress-test

## Counterreading A — "The mother forgot Sakuta because Sakuta had emotionally cut her off"

### Supporting evidence

- Sakuta admits he stopped thinking about his mother's condition;
- he had emotionally separated her from the life he needed to manage;
- he did not notice she had failed to call his name;
- the phenomenon correlates with that buried relation.

### Complicating evidence

- the mother herself explicitly imagines a family of three;
- the global erasure begins from her recognition failure as much as Sakuta's;
- no omniscient mechanism confirms direction of causality.

### Judgment

**TI / live hypothesis, not law.**

The phenomenon dramatizes mutual relational disconnection. It should not be reduced to "Sakuta caused himself to be forgotten."

---

## Counterreading B — "The navel scar proves a literal maternal-bond mechanism"

### Supporting evidence

- the scar runs toward the navel;
- the book foregrounds the navel's prenatal mother-child connection;
- the crisis centers on maternal recognition.

### Complicating evidence

- no explicit physical rule is established;
- scars in AoButa have carried layered, not single-cause meanings.

### Judgment

**Strong symbolic SI; literal mechanism UA.**

---

## Counterreading C — "State B is only a dream"

### Supporting evidence

- Sakuta falls asleep before entering;
- the chapter title invokes a happy dream.

### Counterevidence

- B-Sakuta apparently occupies A;
- notes/letters remain across the exchange;
- Rio treats the world as an adjacent possibility rather than pure imagination;
- A-Sakuta obtains information he did not consciously author.

### Judgment

The pure-dream reading is **too weak**.

Exact ontology remains UA, but State B is textually more substantial than private fantasy.

---

## Counterreading D — "State B is objectively better, so Sakuta is irrational to leave"

### Supporting evidence

- family remains intact;
- ordinary maternal relation exists;
- Hanakaede's crisis appears less destructive;
- Sakuta retains Mai and friends.

### Complicating evidence

- the life is B-Sakuta's authored history;
- A-Sakuta cannot honestly appropriate B-Sakuta's achievements;
- his primary-world relationships remain morally real;
- "better outcome" does not erase identity/history.

### Judgment

The novel rejects simple welfare optimization across selves.

Sakuta's choice is ethically coherent even if State B contains better outcomes.

---

## Counterreading E — "Sakuta chooses suffering because suffering makes life authentic"

### Problem

This romanticizes harm.

### Better reading

Sakuta chooses **authored relation**, not pain.

He returns to a world containing pain because it also contains the commitments for which he is actually responsible.

---

## Counterreading F — "Mai saves Sakuta"

### Supporting evidence

- she is the first primary-world person to recognize him again;
- she provides emotional safety;
- the marriage registration appears to help preserve memory.

### Complicating evidence

- Mai does not resolve the mother-son relationship;
- she explicitly waits while Sakuta goes himself;
- Sakuta must choose the confrontation.

### Judgment

Mai is co-rescuer and secure base, not substitute protagonist.

---

## Counterreading G — "The marriage registration proves love literally defeats Adolescence Syndrome"

### Problem

This turns a nuanced memory/recognition object into sentimental mechanics.

### Better reading

The document is a **strong material recognition anchor**.

The exact supernatural causal pathway remains unresolved.

---

## Counterreading H — "Calling forced independence adulthood justifies the family failure"

### Supporting concern

Mai uses `大人になった` to validate Sakuta's post-crisis competence.

### Complicating evidence

- Sakuta explicitly says he had no choice;
- he breaks down emotionally;
- the novel acknowledges resentment.

### Judgment

The line is best read as validation of Sakuta's present self, not moral approval of the conditions that produced it.

---

## Counterreading I — "The mother is noble because she tried too hard"

### Problem

This risks sanctifying collapse and ignoring the children's costs.

### Better reading

Her effort makes her legible and worthy of compassion.

It does not erase the damage caused when the family lost a functioning parent.

---

## Counterreading J — "Reading the diary is justified because it solves the mystery"

### Judgment

No.

Narrative usefulness does not create consent.

The privacy breach remains an ethical overreach.

---

## Counterreading K — "Family repair means the old four-person household should be restored"

### Counterevidence

- Fujisawa is Sakuta's lived home;
- the Yokohama apartment feels like a place he visits;
- the novel never requires immediate cohabitation;
- final repair is relational recognition, not housing reversal.

### Judgment

**Rejected.**

---

## Counterreading L — "The mysterious girl is literally child-Mai"

### Counterevidence

- no name confirmation;
- editorial profile preserves `???`;
- Rio says she does not fit the possibility-world model;
- she reappears after the main Syndrome crisis is over.

### Judgment

**UA. Resemblance is fact; identity is not.**

---

## Counterreading M — "The girl is only Sakuta's inner child"

### Supporting evidence

- she guides him through a family-origin crisis;
- she uses childlike/home language;
- she may externalize lost childhood needs.

### Counterevidence

- she has independent unexplained behavior;
- Rio cannot fit her into Sakuta's model;
- she reappears one year later.

### Judgment

Useful thematic TI, inadequate literal explanation.

---

## Counterreading N — "B-Ikumi's implied feelings prove current-world Ikumi feels the same"

### Judgment

**Forbidden extrapolation.**

The two histories differ.

Volume 9 establishes current-world Akagi's identity and reunion, not her motive.

---

## Counterreading O — "Kirishima Toko causes the possibility worlds"

### Judgment

Unsupported.

Her name is a cross-world discrepancy marker, not yet an established cause.

---

## Counterreading P — "Sakuta's abdominal scar definitely disappears"

### Judgment

Not directly stated.

The Syndrome stops recurring for a year, but the precise final scar state remains **UA**.

# 17. What Volume 9 changes in the series-so-far model

The cumulative progression can now be expressed as:

| Volume | Governing problem | Series proposition |
|---:|---|---|
| 1 | Recognition | Visibility does not guarantee recognition; one person can resist social erasure by truly knowing another. |
| 2 | Construction | A deliberately made self can still be genuine; adaptation becomes dangerous when it requires self-erasure. |
| 3 | Integration | Contradictory desires do not have to be expelled into separate selves. |
| 4 | Differentiation | A person does not need to defeat or imitate the person against whom she was compared. |
| 5 | Continuity | One body can contain severe autobiographical discontinuity without making either lived continuity meaningless. |
| 6 | Self-valuation | Another person's life can matter absolutely without making one's own life morally disposable. |
| 7 | Interdependence | Love requires shared burden, not unilateral sacrifice or protection. |
| 8 | Inheritance | Another self's life and wishes may be inherited without becoming jurisdiction over the successor. |
| **9** | **Home / adulthood / family** | **A broken relation can be repaired without restoring the old self or old household; family is made through renewed recognition among people changed by the rupture.** |

The strongest new formula is:

> **reconciliation without restoration.**

A related identity proposition:

> **The self produced by survival does not have to disappear when the emergency ends.**

A related family proposition:

> **Biological origin creates history; mutual recognition makes relation livable.**

A related home proposition:

> **Home is not necessarily the place from which one came. It is the place to which one can return without surrendering the self one became.**

A related possible-world proposition:

> **A happier possible life is not automatically a more authentic or morally claimable life.**

A related care proposition:

> **Mature care includes the ability to wait while the other person faces a relationship you cannot face for them.**

# 22. Final synthesis

Volume 9 is the end of the high-school movement in a deeper sense than graduation chronology.

It closes the period in which Sakuta could understand himself primarily as:

- Kaede's older brother;
- Mai's younger boyfriend;
- Rio and Kunimi's classmate;
- the person who handles the next crisis;
- the teenager who already knows how to live without his parents.

The book takes the final identity apart.

Sakuta's apparent independence had been built partly from a necessity he could not admit as loss.

He did not simply decide:

> I no longer need my mother.

He learned:

> I cannot function if I keep waiting for a mother who is not available.

That strategy worked.

Then recovery made the strategy visible.

The possibility world gives Sakuta the most tempting answer imaginable: a life in which the family did not fracture in the same way, his mother makes lunch, Hanakaede is safer, and much of what he values is still present.

The novel refuses to call that life fake.

That is what makes rejecting it meaningful.

Sakuta does not choose the primary world because suffering is noble.

He chooses it because people are historical beings.

His love for Mai is not interchangeable with B-Sakuta's love for Mai.

His relation to Hanakaede is not interchangeable with the relation produced by a different intervention.

His mother cannot be repaired by borrowing the mother-son history another Sakuta made.

And another Sakuta's successful action does not become his moral achievement because they share a face and name.

He returns.

Then the novel gives him a second temptation: let Mai become enough.

She sees him.

She remembers him.

She offers a future family.

She holds him while he cries.

That could become another form of avoidance.

Instead, Mai waits.

Sakuta goes to his mother.

The mother-son repair succeeds because he finally does two things that his old savior ethic made difficult:

1. he treats his own need as morally real;
2. he recognizes another person's pain without making recognition depend upon immediate repayment.

He sees his mother.

Then she sees him.

The exchange is not symmetrical in timing.

That is why it works.

The final sentence:

> `この日、咲太たちは家族になった`

does not mean the prior years were fake.

It means kinship alone was not enough to make the relation inhabitable after catastrophe.

They have to **become** family again as changed people.

This is the mature continuation of Volume 8.

Hanakaede did not honor Kaede by becoming Kaede.

Sakuta does not honor his origin family by becoming the pre-crisis Sakuta.

Mai does not prove love by replacing his mother.

The mother does not regain motherhood by pretending she never broke.

Every successful relation in Volume 9 depends upon continuity **without substitution**.

That is why `ホーム` is the right final chapter title.

Home is not a reset point.

It is not where time rewinds.

It is where a changed person can return and still be recognized as the person he has become.

And just when the novel seems to have closed this entire logic—one quiet year, no new Syndrome, university beginning—the red-scarf girl runs through the new campus.

The family crisis is over.

The girl is not.

The high-school story can end.

The unresolved architecture underneath the phenomena has only become more visible.
