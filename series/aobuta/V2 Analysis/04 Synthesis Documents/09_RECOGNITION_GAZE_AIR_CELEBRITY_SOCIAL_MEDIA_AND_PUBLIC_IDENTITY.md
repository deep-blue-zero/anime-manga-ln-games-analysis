# 09. Recognition, Gaze, Air, Celebrity, Social Media, and Public Identity
## Who becomes real when everyone agrees?


# 1. Governing question
> **How does AoButa move from the schoolroom `空気` of Volume 1 toward celebrity, networked consensus, mass observation, public identity, and the final distinction between seeing and believing?**

The central thesis is:

> **AoButa treats recognition as a social relation rather than a sensory fact. A body can be seen without the person being recognized; a public role can be recognized while the person disappears; a crowd can collectively recognize the wrong identity; and an adult can recognize a person's testimony without sharing her perception.**

The series' recognition arc develops through four scales.

1. **Interpersonal:** Does one person acknowledge another?
2. **Group/social:** What does the room, school, or family treat as real?
3. **Public/institutional:** What do media, records, crowds, and institutions count?
4. **Epistemic/ethical:** Can testimony be taken seriously without shared sight?

This progression connects Volume 1's invisible Mai to Volume 15's Ebina Rin.


# 2. Recognition is not attention
Mai is famous before the first volume begins. Fame does not protect her from nonrecognition.

People can:

- know her image;
- know her name;
- know her career;
- look at her body;

and still fail to acknowledge:

- her current agency;
- her privacy;
- her school presence;
- her first-person experience.

The first volume therefore separates:

> **attention** — directed gaze or public awareness;

from:

> **recognition** — treating the historically particular person as socially and ethically real.

That distinction remains governing through Nene/Toko and Mai/Toko.


# 3. `空気` in Volume 1
`空気` is social authority without a clear author.

No single student has to declare:

> Mai does not exist.

The room learns how not to acknowledge her.

Sakuta's line:

> `もう、空気なんて読んでやるか`  
> — `JP-001`

is an act of resistance against anonymous jurisdiction.

It does not mean:

> social awareness is always bad.

Volume 10 later proves the opposite. The problem is that “the room” can become an authority nobody individually owns yet everyone obeys.

The literal disappearance makes the social logic impossible to deny:

> what the room treats as absent becomes absent to perception, memory, and record.


# 4. Sakuta's public confession: restoration and coercion
The confession helps restore Mai because it forces collective attention toward a person the school has learned to ignore.

Its ethical and mechanical meanings should remain separate.

## Mechanically

Public attention coincides with restoration.

## Ethically

Sakuta uses his own social standing to make the group confront the person it has excluded.

## Boundary tension

He also makes Mai's private relationship publicly legible without a fully negotiated process.

The scene therefore establishes Sakuta's recurring intervention profile:

- courageous;
- effective;
- intrusive.

Later volumes do not negate the success. They complicate whether attention is always the right instrument.


# 5. Peer gaze, self-fashioning, and Tomoe
Tomoe's arc asks what happens when the gaze is anticipated so intensely that it governs self-construction and future choice.

Her made Tokyo self is not counterfeit. The problem is not that she cares what others think.

The problem is that anticipated public judgment can become more authoritative than her own desire.

The series therefore rejects two extremes:

- complete submission to the group;
- a fantasy of a self untouched by social life.

Tomoe's self is socially constructed and genuinely hers.

The adult question becomes:

> Can social information remain input without becoming jurisdiction?


# 6. Rio and the sexualized gaze
Rio's duplication makes the gaze bodily.

One Rio performs visibility online in a way the other finds shameful. The phenomenon externalizes a contradiction:

- wanting to be seen;
- fearing what being seen means;
- rejecting the desiring self as foreign.

The important recognition act is not Sakuta choosing the modest Rio as “real.” It is his refusal to classify the sexualized/needful Rio as someone else.

This becomes a recurring rule:

> **Recognition must include the part of a person she would prefer to place outside herself.**


# 7. Mai/Nodoka — public body is not private person
The crossed embodiment case demonstrates how easily public gaze follows the body and role rather than the person.

Cameras, strangers, and professional assumptions recognize:

> Mai's appearance.

Sakuta recognizes:

> the person inhabiting the situation through voice, biography, and relationship.

This is the first explicit separation among:

- body recognition;
- role recognition;
- autobiographical recognition.

It becomes the template for later public-identity crises.


# 8. Kaede/Hanakaede — gaze, trauma, and the right not to be compared
Hanakaede's fear is not merely being looked at. It is being socially classified through a history she cannot safely inhabit.

The body reacts to:

- school uniforms;
- same-age peers;
- evaluative attention;
- institutional comparison.

In Volume 8, Hanakaede also fears that others preferred Kaede. Recognition becomes dangerous if it means:

> you are accepted only to the extent you reproduce the predecessor.

Sakuta's:

> `どっちも普通だ`  
> — `JP-015`

refuses hierarchy.

The bonuses then show gaze becoming manageable under supported conditions. At the zoo, Hanakaede can participate while still initially hiding behind Sakuta. At work, she can disclose vulnerability and request overlapping shifts.

Recognition here becomes accommodation rather than forced normalization.


# 9. Mai as celebrity: maximum visibility, unstable personhood
Mai's career proves that more visibility does not automatically produce more recognition.

Celebrity creates:

- intense gaze;
- public projection;
- career opportunity;
- loss of privacy;
- interpretive ownership by strangers.

Her public identity is productive and dangerous.

She can author professional performance while audiences still treat the performance as access to her whole self.

The series repeatedly protects a private Mai through:

- domestic scenes;
- travel;
- food;
- family interaction;
- relationship objects;
- the difference between stage jewelry and Sakuta's ring.

These scenes do not reveal the “real Mai” underneath a fake public persona. They show that one person can inhabit professional visibility without being exhausted by it.


# 10. Volume 8 — institutions can embody `空気`
The correspondence-school plot makes recognition institutional.

Hanakaede's academic capacity is not identical to institutional legibility. Attendance history and internal-school evaluation make one pathway appear normal and another deficient.

The alternative school asks a different question:

> What structure permits this student to participate?

The series' recognition ethic therefore moves from:

> see the individual

into:

> design institutions that can count different forms of participation.

This becomes crucial in Volume 13, where police literally cannot count bodies they cannot perceive.


# 11. Uzuki — reading the room is not the same as obeying it
Volume 10 gives the most important correction to a simplistic anti-`空気` reading.

Uzuki gains social attunement. This helps her:

- understand sarcasm;
- notice emotional cues;
- repair relationships.

The capacity becomes harmful when the anonymous social average speaks continuously inside her.

Sakuta's line:

> `今こそ空気を読めよ、づっきー`  
> — `JP-002`

means:

> read the concrete people who have chosen you.

The final distinction is:

> **social awareness is valuable; anonymous social sovereignty is dangerous.**


# 12. Ikumi — recognition of role versus recognition of person
Both worlds can accept the wrong Ikumi because the occupying counterpart performs the role successfully.

This creates a recognition failure hidden by social smoothness.

Everyone says:

> Akagi Ikumi is here.

The first-person continuity says:

> the wrong life is being recognized.

Sakuta's:

> `赤城、見っけ`  
> — `JP-003`

restores particular recognition through traits, history, and relational specificity.

The case proves:

> **socially successful role recognition can still be personal erasure.**


# 13. Sara — recognition does not grant interior access
Sara's clairvoyance exposes another recognition error:

> if I know what you think, I cannot be humiliated by misrecognition.

But total access would eliminate the other's opacity.

Sara's refusal:

> `知りたくない`  
> — `JP-006`

establishes a mature boundary.

Recognizing a person does not create entitlement to:

- thoughts;
- sensory perspective;
- private feeling;
- complete certainty.

This principle later governs Sakuta's refusal to read Miori and Toko's diary without relational authorization.


# 14. Nene/Toko — prestige identity recognized, person erased
Nene's case is the clearest example of recognition attaching to the wrong object.

The public recognizes:

> Kirishima Toko.

The public loses:

- Iwamizawa Nene's name;
- history;
- romantic relation;
- body;
- vocational biography.

The Toko role is culturally dense and biographically open. Many people can occupy it.

Nene's:

> `何者かであるって思いたいの！`  
> — `JP-019`

reveals why the role is attractive. It provides immediate social consequence.

Recovery requires recognition of the failed, ambitious, particular person—not simply removal of attention.

The trophy, Takumi's memory, Nene's own confession, and Sakuta/Ikumi's acknowledgment restore biography.


# 15. Invisible bodies and institutional miscounting
The Santa crowd gives the series' strongest literal social-ontology scene.

Police and spectators cannot perceive many bodies.

They therefore:

- interpret occupied space as open;
- compress the crowd;
- misjudge risk;
- allow a barrier failure.

This produces a general proposition grounded in literal mechanics:

> **A system that cannot recognize people will still be materially affected by the people it fails to count.**

Recognition is no longer only emotional validation.

It is infrastructure.


# 16. `#夢見る` and networked consensus
The dream network changes social knowledge in three stages.

## 16.1 Personal report

A person posts a dream.

## 16.2 Collective comparison

Many reports converge around one event.

## 16.3 Public expectation

People act as though the future identity is settled.

The April crowd arrives expecting Mai=Toko. The event becomes larger because it is known in advance.

Networked recognition therefore does not merely describe reality. It helps constitute the social conditions under which the predicted event occurs.


# 17. Mai/Toko — collective recognition as identity overwrite
Volume 1 showed collective nonrecognition making Mai absent.

The final arc shows collective positive recognition making the wrong identity present.

The crowd chants Toko.

Mai performs Toko.

Mai privately and sincerely experiences:

> she is Toko.

This is not a secret biography exposed. Current Mai had sincerely denied the identity.

The public correction works through another collective recognition event:

- Miori performs;
- Miori unveils her face;
- the complete song restores Toko/Miori attribution;
- Mai can again say she is not Toko.

Recognition can therefore:

- erase;
- restore;
- overwrite;
- reattribute.


# 18. Smartphones and recognition infrastructure
The smartphone motif should not be reduced to technological determinism.

Early Sakuta associates the device with:

- hostile peer communication;
- ambient judgment;
- loss of control over social meaning.

Miori discards hers when grief messages impose a socially legible bereaved identity.

Later, networked circulation makes `#夢見る` and collective expectation possible.

But the finale refuses:

> smartphone ownership causes Syndrome.

Sakuta and Miori both buy phones and remain themselves.

The final distinction is:

> **connection under chosen jurisdiction versus connection as ambient social command.**

The mechanical clue remains partly unresolved (`CLM-026`).


# 19. Miori — observer-specific visibility and friendship
After the Toko correction, Miori becomes invisible to everyone except Sakuta.

This likely relates to her cross-world status and Sakuta's observer role.

The ethical point is clearer than the mechanism.

Sakuta continues to treat Miori as a friend despite public nonperception.

He writes his number on her palm.

The relationship does not require the crowd to certify her.


# 20. Resemblance is not recognition
The series closes two identity loops through restraint.

## 20.1 Red-randoseru girl

She resembles young Mai. Sakuta does not force a literal identity upon her.

## 20.2 Final station lookalike

A stranger resembles Miori. Sakuta checks:

- presence;
- mole;
- reciprocal recognition.

He accepts she is not Miori.

Adult recognition resists substitution.


# 21. `みんな` becomes an object of inquiry
Sakuta's research question:

> `みんなってなんだろうね`  
> — `JP-012`

is the intellectual endpoint of the `空気` arc.

As an adolescent, Sakuta experiences “everyone” as ambient force.

As an adult, he asks:

- Who is included?
- How is consensus inferred?
- What textual traces produce “general opinion”?
- When does the aggregate acquire illegitimate authority?

He does not solve social life by permanently withdrawing.

He studies it.


# 22. Ebina — recognition without sight
Ebina asks whether Sakuta can see the person she sees.

He answers:

> `僕には見えない。でも、君を信じるよ`  
> — `JP-007`

This line must be read carefully.

Sakuta does not say:

> the ghost objectively exists exactly as described.

He says:

> Ebina's testimony is credible enough to deserve attention.

This creates a mature middle position between:

- dismissal;
- over-certification.

The final recognition ethic is:

> **Take the historically particular person seriously before ontology is settled.**

The normative limits of this response—authority, invitation, and non-coercive care—are treated in Document 13 §25.


# 23. Recognition taxonomy
| Type | Question | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Sensory recognition | Can I perceive the body/state? | Sakuta sees Mai. |
| Mnemonic recognition | Do I remember the person/history? | Takumi remembers Nene. |
| Autobiographical recognition | Which continuity is this? | Sakuta identifies counterpart Ikumi. |
| Relational recognition | What history binds us? | Sakuta/Miori friendship; family repair. |
| Public recognition | What identity does the crowd attribute? | Mai=Toko. |
| Institutional recognition | Can systems count/respond? | Invisible Santa crowd; teacher Sakuta. |
| Epistemic recognition | Can I believe testimony without shared perception? | Ebina. |


# 24. Refined recognition taxonomy
Recognition in AoButa should be analyzed across at least six dimensions.

| Dimension | Question | Failure example | Mature form |
|---|---|---|---|
| sensory perception | can the body/image be seen or heard? | Mai becomes imperceptible; Santas are unseen | adult Sakuta admits he cannot see Ebina's ghost |
| attentional salience | is the person noticed? | Kaede fears the gaze; celebrity Mai receives excessive attention | attention is directed without assuming ownership |
| identity attribution | who is this person believed to be? | Nene becomes Toko; R1 Mai becomes Toko | Miori's face/voice and Toko's authorship are publicly distinguished |
| autobiographical recognition | is this history recognized as belonging to this subject? | alternate Ikumi accepted as current Ikumi | `赤城、見っけ`; particular history matters |
| social legibility | can institutions and groups count and respond to the person? | invisible Santas produce crowd danger | teacher/institution makes room for Ebina's testimony |
| epistemic trust | is testimony taken seriously without full corroboration? | Sakuta's middle-school history; Ebina | `僕には見えない。でも、君を信じるよ` |

The final dimension is the series' mature endpoint. It does not replace the others. A person still needs bodily safety, records, names, and social access. But epistemic trust is what prevents uncertainty from becoming abandonment.


# 25. Gaze, gender, and the franchise's own visual tension
The prose repeatedly criticizes social and sexualized observation while the illustrations sometimes participate in the same economy.

Important examples include:

- Mai's bunny costume, both a strategy to test visibility and a marketable image;
- Tomoe illustrations that amplify body-focused jokes;
- Ikumi's message-bearing body presented through eroticized framing;
- the Santa/Toko costume that makes Nene highly consumable to the reader while she is socially invisible inside the story.

The strongest conclusion is not that the series' gaze politics are wholly incoherent. It is that they are **formally conflicted**.

The prose often asks:

> Who controls the meaning of this body?

The visual market often asks:

> How can this body remain appealing to the audience?

A final synthesis should preserve this friction rather than treating visual fanservice as analytically irrelevant or as proof that every recognition argument fails.


# 26. Publicity, privacy, and person-specific authority
Mai's celebrity makes her the clearest example of why recognition must be bounded.

Too little recognition produces V1 invisibility.

Too much interpretive recognition produces the Toko crisis, where millions of apparent witnesses believe they know a hidden truth about her identity.

The relevant distinction is:

> **public interest does not create public ownership of the person.**

Mai can be:

- publicly visible;
- professionally accountable;
- and still retain first-person authority over current identity.

The dream cluster complicates the evidence but does not abolish that authority. A possible Mai's declaration belongs first to that possible continuity.


# 27. Smartphones and networks: substrate, not villain
The refined account avoids both technological determinism and technological innocence.

Smartphones and social platforms provide:

- rapid consensus formation;
- ambient judgment;
- searchable records;
- viral identity attribution;
- mass comparison of dreams;
- and pressure to perform recognizable grief or sociality.

They also provide:

- chosen contact;
- ordinary friendship;
- video calls;
- music;
- Sakuta's research data;
- and Miori's promise of future reachability.

The ethical variable is not possession of a device. It is:

> **whether the person can choose participation and retain authority over how networked recognition enters her life.**


# 28. Recognition and power
Not every recognizer has equal standing.

- A crowd can make a person visible and still misidentify her.
- A lover can recognize a particular history and still overstep privacy.
- A teacher can listen without certifying a metaphysical explanation.
- A platform can make a role legible while erasing its occupant.
- A medical or school institution can provide support only after the person enters its field of legibility.

Recognition should therefore be judged by:

1. accuracy;
2. standing;
3. consent;
4. practical consequence;
5. capacity to preserve difference;
6. openness to correction.


# 29. Refined social-recognition thesis
AoButa is not anti-social. Its characters are socially constituted, and the final goods are shared:

- friendship;
- family;
- institutions;
- work;
- romance;
- public art.

Its target is **unowned consensus**—the authority of `空気` or `みんな` when no particular person accepts responsibility for the judgment and the subject has no room to answer.

The adult response is neither obedience nor withdrawal. It is reflective participation:

> ask who “everyone” is, how the conclusion formed, whose testimony is missing, and what the conclusion permits others to do.


# 30. Strongest counterarguments
## “Recognition is just attention”

Mai's fame, Nene's viral attention, and Toko prestige disprove this.

Attention can intensify misrecognition.

## “Public recognition is always emancipatory”

V1 can invite this reading.

V13–15 reject it. The crowd can make a person visible as the wrong person.

## “The series is anti-social and anti-smartphone”

Early Sakuta's withdrawal supports the reading.

The finale complicates it through chosen phone use, friendship, research, and institutional care.

The target is unexamined social jurisdiction, not society itself.

## “Believing Ebina is irresponsible credulity”

Sakuta does not certify the ghost. He offers listening and support.

His stance is epistemically modest.

## “Recognition is purely interpersonal”

The Santa crowd proves it is also institutional and material.

# 33. Controlled recognition taxonomy lock
The following hierarchy is now fixed for later character documents.

1. **Attention** — being looked at, discussed, followed, desired, or made famous.
2. **Visibility** — being perceptible as a body or image.
3. **Recognition** — being acknowledged as a particular person/continuity.
4. **Social legibility** — being countable and interpretable by institutions or groups.
5. **Epistemic trust** — having one's testimony taken seriously without full shared perception.

The hierarchy is not a ladder in which more is always better. Mai begins with enormous attention and insufficient recognition. Nene acquires a highly legible prestige role while losing autobiographical personhood. Ebina receives epistemic trust even though Sakuta lacks sensory access.


# 34. Japanese lexical refinement
The series' social ontology is carried by several terms that should remain distinct.

- `空気` — ambient social atmosphere; authority without one accountable speaker.
- `認識` — recognition/apprehension of person or state; broader than memory.
- `観測` — quasi-scientific observation vocabulary; does not certify physics.
- `見える` — sensory/perceptual access.
- `信じる` — trust/belief directed toward a person or testimony.
- `みんな` — “everyone,” often functioning as an unexamined aggregate.

The final move from `見える` to `信じる` is therefore not rhetorical decoration. It shifts the ethical basis of care from privileged perception to disciplined trust.


# 35. Recognition and coercion: strengthened counterevidence
Recognition is not automatically benevolent.

## 35.1 Public confession

Sakuta's school-wide declaration restores Mai's public legibility, but it also uses spectacle and exposes both students. The scene is ethically mixed: courageous refusal of `空気`, effective recognition, and coercive public pressure coexist.

## 35.2 Celebrity

Mai demonstrates that a person can be maximally visible while other people claim authority over her body, work, and hidden identity. Fame solves anonymity and intensifies misrecognition.

## 35.3 Prestige identity

Nene's Toko state shows that the group may recognize a role so successfully that the bearer disappears. This is not failure of attention. It is overconfident recognition of the wrong thing.

## 35.4 Institutions

The invisible-Santa crowd demonstrates that social legibility has material consequences. Police and spectators do not merely hurt feelings by failing to perceive people; they miscalculate physical space and risk.

# 37. Phase-3 recognition lock
> **AoButa is not opposed to social awareness. It is opposed to treating anonymous consensus as if it possessed automatic jurisdiction over a person's identity. Mature recognition means seeing the particular person where possible, preserving institutional room where perception fails, and believing testimony without converting belief into ownership of the person's explanation.**


# 38. Final synthesis
AoButa begins with a famous girl who cannot be recognized.

That contradiction contains the whole series.

Fame is not personhood.

Attention is not recognition.

A body is not a biography.

A role is not a self.

A crowd's certainty is not first-person authority.

A record is not the whole memory.

And shared sight is not the only basis for belief.

The series enlarges `空気` from one classroom into:

- school reputation;
- celebrity;
- family expectations;
- follower metrics;
- public Toko;
- mass dreams;
- crowd behavior;
- rewritten reality;
- “general opinion.”

Its final answer is not to become immune to other people.

Mai wants shared scenery.

Miori wants a friend she can call.

Sakuta buys a phone.

He studies social consensus.

He enters a school as an adult.

The answer is participation with reflective boundaries.

The boy who once said he would stop reading the room becomes a teacher who can read the person without requiring the room to agree.

That is why the final line of recognition is adversative:

> I cannot see it.
>
> But I believe you.
