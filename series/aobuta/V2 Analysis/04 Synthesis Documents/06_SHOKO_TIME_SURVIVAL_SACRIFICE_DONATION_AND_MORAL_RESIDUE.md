# 06. Shoko: Time, Survival, Sacrifice, Donation, and Moral Residue
## The future person who teaches Sakuta to count his own life

## Governing question

> **How does Shoko transform the series' treatment of futurity from prediction into responsibility, and what moral residue remains when one person's survival depends upon another person's possible death?**


# 1. Executive thesis
Shoko is not merely the mysterious first love who explains Sakuta's scars. She is the character through whom AoButa turns possible futures into morally significant lives.

Her arc begins from denied futurity.

A girl with severe heart disease cannot confidently imagine becoming older. The future therefore returns in embodied form: an older Shoko who has lived through outcomes the younger girl cannot yet reach. Those possible futures contain real relationships, memories, gratitude, grief, and sacrifice.

The series' decisive move is to refuse two opposite reductions.

## Reduction one

A future that is later overwritten never mattered.

## Reduction two

Because a future was experienced, the current world is morally obligated to preserve it.

AoButa instead treats possible persons as having **moral standing without automatic jurisdiction**.

Future Shoko's love and kindness matter. Her possible marriage to Sakuta matters. The futures in which Sakuta or Mai dies matter. Yet Shoko ultimately chooses a history in which the future self who loved Sakuta may never exist in the same form.

The completed arc therefore yields five linked propositions.

1. **Wanting to live is morally speakable even when another person may bear the cost.**
2. **Survival need not be morally pure to remain worth choosing.**
3. **A donor's body can save another person without transferring donor identity.**
4. **Causal participation in another person's death is not the same as culpability.**
5. **A possible person can continue shaping the present through memory even when her world is not preserved.**

Shoko teaches Sakuta to count his own life among the lives that matter. The final series then returns the lesson: future Shoko's kindness remains inside him without requiring that the impossible older Shoko remain visibly present forever.


# 2. The first Shoko: remembered kindness before explanation
Sakuta initially knows Shoko through memory.

She is the apparently older girl who met him during his own crisis and gave him a vocabulary of care:

- `ありがとう`
- `がんばったね`
- `大好き`

Before the series explains who she is, Shoko functions as an internal moral ancestor. Sakuta's willingness to help others is partly an attempt to continue the kindness he received.

This matters later because the final solution—turning Syndrome into memory—does not make future Shoko irrelevant. Her phrases have already entered Sakuta's character.


# 3. Current Shoko: a child whose future is medically uncertain
The younger Shoko introduces a different scale of vulnerability.

Her future is not merely socially difficult. It is physiologically scarce.

She cannot take for granted:

- high school;
- adulthood;
- marriage;
- ordinary plans.

The school assignment that asks students to describe a future therefore becomes almost cruel. Adolescence normally treats the future as open possibility. Shoko experiences it as a demand to imagine what her body may not permit.


# 4. `わたしだって、生きたいんです`
Young Shoko initially tries to protect Sakuta from grief by asking him not to visit.

This is another form of self-erasure disguised as care.

Sakuta tells her:

> `もうがんばらなくていいんだよ`

She finally says:

> `わたしだって、生きたいんです`

The line is the emotional center of Volume 6.

Shoko has treated desire for survival as dangerous because survival requires:

- a donor;
- another family's loss;
- medical scarcity;
- hope that may fail.

The series validates the desire without pretending the cost disappears.


# 5. Older Shoko: future person rather than simple time traveler
The older Shoko should not be modeled as one uncomplicated adult traveling backward along one fixed timeline.

Across Volumes 6–8, she carries memories associated with multiple future outcomes. Current Shoko later says:

> `わたしだけはずっと覚えていました`

The strongest final model is:

> older Shoko represents genuinely experienced possible future continuities whose information becomes accessible through dream/world-state phenomena.

This preserves her personhood without forcing one simplistic topology.


# 6. Sakuta's scars and the donor future
Future Sakuta dies in a traffic accident on December 24. He is declared brain-dead. His donor card permits organ transplantation. Future Shoko receives surgery on December 27.

Older Shoko tells Sakuta:

> `翔子さんの胸には、僕の心臓があるんですね`

and:

> `わたしは、咲太君に未来をもらったんです`

Rio's duplicate-heart model links the scars to two versions of Sakuta's heart existing across the relevant state relation.

The exact physics remain character theory. The moral structure is explicit:

> Sakuta's death can become Shoko's future.


# 7. Gratitude becomes sacrificial temptation
Sakuta's response is not only fear.

It is gratitude.

Shoko's earlier kindness saved him emotionally. The future offers him a way to repay that debt physically.

The danger is that he turns himself into a moral instrument:

> she gave me a future;
>
> therefore my heart should give her one.

This reasoning makes self-destruction feel like completion rather than loss.


# 8. Mai's standing inside Sakuta's sacrifice
Mai refuses the idea that Sakuta's death is a private gift he may decide alone.

> `ひとりで決めないでよ`

> `だから、一緒に背負う`

Her objection does not negate Shoko's claim to life. It adds another claim.

Sakuta's future also belongs relationally to:

- Mai;
- Kaede/Hanakaede;
- family;
- friends;
- Sakuta himself.

The ethical problem cannot be solved by assigning total moral value to the most medically urgent person.


# 9. `生きていたい`: Sakuta counts his own life
Sakuta finally says:

> `生きていたい`

Shoko answers:

> `よく言えました`

This exchange reverses the apparent moral hierarchy.

Shoko, the person whose survival may require Sakuta's death, gives him permission to value himself.

The scene refuses the idea that altruism must culminate in self-erasure.


# 10. Mai's death and the second donor future
The story then produces a more terrible future.

Mai pushes Sakuta out of the path of the car and dies. Adult Shoko in that future carries Mai's heart.

The consequence is not presented as justice or romantic purity.

Sakuta survives inside a world where:

- Mai is dead;
- Shoko lives;
- future relationships reorganize around the loss;
- gratitude is inseparable from grief.

The series therefore blocks any easy ranking in which one donor future is obviously morally correct.


# 11. Future Shoko as possible spouse
One future Shoko marries Sakuta.

The relationship matters because it demonstrates that possible-world persons are not merely data points. She has:

- history;
- intimacy;
- desire;
- ordinary married life;
- memory of Mai's death.

Yet she does not claim romantic entitlement over current Sakuta.

She says:

> `大好きな人には幸せになってほしい`

The future relationship is real enough to grieve and not sovereign enough to require preservation.


# 12. Shoko's relinquishment
Shoko eventually proposes:

> `わたしと咲太さんが出会わない未来を作るために`

This is not proof that their encounter lacked value.

It is Shoko accepting that preserving the future self who loves Sakuta would require maintaining a causal chain of death and sacrifice.

The choice is profound because it may erase:

- her future memories;
- her marriage;
- the adult person she became.

Possible-person standing therefore does not mean possible-person priority.


# 13. World revision and moral residue
Volume 7 refuses to describe the surviving outcome as morally clean.

Sakuta says the result is not simply right.

The world has been changed through:

- love;
- fear;
- self-preservation;
- sacrifice;
- possible-person loss.

The phrase `ありがとう` recurs across the final schedule because gratitude becomes the alternative to moral purification.

No one can make the whole chain innocent.

They can acknowledge what was received.


# 14. Current Shoko remembers
Volume 8 reveals that current Shoko retains memories from multiple future-experiencing selves.

This gives her a unique burden.

Other people may retain:

- impulses;
- habits;
- affective residue.

Shoko retains explicit possible-world memory.

Her continuity is therefore unusually plural. She is a current child whose autobiography includes lives she will not simply go on to reproduce.


# 15. Ordinary Shoko as the real victory
After the crisis, the most important Shoko scenes are mundane.

- She eats a large chocolate parfait.
- She moves to Okinawa.
- She makes friends.
- She sends letters.
- She complains about heat.
- She encourages Sakuta's exams.

The miracle has become appetite and school.

This is not anticlimax. It is the endpoint the earlier future assignment denied her.


# 16. `いってらっしゃい` rather than farewell
Sakuta and Shoko do not use absolute goodbye language.

They use:

- `いってらっしゃい`
- `いってきます`

The phrases frame separation inside ordinary continuation.

Shoko leaves.

She has a life elsewhere.

The relationship no longer requires impossible co-presence.


# 17. Toko as donor: the final donor history
Volume 14 reveals that current Shoko's heart came from Kirishima Toko, a sixteen-year-old girl killed in a traffic accident.

This reclassifies the donor architecture.

The public Toko identity did not exist in Shoko's remembered futures because the current history emerged after the earlier fatal future was changed.

The causal chain is complex:

- Sakuta and Mai survive;
- Shoko still requires a heart;
- Toko dies and donates;
- Miori later performs Toko's songs;
- the public Toko identity enters history.

The series does not prove that one specific earlier choice mechanically “caused” Toko's death. It establishes downstream difference.


# 18. Organ donation without identity transfer
Toko's heart beats in Shoko.

The novels do not say:

> Toko is alive as Shoko.

That would erase both persons.

Organ donation creates:

- bodily continuity across persons;
- gratitude;
- causal entanglement;
- contact between recipient and donor family.

It does not create autobiographical continuity.

This is consistent with the series' larger identity model: body matters, but body alone does not settle personhood.


# 19. Donor-family contact and bounded care
Shoko communicates with Toko's family through an organ-donation support organization.

She writes regularly without demanding response.

Toko's mother reads the letters for years before agreeing to meet.

The father does not participate in the meeting.

The narrative permits all these positions.

- Shoko may feel gratitude.
- The mother may feel gratitude and grief simultaneously.
- The father may remain unable or unwilling to face the encounter.

Recipient survival does not create entitlement to the bereaved family's emotional availability.


# 20. Survivor guilt and the butterfly effect
Shoko worries that changing the future may have produced Toko's death.

The logic is understandable.

A different set of decisions changes:

- routes;
- meetings;
- timing;
- donor history.

Sakuta invokes the butterfly effect to reject simple backward blame.

The literary point is not that chaos theory scientifically proves innocence.

It is:

> **causal chains this complex cannot support confident moral assignment to one tiny antecedent event.**


# 21. Causal implication versus culpability
Three statements can coexist.

1. Shoko's current survival depends materially on Toko's death.
2. Earlier future changes may belong to the causal history that produced this donor outcome.
3. Shoko is not culpable for Toko's death.

Culpability requires more than causal participation:

- knowledge;
- intention;
- control;
- foreseeability;
- duty.

Shoko possesses none of the relevant causal control.


# 22. Shoko and Miori
Shoko becomes an ethically necessary interlocutor for Miori because she carries Toko's heart without being Toko.

Her presence forces a distinction.

- Toko is dead.
- Shoko lives.
- Toko's body helped create that life.
- Miori's grief cannot be solved by turning Shoko into a substitute.

Shoko also carries the exchange diary and helps Miori reverse the final song's interpretation.

She does not speak as Toko. She helps return Toko's relationship to its proper history.


# 23. Current high-school Shoko
By Volume 15, Shoko has naturally reached high-school age.

The Minegahara uniform once belonged to the impossible older Shoko image in Sakuta's memory. Now current Shoko can wear it through ordinary chronology.

This is one of the series' most elegant transformations of supernatural image into ordinary life.


# 24. Medical-support trajectory without compulsory debt
Shoko studies toward a future involving medical knowledge and rare-disease support.

She explicitly says she does not yet know whether she will become a doctor.

This preserves self-authorship.

A weaker ending would make organ-recipient gratitude dictate one morally pure profession.

The actual ending allows:

- interest;
- purpose;
- uncertainty.


# 25. Future kindness as memory
Sakuta recognizes that the kindness received from older Shoko continues making him.

The future person does not need to remain presently accessible.

This is one of the strongest pieces of evidence for the final `思い出に変える` solution.

A possible person can become:

- memory;
- ethical orientation;
- character formation.

She need not remain the current world's adult Shoko for the encounter to remain true in Sakuta's life.


# 26. Donor-state comparison matrix
| State | Donor/fatal structure | Shoko continuity | Sakuta/Mai consequence | Ethical significance |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| future A | Sakuta dies and becomes donor | Shoko survives into older future | Sakuta absent; future relationship possible | gratitude becomes debt; Sakuta's life excluded |
| future B | Mai dies protecting Sakuta; donor architecture changes | Shoko survival remains tied to another death | Sakuta survives through Mai's loss | role reversal exposes mutual martyrdom |
| revised current history | Toko dies and donates heart | current Shoko survives | Sakuta and Mai live | no participant chose the cost; moral luck replaces direct sacrifice |
| RF | Toko remains donor history; Shoko grows normally | student, club president, support interests | Sakuta carries future-Shoko memory | bodily gift persists without identity transfer or compulsory debt |

The series never discovers a life without cost. It moves from direct, consciously embraced sacrifice toward a tragic history whose cost is not morally owned by the survivors.

## 26.1 Shoko's voices and ages

Older Shoko's address carries first-love intimacy and future knowledge. Younger/current Shoko often speaks with composure that can conceal self-suppression. High-school Shoko can joke and inhabit the Minegahara uniform naturally without becoming identical to the future-wife continuity.

The final synthesis should distinguish:

- current girl;
- future continuity;
- remembered image;
- ethically transmitted kindness.

## 26.2 The right to ordinary selfishness

Shoko needs to be able to say:

- I want treatment;
- I want school;
- I want relationships;
- I want a future.

Without that capacity, kindness becomes a method of disappearing before anyone else has to feel guilty.

## 26.3 Archive versus child

Current Shoko remembers multiple futures. The narrative repeatedly returns her to food, school, letters, friends, club, and future study so that impossible memory does not become her only identity.

## 26.4 Comparative grief

Shoko's arc prepares Miori/Toko. A possible future person can be lost and still matter; a dead person can remain through body, art, and memory without being resurrected.

## 26.5 Institutional donation ethics

The donor-support organization creates a channel without forcing contact. It permits gratitude, delay, nonresponse, and different grief timetables. This contrasts with Sakuta's early assumption that urgent meaning justifies direct access.

This table clarifies why “older Shoko was just a dream” and “older Shoko was one fixed time traveler” are both insufficient. The text treats future continuities as experienced enough to create love, grief, and moral standing, while allowing their exact topology to remain partly unresolved.


# 28. The sacrifice triangle and standing
The central fatal architecture contains at least three agents with legitimate claims.

## 28.1 Shoko

Shoko is allowed to say:

> `わたしだって、生きたいんです`

Her desire is morally basic. The fact that saving her may require another body does not make the desire itself corrupt.

## 28.2 Sakuta

Sakuta initially treats his death as an elegant solution because it converts gratitude into repayment. His later:

> `生きていたい`

is not moral failure. It is recognition that his life belongs among the lives being counted.

## 28.3 Mai

Mai has standing not only because she loves Sakuta but because Sakuta's future decisions materially determine the life she is trying to build with him. His private martyrdom would leave her with the consequences while denying her participation.

The novels therefore do not solve the triangle by ranking whose love is purest. They expose why unilateral sacrifice is morally incomplete:

> **the person who dies cannot treat the survivors' futures as collateral belonging only to himself.**


# 29. Donor histories and identity
| Donor history | Recipient outcome | Personhood rule |
|---|---|---|
| Sakuta donor future | Shoko survives; Sakuta dies | body can save another life without transferring Sakuta's personhood |
| Mai donor future | Shoko and Sakuta survive; Mai dies | substituted donor does not make outcome morally clean |
| Toko donor history | current Shoko survives; Toko remains dead | transplanted heart creates bodily causality, not identity transfer |

The final history is emotionally preferable because Sakuta and Mai live. It is not causally innocent. Toko's death introduces downstream moral residue nobody intended or could have predicted.


# 30. Donor-family ethics
The Shoko/Toko-family contact provides one of AoButa's strongest examples of bounded institutional care.

The support organization permits communication without forcing direct contact. Shoko writes. The family may answer or not. Toko's mother takes years to respond. Toko's father does not join the meeting.

The structure recognizes several truths:

- recipient gratitude is real;
- bereaved parents retain boundaries;
- bodily donation does not create relational entitlement;
- different family members may grieve at different speeds;
- contact can be meaningful without becoming compulsory reconciliation.

This is a mature contrast to Sakuta's earlier tendency to force encounters because he knows they may be useful.


# 31. Possible persons and memory
Future Shoko's standing does not disappear when her world ceases to be current.

Sakuta explicitly understands that the kindness he received continues to make him. This supplies the final series with a model for possible-person memory:

> **a person can cease to be presently accessible without becoming retroactively unreal or ethically inert.**

That model is necessary for the finale's memory solution. Sakuta can relinquish the current supernatural actualization of possible worlds without declaring the people he met in them meaningless.


# 32. Ordinary Shoko is not a diminished Shoko
The series repeatedly reduces the spectacularity surrounding Shoko:

- parfait;
- school friends;
- weather in Okinawa;
- letters;
- Minegahara uniform;
- animal-club presidency;
- study plans.

This is not a loss of narrative importance. It is the fulfillment of the original moral aim: Shoko should be allowed a future ordinary enough that the plot no longer needs her body as the hinge of impossible sacrifice.

Her medical interest is similarly careful. She may pursue medicine or broader serious-illness support. The series does not turn survival into a compulsory vocation.


# 33. Shoko and Mai
The Shoko/Mai relationship is often flattened into romantic rivalry. The full series produces a more complicated relation.

Mai is jealous and has standing as Sakuta's partner. Shoko has a genuine possible-world history with Sakuta. Neither woman is reducible to an obstacle for the other.

Current Shoko and Mai eventually coexist ordinarily. Mai can say:

> `翔子ちゃん、おかえりなさい`

The greeting matters because it replaces fatal competition with present social relation. The impossible older first-love figure becomes a younger person with her own future.

# 36. Strongest counterreadings
## 36.1 Older Shoko is simply one time traveler

The multi-future memory structure and later possible-world architecture make this too simple.

She is better understood as a genuinely experienced possible future continuity whose information crosses state boundaries.

## 36.2 Shoko manipulates Sakuta through romance

Older Shoko sometimes withholds information and guides his choices.

Yet the complete arc shows her relinquishing the future that gives her the strongest romantic claim. Manipulation is an ethical tension, not a sufficient character summary.

## 36.3 The series endorses organ donation as simple moral salvation

The donor plot values donation, but it also preserves:

- death;
- family grief;
- survivor guilt;
- impossible causal questions.

The donor is not reduced to a device whose death becomes good because another person lives.

## 36.4 Toko lives on as Shoko

Rejected.

The body link is literal.

Personal continuity is not.

## 36.5 Shoko's final medical interest is debt repayment

The text preserves uncertainty and broader support goals. Her future is influenced by survival but not totally owned by it.

The table prevents two common simplifications.

# 38. Scar and heart architecture
Sakuta's chest scars, Shoko's failing heart, and future donors create a literal bodily network.

## 38.1 The scars are not merely symbolic guilt

They appear physically and are linked by Rio's working model to the improper coexistence of Sakuta hearts across states.

## 38.2 The heart is not the person

A donor heart carries biological life.

It does not carry:

- donor autobiography;
- donor name;
- donor relationship ownership;
- personal identity.

This is why Toko does not become Shoko.

## 38.3 The body still produces relationship and guilt

Although identity does not transfer, the transplant creates:

- gratitude;
- survivor guilt;
- donor-family contact;
- new causal history;
- bodily dependence upon another person's death.

The series therefore avoids both spiritualized identity transfer and morally neutral medical abstraction.


# 39. Sacrifice audit
| Sacrificial path | Person saved | Person lost / endangered | Who chooses? | Ethical problem |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sakuta accepts fatal future | Shoko | Sakuta | Sakuta, under gratitude/debt pressure | excludes Mai, family, and Sakuta's own desire from standing |
| Mai dies saving Sakuta | Sakuta | Mai | Mai in immediate crisis | proves reciprocity of willingness, not morality of martyrdom |
| future Shoko relinquishes fulfilled future | Sakuta and Mai histories | future Shoko's current world/self | Shoko | altruistic but creates moral residue; possible person gives up jurisdiction |
| Toko donor history | Shoko | Toko | Toko's prior donor registration; accident not chosen | donation can be generous without making death desirable |

The series' development is not from selfishness to sacrifice.

It is from:

> **sacrifice as the cleanest proof of care**

toward:

> **care as a process in which every affected life receives standing and no one person's death is romanticized as a frictionless solution.**


# 40. `生きていたい` and the moral admission of self
Sakuta's:

> `僕は、生きていたい……`

is central because the desire is initially difficult for him to say.

His gratitude toward Shoko has made survival feel morally suspect.

The line performs three corrections.

1. Sakuta's life is not merely a donor resource.
2. Wanting to live does not cancel gratitude.
3. A moral decision must include the chooser as one of the persons affected.

Volume 7's later:

> `生きたいとはじめて思った`

shows how Mai's death makes the desire undeniable.

The tragedy is that he must experience loss before fully admitting his own standing.


# 41. Mai and Shoko are not rival claimants to Sakuta
The romantic surface can encourage a triangle reading:

- first love Shoko;
- present girlfriend Mai;
- Sakuta choosing between them.

The deeper conflict is not which woman “wins” Sakuta.

It is which future can exist without requiring another person to die.

Future Shoko's possible marriage is emotionally real. Mai's present relationship is emotionally real. The ethical problem cannot be solved by ranking romantic purity.

The relevant questions are:

- who lives;
- who chooses;
- who knows the cost;
- whose future is being used as resource;
- whether the affected person has standing.

This is why Shoko's relinquishment should not be reduced to losing a romance contest.


# 42. Donor-family ethics and asymmetrical grief
The Toko donor history adds an adult institutional layer.

Shoko writes through a donor-support organization. The organization preserves:

- anonymity;
- delay;
- choice;
- the family's right not to answer.

Toko's mother eventually meets Shoko.

Toko's father does not.

The narrative permits both responses.

Recipient gratitude does not create a right to the donor family's grief.

Likewise, donor-family pain does not make the recipient's continued life wrongful.

The relationship is materially intimate and personally nonidentical.


# 43. Moral luck: causal implication is not culpability
Volume 14 produces three counterfactual guilt narratives.

## Miori

If she had not asked Toko to buy curry bread, the fatal timing might differ.

## Shoko

If she had not survived through a changed donor history, Toko might not have died in the same branch.

## Sakuta

If he had not altered the earlier fatal futures, the current donor history might not exist.

All three stories may identify some causal participation.

None establishes ordinary culpability because the relevant agents lack:

- intent;
- control;
- reasonable foreseeability;
- adequate causal knowledge;
- violated duty regarding the distant outcome.

The mature response is not to prove causal purity.

It is to live responsibly without converting every downstream tragedy into personal guilt.


# 44. Ordinary Shoko and the refusal of miracle identity
The series repeatedly makes Shoko more ordinary as it values her life more fully.

She eats a parfait.

She writes letters from Okinawa.

She attends school.

She makes friends.

She grows into the Minegahara uniform naturally.

She becomes animal-club president.

She considers medical study without being forced into one profession.

These developments matter because Shoko could easily remain:

- mysterious savior;
- tragic donor recipient;
- perfect first love;
- metaphysical key.

Instead, the final series gives her adolescence back.


# 45. Memory of a possible person
The final volume provides one of the strongest answers to the status of overwritten future persons.

Sakuta recognizes that future Shoko's kindness:

- made his current self;
- remains causally active;
- will continue shaping his future.

The future person does not have to remain presently embodied for the relationship to matter.

This is why `思い出に変える` is not denial.

The possible person becomes memory and ethical inheritance.


# 46. Cross-document boundary and controlled Shoko formulation
Document 06 is the primary home for:

- Shoko's future states;
- sacrifice;
- donor architecture;
- survivor guilt;
- moral residue.

World topology belongs in Document 11. Grief comparison with Kaede/Toko belongs in Document 12. Intervention ethics belong in Document 13. Personhood substrates belong in Document 10.

The controlled Phase-4 formulation is:

> **Shoko transforms futurity from prediction into responsibility. Her possible adult selves are real enough to love and grieve, but not entitled to preserve a world through another person's death. Her final importance lies not in solving time but in teaching Sakuta that gratitude does not require self-erasure, that survival can remain morally entangled without becoming guilty, and that the kindness of a possible person can continue through memory after the possibility is relinquished.**


# 48. Final thesis
Shoko begins as a future that should not exist and ends as a current girl with an ordinary future.

The path requires the series to ask questions that cannot be morally purified.

- Should Sakuta die so Shoko can live?
- Should Mai's death be accepted because it saves Sakuta and Shoko?
- Does changing the future erase people who genuinely lived?
- Does a recipient owe the donor?
- Did one survival cause another death?

The final answer is not an equation ranking lives.

It is a discipline of standing and memory.

> **Shoko is allowed to want to live. Sakuta is allowed to want to live. Mai is allowed standing in Sakuta's future. Future Shoko's love matters without owning current Sakuta. Toko's body saves Shoko without becoming Shoko. Causal entanglement creates grief and gratitude without automatically creating guilt.**

The moral residue remains.

That is not failure of resolution.

It is the truth the Shoko arc contributes to the series' adult world:

> a life may be worth living even when nobody can prove that its causal history is perfectly clean.
