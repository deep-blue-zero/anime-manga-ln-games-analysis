# 13. Ethics, Autonomy, Privacy, Self-Sacrifice, Intervention, and Care
## Helping without taking ownership of another person's life

## Governing question

> **When does Sakuta's willingness to intervene constitute courageous recognition, and when does it become paternalistic override, privacy violation, or unsustainable self-sacrifice?**

# 1. Executive thesis

AoButa gives Sakuta a moral advantage that repeatedly saves other people:

> he is willing to act when the surrounding world has decided that nothing real is happening.

That advantage is indispensable.

It is also dangerous.

A person who alone recognizes a crisis can begin to assume that recognition gives him:

- authority to define the problem;
- authority to select the solution;
- authority to access private information;
- authority to spend his own body;
- authority to decide which risks other people should accept.

The complete ethical arc therefore cannot be summarized as:

> Sakuta learns to care.

He cares from the beginning.

The real development is:

> **Sakuta learns that care does not automatically create jurisdiction.**

The series tests this through repeated distinctions.

- Rescue is not the same thing as legitimacy.
- Effectiveness is not retroactive consent.
- Protection is not ownership.
- Sacrifice is not proof of love.
- Access to information is not a right to know.
- Understanding a person's conflict is not permission to choose for them.
- Being the only available helper does not mean one must remain the only helper.
- Believing testimony does not require certifying every explanation.

Sakuta's mature trajectory is:

> **exceptional rescuer → reciprocal partner → option-builder → distributed helper → informationally restrained friend → adult listener.**

The series' strongest ethical achievement is that it does not solve Sakuta's overreach by making him passive.

He still intervenes.

He still takes risks.

He still sometimes overrides.

But the normative center gradually shifts toward:

- standing;
- consent;
- proportionality;
- reversible support;
- shared risk;
- distributed competence;
- institutional care;
- epistemic humility.

The final Ebina scene states the mature form:

> `僕には見えない。でも、君を信じるよ`

Sakuta neither dismisses the student nor claims ownership of her reality.

He offers:

> `僕が話を聞く`

The adult response is availability before control.

# 2. A controlled ethical framework

The series does not provide an explicit moral theory, but its interventions can be assessed through recurring dimensions.

## 2.1 Standing

Who is morally entitled to decide?

- the person harmed;
- the person whose body is at risk;
- a partner whose future is directly implicated;
- a caregiver;
- an institution;
- Sakuta as witness;
- the crowd;
- no one alone.

Standing is central in Volume 11, where only Sakuta has the strongest claim to say `今さら` about his middle-school injury.

## 2.2 Consent

Was the intervention:

- requested;
- accepted;
- tolerated;
- performed without knowledge;
- performed against an explicit boundary;
- impossible to consent to because of emergency timing?

## 2.3 Proportionality

Was the method proportionate to:

- immediacy of danger;
- probability of harm;
- availability of alternatives;
- cost imposed on helper and subject?

## 2.4 Privacy

Did the helper obtain or use:

- personal records;
- phones;
- diaries;
- private conversations;
- images;
- bodily access;
- medical information?

Was access necessary, authorized, or merely useful?

## 2.5 Epistemic humility

How certain is the helper about:

- what happened;
- why it happened;
- what the person wants;
- what the future will do;
- whether the proposed cure is causal?

## 2.6 Agency preservation

Does the intervention:

- create options;
- restore the person's ability to choose;
- stage one emotionally forced answer;
- substitute the helper's preference;
- make the subject indispensable to the helper?

## 2.7 Risk distribution

Does one person absorb all cost?

Or are there:

- friends;
- family;
- professionals;
- institutions;
- emergency services;
- ordinary support structures?

## 2.8 Reversibility

Can the subject later revise the choice?

A temporary accommodation, trial, or pause may preserve autonomy better than an irreversible dramatic solution.

# 3. Volume 1: courageous recognition and public coercion coexist

Sakuta's response to Mai establishes the ethical ambiguity that the series spends fifteen volumes refining.

## 3.1 Strongest defense of Sakuta's action

Mai is literally becoming unrecognizable.

Ordinary private support is failing.

The crisis is socially constituted.

The school's collective attention is relevant to her restoration.

Sakuta's public declaration therefore addresses the phenomenon at the same scale at which it operates.

He also accepts substantial personal cost:

- ridicule;
- humiliation;
- school-wide exposure.

The intervention is courageous.

## 3.2 Strongest criticism

The confession publicly defines:

- Mai;
- their relationship;
- Sakuta's feelings;
- her private crisis.

Mai did not fully author the spectacle.

The method risks converting recognition into possession:

> the girl becomes visible because the boy claims her publicly.

The later one-month requirement matters ethically because Mai refuses to let one spectacular act settle the relationship.

She demands duration:

> `今の、一ヵ月後にもう一度言って`

The relationship becomes something she helps authorize.

## 3.3 Controlled judgment

> **Justified emergency intervention with real coercive cost; not a general license for public romantic spectacle.**

# 4. Tomoe: protection through social engineering

Sakuta participates in fake dating and repeated future states to protect Tomoe from humiliation and exclusion.

## 4.1 Defense

Tomoe's fear is not trivial vanity.

She experiences socially visible solitude as shame:

> `ひとりは……恥ずかしい`

The school environment makes social positioning materially consequential.

Sakuta helps her navigate a problem the surrounding peers would not treat compassionately.

He also recognizes her present constructed self rather than demanding an original rural Tomoe:

> `それが古賀だよ。前がどうだろうと、今のその姿が古賀`

## 4.2 Criticism

Sakuta is comfortable with deception as social management.

He collaborates in presenting a relationship that is not real.

The method can reinforce the premise that the room's judgment must be managed rather than challenged.

## 4.3 Controlled judgment

> **Protective accommodation under hostile social conditions, ethically mixed because it relies on deception but broadly preserves Tomoe's agency.**

# 5. Rio: nonselection and privacy violation

Rio's split gives Sakuta one of his strongest personhood responses:

> `僕は双葉理央の味方だ`

He refuses to choose one body as the authentic Rio.

That preserves the disowned self's standing.

Yet the arc also involves access to:

- phones;
- photographs;
- anonymous sexualized posts;
- private emotional material.

## Strongest defense

The duplicated state is dangerous and difficult to understand.

Information is necessary to identify that the sexualized posting predates the duplication and belongs to Rio's conflict rather than to a disposable impostor.

## Strongest criticism

The fact that information is explanatory does not make access harmless.

Rio's sexual self-presentation is especially sensitive material.

A helper can reproduce the very gaze and loss of bodily control that contributed to the crisis.

## Controlled judgment

> **Excellent refusal of authenticity ranking; ethically costly information use that the series later learns to handle more carefully.**

# 6. Nodoka and Mai: bodies, property, and explicit privacy boundaries

Volume 4 creates unusually clear privacy evidence.

Sakuta is told:

> `勝手に見ないで`

He nevertheless accesses material in the cabinet/letters context.

## Defense

The letters become crucial evidence that Nodoka's attachment to Mai is not reducible to maternal comparison.

They help the sisters recognize a real relationship that each has misread.

## Criticism

An explicit boundary is the strongest form of nonconsent.

The later good outcome cannot convert the earlier act into consent.

The body-swap itself also raises a separate autonomy problem:

> one sister's consent cannot provide full jurisdiction over the other sister's body.

Mai's refusal to treat her own willingness as sufficient for Nodoka's bodily exposure is ethically significant.

## Controlled judgment

> **The sister differentiation is ethically strong; Sakuta's evidence-gathering method remains a clear privacy violation.**

# 7. Kaede care: lifesaving commitment and parentification

Sakuta's care for Kaede is both admirable and structurally unfair.

His father says:

> `かえでのこと、頼むぞ`

This sentence contains:

- trust;
- love;
- delegation;
- parentification.

## What Sakuta's care accomplishes

- daily safety;
- emotional continuity;
- educational support;
- a stable home;
- access to ordinary goals;
- protection from overwhelming exposure.

## What the family system costs him

- concentrated responsibility;
- limited adolescent life;
- belief that his body and future are available for family crises;
- difficulty asking adults for help;
- suppressed need.

The ethical lesson is not:

> Sakuta should have cared less.

It is:

> **care should not require one teenager to become the entire institution.**

# 8. Volumes 6–7: self-sacrifice and the moral standing of one's own life

The Shoko arc is the series' most direct critique of self-sacrifice.

Sakuta initially reasons from gratitude:

> Shoko saved him;
> Shoko may die;
> his death can save her;
> therefore dying may be the most moral use of his life.

The flaw is not merely emotional.

It is ethical.

Sakuta treats his own life as though it has no independent claimant.

Mai rejects that model.

She says:

> `私は咲太と未来の話をしていたいの`

and later:

> `ふたりで幸せになるの。私と咲太のふたりで`

## 8.1 Standing

Sakuta's body is his.

But his death directly alters:

- Mai's life;
- family;
- friends;
- future relationships.

Autonomy does not mean other persons have no standing in a decision to disappear from their lives.

## 8.2 Sacrifice is not morally pure because the victim is oneself

Self-sacrifice can still be:

- coercive toward loved ones;
- evasive of one's own desire;
- gratitude debt made bodily;
- a way of avoiding uncertainty.

## 8.3 Sakuta's decisive admission

> `僕は、生きていたい……`

This is not selfish failure.

It is the moment his life enters the moral ledger.

## 8.4 Controlled judgment

> **The series rejects unilateral martyrdom and replaces it with reciprocal standing, distributed risk, and the right to want one's own future.**

# 9. Mai's sacrifice does not vindicate Sakuta's sacrifice logic

When Mai dies saving Sakuta in one future, the result demonstrates the cost of the moral model rather than proving it noble.

Sakuta experiences:

- grief;
- horror;
- the revelation that being saved through another person's death can be intolerable.

The scene teaches him what his own planned death would have done to Mai.

The correct lesson is not:

> Mai's sacrifice is beautiful, so Sakuta should imitate it.

It is:

> **love cannot be reduced to deciding alone which person will bear the fatal cost.**

# 10. Future revision and moral residue

Volume 7 does not produce a morally clean outcome.

Shoko's fulfilled future may be relinquished.

Possible persons disappear from current actuality.

Later Toko dies in the history that saves current Shoko.

The ethical result is:

> survival can be the right choice without becoming causally innocent.

This is why the series needs moral residue.

A decision can be justified under uncertainty while leaving grief and counterfactual concern.

# 11. The mother diary: information, family repair, and invasion

Volume 9 gives Sakuta access to his mother's private diary.

The information helps him understand:

- her collapse;
- her effort;
- her inability to recognize him;
- family history.

## Defense

The family is trapped in radical information asymmetry.

The diary contains evidence unavailable through ordinary conversation.

Sakuta's understanding becomes essential to the later reconciliation.

## Criticism

The diary is private.

A good outcome does not erase the breach.

The scene continues Sakuta's early pattern:

> crucial information is treated as sufficient justification for access.

## Retrospective significance

Volume 14's exchange-diary refusal shows real development.

Sakuta later has an opportunity to read highly relevant private material and says:

> `……やめとく`

because:

> `美東とは、そろそろ『友達』になりたいと思ってたから`

He now recognizes that the relationship he wants may impose epistemic limits.

# 12. Volume 8: option-building rather than outcome control

Hanakaede's school choice is one of Sakuta's clearest ethical improvements.

He still stages encounters and investigates alternatives without full transparency.

But he eventually recognizes:

> `決めるのは咲太じゃない。花楓が決めることだ`

His task becomes:

- create options;
- provide testimony about his own feelings;
- remain available;
- relinquish the final decision.

Mai's criticism of his secrecy is important.

A backup plan can be experienced as:

> everyone already expects me to fail.

The care therefore remains mixed.

Still, the volume's final structure is ethically strong because Hanakaede chooses correspondence school **after** Minegahara becomes available.

Her alternative cannot be dismissed as coerced consolation.

# 13. Accommodation is not compromised autonomy

The bonus novels make this principle practical.

Hanakaede asks that her early work shifts overlap with Sakuta's.

She discloses her school nonattendance.

The manager agrees.

This is not dependence invalidating autonomy.

It is:

> **the person identifying the conditions under which participation is possible.**

The support creates skill.

It does not replace skill.

Later, the manager asks her to continue.

Hanakaede says:

> not yet.

The story respects that limit.

Successful exposure does not create an obligation to accept permanent exposure.

# 14. Tutoring: the helper who can become unnecessary

Sakuta's teaching trajectory offers a direct ethical correction to rescue identity.

A compulsive helper needs to remain indispensable.

A good teacher can:

- explain;
- refer;
- adjust;
- let the student surpass him;
- become unnecessary.

When Sara prefers Sakuta as tutor, his stated priority is her learning rather than preserving his special role.

That is an adult care structure because it gives the recipient's good priority over the helper's identity.

# 15. Ikumi: goodness as dependency

Ikumi's case shows that sincere care can still become ethically dangerous.

Kosaka says:

> `誰かを支えることで、自分を支えている`

The care is not fake.

The problem is structural.

If the helper's selfhood depends on another person's need, then the recipient's vulnerability becomes useful to the helper.

No conscious exploitation is required.

The relationship can still become asymmetrical.

## 15.1 Sakuta's fake distress post

Sakuta uses Ikumi's inability to ignore suffering as investigative leverage.

### Defense

The post is low-risk, reveals her pattern, and helps identify the phenomenon.

### Criticism

It exploits precisely the compulsion he claims to be concerned about.

### Judgment

> **Effective manipulation with ethically relevant use of a person's vulnerability.**

## 15.2 The reunion

Ikumi tries to create a public reckoning for Sakuta's old injury.

Her facts have moral force.

Her standing is incomplete.

Sakuta is the person most entitled to decide whether he wants public victimhood performed in his name.

The scene's strongest principle is:

> **An ally cannot force the harmed person into the form of justice the ally needs.**

# 16. Sara: privacy, clairvoyance, and the right not to know

Sara's phenomenon turns privacy into a literal ethical problem.

She can access another person's perceptions and thoughts under certain conditions.

Sakuta also discovers reciprocal access and initially does not fully disclose it.

He later uses that hidden access to listen to Sara and Mai.

## Defense

Mai may be in danger.

The information could be valuable.

Sakuta has strong reason to treat warnings seriously.

## Criticism

Urgency does not manufacture consent.

The power grants access.

It does not grant a right.

## Sara's decisive refusal

Mai offers certainty:

> look into my mind.

Sara refuses:

> `知りたくない！`

That is one of the series' strongest autonomy moments.

Love and recognition require room for the other person to remain opaque.

# 17. Nene's trophy: preserving an archive against the owner's instruction

Nene discards the Miss Contest trophy carrying her name.

Sakuta refuses to throw it away.

The trophy later helps Takumi remember Nene.

## Strongest defense

Nene is in a state of autobiographical self-erasure.

The discarded object may be necessary to restore access to a life she is no longer capable of recognizing.

The intervention is limited:

- preserve the object;
- do not destroy it;
- allow later reconsideration.

## Strongest criticism

The object belongs to Nene.

She made a choice.

Sakuta overrides it because he decides her present self lacks sufficient authority.

This is classic paternalism.

## Controlled judgment

> **Defensible preservation under extraordinary identity impairment, but still an override whose success does not become retroactive consent.**

# 18. Miori's exchange diary: restraint as intervention

The diary is highly relevant to the final mystery.

Sakuta could read it.

He refuses.

This is ethically significant because the refusal itself becomes a form of care.

He chooses:

- relationship over immediate information;
- Miori's future disclosure over investigative extraction;
- patience over epistemic control.

The later disclosure occurs through Shoko, who has been entrusted with the diary and believes one page must reach Miori.

The ethics are contextual rather than absolute.

The series does not say:

> private records can never be shared.

It says:

> **availability of information is not enough; standing, custody, purpose, and relationship matter.**

# 19. R1: no one person has jurisdiction over the world

R1 is the largest-scale autonomy problem.

Sakuta remembers R0.

Other people inhabit R1.

Some explicitly prefer it.

- Rio wants the Kunimi relationship.
- Kaede wants her current life with Sakuta.
- Sweet Bullet has Budokan.
- Tomoe's university history differs.

A simple restoration decision risks treating everyone else as scenery in Sakuta's biography.

Yet keeping R1 may preserve histories produced without those people's original consent.

The correct ethical conclusion is not:

> count preferences and maximize happiness.

Nor:

> Sakuta remembers R0, therefore R0 automatically wins.

The conflict involves:

- autobiographical continuity;
- current preference;
- displaced persons;
- world-level nonconsensual change;
- future authorship.

# 20. Kaede's consent and world resolution

R1 Kaede is the person most visibly endangered by leaving R1.

The series gives her:

- relevant information;
- time;
- fear;
- a direct question;
- a stable answer.

She says:

> `かえでは帰りたいです`

and the narration says her will does not waver.

This matters because the transition would be much harder to defend if Sakuta simply selected Hanakaede/R0 for everyone.

Yet the ethics remain incomplete.

Kaede's language of return may not map neatly onto the metaphysical event.

The series does not prove full informed consent to every ontological consequence.

Controlled conclusion:

> **Her agency is necessary and heavily legitimating; it does not eliminate residual uncertainty about continuity loss.**

# 21. Emergency rescue versus planned martyrdom

Sakuta's speaker rescue in Volume 13 should be distinguished from his V6 self-sacrifice plan.

At the police event:

- danger is immediate;
- Mai is directly under a falling object;
- deliberation is impossible;
- no safer alternative is evident.

The intervention is proportionate to an emergency.

He is injured.

But he does not plan his death as the solution.

The distinction matters:

> criticism of martyrdom does not require passivity in emergencies.

# 22. Reciprocal protection

Sakuta says:

> `麻衣さんのことは僕が守るから`

Mai answers:

> `咲太のことは私が守ってあげる`

At the event:

- he catches the speaker;
- she catches him.

The series' mature protection ethic is not absence of risk.

It is:

> **risk and standing circulate rather than being monopolized by one self-disposing rescuer.**

# 23. Distributed care

The hospital network after Sakuta's injury is one of the series' strongest ethical images.

Care comes from:

- Mai;
- Rio;
- Kunimi;
- Ikumi;
- Hanakaede;
- Tomoe;
- Sara;
- Nodoka;
- Uzuki;
- institutions.

People help and then return to:

- work;
- family;
- shifts;
- obligations.

This is not insufficient devotion.

It is sustainable care.

The injured person does not become the entire social world.

# 24. Institutions and ethical competence

The later series increasingly moves care into roles:

- firefighter;
- nurse;
- teacher;
- tutor;
- childcare worker;
- donor-support organization;
- hospital.

Institutional care adds:

- training;
- scope;
- handoff;
- accountability;
- rest;
- repeatability.

This is the adult alternative to the hero fantasy.

It does not replace friendship or love.

It prevents every crisis from depending upon one extraordinary teenager.

# 25. Ebina: belief without epistemic conquest

The recognition taxonomy belongs to Document 09. The ethical question here is narrower: **what may an adult with institutional authority legitimately do when a student reports something he cannot perceive?**

Sakuta neither certifies the ghost nor dismisses the student. He states the perceptual asymmetry—`僕には見えない`—then offers person-specific trust and an invited channel of care: `でも、君を信じるよ` and `何か困ってるなら、僕が話を聞く`.

The intervention is deliberately minimal. It preserves Ebina's authority over her own testimony, avoids diagnosis or metaphysical conquest, and prevents uncertainty from becoming abandonment. This is the final ethical form of Sakuta's development.

# 27. Sakuta's longitudinal ethical development

## Early model

> If I see the problem and no one else acts, I must solve it.

## Middle correction

> My life and the lives of people who love me have standing.

## Late correction

> Information access does not equal a right to know.

## Final model

> I can believe the person, listen, create options, and involve others without claiming ownership of the person's reality.

# 28. Strongest counterreadings

## 28.1 The series excuses Sakuta because he is the protagonist

There is real evidence for this criticism.

Sakuta's boundary violations often lead to good outcomes.

Other characters forgive him.

The plot rewards his intervention instinct.

A controlled synthesis should not deny protagonist privilege.

The strongest response is that the series increasingly exposes the cost and provides counterexamples:

- Mai resists sacrifice;
- Hanakaede chooses;
- Sara refuses knowledge;
- Sakuta refuses the diary;
- Ebina is listened to rather than solved.

The ethical development is real, though uneven.

## 28.2 Privacy is a luxury in supernatural emergencies

Sometimes urgent access is justified.

A falling speaker or medical crisis does not permit ordinary consent procedures.

But many privacy violations occur without immediate emergency timing.

The series itself eventually proves that restraint can be effective.

Therefore:

> emergency necessity must be demonstrated, not assumed from the existence of Syndrome.

## 28.3 Mai's reciprocal protection romanticizes dangerous codependency

The concern has force.

The couple repeatedly treats each other's survival as central.

But the later relationship also preserves:

- separate careers;
- disagreement;
- ordinary schedules;
- distributed support;
- no final requirement of mutual self-destruction.

The controlled conclusion is reciprocity, not fusion.

## 28.4 Kaede's consent is narratively engineered

True in one sense.

The author gives Kaede the choice partly to legitimate the final world transition.

That does not make the choice meaningless.

The correct critical stance is:

- recognize the structural convenience;
- preserve the character's stated agency;
- retain residual concern.

## 28.5 Institutional care is idealized in the epilogue

The final careers are somewhat neat.

Yet the institutions shown earlier also fail:

- schools disbelieve;
- records misrecognize;
- crowds cannot be counted;
- families offload care.

The finale's institutional optimism is therefore aspirational rather than naïve certainty.

# 32. Final ethical synthesis

Sakuta's first great moral act is refusal.

He refuses to accept that the room's judgment settles whether Mai exists.

That refusal saves people throughout the series.

But refusal can become another authority.

If Sakuta is always the person who sees through the room, then he can begin to treat:

- his interpretation;
- his body;
- his information access;
- his chosen solution

as morally privileged.

The series spends fifteen volumes putting limits around that privilege.

Mai tells him his death is not his alone to spend.

Hanakaede tells him the final school belongs to her.

Sara refuses knowledge.

Nene's trophy demonstrates the remaining ambiguity of paternalistic preservation.

Miori's diary teaches him not to read simply because he can.

Kaede's choice makes clear that world selection cannot treat her as a reward.

Ebina gives him the final test.

He cannot see.

He cannot prove.

He can still avoid the old institutional violence of disbelief.

So he says:

> `僕には見えない。でも、君を信じるよ`

Then offers to listen.

That is the mature form of Sakuta's original refusal.

Not:

> everyone is wrong, and I alone know the truth.

But:

> **the fact that I do not know is not permission to abandon you.**
