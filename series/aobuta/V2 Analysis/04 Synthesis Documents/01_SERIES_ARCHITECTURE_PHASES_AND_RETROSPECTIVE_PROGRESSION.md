# 01. Series Architecture, Phases, and Retrospective Progression
## From one invisible girl to an adult who believes what he cannot see

## Governing question

> **How does AoButa transform a local school-social recognition problem into a full theory of memory, identity, possible worlds, grief, and adulthood without abandoning the interpersonal scale with which it began?**


# 1. Executive thesis
AoButa is not best understood as fifteen detachable supernatural-romance cases arranged around a witty male protagonist. Nor is it best understood as one continuous hard-science mystery whose early episodes merely introduce rules later solved by a final cosmology. Its architecture is cumulative in a different way.

Each title volume takes a conflict that could initially be stated in ordinary social or emotional language—being ignored, fearing public rejection, disliking one's body, living under comparison, losing autobiographical memory, fearing death, wanting a different life—and makes the conflict impossible to ignore by granting it literal ontological force. The phenomenon then destabilizes ordinary assumptions about who a person is, what counts as evidence, whose desire has standing, and what another person is entitled to do in response.

The series' deepest continuity lies not in a single mechanical formula but in a sequence of progressively harder recognition problems:

1. **Can a person remain socially real when others stop acknowledging her?**
2. **Can contradictory or deliberately constructed selves remain genuine?**
3. **Can body, memory, name, and role diverge without one axis monopolizing personhood?**
4. **Can one value another person's life without erasing one's own?**
5. **Can an attractive possible life be respected without appropriating or imposing it?**
6. **Can social recognition become so powerful that it overwrites the person it claims to identify?**
7. **Can adulthood preserve the ethical truth of an experience after one no longer shares its perception?**

The full-series movement is therefore:

> **seeing → remembering → recognizing → believing without shared sight.**

Volume 1 begins with Sakuta's exceptional sensory access. He can see Bunny Girl Mai when others cannot. That privilege makes the first intervention possible, but it is not the final ethical ideal. Volume 15 removes the privilege. Education-trainee Sakuta cannot see the person or ghost Ebina Rin reports. He says:

> `僕には見えない。でも、君を信じるよ`

The endpoint is not supernatural omniscience. It is institutionalized epistemic humility: another person's testimony can deserve care before the adult has settled its ontology.

This progression is supported by a second architectural movement:

> **exceptional rescue → reciprocal care → distributed support → ordinary institutional competence.**

Early Sakuta often becomes indispensable through unusually invasive or risky action. By the Shoko duology, that model nearly consumes his life. Mai, Shoko, Rio, Kunimi, family members, teachers, medical staff, coworkers, and former title heroines gradually redistribute the work of care. The final Sakuta does not become less willing to help. He becomes less committed to being the only person capable of helping.

A third movement organizes the series' relation to the supernatural:

> **phenomenon as immediate present → phenomenon as possible world → phenomenon as memory and ethical knowledge.**

The finale does not reveal that Adolescence Syndrome was imaginary. Sakuta explicitly rejects making it `なかったこと`. He instead says:

> `思春期症候群を思い出に変えるだけだ`

The adult relation to adolescence is transformation of mode, not repudiation of truth. The ghost no longer has to remain perceptually present. The ceiling pattern remains.


# 2. Phase I — Recognition and unstable selfhood
## Volumes 1–5

Phase I establishes the basic grammar. Its phenomena remain comparatively local—one school, one relationship, one body, one family—but the conceptual range is already broad.

## 2.1 Volume 1: recognition is not attention

Mai is famous before she becomes invisible. This distinction is essential. Celebrity means many people know an image, a name, or a career. It does not mean the person is recognized as a subject whose wishes and suffering have standing.

The early school environment treats Mai as `空気`: not necessarily through one coordinated act of cruelty, but through ambient nonacknowledgment. The phenomenon literalizes that social condition. The public confession then forces school-wide attention, and visibility returns. Yet the completed series later prevents the reader from treating attention as the whole cure. Collective recognition can restore, distort, or overwrite. Volume 1 therefore supplies the founding problem rather than the final law. See `CLM-001` and `CLM-002`.

Sakuta's defining line of resistance is:

> `もう、空気なんて読んでやるか`

This does not mean he cannot read the room. Volume 2 and later volumes repeatedly establish that he often reads it accurately and refuses its jurisdiction. The series begins from a boy who protects autonomy by stepping outside ambient consensus.

## 2.2 Volume 2: a constructed self can be real

Tomoe complicates the intuitive opposition between authentic interior self and socially manufactured exterior self. She has deliberately changed presentation, speech, and conduct after moving. Yet she says of the present self:

> `気に入ってる。すごく好き`

The repeated June sequences do not uncover a pristine rural Tomoe who should replace the Tokyo one. They reveal that self-fashioning becomes destructive when it must eliminate every desire or conflict likely to produce social embarrassment.

The phenomenon also gives the series its first major experiment with experienced possibility. Tomoe and Sakuta do not merely imagine rejected outcomes; they accumulate emotional experience across recursive states. That prepares the series to treat possible lives as morally and autobiographically significant even when they do not become the final public history.

## 2.3 Volume 3: contradiction belongs to one person

Rio's division into two complete bodies literalizes a defense that many later characters repeat in less direct form: the unacceptable desire is treated as though it belongs to another self.

Sakuta's key response is not to decide which Rio is real. He says:

> `僕は双葉理央の味方だ`

and:

> `自分のことは自分で決めるもんだろ`

The architecture shifts from recognition as detecting the hidden person to recognition as refusing to exclude an unwanted part of the person. Integration does not require liking every desire. It requires ceasing to treat one desire as a foreign claimant with no right to exist.

## 2.4 Volume 4: embodiment is not identity

The Mai/Nodoka case moves the problem from two bodies belonging to one autobiographical self toward public recognition of the wrong person through the right-looking body. Cameras, fans, family expectations, and professional roles follow appearance. Sakuta distinguishes the sisters through biography, voice, intention, and relationship.

The decisive development is not that one sister defeats the other. Nodoka refuses to become Mai:

> `なりたくない`

while retaining admiration. The series establishes differentiation as a positive value. Love need not eliminate envy; sisterhood need not require interchangeability.

## 2.5 Volume 5: autobiographical continuity can die while the body survives

The Kaede/Hanakaede arc is the phase's culmination because it removes the easy solution that the same body guarantees the same person.

The medical account identifies dissociative disorder. The narrative simultaneously insists that `かえで` has:

- a distinct first-person register;
- memories and habits;
- relationships;
- goals;
- a concept of her own difference from `花楓`;
- and a wish concerning how she will be remembered.

Her statement:

> `かえではかえでだから、花楓さんじゃないんです。`

prevents “recovery” from becoming a morally uncomplicated restoration. Hanakaede's return is real. So is Sakuta's bereavement for Kaede. The phase therefore ends with the proposition that recognition can conflict with clinical continuity, and that a successful medical outcome can still contain loss.

## 2.6 Phase-I synthesis

Phase I moves from social visibility to a multi-axis model of selfhood:

| Volume | Foundational problem | Architectural gain |
|---:|---|---|
| 1 | A visible/famous person is not recognized | attention ≠ recognition |
| 2 | A socially made self appears “fake” | construction can be genuine |
| 3 | Contradictory desires appear as rival selves | unwanted contradiction still belongs to the person |
| 4 | Body and public role point to the wrong sister | embodiment ≠ personhood |
| 5 | One body carries discontinuous autobiographical persons | bodily continuity ≠ autobiographical continuity |

By the end of Volume 5, the series has already made any single-substrate account of identity untenable.


# 3. Phase II — Futurity, sacrifice, world revision, and family
## Volumes 6–9

Phase II expands the stakes from self-recognition to competing lives and histories. The question is no longer only “Who is this person?” It becomes:

> **Which person's future counts, and who is entitled to decide?**

## 3.1 Volumes 6–7: Shoko and the ethics of one life against another

Shoko's heart disease makes futurity materially scarce. Older Shoko embodies futures in which Sakuta or Mai dies and becomes donor. The case exposes Sakuta's most dangerous moral assumption: that his own life is the easiest life to spend.

Mai directly rejects unilateral decision:

> `ひとりで決めないでよ`

and claims shared burden:

> `だから、一緒に背負う`

Sakuta's crucial development is the apparently simple confession:

> `生きていたい`

The series does not answer the fatal-future conflict by discovering an outcome with no moral cost. Volume 7 explicitly refuses purification. Sakuta, Mai, and Shoko survive through a changed history, but the experience leaves moral residue. The relationship thesis also changes from one-sided rescue to co-authored life:

> `ふたりで幸せになるの`

This phrase is a major series hinge. Love is no longer measured by willingness to die for the other. It is measured by willingness to remain and build a future with the other.

## 3.2 Possible persons become morally significant

The Shoko duology also changes the ontological architecture. Future selves are not disposable hypothetical illustrations. They possess:

- memory;
- attachment;
- grief;
- fulfilled relationships;
- and the capacity to relinquish claims.

Older Shoko's married future is real enough to matter and not final enough to govern current Sakuta. This becomes the template for later R1 ethics: a possible life can have standing without becoming destiny.

## 3.3 Volume 8: inheritance without conscription

Volume 8 returns to the Kaede/Hanakaede case after the Shoko expansion. Hanakaede interprets the life she inherited as debt. The phrase `もうひとりの私` holds relation and difference together without resolving the metaphysics.

Sakuta refuses ranking:

> `どっちも普通だ`

and rejects the idea that Hanakaede must complete Kaede's wish. Hanakaede ultimately chooses her own school after she has the option to attend Minegahara. The sequence matters: correspondence school is not merely consolation after failure. It becomes a self-authored future.

The phase's personhood thesis now acquires a normative form:

> **To recognize a predecessor does not give the predecessor jurisdiction over the successor's future.**

## 3.4 Volume 9: a family cannot be restored by pretending rupture did not happen

Volume 9 takes recognition into the family. Sakuta's mother fails to recognize him. The adjacent family world offers a materially happier biography, but Sakuta rejects appropriation of another Sakuta's authored life.

The resolution does not say the old family came back. The narration says:

> `この日、咲太たちは家族になった`

The absence of “again” matters. Reconciliation creates a new relationship among people changed by rupture.

This is also where the series' world architecture becomes explicitly adjacent rather than merely future-directed. The alternative family possesses its own history. Sakuta's choice establishes a rule later tested in reverse:

> **A happier possibility is not automatically a life one is entitled to take.**

## 3.5 Phase-II synthesis

Phase II makes three decisive expansions.

### From identity to moral standing

A person may be one of several possible or discontinuous selves and still have claims that must be heard.

### From rescue to reciprocity

Sakuta's own survival becomes a legitimate good. Mai's relationship claims shared authorship rather than sacrificial proof.

### From restoration to reconstruction

Hanakaede does not become Kaede. The family does not become its untouched former self. A viable future is made under changed conditions.


# 4. Interpolated ordinary-life bridge
## *Animal Land* and *Spring Days*

The bonuses sit after Volume 9's principal March narrative and before the university arc in story chronology, but they were written much later. They should therefore be used as canonical retrospective interpolation, not as proof that readers of the original V5–9 publication sequence already possessed their exact elaborations.

## 4.1 *Animal Land*: remembrance after differentiation

Once Hanakaede no longer believes she must become Kaede, she becomes able to ask who Kaede was.

The zoo, annual pass, photograph, and panda function as a distributed archive. The story's crucial development is that inherited objects no longer issue commands. They permit relation.

Sakuta can return to the zoo and remember Kaede without demanding maximal pain as proof of loyalty. Hanakaede can request the photograph without treating it as a template she must reproduce.

The bonus therefore supplies a mature continuing-bond rule:

> **Differentiation makes remembrance safer.**

## 4.2 *Spring Days*: recovery becomes range rather than cure

Hanakaede's part-time work is carefully scaffolded. She asks for overlapping shifts with Sakuta, makes mistakes, learns from coworkers, and later sets a limit rather than converting a successful trial into permanent obligation.

Autonomy is not defined as unsupported independence. It appears as:

- identifying needed support;
- participating with it;
- gaining skill;
- deciding what comes next;
- and turning received care outward through gifts and practical reciprocity.

The bonuses are architecturally important because they demonstrate what AoButa increasingly values as the endpoint of supernatural crisis:

> **ordinary competence.**


# 5. Phase III — University, social permeability, and alternate selfhood
## Volumes 10–12

The university arc does not abandon the high-school grammar. It changes the social field. Adolescence now meets:

- work;
- professional ambition;
- adult romance;
- institutional roles;
- broader networks;
- and more sophisticated forms of social self-construction.

## 5.1 Volume 10: social knowledge is not the same as social jurisdiction

Volume 10 makes social attunement itself ambivalent. Uzuki's new awareness helps her understand sarcasm, hurt, and interpersonal cues; the crisis begins only when an anonymous social average acquires jurisdiction over her own desire.

Sakuta's `今こそ空気を読めよ、づっきー` therefore revises rather than retracts his Volume-1 defiance: read the concrete people who have chosen to remain beside you, not a faceless aggregate. Its architectural function is to distinguish **social awareness** from **social sovereignty**. The full lexical and recognition analysis belongs to Document 09 §11.

## 5.2 Volume 11: the ideal helper can become a substitute for the failed self

Ikumi does genuine good. Her problem is not hypocrisy. It is that “being the person who helps” has become the only self she can tolerate.

The alternate-world exchange gives her a biography in which she can occupy the ideal role. Yet seamless social substitution produces erasure:

> `代わりの私でもいいなら、私ってなんなんだろう。`

Sakuta's `赤城、見っけ` recognizes person-specific difficulty rather than only successful function. The volume also gives one of the series' clearest adult answers to trauma: ordinary days accumulate until the injury no longer monopolizes the present.

## 5.3 Volume 12: love requires opacity

Sara's access to other people's interiority appears to solve romantic uncertainty. It instead threatens to replace relation with certainty.

Her decisive act is refusal. When Mai invites her to look, Sara says no. Recognizing another person does not confer entitlement to total interior access.

The volume therefore adds a boundary principle that later becomes central to Sakuta's refusal to read Miori's diary:

> **Care may require choosing not to know.**

It also confirms Sakuta's teaching direction. The good teacher is not the helper who remains indispensable. A teacher can succeed by making the student less dependent on him.

## 5.4 Phase-III synthesis

The university arc transforms the problem of authenticity into one of **social permeability**.

- Uzuki takes too much of the social world inside.
- Ikumi uses the ideal social role to replace the failed self.
- Sara tries to eliminate relational uncertainty through interior access.

All three cases converge on one principle:

> **A viable adult self remains relationally shaped without surrendering the right to opacity, failure, and revision.**


# 6. Phase IV — Collective recognition, Toko/Miori, reality selection, and adulthood
## Volumes 13–15

Phase IV scales the series from person-level recognition to crowd, media, institution, and world.

## 6.1 Volume 13: the public role becomes more real than its occupants

Nene's crisis is not that social construction is inherently false. Her Toko state becomes destructive because a prestigious, biographically empty public identity replaces the particular history of Iwamizawa Nene.

The mass Santa event expands the problem. Many people believe themselves to be Toko. Others cannot perceive them. Their bodies nevertheless occupy space, causing crowd-density failure.

Recognition becomes infrastructural:

> **A system that cannot count people can still be broken by the bodies it fails to recognize.**

Volume 13 also corrects the original-Toko identification. Nene is an imitator/occupant, not the source of the public artist identity.

## 6.2 Volume 14: a desirable possibility is not automatically the rightful reality

The public's April 1 expectation helps rewrite Mai as Toko. Sakuta then enters R1, a state containing genuine counterfactual goods:

- Kaede alive and grown;
- Hanakaede also existing;
- Rio with Kunimi;
- Tomoe at Sakuta's university;
- Uzuki retaining university alongside idol success.

The world is not morally worthless. Rio says:

> `私はこのままがいい`

The phase therefore reverses Volume 9. Sakuta is no longer outside a happier life refusing to steal it. A rewritten life has arrived around him, and people inside it possess reasons to keep it.

Miori's history then reveals that serial possibility functions as a refuge from irreversibility. After the final Toko song is exhausted, reality itself stops remaining final.

## 6.3 Volume 15: the ghost becomes a pattern

The finale resolves the major ethical architecture in three linked movements.

### Miori grieves rather than engineers resurrection

The complete song contains both:

> `君に出会わなければよかった`

and:

> `君に出会えてよかった`

Miori finally cries and says `透子を返して`. The impossible demand is permitted as grief rather than treated as a world-selection command.

### Desirable possibilities return to authorship

Uzuki says:

> `今度は、現実にしよう`

and:

> `自分で摑み取ってこその武道館だよ`

The epilogue later gives Sweet Bullet a real Budokan performance through ordinary history—and complicates it by making it their final concert. Reality is not an inferior version of the dream. It is a history in which meaning emerges from time, work, and other people's freedom.

### Sakuta changes his relation to Syndrome

Counterpart Sakuta asks for denial. Sakuta refuses and chooses:

> `思春期症候群を思い出に変えるだけだ`

Mai's ceiling-pattern metaphor supplies the form. The pattern remains; the ghost no longer needs to appear.

The final adult test is Ebina. Sakuta cannot see her ghost. He believes her and offers to listen.


# 7. The title-heroine serial structure
The repeated title pattern can initially resemble a rescue-series or harem architecture:

> one girl → one Syndrome → Sakuta intervenes → one emotional resolution.

The completed series complicates that impression in four ways.

## 7.1 Earlier heroines remain active

Rio, Tomoe, Nodoka, Uzuki, Ikumi, Sara, Nene, and Miori continue to:

- work;
- study;
- advise;
- disagree;
- assist;
- form relationships;
- and make choices beyond their title volumes.

They become a cumulative social world rather than a collection of solved cases.

## 7.2 Sakuta does not become the universal romantic endpoint

Tomoe explicitly chooses to graduate from attachment to him. Rio's adult romantic future develops elsewhere. Nene/Takumi, Kunimi/Saki, and Miori/Toko/Sakuta friendship structures resist protagonist-centered romantic capture.

## 7.3 The title heroine often changes Sakuta

The flow of care becomes reciprocal:

- Mai gives him shared-future ethics and the final adulthood metaphor.
- Shoko gives him language of kindness and validates his desire to live.
- Rio models uncertainty and disciplined revision.
- Hanakaede/Kaede teach him the difference between love and replacement.
- Miori makes him confront memory, grief, and friendship.

## 7.4 The final “case” validates institutional inheritance

Ebina is not given a full new volume. Her function is to show that the lessons can outlive the serialized heroine structure. Sakuta can now meet the next adolescent inside an institution.


# 8. The Sakuta/Mai continuous spine
The series would be structurally weaker if Mai were merely the first heroine and recurring girlfriend. She is the stable co-protagonist who carries the continuity across otherwise rotating cases.

The relationship's architecture is:

1. Sakuta sees Mai when others do not.
2. Mai delays acceptance so the relationship must survive ordinary time.
3. She becomes involved in later cases as an agent, not an ornament.
4. She refuses Sakuta's unilateral sacrifice.
5. She defines love as `ふたりで幸せになる`.
6. She helps rebuild family and ordinary life.
7. She trusts Sakuta against her own Toko self-perception.
8. She gives the ceiling-pattern metaphor.
9. She asks for `同じ景色`.
10. The final image gives two side-by-side footprints.

The romance therefore develops from:

> exceptional recognition

into:

> shared reality without fusion.


# 9. Escalation of phenomenon scale
| Scale | Representative cases | Architectural effect |
|---|---|---|
| individual social presence | Mai, Tomoe | makes ambient social pressure literal |
| divided/compared self | Rio, Nodoka/Mai | separates body, desire, role, and personhood |
| family/autobiographical continuity | Kaede/Hanakaede, V9 | makes recognition a family and historical problem |
| competing future lives | Shoko, V7 | gives possible persons moral standing |
| alternate biography | V9, Ikumi | distinguishes authored life from attractive substitution |
| public prestige identity | Nene/Toko | shows role recognition can erase biography |
| crowd/institution | mass Santas | makes nonrecognition physically infrastructural |
| world-state | R1/Miori/Sakuta | makes collective possibility and observation reality-shaping |
| adult institution | Ebina/Sakuta | relocates the lesson from private miracle to public responsibility |

The escalation is not simply “bigger powers.” Each new scale tests whether the recognition ethic still works when more people, records, bodies, or histories disagree.


# 10. Escalation of recognition scale
The recognition problem progresses through distinct layers:

1. **sensory perception** — can anyone see Mai?
2. **social attention** — is the person treated as a subject or background?
3. **memory** — can the person be remembered?
4. **biographical recognition** — is the correct continuity being named?
5. **role recognition** — is a function being mistaken for a person?
6. **institutional recognition** — can systems count the people physically present?
7. **epistemic trust** — can testimony be taken seriously without shared perception?

The final layer does not replace the earlier ones. It is what remains available when the adult no longer has privileged sensory access.


# 11. From rescue to distributed care
Sakuta's interventions begin with real courage and real danger. The series does not later reveal that he should have done nothing. It distinguishes:

- noticing from owning;
- helping from deciding;
- risk from martyrdom;
- information from entitlement;
- success from ethical legitimacy.

The care architecture changes through several corrections:

- Mai makes sacrifice reciprocal.
- Hanakaede's school decision belongs to Hanakaede.
- the bonuses show supported autonomy.
- Ikumi shows that needing to be the helper can become self-erasure.
- Sara shows that information access can violate relationship.
- Nene's trophy and Miori's diary create difficult archive ethics.
- institutions increasingly carry care through teaching, medicine, nursing, firefighting, and donor mediation.

The adult endpoint is not reduced emotional intensity. It is **repeatability**. Care should be compatible with the caregiver's continued life.


# 12. From adolescence to adulthood
AoButa does not define adulthood as no longer caring about adolescent experience.

Nor does it define adulthood as legal age. Sakuta turns twenty before he knows what to do.

The developmental sequence is:

- protect oneself by withdrawing from the social network;
- recognize others through exceptional proximity;
- overidentify helping with personal responsibility;
- discover one's own life has standing;
- accept relational limits and distributed care;
- preserve memory without requiring the phenomenon to remain current;
- enter an institution able to hear the next person.

Sakuta's smartphone adoption and research topic express this shift especially well. He does not remain outside society forever. He chooses how to reenter it and turns `みんな` into a question rather than an authority.


# 13. Phase-transition control matrix
The four phases should not be treated as four unrelated genres. Each takes the same governing concerns—recognition, personhood, care, social pressure, and future authorship—and changes their scale.

| Phase | Dominant social unit | Literal scale of phenomenon | Principal personhood problem | Typical Sakuta role | Mature corrective introduced |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| I — V1–5 | school, peer group, family | person/body/local loop | who is real when visibility, body, and autobiography diverge? | exceptional recognizer and invasive helper | difference must be recognized rather than normalized away |
| II — V6–9 | couple, family, possible future | death, donor history, adjacent life | whose life may be spent for another, and who owns a possible biography? | sacrificial protector learning reciprocity | one's own life counts; family is reconstructed rather than restored |
| Bonus bridge | household, workplace, ordinary public space | little or no new supernatural escalation | can recovery become an ordinary range of action? | scaffold-builder rather than crisis solver | support can preserve autonomy; remembrance need not command imitation |
| III — V10–12 | university, work, peer networks | social attunement, alternate biography, shared interior access | how much social or interpersonal knowledge may govern the self? | tutor, investigator, increasingly bounded helper | empathy requires limits; love requires opacity |
| IV — V13–15 | mass public, media network, world state, institution | crowd, identity replication, reality rewrite | can collective recognition or desirable possibility overwrite particular lives? | observer implicated in the problem; later adult listener | possibility becomes memory and self-authored aspiration rather than compulsory reality |

This matrix guards against two misleading accounts.

The first says the series simply “gets bigger.” It does, but scale alone is not the point. The crowd and world phenomena of the final phase remain legible because the series keeps returning to the same interpersonal question first posed by Mai: **who gets to say what is happening to this person?**

The second says the later cosmology replaces the school-social drama. It does not. The final observer crisis is generated by the same ethical habit that saved Mai: Sakuta refuses to dismiss another person's impossible experience. The finale has to mature that habit without repudiating it.


# 14. The title-heroine structure and the continuous-series spine are deliberately asymmetrical
AoButa uses two narrative organizations at once.

## 14.1 Local title-heroine organization

A title usually identifies the character whose crisis supplies the volume's most visible conflict. This creates strong local closure and a recognizable serial rhythm.

The title heroine is not necessarily:

- the sole person transformed;
- Sakuta's romantic alternative;
- or the only character carrying the volume's governing idea.

Volume 11, for example, is structurally “about” Ikumi, but it also revises Sakuta's relation to middle-school harm, forgiveness, professional care, alternate worlds, and public reckoning. Volume 15 is titled around the “dear friend,” yet its final argument belongs equally to Miori/Toko, Sakuta/Mai, Kaede/Hanakaede, the ensemble, and Ebina.

## 14.2 Continuous Sakuta/Mai and Sakuta/family spine

The second organization is longitudinal. Mai, the Azusagawa family, Rio, Kunimi, Shoko, and later the university ensemble persist across cases. Their continuity prevents the title-volume format from becoming anthology.

Mai's importance is especially architectural. She is:

- the first recognition crisis;
- Sakuta's romantic partner;
- an independent professional subject;
- a critic of his rescue ethics;
- a participant in family reconstruction;
- the target of public identity rewrite;
- and the source of the final ceiling-pattern and shared-scenery formulations.

She therefore links the first volume's question to the final volume's answer.

## 14.3 Earlier title heroines become later agents

The series outgrows its serial-case appearance when former title heroines stop existing only as solved problems.

- Rio becomes a recurring model-builder and friend.
- Tomoe becomes coworker, witness, and later an independent young adult.
- Nodoka becomes sister, idol, and practical member of the care network.
- Uzuki articulates the final self-authorship answer to dream-Budokan.
- Ikumi becomes logistical and interpretive support in Nene's case.
- Sara contributes ordinary observation after losing supernatural usefulness.
- Nene becomes an announcer through work rather than borrowed identity.
- Miori becomes a friend whose absence is carried by song.

This longitudinal agency is the strongest reason the final corpus should not be organized as fifteen isolated “girls.”


# 15. Scale escalation never abolishes ordinary-life evidence
The most reliable evidence of development is often quieter than the Syndrome climax.

Sakuta's maturation appears in:

- asking his father for university help;
- learning to drive so Mai need not always drive;
- working as a tutor;
- refusing to read Miori's diary;
- buying a smartphone for chosen contact;
- entering a classroom as an education trainee.

Hanakaede's maturation appears in:

- email;
- a short-term shift;
- a bank deposit;
- choosing a school;
- saying “not yet” to indefinite work;
- entering university.

Mai and Sakuta's romance appears in:

- meals;
- schedules;
- transport;
- teasing;
- work interruptions;
- domestic planning;
- and the expectation that both careers will continue.

The architecture therefore treats ordinary life not as decompression between “real” plots but as the place where supernatural insight becomes sustainable biography.


# 16. Controlled correction categories for the specialist corpus
Later volumes alter earlier claims in several different ways. The corpus should use the following distinctions consistently.

| Category | Definition | AoButa example |
|---|---|---|
| `GENUINE_DEVELOPMENT` | the character's disposition, capacity, or relationship changes through experience | Sakuta moves from unilateral rescue toward adult listening |
| `REVELATION_OF_PRIOR_STATE` | later evidence discloses something that was already true within the biography | Miori's childhood relation to Toko |
| `RETROSPECTIVE_RECONTEXTUALIZATION` | a prior fact remains true but acquires different significance | Sakuta's anti-smartphone stance becomes relevant to observer continuity |
| `WORLD_STATE_CHANGE` | the biography itself differs because reality/world state differs | R1 Rio/Kunimi, R1 Kaede |
| `RECOGNITION_STATE_CHANGE` | perception, memory, or social legibility changes without ordinary biography alone explaining it | Mai invisibility; Nene/Toko; returned Ikumi |
| `AUTOBIOGRAPHICAL_CONTINUITY_CHANGE` | the first-person remembered subject changes while body or records may persist | Kaede/Hanakaede |
| `PERFORMANCE_OR_ROLE_CHANGE` | public role changes without necessarily changing the whole person | public Toko; Mai's acting roles |
| `RELATIONSHIP_SPECIFIC_EXPRESSION` | a trait becomes visible only within a sufficiently trusted relationship | Mai's domestic teasing and vulnerability with Sakuta |
| `UNRESOLVED_MIXTURE` | more than one category remains plausible | Miori's cross-world status; the red-randoseru girl |

This classification prevents “character development” from becoming a catch-all. It also protects the local first-pass artifacts from retrospective laundering.


# 17. Refined architecture thesis
The Phase-3 refinement yields a more exact formulation of the full series.

AoButa begins with **social reality failing one person**. It then separates the normally aligned elements by which society recognizes persons—body, memory, name, role, history, and shared world—until no single element can function as an unquestioned identity test. It expands possibility far enough that even a better world can become a threat when imposed. It then returns to ordinary adult practice.

The completed progression is not merely:

> seeing → believing.

It is:

> **perception → recognition → differentiation → moral standing → self-authorship → memory → institutional trust.**

The final teacher does not possess less ethical knowledge because he possesses less exceptional perception. He possesses a more transferable form of it.


# 18. What later volumes genuinely correct
Later evidence materially overturns several early models. The detailed history belongs to Document 19, but the architectural corrections are:

- **Mai restoration:** public attention is important but not a universal cure law.
- **Shoko:** older Shoko is not simply a visitor from one fixed future.
- **Toko:** Nene is not the originating Toko; the public artist is a Toko/Miori composite.
- **Miori:** she is not finally established as the direct reality author; counterpart Sakuta assigns observer significance to current Sakuta.
- **R1:** it is not safely described as one ordinary alternate timeline.
- **smartphones:** concentrated relevance does not become a simple phone-causes-Syndrome rule.
- **adulthood:** the ending is not denial or disenchantment.


# 19. What later volumes deepen rather than overturn
Other claims survive because later volumes enlarge their scope:

- recognition is not visual presence;
- social construction is not necessarily inauthenticity;
- body continuity is not sufficient for personhood;
- love does not confer total jurisdiction;
- self-sacrifice is not automatically morally superior;
- ordinary life is a substantive good;
- memory objects preserve access without containing the person;
- possible lives can matter without becoming destiny.

The final synthesis should distinguish these expansions from genuine corrections.


# 20. Strongest counterreading: episodic harem formula rather than cumulative architecture
The strongest skeptical reading says that AoButa remains fundamentally a market-friendly sequence of attractive heroines whose psychological problems are externalized into convenient supernatural arcs, with Sakuta's wit and romance supplying continuity. The escalation to possible worlds and mass identity may then look like franchise inflation rather than organic development.

That reading has real support:

- the repeated title-heroine format;
- Sakuta's centrality to many female crises;
- recurring sexualized focalization and illustrations;
- mechanical rules that remain case-specific;
- several resolutions hinging on Sakuta's unusually privileged position.

The cumulative architecture nonetheless exceeds the formula for three reasons.

First, title heroines retain post-arc agency and increasingly help one another and Sakuta. Second, the series repeatedly criticizes Sakuta's indispensability, eventually making it a problem. Third, the final ethical test removes his special sight. The ending would be incoherent if the whole project were only “Sakuta sees special girls better than everyone else.”

The harem/rescue surface is therefore not imaginary, but the series gradually reworks it into an ensemble and an adult care ethic.


# 21. Final-series architecture: seeing, remembering, believing
The architecture can finally be condensed into three movements.

## Seeing

The adolescent beginning depends on exceptional perception.

Sakuta sees Mai.

He sees the person before the group does.

## Remembering

The middle and final arcs make memory more important than immediate visibility.

Kaede, future Shoko, Toko, possible lives, and prior worlds continue to matter because they remain active in biography, objects, places, and relationships.

Sakuta's answer is not to keep every impossible state present. It is to let the experience become memory.

## Believing

The adult endpoint no longer requires sight.

Ebina asks whether Sakuta sees what she sees.

He does not.

He believes her and offers to listen.

The final recognition ethic is therefore:

> **Take the historically particular person seriously before demanding that your own senses, the crowd, or the institution settle every fact.**

That answer preserves the ethical truth of Volume 1 without requiring Sakuta to remain trapped in Volume 1's mode of access.


# 22. Controlled terminology lock
Phase 3 requires a common vocabulary before the character documents can begin. The following terms are now fixed for the remainder of the specialist corpus unless later primary evidence forces revision.

| Term | Controlled use | Excluded shortcut |
|---|---|---|
| **attention** | directed gaze, fame, publicity, or awareness | recognition |
| **recognition** | treating a historically particular person or continuity as socially and ethically real | mere visibility |
| **observation / `観測`** | diegetic character vocabulary for access to a state or possibility | narrator-certified quantum measurement |
| **world state** | a coherent experienced arrangement of biographies and records | every case being called a timeline |
| **rewrite / `書き換え`** | revision of an already legible reality/history | ordinary future change |
| **continuity** | persistence along one or more identity axes | guaranteed metaphysical sameness |
| **personhood** | the historically particular subject carried through body, memory, relation, record, and choice | any one substrate alone |
| **resolution** | change in relation to conflict sufficient to end or transform manifestation | proof that the problem was unreal |
| **adulthood** | increased capacity for ordinary responsibility, reflection, and care | disenchantment or emotional completion |

The phase documents should therefore use `R0`, `R1`, `RF`, and `W1` consistently and avoid silently replacing them with “the real timeline,” “the fake timeline,” or “the original universe.” `R0` is the principal continuity remembered through Volume 13; `R1` is the composite rewritten state; `RF` is the final stabilized adult history; `W1` is the specific adjacent counterpart world.


# 23. Evidence and counterevidence refinement
The initial architecture draft correctly identified cumulative progression, but the final lock requires four qualifications.

## 23.1 Escalation is not simple improvement

Later volumes are larger in ontological scale, but they are not automatically more psychologically mature. Volume 1's school-level recognition problem remains structurally present in the final Ebina scene. The series expands scale while repeatedly returning to the same interpersonal question:

> Will one person take another person's experience seriously before the group has agreed what it means?

## 23.2 The title-heroine structure remains commercially legible

The cumulative reading does not erase the market-facing form. The novels continue to feature one prominently titled girl, romantic/sexual comedy, and illustrations shaped by bishōjo convention. The stronger claim is not that AoButa ceases to have a heroine-of-the-volume surface. It is that the surface becomes narratively insufficient because title heroines retain lives, occupations, friendships, and later care functions after their own crises.

## 23.3 Ethical progress does not erase Sakuta's boundary failures

Sakuta's development should not be narrated as a smooth transition from bad intervention to good listening. Even in the university arc he uses deception against Ikumi, withholds the reciprocal nature of Sara's access, and preserves Nene's trophy against her instruction. The mature endpoint is credible because regression and residue remain visible.

## 23.4 RF is not morally clean restoration

The finale's preference for authored continuity over magical optimization is strong, but R1 losses remain real analytic residue. Rio's `私はこのままがいい` and R1 Kaede's current personhood prevent the ending from becoming an uncomplicated return to “the correct world.” The final corpus should treat the ethical resolution as stronger than the metaphysical accounting.

# 25. Phase-3 refinement lock
The architecture is now considered **Phase-3 final-locked** under the following proposition:

> **AoButa begins with exceptional perception because the adolescent social world has failed to recognize a particular person. It ends by converting that exceptional perception into memory, ordinary competence, and an adult willingness to believe testimony without requiring shared sight.**

This lock preserves three non-negotiable tensions:

1. the impossible events are literal within the narrative and literary in function;
2. socially constituted identity can be genuine while collective recognition can still overwrite persons;
3. the final ethical answer is clearer than the final cosmology.


# 26. Final synthesis
AoButa's cumulative achievement is not that it discovers one secret explanation for adolescence. It builds an increasingly demanding test of what it means to recognize a person when the usual evidence fails.

A body may point to the wrong sister.

A memory may belong to another world.

A name may become a prestige role anyone can occupy.

A future may be real enough to grieve and not rightful enough to impose.

A dead friend's song may preserve relation while also hiding authorship.

A more desirable world may contain real persons and still violate self-authorship.

Across these cases, the series refuses two opposing reductions.

It refuses to say:

> only the inner self is real and society is merely false.

Its characters are deeply socially made.

It also refuses to say:

> whatever society recognizes is therefore the person.

Society repeatedly recognizes the wrong body, role, biography, future, or consensus.

The mature person is relationally constituted and still entitled to self-authorship.

That is why the finale's ordinary futures matter. Budokan, announcing, nursing, firefighting, university, teaching, cooking, driving, and calling one another are not anticlimax. They are the conditions under which identity becomes something people continue making rather than something crisis temporarily proves.

The full series begins with a boy who can see.

It ends with a teacher who cannot see and still listens.

That is not a retreat from the supernatural.

It is the point at which the supernatural has successfully become ethical knowledge.
