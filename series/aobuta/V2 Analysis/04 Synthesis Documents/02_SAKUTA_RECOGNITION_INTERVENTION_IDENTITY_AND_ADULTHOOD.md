# 02. Sakuta: Recognition, Intervention, Identity, and Adulthood
## The exceptional witness who becomes an ordinary adult listener

## Governing question

> **How does Sakuta move from being valuable because he sees what others cannot toward being valuable because he can take another person seriously even when he cannot share their perception?**


# 1. Executive thesis
Sakuta's development is often described as the maturation of a kind, sarcastic boy who repeatedly helps girls suffering from Adolescence Syndrome. That description is not wrong, but it conceals the conflict that makes him one of the series' most coherent protagonists.

His strongest virtue and his most persistent danger are the same disposition:

> **When another person is suffering and the surrounding world refuses to respond, Sakuta treats the failure as permission—sometimes obligation—to act.**

That disposition allows him to recognize Mai when everyone else treats her as `空気`. It makes him willing to accompany Tomoe through socially dangerous repetition, refuse to choose one “real” Rio, care for Kaede, and take Shoko's impossible future seriously. It also makes him intrusive, manipulative, and dangerously ready to spend his own body. He lies, stages situations, reads or seeks private information, uses other people's needs as investigative leverage, and repeatedly assumes that the absence of a better responder makes him the rightful decision-maker.

The complete arc is therefore not:

> selfish loner → selfless hero.

Nor is it:

> cynical boy → emotionally open boyfriend.

It is:

> **socially injured witness → exceptional intervener → sacrificial rescuer → boundary interpreter → distributed helper → adult listener.**

The final Sakuta is not less willing to care. He is less committed to three early assumptions:

1. that seeing the problem gives him sole jurisdiction over it;
2. that his own life is the easiest morally acceptable cost;
3. that useful information automatically justifies access.

His adult vocation gives the change institutional form. Education-trainee Sakuta cannot see the person or ghost Ebina Rin reports. He says:

> `僕には見えない。でも、君を信じるよ`

and offers to listen.

This is a more mature form of recognition than Volume 1's exceptional sight. Sakuta no longer needs privileged perception to refuse abandonment. He can distinguish:

- believing the person;
- from certifying every explanation;
- listening;
- from taking ownership of the person's life.

His final distinction is therefore not supernatural access.

It is knowledge of what disbelief can do.


# 2. Middle-school social injury: the origin of Sakuta's ethics and defenses
Sakuta enters Volume 1 after a social collapse that remains partially offstage but governs his behavior across the series. His mother's breakdown, Hanakaede's crisis, his scars, disbelief from classmates, and the circulation of impossible explanations make him a person whose testimony has already failed under ordinary social standards.

This history produces at least four durable adaptations.

## 2.1 Refusal of ambient authority

Sakuta's early declaration:

> `もう、空気なんて読んでやるか`

is not a claim that he cannot read the room. He usually reads it very well. He notices hierarchy, attraction, shame, fear, avoidance, and social risk. What he rejects is the unowned authority of the room: the idea that because “everyone” behaves as though something is settled, no individual must justify the judgment.

This is why he can recognize Mai. The school treats her as air. Sakuta's prior injury makes that atmosphere less authoritative to him than it would be to a socially secure student.

## 2.2 Sarcasm as protection and test

Sakuta's humor is not only comic characterization. It performs several jobs:

- keeps pity at a distance;
- converts embarrassment into something he can control;
- tests whether another person can tolerate directness;
- makes intimacy deniable;
- and allows him to say emotionally charged things without fully exposing the need beneath them.

With Mai, sarcasm becomes mutual play and eventually intimacy. With strangers, it can remain abrasive. With a person in crisis, it can either cut through social falseness or make him appear less careful than he is.

## 2.3 Anti-smartphone withdrawal

Sakuta's refusal to own a smartphone is initially a boundary created after social-media and peer-group harm. The device represents:

- rumor;
- ambient surveillance;
- hostile group communication;
- the constant availability of other people's judgments.

It is a defensive adaptation, not a philosophical proof that analog life is purer.

The finale confirms this by letting him revise the boundary. He buys a smartphone after deciding what kind of adult he wants to be. His first call is to Mai. His number becomes a promise of future friendship to Miori. The same medium that once represented social jurisdiction becomes chosen reachability.

## 2.4 Identification with unsupported testimony

Most important, Sakuta acquires a standing presumption:

> the fact that a claim sounds impossible does not make the claimant dishonest or unworthy of help.

This presumption is ethically valuable because the series repeatedly places real impossible events behind apparently unbelievable testimony.

It is also dangerous if converted into:

> therefore Sakuta's first interpretation must be correct.

His mature arc consists partly in preserving the presumption of care while increasing epistemic restraint.


# 3. Volume 1: exceptional recognition and heroic overreach begin together
Sakuta's role in Mai's first crisis contains both the best and worst tendencies of his early intervention model.

## 3.1 What he gets right

He treats Mai's experience as real before a complete explanation exists. He observes patterns, compares conditions, involves Rio, and refuses to let the school's atmosphere determine Mai's personhood.

The crucial act is not that he immediately “solves” her. It is that he remains in relation long enough for evidence and trust to accumulate.

He recognizes a distinction that the school does not:

> Mai can be nationally famous and still be socially unrecognized as a person.

This becomes the foundation of his ethics.

## 3.2 Public confession as intervention and coercion

Sakuta's school-wide confession forces attention and correlates with Mai's restoration. It is courageous. It is also invasive.

He publicly binds:

- Mai's identity;
- their relationship;
- and his own social standing

into a spectacle she did not fully author.

The scene works because the atmosphere itself is part of the crisis and because Mai responds. That success does not create a general rule that public romantic spectacle is justified whenever another person is socially erased.

The ethical pattern is set early:

> **Sakuta's interventions are often effective before they are fully legitimate.**

## 3.3 The first rescuer asymmetry

Mai initially depends upon Sakuta's perception in a literal way. This creates an asymmetry that the romance later has to outgrow.

If the relationship remained “the girl exists because the boy sees her,” Mai would never become a co-equal partner. The later series repeatedly reverses the direction of recognition and rescue until Mai becomes one of the people who recognizes Sakuta when he cannot recognize his own moral limits.


# 4. Sakuta's intervention habits in Volumes 2–4
The next three volumes test whether his anti-`空気` stance can become care without turning every problem into his jurisdiction.

## 4.1 Tomoe: selective disobedience and protective lying

Sakuta understands Tomoe's fear of social exclusion. He agrees to fake-dating arrangements, participates in repeated future states, and protects her from immediate humiliation.

His strength is that he does not romanticize an imagined “original” Tomoe. He recognizes the present self she has deliberately made:

> `それが古賀だよ。前がどうだろうと、今のその姿が古賀`

This is one of his earliest strong personhood judgments. Social construction does not make the current self fake.

His weakness is that he is comfortable using lies as social engineering. The strategy is defensible in context, but it reinforces his assumption that he may manage the room if he has correctly understood the person's needs.

## 4.2 Rio: refusing selection, violating privacy

Sakuta's best response to Rio's split is:

> `僕は双葉理央の味方だ`

He refuses to decide which body expresses the authentic Rio. He helps create conditions in which the two states can recognize that the disowned desire belongs to one person.

Yet the same arc exposes his boundary problem. He uses phones, photographs, and private information instrumentally. The series itself does not require the reader to excuse every violation because integration follows.

The lesson is mixed:

- participation can be necessary;
- information can save;
- information access still has ethical cost.

## 4.3 Nodoka/Mai: biography beyond body

Sakuta distinguishes Mai and Nodoka when public recognition follows the wrong body. He knows the sisters through:

- speech;
- habits;
- professional competence;
- memory;
- relational history.

This is important development in recognition.

At the same time, he again presses against a direct privacy boundary. `勝手に見ないで` is explicit. The fact that private material helps the sisters does not make the access automatically harmless.

By the end of Volume 4, Sakuta has become better at respecting **choice** and still remains too willing to violate **information boundaries**.


# 5. Kaede caregiving: devotion, burden, and the hidden son
The Kaede/Hanakaede history gives Sakuta a role more sustained than any one title-volume rescue.

He is not only the boy who notices a supernatural case. He is a teenage caregiver inside a family whose adults have partly collapsed.

## 5.1 What Sakuta provides

He provides:

- environmental safety;
- routine;
- food;
- accompaniment;
- interpretation of the outside world;
- recognition of `かえで` as a person rather than only illness;
- and persistence during gradual public exposure.

His naming of Kaede and his willingness to take her goals seriously create social room in which she can become a subject.

## 5.2 The danger of making Sakuta the whole care system

The family arrangement also overburdens him.

He becomes:

- brother;
- quasi-parent;
- crisis manager;
- liaison;
- emotional shield.

His father's trust in him is loving and unfair. His mother's condition is understandable and still leaves him carrying work no teenager should have to carry alone.

This is why Volume 9 matters so much. Sakuta's competence has hidden the son who still wants recognition and support from his parents.

## 5.3 Kaede's disappearance and Sakuta's bereavement

When Hanakaede returns, Sakuta does not simply celebrate recovery. He experiences Kaede's active continuity as gone.

That grief validates his earlier recognition: he had not been caring for a disposable temporary mask.

Older Shoko's intervention after Sakuta collapses is one of the first times the series clearly reverses the care direction. The rescuer must be rescued.


# 6. Shoko and the violence inside beautiful self-sacrifice
The Shoko duology is the moral center of Sakuta's development because it makes his willingness to spend himself literal.

## 6.1 Gratitude becomes existential debt

Sakuta believes Shoko saved him during his earlier crisis. He therefore treats his own future heart and life as something that may legitimately be returned.

This is gratitude transformed into debt:

> she gave me a life;
> therefore my life is available to save hers.

The thought sounds noble because it takes obligation seriously. It is dangerous because it excludes Sakuta as a person with independent standing.

## 6.2 `生きていたい`

Volume 6's most important change is Sakuta finally saying he wants to live.

This does not instantly settle the decision. It establishes that his desire counts as evidence rather than moral weakness.

The series thereby rejects an ideal in which the cleanest proof of love or gratitude is self-erasure.

## 6.3 Mai creates shared moral jurisdiction

Mai's objection is sometimes reduced to selfish romantic preference. That misses the ethical development.

Because she loves Sakuta and is building a future with him, his life is not solely his private sacrificial resource. Her standing does not mean she owns him. It means his decisions create consequences inside a shared relationship.

The crucial development is procedural. In Volume 7, Sakuta tells Mai before taking the next world-changing step. He no longer treats secrecy as kindness.

This is one of his largest advances:

> **partnership requires sharing moral jurisdiction before acting, not asking forgiveness after the sacrifice.**

## 6.4 Surviving without innocence

Sakuta's final Shoko-arc position is not “I chose correctly and everyone is saved.” He knows the third path may contain hidden cost.

This matters later when Toko's donor history appears. The series refuses to grant him causal purity.

Sakuta matures not by proving survival harmed nobody, but by learning to live responsibly without impossible certainty.


# 7. Volume 7–9: recognition turns back upon Sakuta
The series repeatedly makes Sakuta undergo the structures he has witnessed in others.

## 7.1 Invisible Sakuta

In the future state, Sakuta becomes socially imperceptible. He has to ask Tomoe:

> `僕を見つけてくれよ！`

Her recognition restores wider visibility.

The inversion teaches him that being the witness and being the unseen person are not stable roles.

## 7.2 Two Sakutas and the limits of self-certainty

The Shoko architecture and later W1 family world produce multiple Sakutas with different histories.

Current Sakuta sometimes judges the other version more competent. Yet the series gradually questions whether problem-solving competence is the only moral standard.

The adult Sakuta's ability to say:

> I do not know how long your recovery will take;
> I cannot see what you see;
> I will listen

may be more ethically mature than always finding the fastest solution.

## 7.3 Volume 9: the caretaker becomes the son

Sakuta's alternate family world and mother's nonrecognition expose a need he has hidden inside competence.

He has spent years acting as though the family only needs him as caretaker. He discovers that he wants:

- his name spoken;
- his effort recognized;
- a place as son rather than only helper.

The resolution does not restore the untouched family. It creates a new relationship among people changed by rupture.

Sakuta's promise to keep returning even before recognition is a form of care. His mother's eventual `咲太` is also something he receives.


# 8. From rescuer to option-builder in Volume 8 and the bonuses
Hanakaede's school decision is a major test of whether Sakuta can help without authoring the future.

He researches correspondence education, arranges information, speaks with adults, creates options, and still has to relinquish the final decision.

The decisive thought is:

> `決めるのは咲太じゃない。花楓が決めることだ。`

This does not make him a perfect nonintervener. He still stages meetings and withholds some plans. But the ethical direction is clear:

> **provide access, tell relevant truth, remain available, and let the person choose.**

*Animal Land* and *Spring Days* make this development mundane.

Sakuta supports:

- a zoo visit;
- gradual memory discussion;
- a part-time job;
- workplace scaffolding;
- bounded commitments.

The strongest sign of his growth is sometimes silence. When Hanakaede says she is not ready for indefinite work, Sakuta does not convert successful exposure into obligation.


# 9. University Sakuta: teaching, boundaries, and the temptation of usefulness
The university arc gives Sakuta a social identity not derived from being the only witness to a supernatural crisis.

He becomes:

- student;
- tutor;
- coworker;
- future teacher;
- friend within a larger network.

## 9.1 Tutor ethics

His best teaching principle appears when he tells Sara that what matters is helping her, even if somebody else would be the better instructor.

This is a direct correction to the indispensable-helper model exposed through Ikumi.

The compulsive helper needs to remain needed.

The good teacher can become unnecessary and still have succeeded.

## 9.2 Uzuki and boundary interpretation

Sakuta helps Uzuki not by telling her to return to ignorance of social judgment but by distinguishing:

- the anonymous average;
- from the concrete people who continue choosing her.

His climactic `今こそ空気を読めよ、づっきー` is not an instruction to obey society. It asks her to read the actual relational field rather than the abstract `みんな` inside her head.

## 9.3 Ikumi and the failed helper

Sakuta recognizes Ikumi's compensatory helping because it resembles his own history.

His question:

> how many people must you save before the regret disappears?

is also a question he could ask himself.

Yet he still manipulates Ikumi through a fake distress post. His ability to diagnose over-responsibility does not make him immune to exploiting it.

## 9.4 Sara and reciprocal privacy violation

Sara's Syndrome makes interior access literal. Sakuta discovers the connection works both ways and withholds that fact. He later uses the access to listen to Sara and Mai.

The protective motive—fear that Mai is in danger—is understandable.

The act remains a privacy violation.

His later relief that the power disappears is meaningful precisely because the useful capability is ethically corrupting.


# 10. Nene and Miori: connection, archives, and informational restraint
The final arc gives Sakuta two contrasting tests.

## 10.1 Nene: paternalistic archive preservation

Sakuta refuses to throw away Nene's Miss Contest trophy, transports it to Takumi, and helps force a confrontation.

The intervention works. It restores biography and memory.

It also overrides Nene's current instruction.

The final synthesis should not erase the paternalism. Under extraordinary recognition collapse, Sakuta decides that the self discarding the archive is not sufficiently connected to Nene's whole history to make an irreversible choice.

This is defensible emergency reasoning and remains an override.

## 10.2 Miori: refusing the diary

Shoko offers Sakuta the Toko/Miori exchange diary. It might contain decisive evidence.

Sakuta says:

> `……やめとく`

because:

> `美東とは、そろそろ『友達』になりたいと思ってたから`

This is one of the clearest markers of development in the entire series.

Earlier Sakuta often treats useful information as sufficient justification for access.

Here he decides that the relationship he wants to build imposes an epistemic limit.

He wants Miori to tell him.

When Shoko later shows the final page through entrusted custodianship and a specific relational purpose, the circumstances are different.

Sakuta has not adopted an absolute privacy rule. He has learned contextual restraint.


# 11. Sakuta as observer: his virtue becomes the final mechanical danger
Counterpart Sakuta's strongest model says current Sakuta is the observer whose recognition helps actualize possible worlds.

Whether that cosmology is completely correct remains unresolved. Its thematic accuracy is strong.

Sakuta's whole life has trained him to refuse dismissal of impossible realities. That refusal becomes so powerful that it may help keep mutually incompatible possibilities current.

The final test therefore attacks his greatest virtue.

The brutal proposed solution is:

> `今すぐ思春期症候群を否定しろ`

Sakuta refuses because denial would make formative relationships and experiences `なかったこと`.

The adult solution must preserve ethical truth without preserving every supernatural state as present reality.


# 12. `思春期症候群を思い出に変える`
Sakuta's final formula is neither belief forever nor disbelief forever.

> `僕は思春期症候群を思い出に変えるだけだ`

The ceiling-pattern metaphor explains the operation.

A child sees a ghost in the ceiling.

Later the same pattern is seen differently.

The pattern did not vanish.

The experience became memory.

For Sakuta, this means:

- Mai's invisibility mattered;
- Tomoe's futures mattered;
- Rio's split mattered;
- Kaede mattered;
- future Shoko mattered;
- Miori's worlds mattered.

They do not need to remain active supernatural states to remain part of his identity.

This is the point where Sakuta becomes capable of adulthood without cynicism.


# 13. The red-randoseru girl and Sakuta's memory pilgrimage
The girl's literal ontology remains unresolved. Her final function is clearer.

She guides Sakuta through places associated with earlier Syndrome experiences:

- the library;
- aquarium;
- coast;
- lantern.

Sakuta recognizes:

> `あの体験が、自分という人間を作ってくれた`

He lists the people now around him and says life has become lively.

The girl concludes:

> `じゃあ、もう迷子にならないね`

The adolescent witness no longer needs a liminal guide because his identity is now held by memory and a social network rather than by solitary exceptional perception.


# 14. Teaching as the ethical synthesis of Sakuta's life
Sakuta's teacher vocation is not merely career selection.

It translates private ethical knowledge into an institutional role.

He says he wants to become the kind of teacher who can listen to a student confused by a bunny girl only she can see:

> `僕にはもう見えなくても`

This formulation contains the complete maturation.

## 14.1 He no longer needs to be the supernatural expert

The teacher need not solve ontology before preventing isolation.

## 14.2 He has authority and therefore needs humility

As an adult inside school, Sakuta can reproduce the original harm more efficiently than a peer could if he dismisses the student. His experience gives him reason to suspend premature judgment.

## 14.3 He creates a channel rather than possessing the case

With Ebina, he says:

> I cannot see him;
> I believe you;
> I will listen if you are in trouble.

He does not claim:

- that the ghost is certainly real;
- that he alone can help;
- that she must explain herself publicly;
- or that her life is now his project.

This is recognition stripped of heroic possession.


# 15. Smartphone adoption and reflective participation
Sakuta's purchase of a smartphone completes a smaller but important arc.

He does not return to the network because everyone was right and his boundary was childish.

He returns because:

- circumstances changed;
- he has stronger relationships;
- he has a chosen use;
- he no longer needs total withdrawal to preserve selfhood.

The final adult uses:

- messaging;
- video calls;
- music apps;
- research data;
- wireless earphones.

He remains critical of ambient consensus.

The difference is that criticism has become inquiry.


# 16. `みんなってなんだろうね`: from subjection to study
Sakuta's graduation research concerns the current value and position of general opinion visible through social-media posts.

When Takumi asks for the short version, Sakuta says:

> `みんなってなんだろうね`

This is a near-perfect intellectual endpoint.

Young Sakuta experiences “everyone” as:

- disbelief;
- atmosphere;
- judgment;
- unowned social authority.

Adult Sakuta asks:

- who is counted as everyone;
- how apparent consensus forms;
- what traces are treated as general opinion;
- what the category permits people to do.

He has moved from:

> subjection

through:

> refusal

into:

> inquiry.

The social-ontology mechanics of `みんな` belong to Document 09; the university and institutional setting that makes this inquiry possible belongs to Document 14.


# 17. Sexual and heterosexual focalization
Sakuta's narration repeatedly notices women's bodies and uses sexual humor. This should neither be sanitized nor allowed to consume his character analysis.

## 17.1 What it establishes

- Sakuta is heterosexual and embodied rather than a desexualized moral observer.
- Attraction and recognition can coexist.
- His intimacy with Mai includes deliberate erotic teasing.
- His gaze is not distributed identically across every relationship.

## 17.2 Ethical limits

Some jokes or observations risk making another person's vulnerable body into comic material.

His internal narration does not equal action, but the series' publication gaze sometimes amplifies the same body-focused framing.

## 17.3 Why it does not make the series a simple harem subjectivity

Sakuta's romantic commitment remains unusually stable. Attraction does not produce an entitlement to romantic access. Tomoe, Rio, Nodoka, Sara, and others develop independent lives.

The more important question is not whether Sakuta notices bodies.

It is whether he can recognize a person beyond:

- desire;
- appearance;
- role;
- usefulness.

The complete series says increasingly yes, though never without friction.


# 18. Longitudinal intervention audit
The following table makes the development visible without pretending it is linear or complete.

| Case | Sakuta's method | What the method achieves | Boundary/risk cost | Retrospective status |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mai V1 | private accompaniment, investigation, public confession | restores recognition and creates trust | publicly scripts Mai and the relationship | courageous and coercive |
| Tomoe V2 | participates in fake relationship and repeated futures | protects present self and permits confession | deception and social management | mixed, proportionate to limited crisis |
| Rio V3 | refuses real/fake selection, uses phone/photo evidence | supports integration | serious privacy intrusion | ethically mixed |
| Nodoka/Mai V4 | identifies persons beyond bodies, accesses private material | restores differentiation | explicit privacy boundary crossed | insight does not erase overreach |
| Kaede V5 | sustained caregiving, environmental support, recognition | creates safety and ordinary futurity | teenage overburdening, caregiver centralization | loving but structurally unsustainable |
| Shoko V6–7 | offers his own life, later shares decision with Mai | takes Shoko's life seriously; eventually finds nonfatal path | self-erasure, coercive secrecy, bodily danger | decisive correction toward shared jurisdiction |
| Hanakaede V8 | researches options, stages encounters, relinquishes school choice | expands available futures | retains paternalistic choreography | major progress toward supported autonomy |
| mother V9 | returns, witnesses, persists, does not demand instant recognition | permits new family relation | personal emotional risk | mature care without guaranteed reward |
| Uzuki V10 | interprets social field, reframes `空気` | restores chosen aspiration | still directs from privileged insight | effective boundary interpretation |
| Ikumi V11 | investigates, fabricates distress post, recognizes specific person | exposes counterpart crisis and helper compulsion | exploits her inability to ignore suffering | morally mixed |
| Sara V12 | tutors, conceals reciprocal access, listens secretly | helps academically and learns about Mai danger | privacy violation | useful motive does not justify method |
| Nene V13 | preserves trophy, recruits Takumi, forces confrontation | restores biography and social legibility | unconsented archive preservation and staging | emergency paternalism with real cost |
| Miori V14–15 | refuses diary, discloses his own history, offers friendship | permits grief and reciprocal trust | less control, more uncertainty | strongest mature model |
| Ebina epilogue | believes testimony, offers listening | prevents immediate institutional abandonment | no claim to solve ontology | final adult method |

The audit demonstrates that Sakuta does not simply become less interventionist. He becomes more capable of selecting an intervention appropriate to:

- immediacy;
- available consent;
- institutional alternatives;
- privacy;
- the person's own capacity to choose.


# 19. Sakuta's relation-specific modes
Sakuta is not one stable personality expressed identically toward everyone.

## 19.1 With Mai

He is:

- erotic;
- teasing;
- dependent;
- morally answerable;
- increasingly transparent.

Mai is the person most able to challenge his rescue identity without losing relational security.

## 19.2 With Kaede/Hanakaede

He is:

- brother;
- caregiver;
- quasi-parent;
- archive holder;
- later option-builder.

The family role reveals his tenderness and his danger of overfunctioning.

## 19.3 With Rio

He is intellectually trusting enough to seek models and emotionally trusting enough to show confusion.

He also assumes too much permission to access information.

## 19.4 With Tomoe

He is senpai, protector, and person capable of honest romantic refusal.

The relation later becomes ordinary coworker friendship.

## 19.5 With Shoko

He oscillates among:

- rescued child;
- debtor;
- romantic memory;
- protector;
- witness to possible lives.

The relation is where gratitude most nearly becomes self-annihilation.

## 19.6 With Miori

He cannot rely on instant intimacy or rescuer status.

Friendship has to be offered without certainty that she will remain in the same world.

This becomes his most mature nonromantic adult relation.

## 19.7 With students

As tutor and teacher, he must convert person-specific instinct into repeatable practice.

That is the institutional challenge of the epilogue.


# 20. First-person narration: moral authority and blind spots
Because Sakuta is the focalizer, readers often receive his interpretations before any independent correction.

This gives him narrative authority and creates several risks.

## 20.1 Sarcastic compression

He can understate fear, grief, and need through jokes. A scene may sound emotionally light while his body or later action reveals severe distress.

## 20.2 Heterosexual focalization

He notices women through attraction and body. The narration thereby preserves embodiment and adolescent voice. It can also narrow the representation of women if read uncritically.

## 20.3 Rescue-centered interpretation

Sakuta often asks:

> what must I do?

before asking:

> who else has standing or capacity?

Later volumes repeatedly correct the sequence.

## 20.4 Retrospective humility

Adult Sakuta increasingly says:

- I do not know;
- this is only what happened to me;
- I cannot see it.

That linguistic change matters as much as any phenomenon resolution.


# 21. Sakuta and family: competence should not erase need
Sakuta's family role supplies a crucial corrective to the heroic reading.

He is capable because the family needed capability.

That competence can conceal:

- fear;
- anger;
- desire for parental recognition;
- need for rest;
- need to be a son.

Volume 9's family reconstruction therefore does not merely “solve mother Syndrome.” It reveals a Sakuta identity that his own caretaker performance had obscured.

His request for university help and his willingness to return to his mother are ordinary acts of dependence.

They are adult progress, not regression.


# 22. Ordinary competence as anti-sacrificial masculinity
Late Sakuta's most important achievements are intentionally mundane.

- He tutors.
- He plans teacher certification.
- He drives.
- He buys a phone.
- He studies general opinion.
- He listens to a student.

Each replaces the early equation:

> love = willingness to be destroyed

with:

> **care = durable capacity to remain present.**

This is also why the finale's “heroism” is institutional rather than spectacular. A teacher who listens can help more than one person over time without requiring each case to nearly kill him.


# 23. Longitudinal state matrix
The following matrix separates genuine development from changes caused primarily by revelation, world state, or recognition.

| Phase | Dominant Sakuta role | Genuine development | Persistent residue | Non-developmental complication |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| V1–4 | exceptional witness and improvised intervener | learns to reject ambient consensus and recognize constructed/contradictory selves | sarcasm, secrecy, information overreach | phenomena give him privileged evidence unavailable to others |
| V5 | brother-caregiver and quasi-parent | recognizes Kaede as a person and sustains ordinary goals | overfunctioning; treats himself as default care infrastructure | family adults are impaired or absent, making his indispensability partly structural |
| V6–7 | sacrificial debtor and future-state actor | finally states `僕は、生きていたい`; begins sharing decision jurisdiction with Mai | still treats his body as an emergency resource | multiple future states create genuinely impossible choices rather than merely bad judgment |
| V8–9 + bonuses | option-builder, son, and recipient of care | relinquishes Hanakaede's final choice; asks parents for help; accepts family recognition | stages encounters and manages information | adjacent-world/family phenomena expose needs that had always existed |
| V10–12 | tutor, coworker, and university investigator | increasingly accepts bounded roles and the possibility that another helper may be better | deception and privacy violations continue | new social network makes him less uniquely necessary even before he fully accepts it |
| V13–15 | connector, observer, friend, prospective teacher | refuses useful private information; turns Syndrome into memory; believes testimony without shared sight | still responds reflexively to danger and still needs Mai/ensemble grounding | observer status gives his recognition world-level causal significance |
| RF | education trainee and researcher | converts personal injury into institutional listening and inquiry | remains fallible, sarcastic, and relationally dependent | Ebina's ontology is unresolved; his ethical response does not require resolution |

This matrix prevents two distortions.

First, Sakuta does not improve simply because later volumes make him older. Second, not every altered behavior is psychological growth. R1 and counterpart states change what evidence is available, what histories exist, and what other people expect of him. Development is best identified where Sakuta makes a different kind of choice under a recognizably similar pressure.

The clearest examples are:

- V1 public spectacle versus V15 private/institutional listening;
- V6 secret sacrifice versus V7 shared decision jurisdiction;
- V4 private-record access versus V14 refusal to read Miori's diary;
- early self-disposability versus late ordinary competence;
- anti-smartphone withdrawal versus chosen digital participation.


# 24. Intervention audit: effectiveness, legitimacy, and cost
Sakuta's intervention history should not be sorted into “good rescues” and “bad violations” by outcome alone.

| Case | What Sakuta supplies | Why it works | Boundary or cost | Mature retrospective judgment |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mai V1 | belief, investigation, public attention | the social atmosphere is part of the mechanism | public romantic spectacle partly authored for her | courageous and effective; not a general license for public exposure |
| Tomoe V2 | companionship, social cover, honest refusal | he understands the concrete peer risk | lying and managing others' impressions | proportionate to a bounded social crisis, but still managerial |
| Rio V3 | refusal to rank selves, practical coordination | both states need acknowledgment | private/device information used instrumentally | ethically mixed; integration does not erase privacy cost |
| Nodoka/Mai V4 | person-specific recognition and mediation | he distinguishes biography from body | explicit `勝手に見ないで` boundary is crossed | successful but not harmless |
| Kaede V5 | sustained daily care and recognition | recovery requires continuity, patience, and safety | Sakuta is parentified and made indispensable | admirable care inside an unjust family distribution |
| Shoko V6–7 | willingness to take impossible future seriously | conventional disbelief would abandon Shoko | Sakuta offers his own life as resource and withholds plans | moral center of his need to count himself |
| Hanakaede V8 | options, testimony, continued presence | choice becomes possible without false normality | some encounters are staged and plans withheld | major progress because final jurisdiction is relinquished |
| Ikumi V11 | recognition of compulsive helper identity | he sees the self-punishment beneath useful care | fake distress post exploits the same compulsion | diagnostically perceptive and ethically invasive |
| Sara V12 | tutoring and relational clarification | bounded teacher role gives support without romance reward | reciprocal clairvoyant access is not immediately disclosed; later eavesdropping | a serious privacy failure inside otherwise mature teaching behavior |
| Nene V13 | archive preservation, connection to Takumi | biography and relationship restore specificity | trophy is preserved against instruction; confrontation is orchestrated | narratively productive paternalism; outcome does not create consent |
| Miori V14–15 | friendship, autobiographical disclosure, refusal of illicit access | Miori needs durable relation, not another solver | Sakuta still presses and interprets, but accepts opacity | strongest example of relational restraint and nonpossessive care |
| Ebina RF | credibility and listening | the institutional adult refuses premature dismissal | ontology remains unsettled | final mature model: attention before explanation |

The audit shows why the strongest characterization of Sakuta is neither “hero” nor “problematic savior” alone.

His courage is real. So is his tendency to convert urgency into jurisdiction.

The late series does not remove that tension by making him passive. It gives him more methods:

- ask;
- connect;
- wait;
- share information;
- preserve options;
- create institutional access;
- admit uncertainty;
- let another person decide.


# 25. Quiet evidence: Sakuta outside the Syndrome climax
Sakuta's character cannot be reconstructed accurately from climactic rescue scenes alone.

## 25.1 Food and household labor

He cooks for:

- Kaede/Hanakaede;
- Mai;
- Nodoka;
- Shoko;
- himself.

Food is one of the ways his care becomes repeatable rather than spectacular. It also reveals the burden placed upon him. Competence is not neutral evidence that the family arrangement was fair.

## 25.2 Work

The family restaurant gives Sakuta a role with:

- shifts;
- managers;
- coworkers;
- customers;
- bounded responsibility.

This is an important adult counterweight to Syndrome work. At the restaurant, he cannot decide that every distressed stranger belongs to him. He works inside a system.

## 25.3 Tutoring

Tutoring turns his directness into something other than crisis intervention. A student may improve and then leave him. The endpoint is not permanent dependence.

His willingness to recommend another instructor if that person could help more is among the clearest corrections to the indispensable-helper fantasy.

## 25.4 Driving

Driving is a mundane competence with major symbolic weight. Sakuta first learns partly so Mai does not always carry the transport burden. Later he drives Shoko and Miori through histories he cannot control.

He can choose direction.

He cannot choose what reality waits at the destination.

## 25.5 Calling for help

The R1 restaurant collapse is a crucial adult test. The earlier Sakuta might have withdrawn after nobody believed him. Current Sakuta keeps calling until Shoko answers.

The important development is not that he finds the correct magical expert. It is that he refuses to equate dependence with failure.


# 26. Masculinity, heterosexual focalization, and the limits of the “rascal” persona
The title's `青春ブタ野郎` and Sakuta's narration give the series a deliberately adolescent male surface.

He notices:

- breasts;
- legs;
- clothing;
- bodily proximity;
- sexual embarrassment;
- the difference between attraction and emotional safety.

This prevents the narration from becoming falsely disembodied. Sakuta is a heterosexual adolescent and later young man. His desire is part of the relationship with Mai and part of his social perception.

But the focalization creates three limits.

## 26.1 Women's bodies can become comic evidence before their interiority is established

The novels and illustrations sometimes turn bodily exposure into humor or spectacle even when the plot is concerned with gaze, shame, or involuntary embodiment.

## 26.2 Sakuta's perception is not neutral access

He is observant, but he is still a male narrator with preferences, blind spots, and a tendency to classify before asking.

## 26.3 The “rascal” pose can disguise vulnerability

Sexual jokes often let Sakuta avoid saying:

- I am frightened;
- I need you;
- I am jealous;
- I do not know what to do.

Mai's reciprocal banter works because she understands the defense and can answer without being reduced to its object.

The mature Sakuta does not become nonsexual. He becomes better able to let desire coexist with:

- privacy;
- professional respect;
- another person's opacity;
- nonromantic friendship.

Miori is especially important here. She becomes a “dear friend” rather than an unfulfilled romance route, even though the title-heroine format could easily have used her otherwise.


# 27. Observer privilege and protagonist centrality
The final observer model risks giving Sakuta the strongest possible protagonist privilege:

> his recognition can rewrite reality.

This can support a wish-fulfillment reading in which the hero's perception is cosmically authoritative.

The series partly resists that reading in four ways.

## 27.1 Sakuta does not consciously control the results

R1 contains outcomes he did not deliberately choose and costs he does not welcome.

## 27.2 Other observers correct him

Rio, Mai, Shoko, Miori, and counterpart Sakuta repeatedly revise his models.

## 27.3 His preferred world is ethically contested

Rio wants R1. Kaede has current standing. Sakuta cannot simply declare his memory sovereign.

## 27.4 The endpoint removes exceptional perception

The final ethical proof is not another world rewrite.

It is Ebina.

Sakuta cannot see the ghost.

His value survives.

This is the strongest evidence that the series ultimately wants to convert protagonist privilege into a teachable adult practice rather than preserve it as permanent supernatural exceptionalism.

# 29. Strongest counterreadings
## 29.1 Sakuta is merely a wish-fulfillment savior

The strongest evidence for this reading is real:

- many attractive girls disclose vulnerability to him;
- he repeatedly becomes indispensable;
- extraordinary mechanics privilege his perception;
- the narrative remains primarily in his first-person viewpoint.

The counterevidence is longitudinal.

- His interventions have explicit costs and ethical failures.
- Mai challenges rather than rewards self-sacrifice.
- former title heroines develop beyond him;
- he increasingly depends upon an ensemble;
- the final role is listener rather than magical solver.

The series does not fully escape protagonist centrality. It significantly complicates the fantasy.

## 29.2 Sakuta's boundary violations are justified because they work

Rejected.

Effectiveness is one ethical consideration, not retroactive consent.

The corpus should preserve:

- Rio privacy violations;
- Nodoka/Mai cabinet access;
- Sara eavesdropping;
- Nene trophy override;
- fake distress post for Ikumi.

His refusal of the Miori diary matters because it shows he can learn another way.

## 29.3 Sakuta is suicidal until Mai cures him

Too simple.

His self-disposability arises from:

- family burden;
- gratitude to Shoko;
- social injury;
- responsibility reflex;
- repeated reinforcement that his body can solve others' problems.

Mai is essential to the correction, but so are:

- Rio;
- Shoko;
- Kunimi;
- parents;
- the ensemble;
- ordinary adulthood.

## 29.4 Final Sakuta is a perfect therapist-like adult

No.

He is an education trainee who offers to listen. He remains sarcastic, relationally dependent, and fallible.

The finale gives him a sound first response, not proof of perfect future practice.



# 31. Final character synthesis
Sakuta begins the series believing that social reality cannot be trusted when it abandons a suffering person.

He is right.

He then repeatedly makes a second inference:

> if I am the person who sees the problem, I must be the person who solves it.

That is only sometimes right.

The series spends fifteen volumes separating recognition from possession.

Sakuta learns:

- to count his own life;
- to tell Mai before acting;
- to build options instead of choosing for Hanakaede;
- to let institutions and friends help;
- to allow a student or loved one to remain partly opaque;
- to refuse private information even when useful;
- to relinquish supernatural actuality without denying memory.

His final ethical competence is deceptively ordinary.

A student says nobody believes her.

He cannot see what she sees.

The boy from Volume 1 might have feared that without sight he had no evidence.

The adult knows enough to answer:

> `僕には見えない。でも、君を信じるよ`

The line does not make him omniscient.

It makes him available.

That is a more sustainable form of heroism than bleeding until the world changes.
