# 04. Sakuta and Mai: Romance, Mutuality, Shared Reality, and Future
## From recognition to reciprocity

## Governing question

> **What makes Sakuta and Mai's relationship durable, and how does it move from rescuer/rescued asymmetry toward reciprocal adult partnership?**


# 1. Executive thesis
Sakuta and Mai's relationship is durable because the series does not leave it frozen in the emotional logic of Volume 1.

The beginning contains an unavoidable asymmetry:

- Mai is disappearing;
- Sakuta can still see her;
- Sakuta becomes the person whose recognition keeps her socially present;
- his public action contributes to restoration.

If the relationship retained that structure, it would become a rescue romance in which Mai's reality depends permanently upon Sakuta's exceptional status.

Instead, the series gradually converts the relation into a mutual project governed by four increasingly explicit principles.

## 1.1 Crisis intensity is not enough

Mai makes Sakuta repeat his confession after ordinary time passes.

## 1.2 Love does not grant unilateral jurisdiction

Both partners acquire standing in decisions that threaten the other's life or reorganize their shared future.

## 1.3 Protection must become reciprocal

Each protects the other; neither is entitled to disappear as a gift.

## 1.4 Shared reality does not mean fused identity

Their final desire is `同じ景色`: two people inhabiting one world while retaining separate careers, perceptions, and choices.

The relationship therefore develops:

> **recognition → tested attraction → public commitment → conflict and privacy negotiation → reciprocal survival ethic → future family → ordinary logistics → trust under altered reality → shared adulthood.**

Its most important achievement is not that the couple never separates.

It is that they learn to remain a couple without making one person's self-erasure the proof of love.


# 2. Attraction begins with recognition, not rescue reward
Sakuta is attracted to Mai before he solves the phenomenon.

His attraction includes:

- physical desire;
- fascination with her refusal of ordinary deference;
- recognition of shared social estrangement;
- admiration for her competence;
- pleasure in her ability to return his sarcasm.

Mai is not romantically obligated because Sakuta recognizes her.

The one-month delay protects that distinction.

> `今の、一ヵ月後にもう一度言って`

She requires the relationship to survive:

- restored visibility;
- return to acting;
- ordinary school time;
- the fading of crisis dependence.

The series thereby establishes a useful romance test:

> **Would these two people still choose one another when the emergency no longer organizes every interaction?**

They do.


# 3. The public confession: romantic courage and boundary tension
Sakuta's school-wide declaration is undeniably romantic in affective structure.

He risks humiliation to make Mai recognizable to the whole school.

But the act also binds:

- the public restoration mechanism;
- Mai's name;
- Sakuta's romantic claim.

The series does not give Mai prior procedural control over the method.

Her later acceptance matters, but the scene remains an early example of Sakuta acting first and asking the affected person to respond afterward.

The relationship improves because Mai does not merely reward the intervention. She sets the temporal test.


# 4. Why their banter matters
Sakuta and Mai's sexual and sarcastic banter is not an ornamental layer separate from the relationship's ethics.

It performs several functions.

## 4.1 Reciprocal control of embarrassment

Each can embarrass the other. Neither permanently owns the superior position.

## 4.2 Desire without solemn possession

Sakuta's heterosexual attraction is explicit. Mai often acknowledges, redirects, or teases it. The relation can contain bodily desire without pretending desire creates entitlement.

## 4.3 Private language

The jokes let Mai stop performing the perfect public celebrity and let Sakuta stop performing indifference.

## 4.4 Testing safety

The banter remains healthy only when each can refuse, counter, or change the subject. The strongest scenes show Mai exercising that capacity.

The relationship is therefore sexually charged without becoming structurally dependent on sexual access.


# 5. Privacy becomes a shared problem
Mai tells Sakuta:

> `ふたりの問題でしょ？`

This is an early statement of relational standing.

A romantic partnership does not make every private fact jointly owned. But actions that materially reorganize the relationship cannot be defended as:

> my life, my decision, no consequences for you.

The series repeatedly revisits the distinction.

- Public lies involving other girls have relationship consequences.
- Career and media decisions affect privacy.
- Life-risk decisions affect both partners.
- Secret contingency plans may undermine trust even when benevolent.
- Future family plans require shared authorship.

The relationship matures by specifying what is genuinely shared rather than treating love as total access.


# 6. Public visibility and the relationship
Mai's celebrity makes privacy difficult from the beginning.

Dating her means:

- being photographed;
- becoming a public status object;
- navigating rumor;
- understanding that ordinary gestures may become media narratives.

Sakuta's public position is ambivalent.

He sometimes enjoys the status reversal of dating a famous woman. He later worries about converting Mai's achievement into his own social capital.

That self-awareness matters. The relationship is healthiest when pride in Mai does not become ownership of Mai.


# 7. Volume 4: body exchange tests whether desire tracks person or appearance
The Mai/Nodoka exchange creates a direct romance test.

If Sakuta's love were reducible to Mai's body, the person in that body would become interchangeable.

The arc instead repeatedly forces him to distinguish:

- appearance;
- gesture;
- speech;
- memory;
- relationship;
- person.

Mai remains Mai in Nodoka's body.

Nodoka does not become Mai by occupying Mai's.

The romance therefore participates in the series' broader personhood thesis:

> **the beloved is not merely the visible body, though the visible body remains part of embodied desire.**


# 8. Mai's public confirmation
Mai eventually confirms the relationship publicly:

> `はい、事実です`

This matters because the public confession is no longer only Sakuta's unilateral declaration.

Mai claims the relationship in her own public voice.

The sequence is:

- Sakuta speaks first under crisis;
- Mai tests ordinary durability;
- Mai later chooses public acknowledgment.

That is a movement from rescue-generated claim to reciprocal ownership.


# 9. Kaede's crisis: the relationship enters family life
The Kaede/Hanakaede arc tests whether Mai remains only Sakuta's romantic refuge or becomes an independent participant in his family world.

Mai develops relationships with both continuities.

She:

- cooks;
- studies;
- listens;
- challenges Sakuta's assumptions;
- becomes a stable adult-like presence in the household.

This does not make her substitute mother.

It makes the romance capable of containing family without absorbing family into the couple.


# 10. Shoko and the limits of romantic exclusivity
Sakuta's bond with Shoko cannot be reduced to an obstacle heroine.

Shoko is:

- formative first love;
- possible future spouse;
- recipient of Sakuta's or Mai's heart in different futures;
- person whose survival creates real moral claims.

Mai's response is not emotionally frictionless. She is jealous and afraid.

But she does not solve the problem by demanding that Sakuta stop caring about Shoko.

The relationship must become strong enough to accommodate morally important bonds outside itself.


# 11. Volume 6: unilateral sacrifice as relational dispossession
Sakuta initially treats his death as a decision he may make for Shoko's survival.

Mai says:

> `ひとりで決めないでよ`

> `だから、一緒に背負う`

This is one of the series' most important procedural objections.

Sakuta's body is his.

But the decision's emotional and future consequences do not belong only to him.

Mai's claim is not:

> you may never risk yourself because I own you.

It is:

> **you may not convert me into the grateful recipient of a life you removed from our shared future without treating me as a participant.**


# 12. `未来の話`: romance as futurity
Mai says:

> `私は咲太と未来の話をしていたいの`

The line opposes two forms of love.

### Memorial love

One partner plans the beautiful meaning of his death for the survivor.

### Future love

Both remain alive enough to discuss what comes next.

The relationship's mature orientation is not toward the perfect sacrifice.

It is toward continued conversation.


# 13. Mai's death and the impossibility of pure reciprocity
Mai later saves Sakuta and dies.

The event demonstrates that emergencies can produce asymmetry even inside a relationship committed to reciprocity.

There is no time for procedural co-authorship when a car is about to hit someone.

But the future created by the act is not treated as romantically complete.

Sakuta's survival becomes grief.

The series therefore distinguishes:

- immediate rescue;
- planned martyrdom;
- moral residue after survival.

Mai's sacrifice is understandable, loving, and catastrophic.


# 14. Volume 7: `ふたりで幸せになる`
Mai says:

> `幸せにしてもらわなくていい`

> `ふたりで幸せになるの`

This is the relationship's normative center.

It rejects a gendered and sacrificial model in which one person proves love by producing happiness for the other.

The phrase requires:

- both persons remain subjects;
- both possess futures;
- neither converts death into the other's romantic debt.

Later, Mai uses the same logic in ordinary conversation with Sara:

> `ふたりで幸せになるため`

The crisis statement becomes a general definition of dating.


# 15. Shared risk and changed past
When Sakuta and Shoko consider changing the past, Sakuta tells Mai.

Mai says:

> `過去を変えてもいいよ`

Her consent does not make the causal risk disappear.

It changes the procedure.

The couple enters the uncertainty together rather than one person deciding that the other will receive whatever remains.


# 16. The relationship after crisis: ordinary continuity
Volumes 8–12 are essential because they prove the romance can survive without constant catastrophe.

The couple's life includes:

- meals;
- driving;
- studying;
- work schedules;
- university planning;
- family errands;
- teasing;
- disagreement;
- waiting at home;
- trust around investigations.

These scenes are not lower-value filler after the dramatic romance.

They are the evidence that the relationship passed Mai's original one-month test on a larger scale.


# 17. Home and waiting
Mai's line before Hanakaede's exam—`帰るまでが今日の試験`—also reveals her relationship ethic with Sakuta.

Care may mean:

- not following;
- not solving;
- remaining a reliable return point.

The Sakuta/Mai relationship increasingly provides home in this sense.

Not confinement.

A place from which each can leave and to which each can return.


# 18. `いつか、ふたりで家族になろう`
Mai's Volume-9 statement:

> `いつか、ふたりで家族になろう`

has several important constraints.

## `いつか`

The future is open rather than demanded immediately.

## `ふたりで`

Family is co-authored.

## `なろう`

They will become family; the relationship is not treated as automatically complete because they love each other.

The line offers chosen family without erasing Sakuta's origin family or making Mai its replacement.


# 19. The marriage form and future materialization
Mai's signed marriage-registration form turns future possibility into a physical object.

The object does not force immediate marriage.

It says:

> the future family is sufficiently real to be materially represented.

The relationship repeatedly uses objects this way:

- ring;
- form;
- car;
- food;
- shared home routines.

Abstract love becomes logistics.


# 20. Driving and burden-sharing
Mai often drives because fame makes ordinary public movement difficult and because she possesses the skill.

Sakuta later enters driving school partly so she will not always have to.

This is one of the best examples of the romance's adult development.

Early care:

> risk the body.

Adult care:

> **learn a practical skill so the other person can rest.**


# 21. Trust without surveillance
Mai permits Sakuta to attend the nursing-student group date because she understands the investigative context.

This is not indifference.

It is confidence that the relationship does not need continuous surveillance to remain real.

Trust also permits disagreement. Mai asks whether Sakuta has forgiven Ikumi; he cannot answer. The relationship does not collapse because one partner introduces a question the other cannot resolve.


# 22. The ring: private continuity under public rewrite
The birthday ring is especially important during the Toko crisis.

Mai removes stage jewelry and puts the heart ring back on.

The world has changed one public identity proposition.

The private relation remains materially legible.

The ring is not proven magical.

That is better. It represents relationship without becoming a mechanism that replaces trust.


# 23. R1 Mai/Toko: trust when self-perception conflicts
R1 Mai sincerely believes she is Toko.

Sakuta believes she is Mai and not Toko.

The relationship reaches an extraordinary epistemic problem:

> What can one partner ask when the other's first-person apprehension contradicts the shared history he remembers?

Sakuta does not tell Mai that her interior experience is irrelevant.

He asks whether she trusts him enough to act despite the conflict.

Mai says yes.

This is not submission because:

- she evaluates Sakuta's credibility;
- she chooses cooperation;
- she remains professionally active;
- she later states her own restored identity.


# 24. Mutual protection in Volume 13
Sakuta says:

> `麻衣さんのことは僕が守るから`

Mai answers:

> `じゃあ、咲太のことは私が守ってあげる`

The repetition of `守る` refuses the fixed protector/protected hierarchy.

At the event:

- Sakuta catches the speaker;
- Mai catches Sakuta.

The scene physically diagrams reciprocal protection.


# 25. `同じ景色`: shared reality without fusion
Mai's final relationship thesis is:

> `私はね、咲太と同じ景色を見ていたい`

> `同じ景色を見て、この先も生きていきたい`

The phrase is a direct answer to a series of divergent worlds and identities.

She does not ask:

> see everything exactly as I do.

In fact, Mai cannot see Miori on April 10, and Sakuta eventually cannot see Ebina's ghost.

`同じ景色` therefore means:

> **choose one shared world in which different observers can remain related.**


# 26. Two footprints
The final Chapter-3 image shows:

- Sakuta and Mai side by side;
- joined hands;
- two footprints continuing.

This is the mature romance icon.

Not one body carrying the other.

Not one death purchasing the other's future.

Two tracks.

The visual form preserves difference and connection simultaneously.


# 27. Career independence after romantic stability
Three years later, Mai remains a major actor and takes another university leave for a taiga drama.

Sakuta is completing education training.

The relationship has not made their trajectories identical.

They coordinate:

- time;
- food;
- work;
- school.

The finale does not narrate marriage and should not be forced to. Durable partnership is already evidenced through years of ordinary co-authorship.


# 28. Why mutuality does not mean total symmetry
A healthy relationship does not require equal fame, equal income, identical risk, identical professional flexibility, or identical perception.

Sakuta and Mai remain asymmetrical in many ways.

- Mai is nationally famous.
- Sakuta is comparatively anonymous.
- Sakuta often possesses unusual phenomenon knowledge.
- Mai often possesses stronger professional/social competence.
- Each becomes the other's support in different contexts.

Mutuality means:

> **asymmetry does not eliminate the other's standing.**

## 29.1 Conflict as evidence of security

The couple's durability is not proved by absence of conflict. It is proved by conflict not automatically becoming relationship collapse.

They argue about:

- Sakuta withholding information;
- jealousy;
- public exposure;
- Sakuta's bodily risk;
- career schedules;
- Shoko;
- what counts as protection;
- whether a world should remain.

They can disagree because both expect the relationship to continue after disagreement. That security is a major contrast with Tomoe's fear that one socially embarrassing event will destroy belonging.

## 29.2 Reciprocity criteria

The final relationship is reciprocal not because both partners perform identical acts but because each has meaningful capacity to:

1. refuse;
2. challenge;
3. receive care;
4. give care;
5. maintain independent work;
6. affect shared decisions;
7. remain a distinct person.

Mai's greater celebrity and Sakuta's exceptional observer role create asymmetries. Reciprocity does not require identical social power. It requires that asymmetry not become unilateral jurisdiction.

## 29.3 Ordinary-life evidence

| Ordinary act | Relationship function |
|---|---|
| meals and cooking | care becomes embodied and repeatable |
| driving | burden-sharing and private space under celebrity constraints |
| school/exams | mutual investment in independent futures |
| gifts/ring | chosen material memory without immediate legal closure |
| family visits | romance expands into a wider relational world |
| schedule negotiation | career difference becomes practical rather than abstract |
| teasing | vulnerability becomes manageable through shared language |
| waiting at home | support without takeover |
| handholding/footprints | final visual reciprocity without rescue hierarchy |

Spectacular sacrifice can make a relationship look profound while remaining unsustainable. The mundane scenes are the stronger proof of durability.

## 29.4 Comparative relationship test

The Sakuta/Mai bond becomes clearer beside other relationships.

- Shoko/Sakuta teaches kindness and debt but does not become the final shared adult life.
- Tomoe/Sakuta ends healthily only when Tomoe can move beyond him.
- Rio/Kunimi demonstrates recognition without entitlement and desire without destiny.
- Miori/Toko demonstrates profound friendship damaged by presumed total understanding and refusal of irreversibility.

The distinguishing feature of Sakuta/Mai is not that it contains the strongest emotion in the abstract. It develops the most durable practice of reciprocal future authorship.


# 30. Strongest counterarguments
## 30.1 The relationship is codependent because crises repeatedly bind them

The concern is reasonable.

They become central to one another under extreme circumstances. Sakuta often relies on Mai as emotional ground, and Mai repeatedly enters Sakuta's family crises.

The counterevidence is ordinary independence.

- Each has work and friendships.
- Mai's career continues.
- Sakuta develops a vocation.
- Trust does not require surveillance.
- Disagreement does not threaten immediate dissolution.
- The final image preserves two tracks.

## 30.2 Mai is too idealized for the relationship to feel reciprocal

Mai's fame, beauty, intelligence, and competence create a wish-fulfillment structure.

But reciprocity is not measured by equal social prestige.

Mai receives from Sakuta:

- recognition;
- ordinary treatment;
- willingness to oppose public consensus.

Sakuta receives from Mai:

- ethical correction;
- future orientation;
- family participation;
- professional confidence;
- conceptual language for adulthood.

The relation is narratively unequal in glamour and increasingly reciprocal in authorship.

## 30.3 The harem structure trivializes the central romance

Multiple girls develop feelings for Sakuta or occupy emotionally intimate title arcs.

The central romance survives because the series does not continually reset Mai's status.

- Tomoe's love is not reciprocated.
- Rio's intimacy remains friendship.
- Sara's attraction is bounded by teacher role.
- Miori becomes friend.
- Shoko's possible marriage is released rather than preserved as active entitlement.

The ensemble can be emotionally close without suspending the final romantic choice.

## 30.4 Their sacrifices prove the relationship remains unhealthy

The sacrifice logic is never fully absent. Emergency protection still produces risk.

But the series shifts its normative center toward:

- disclosure;
- shared decision;
- practical burden-sharing;
- institutional care;
- continued life.

The final romantic climax is handholding, not death.

The matrix shows that durability is not produced by eliminating asymmetry. The relationship repeatedly acquires new asymmetries:

- perception;
- fame;
- age/experience;
- knowledge of possible worlds;
- professional schedule;
- risk.

Its mature form depends on keeping those asymmetries negotiable rather than pretending they do not exist.


# 32. Why the one-month delay matters
Mai's response to the Volume-1 declaration is analytically important:

> `今の、一ヵ月後にもう一度言って`

and then:

> `ちゃんと一ヵ月続けなさいよ`

The delay performs several functions.

## 32.1 It refuses rescue entitlement

Sakuta's spectacular public intervention does not purchase immediate romantic possession.

## 32.2 It tests continuity after crisis

The relationship must survive the return of ordinary life, embarrassment, school rumor, and repeated speech.

## 32.3 It gives Mai temporal agency

Mai sets the terms under which she will interpret Sakuta's desire.

## 32.4 It changes confession into practice

One declaration can be an impulsive climax.

A month of repeated declarations becomes a routine.

The romance begins moving from exceptional rescue toward ordinary continuity immediately after its most spectacular scene.


# 33. Conflict and repair
The couple's stability does not come from never disagreeing.

They disagree over:

- disclosure;
- Shoko;
- danger;
- professional decisions;
- privacy;
- who gets to decide what counts as acceptable sacrifice.

The important question is what disagreement does to the bond.

## 33.1 Conflict does not automatically threaten abandonment

Mai can become angry without the relationship becoming narratively fragile.

Sakuta can admit fear or error without losing all desirability.

## 33.2 Repair increasingly precedes action

Early Sakuta often acts and explains later.

After Volume 7, the relationship increasingly requires:

- tell me;
- let me participate;
- do not decide alone.

## 33.3 Humor allows repair but can delay vulnerability

Banter helps them return to ordinary relational rhythm.

It can also let Sakuta evade direct admission. Mai's skill is that she often hears the admission anyway.


# 34. Privacy and the right not to know everything
The relationship repeatedly faces information asymmetry.

Sakuta knows:

- Syndrome facts;
- possible futures;
- some dangers before Mai does.

Mai knows:

- celebrity systems;
- adult professional consequences;
- Sakuta's tendencies before he admits them.

A weak romance model would treat total disclosure as the proof of love.

AoButa moves toward a more careful position.

- Significant risks must not be hidden as “protection.”
- Private interiority does not become jointly owned.
- Trust can operate where proof is unavailable.
- Another person may remain partly opaque.

Volume 12's Sara case helps clarify the distinction. Sara's ability to know another person's interiority does not make love safer. Her refusal `知りたくない` demonstrates that intimacy can require leaving something unpossessed.

Sakuta and Mai's late trust depends on that same principle. Mai believes Sakuta during the Toko rewrite even while her own current self-perception says something else. She does not grant him permanent epistemic sovereignty. She trusts the history of the person speaking.


# 35. Shared future versus fused identity
`ふたりで幸せになる`, `ふたりで家族になろう`, and `同じ景色` form a clear progression.

## 35.1 `ふたりで幸せになる`

The future must contain both persons.

It rejects a solution in which one person becomes the other's sacrificial resource.

## 35.2 `ふたりで家族になろう`

Family is a future they will construct.

It does not erase Sakuta's origin family or make Mai its replacement.

## 35.3 `同じ景色`

The final phrase is the most philosophically mature because it preserves two observers.

They do not become one consciousness.

They agree to live in one shared reality.

This is why the series' personhood architecture matters to the romance. A relationship is good only if it can survive the fact that the beloved remains another person.


# 36. Ordinary logistics as romantic evidence
The relationship's strongest late evidence is often logistical.

- Mai drives.
- Sakuta learns to drive.
- They coordinate university and work.
- Mai manages public appearances.
- Sakuta works and studies.
- They plan meals.
- They communicate by phone.
- They wait for each other.

These scenes resist the idea that crisis intensity is the truest measure of love.

A relationship capable of surviving ordinary scheduling may be ethically stronger than one proved only by willingness to die.


# 37. Counterfactual and possible-world tests
The romance is tested by versions of each person who possess different histories.

## 37.1 Future Shoko marriage

A possible future relationship with Shoko is emotionally real without invalidating current Sakuta/Mai.

## 37.2 Mai's fatal future

Mai's death demonstrates love and exposes the injustice of treating sacrifice as a clean gift.

## 37.3 W1 and R1

Different worlds contain different biographies.

The romance cannot rely on the metaphysical claim that one pair is cosmically destined in every state.

It relies on what current Sakuta and current Mai repeatedly choose in the history they inhabit.

## 37.4 Mai/Toko

Sakuta must trust a relational continuity when one autobiographical proposition changes.

Mai must trust Sakuta when his claim conflicts with her immediate self-apprehension.

This is love as historical epistemic trust, not magical soul recognition.


# 38. Stronger adversarial reading: does the romance remain too idealized?
The criticism has force.

Mai is:

- beautiful;
- famous;
- academically exceptional;
- professionally disciplined;
- domestically capable;
- emotionally perceptive;
- intensely committed to Sakuta.

Sakuta receives a partner who can appear tailored to adolescent fantasy.

The response should not be that Mai has no flaws.

The stronger defense is structural.

She possesses:

- independent work;
- a family conflict not caused by Sakuta;
- a sister relationship;
- refusal capacity;
- the power to criticize him;
- an adult future not subordinated to his career.

The romance remains idealized in some market-facing dimensions. It is not narratively empty wish fulfillment.


# 39. Cross-document boundary and controlled romance formulation
Document 04 is responsible for the relationship as a relation:

- mutuality;
- trust;
- privacy;
- protection;
- future family;
