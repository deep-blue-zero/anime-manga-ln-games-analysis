# 03. Mai: Visibility, Vocation, Personhood, and Co-Protagonist Role
## The girl first made invisible who becomes the series' most stable adult presence

## Governing question

> **How does Mai preserve personhood under celebrity visibility, family projection, public misrecognition, romance, and increasingly reality-level distortions?**


# 1. Executive thesis
Mai's full-series arc is not simply the story of the first heroine who becomes Sakuta's girlfriend and then supports later cases. She is the series' most continuous counterforce to Sakuta's tendency to confuse recognition with sole responsibility.

She begins in a paradox:

> nationally visible, socially unrecognized, professionally famous, and insufficiently authoritative over her own body and future.

Her development therefore concerns more than becoming visible again. It concerns acquiring and defending **jurisdiction over the person who is visible**.

Across fifteen volumes, Mai repeatedly confronts different forms of public and intimate ownership:

- her mother's control over childhood work;
- school nonrecognition;
- the audience's claim upon celebrity image;
- Nodoka's comparison and bodily exchange;
- Sakuta's willingness to spend his life without her participation;
- public certainty that she is Kirishima Toko;
- and the possibility that one world may define her differently from another.

Her mature answer is neither withdrawal nor surrender to publicity. She continues acting, studying when possible, loving Sakuta, caring for Hanakaede, and participating in public correction. She becomes highly visible **without allowing visibility to exhaust personhood**.

Mai is also a co-protagonist because the series' final ethical vocabulary does not belong to Sakuta alone.

She gives him:

- standing against self-sacrifice;
- `ふたりで幸せになる`;
- future-family language;
- reciprocal protection;
- trust when her own self-perception has been rewritten;
- the ceiling-pattern metaphor;
- and `同じ景色`.

Sakuta begins as the person who sees Mai.

By the end, Mai is one of the people who helps Sakuta decide **how to live after seeing**.


# 2. Volume 1: hypervisibility without authorship
Mai's first paradox is often summarized as “the famous girl becomes invisible.” The more important distinction is between **attention** and **recognition**.

Before the Syndrome escalates, Mai already possesses enormous public attention. Her image circulates. Her acting career gives strangers a sense of familiarity. At school, however, she is treated as `空気`.

This establishes that public visibility can coexist with social erasure.

## 2.1 Childhood work and bodily jurisdiction

Mai's career begins under maternal control. Her body, schedule, and public image become resources managed by adults before she can exercise full authorship.

Her withdrawal from acting is therefore not best read as simple rejection of vocation. It is an attempt to reclaim the right to decide what work means and when the public may access her.

The bunny-girl experiment belongs to that history. She chooses a costume designed to be conspicuous and walks through public space testing whether anyone can perceive her.

The image is sexually marketable, but inside the story it is also an experiment in visibility conducted by the person whose visibility has ceased to belong to her.

## 2.2 Sakuta's recognition does not automatically solve authorship

Sakuta can see Mai. That gives him access and responsibility.

It does not make him owner of her crisis.

The public confession helps restore visibility and creates a relationship. It also turns her identity into a school-wide spectacle. The ethical ambiguity matters because Mai's whole arc concerns the right to participate in how she becomes public.

## 2.3 Returning to acting

Mai's later return to work should not be read as simple restoration of the pre-crisis career. She returns as someone more capable of negotiating:

- desire;
- schedule;
- privacy;
- public image;
- and adult professional identity.

Her vocation survives the critique of childhood control.

This is one reason her final state as a major actor remains important. Self-authorship does not require abandoning the public career that once constrained her.


# 3. The bunny-girl image: visibility, eroticism, and interpretive conflict
The bunny costume is the series' most recognizable image and one of its most analytically unstable.

It is simultaneously:

- Mai's chosen visibility test;
- a sexualized performance image;
- a joke about Sakuta's heterosexual attention;
- franchise iconography;
- and the memory that later becomes Sakuta's model for the student he hopes to hear.

A mature reading should preserve all of those functions.

## 3.1 Chosen display does not eliminate audience power

Mai chooses the costume in the original experiment. The franchise then reproduces the image for readers and viewers beyond her diegetic intention.

The narrative therefore cannot wholly stand outside the gaze it analyzes.

## 3.2 The final teacher callback transforms the image

Sakuta says he wants to listen to a student confused by a bunny girl only she can see:

> `僕にはもう見えなくても`

The costume has moved from erotic spectacle to ethical memory.

That transformation does not erase the sexualized history. It demonstrates how one image can accumulate new relational and institutional meaning.


# 4. Mai's professional self: competence rather than abstract “strong heroine” status
Mai's adulthood is built through repeated practical evidence.

She:

- understands sets and media;
- controls public speech;
- makes scheduling decisions;
- studies seriously despite career disruption;
- drives;
- manages crises without collapsing into Sakuta's dependent;
- and continues working after identity restoration at the live broadcast.

This competence matters because “strong female character” can otherwise become a vague compliment.

Mai's strength is not immunity to vulnerability. It is her ability to act while:

- frightened;
- jealous;
- grieving;
- publicly exposed;
- professionally obligated.


# 5. Mai and her mother: love, exploitation, and incomplete repair
Mai's mother occupies an ethically difficult position.

She is not reducible to villainous manager. Her actions exist inside:

- entertainment labor;
- parental ambition;
- economic and reputational pressure;
- a real relationship with her daughter.

But the ambiguity should not excuse the central harm: Mai's childhood body and career were governed in ways that undermined her authorship.

The later series does not offer one grand reconciliation scene that erases this history. Mai's adult professional competence partly consists of building a vocation no longer wholly mediated by maternal control.

The absence of complete maternal repair is important. Not every family relation receives the Azusagawa-style reconstruction.


# 6. Nodoka and sisterhood: difference without abandonment
Volume 4 gives Mai an independent relationship arc beyond Sakuta.

Nodoka has grown under comparison to Mai. She admires, envies, resents, and loves her sister.

The body exchange makes social recognition follow appearance. Mai's career and public identity appear transferable because the correct body is present. The experiment reveals the labor and biography that are not transferable.

## 6.1 Mai's ambivalence is real

Mai does not need to become an idealized perfectly affectionate sister.

She can dislike aspects of Nodoka, be frustrated by her, and still love her.

The letters and final differentiation do not reveal that all negative feeling was fake. They show that sisterhood can survive contradiction.

## 6.2 Bodily jurisdiction

Mai's insistence on consent and professionalism during the swap is a major personhood statement.

A body that looks like Mai is not automatically available for any act associated with Mai's career.

Her knowledge of:

- posture;
- camera;
- work expectation;
- and bodily risk

is accumulated labor.

## 6.3 Sisterhood after the title volume

Nodoka continues as:

- idol;
- family member;
- commentator on Mai's fame;
- practical participant in care;
- person pursuing her own educational credentials.

The sisters remain distinct rather than resolving into one hierarchical model.


# 7. Mai as independent caregiver to Kaede and Hanakaede
Mai's relation to Sakuta's sister is one of the strongest proofs that she is not merely a romantic reward.

She:

- cooks;
- studies with Hanakaede;
- gives a laptop;
- helps her re-enter digital communication;
- understands the importance of a reliable home;
- critiques Sakuta's secrecy about alternative school plans;
- and participates in family ordinary life.

Her line before Hanakaede's exam:

> `花楓ちゃんにとっては、咲太の待つ家に帰るまでが今日の試験なんだから`

shows mature care.

The supporter does not always accompany the person into every room.

The supporter makes sure there is a home to return to.

Mai's care is relationally independent. Hanakaede is not only “Sakuta's sister” whom Mai tolerates for romance. Their connection acquires its own practical and emotional content.


# 8. Romantic agency: Mai chooses rather than merely responds
Mai's relationship with Sakuta deserves a separate primary document, but her independent character study must preserve her agency within it.

She does not become girlfriend immediately because he sees her.

She tests whether the connection can survive:

- ordinary time;
- public visibility;
- separation;
- work;
- jealousy;
- conflict.

She:

- sets boundaries;
- initiates teasing;
- declares love;
- signs a marriage registration form;
- plans university;
- buys/requests material symbols;
- and repeatedly tells Sakuta what his choices mean for her.

The romance is not something Sakuta wins by rescuing her once.

It is something Mai continues choosing.


# 9. Volumes 6–7: Mai refuses the moral beauty of Sakuta's disappearance
Mai's role in the Shoko duology is one of the strongest reasons to treat her as co-protagonist.

Sakuta interprets death as repayment.

Mai interprets his life as part of a shared future.

## 9.1 “Selfishness” as moral standing

A superficial reading calls Mai selfish because she prioritizes Sakuta over Shoko.

The stronger reading asks:

> why should Sakuta's self-sacrifice be treated as morally neutral to the person who loves him?

Mai's desire does not automatically decide the outcome. It creates standing.

His life is not a private token he can spend while expecting her gratitude for the sacrifice.

## 9.2 Mai's own sacrifice does not prove martyrdom is correct

Mai later dies protecting Sakuta in one future state.

This is not the series vindicating sacrificial love.

It is the nightmare form of the same logic. Each person tries to make the other live by spending herself.

The duology resolves only when care becomes:

- disclosed;
- reciprocal;
- distributed;
- and oriented toward both lives.

## 9.3 `ふたりで幸せになる`

The phrase becomes the romance's governing ethic.

Not:

> one person proves love by disappearing.

But:

> both persons have standing in the future they share.

Mai is central to that correction.


# 10. Future family and ordinary adulthood
Mai's adult orientation appears in phrases and logistics rather than one final domestic endpoint.

She tells Sakuta:

> `いつか、ふたりで家族になろう`

The signed marriage-registration form and later ring point toward future family without replacing the present relationship with a predetermined conclusion.

She also structures adulthood through:

- exams;
- university;
- work leaves;
- driving;
- housing;
- family visits;
- cooking;
- and schedule negotiation.

The series never confirms marriage by the final epilogue.

That restraint protects the relationship from being reduced to legal closure. It is already durable through practice.


# 11. Volume 9: Mai offers chosen family without substituting for origin family
Sakuta's family crisis might invite a romance solution in which Mai replaces his parents.

She does not.

Her future-family promise gives Sakuta a future while leaving the origin-family wound visible and repairable.

She also knows when to remain available rather than entering the final mother/Sakuta confrontation.

Her stance resembles her best care for Hanakaede:

> do not take over the person's decisive encounter;
> make sure the person has somewhere and someone to return to.

This is one of Mai's most mature forms of support.


# 12. University-era Mai: intimacy under celebrity constraint
Mai's public status changes the material form of ordinary intimacy.

The couple uses:

- cars;
- private spaces;
- schedule coordination;
- home meals;
- controlled public appearances.

This is not glamorous decoration. It is infrastructure for a relationship under celebrity observation.

Mai remains able to be:

- playful;
- sexually teasing;
- jealous;
- tired;
- domestic

because the relationship creates a space not wholly governed by public performance.

Her private self is not the “true Mai” while public Mai is fake. Both are authored aspects of one person. The difference is jurisdiction and audience.


# 13. Mai and Sara: adult love without prestige logic
Sara approaches romance partly through status, desirability, and certainty.

Mai's answer clarifies her own relationship ethic.

She says she and Sakuta are together:

> `ふたりで幸せになるため`

and suggests he may be:

> `そう思えるたったひとりの人`

This is not a ranking claim that Sakuta is objectively the highest-value man.

It is a person-specific commitment:

> this is the person with whom I want to build mutual happiness.

Mai also offers Sara access to her interiority and accepts Sara's refusal. That scene respects another person's right not to know everything.


# 14. Mai/Toko: maximum recognition becomes identity overwrite
The final arc gives Mai the inverse of Volume 1.

## Volume 1

Others do not recognize Mai enough. She becomes imperceptible.

## Volumes 13–15

Millions recognize Mai too confidently as Kirishima Toko. The public identity enters her own first-person apprehension.

This is not ordinary lying or hidden-biography revelation.

Current Mai had sincerely denied being Toko. Inside R1 she sincerely says:

> `私は私が霧島透子だと今でも思ってる`

The series thereby shows that first-person certainty deserves respect and can still be altered by a recognition phenomenon.

## 14.1 Trust against self-perception

Sakuta cannot simply prove Mai wrong.

He asks whether she believes he would lie.

Mai says no.

Then she chooses to trust him despite her own current factual apprehension.

This is one of her strongest acts of agency.

She is not passively “restored.” She cooperates in the public correction.

## 14.2 Professional action after restoration

When the Toko identity releases and Mai wakes, she immediately recognizes that the broadcast requires explanation.

She resumes responsibility.

The scene prevents the climax from turning her into a body retrieved by Sakuta and Miori.


# 15. The ceiling-pattern metaphor: Mai authors the final adulthood language
Mai tells Sakuta about a childhood ceiling pattern that appeared to be a ghost.

As one grows, the pattern is seen differently.

The experience becomes memory.

This gives Sakuta the conceptual path between:

- denying Adolescence Syndrome;
- and keeping every impossible state actual forever.

The metaphor is not an incidental girlfriend insight. It provides the final series' governing maturation model.

Mai thereby becomes one of the principal interpreters of the entire narrative.


# 16. `同じ景色`: shared reality without fusion
Mai says:

> `私はね、咲太と同じ景色を見ていたい`

and:

> `同じ景色を見て、この先も生きていきたい`

The language is precise.

She does not ask:

- to become one person;
- to share every thought;
- to erase separate vocation;
- to inhabit the optimized world.

She asks for a shared reality in which two persons remain distinct.

This is the mature answer to the whole series' body-swap, counterpart, and possible-world architecture.


# 17. Career continuation and the refusal of domestic reduction
Three years later, Mai is working in a taiga drama and taking another university leave.

The relationship persists.

Her education remains incomplete or delayed relative to ordinary schedule.

Her career remains demanding.

The epilogue does not resolve her into:

- wife;
- mother;
- retired celebrity;
- or reward for Sakuta's adulthood.

She continues choosing professional life.

The romance has to accommodate that life rather than replace it.


# 18. Why Mai is a co-protagonist
Mai qualifies as co-protagonist not because she receives equal first-person narration—she does not—but because she carries functions without which the series' architecture would collapse.

She is:

1. the first case that establishes recognition;
2. the central romantic partner whose standing corrects Sakuta's sacrifice ethic;
3. an independent professional/personhood arc;
4. a sisterhood protagonist;
5. a caregiver in the Azusagawa family;
6. a future-family co-author;
7. the target of the final mass-recognition crisis;
8. the person who gives Sakuta the ceiling-pattern metaphor;
9. the partner who defines shared reality through `同じ景色`.

Sakuta remains the focalizer.

Mai remains one of the primary authors of what his development means.


# 19. Mai's longitudinal agency matrix
| Domain | Early condition | Development | Final state |
|---|---|---|---|
| body | childhood work and public image partly governed by others | asserts consent and professional control, including during body swap | remains embodied professional subject rather than public property |
| vocation | withdrawal from acting after loss of authorship | returns on increasingly self-authored terms; balances education | major actor with continuing career costs and choices |
| school/education | isolated, intermittently absent, publicly conspicuous | takes exams, enters university, negotiates leaves | education remains active but not narratively complete |
| sisterhood | comparison and maternal division | accepts contradiction, writes/receives evidence of care | durable distinct relationship with Nodoka |
| romance | begins under rescue asymmetry | tests durability, creates shared moral jurisdiction | reciprocal partner in shared reality |
| family | initially external to Azusagawa crisis | independent caregiver and future-family participant | integrated without replacing origin family |
| public identity | hypervisible yet socially erased | learns public/media control | survives mass Toko misrecognition and reasserts self |
| interpretation | first “case” of recognition | increasingly diagnoses Sakuta's ethical errors | supplies final adulthood metaphor |


# 20. Mai's public and private voices
Mai's voice changes with audience without requiring a true-self/fake-self split.

## Public/professional Mai

- measured;
- media-aware;
- capable of humor under pressure;
- careful about commitments;
- attentive to schedule and consequence.

## Intimate Mai

- teasing;
- direct;
- jealous;
- bodily relaxed;
- willing to ask for gifts, food, time, reassurance.

## Sister Mai

- impatient;
- protective;
- resistant to sentimental simplification;
- capable of admitting love without erasing anger.

The distinction is not performance versus authenticity. It is audience-specific expression under different responsibilities.


# 21. Mai as an ethical critic of Sakuta
Mai repeatedly supplies objections Sakuta cannot generate alone.

1. **Your life is not morally negligible.**
2. **Secrecy is not automatically protection.**
3. **Support can mean waiting rather than entering.**
4. **A partner has standing in a shared future.**
5. **Growing up need not mean denial.**
6. **One shared scenery matters more than infinite optimized possibilities.**

This is why “supportive girlfriend” is too weak a category. Mai actively changes the series' normative framework.


# 22. Quiet evidence of personhood
Mai's strongest personhood evidence often appears outside the climaxes.

- cooking with Hanakaede;
- studying;
- driving;
- asking for a birthday ring;
- making curry;
- correcting Sakuta's social assumptions;
- planning work and school;
- waiting for him to return.

These scenes show a woman whose life cannot be reduced to either celebrity image or supernatural heroine status.


# 23. Limit of access: the Sakuta focalization problem
The final synthesis should state a genuine limitation.

Mai is a co-protagonist filtered primarily through Sakuta's narration.

Readers know her through:

- speech;
- action;
- professional choices;
- how Sakuta interprets her.

We receive less sustained direct interiority than her architectural importance might warrant.

This does not make the co-protagonist reading false. It means the evidence is behavioral and relational rather than equally focalized.


# 24. Strongest counterreadings
## 24.1 Mai becomes secondary after Volume 1

There is evidence for the feeling:

- later volumes bear other heroines' titles;
- Sakuta's viewpoint limits her interiority;
- she often appears as stable partner around another person's crisis.

But stability should not be mistaken for narrative absence.

Her interventions repeatedly change:

- Sakuta's ethics;
- family life;
- romantic structure;
- public identity;
- final adulthood theory.

She is less often the unstable case because she becomes a person capable of helping carry the series.

## 24.2 Mai is idealized beyond criticism

Mai is highly competent, attractive, famous, academically strong, and emotionally perceptive.

The text does idealize her.

It also gives her:

- jealousy;
- professional rigidity;
- teasing that can be cutting;
- large life decisions built around Sakuta;
- imperfect access to his interiority;
- and vulnerability to public recognition.

The strongest critique concerns how much her perspective remains mediated through Sakuta's admiration. A final synthesis should acknowledge that limitation without reducing her demonstrated agency.

## 24.3 The romance absorbs Mai's independent identity

Rejected by the final career trajectory and sister/family roles.

Mai's adult life remains too large to be contained by the relationship.

## 24.4 Mai's sacrifice proves love requires death

Rejected by the duology's entire corrective structure.

Her death future is the catastrophic consequence of mutual martyrdom, not its moral endorsement.


# 25. Visibility-state matrix
Mai's longitudinal arc is easiest to distort if visibility is treated as one variable. The series repeatedly separates public attention, person-specific recognition, role legibility, and private self-possession.

| State | Public attention | Person-specific recognition | Self-identification | Main danger |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| V1 pre-erasure | very high as celebrity | weak inside school and family systems | intact but exhausted | being known as image while not encountered as person |
| V1 erasure | declining toward literal nonperception | Sakuta remains primary witness | increasingly endangered by isolation | social atmosphere becomes ontologically decisive |
| V4 body exchange | body remains highly visible | wrong sister is recognized through Mai's body | Mai remains autobiographically herself | body/public role outranks biography |
| V6–7 fatal futures | public image persists in records and grief | Sakuta recognizes Mai as irreplaceable person | future continuities diverge | love is converted into sacrificial substitution |
| university era | maximum celebrity visibility | strong private recognition through Sakuta, Nodoka, family | professionally stable | public life claims logistical control over intimacy |
| V13–15 Mai/Toko | public expectation becomes overwhelming | Sakuta and close allies preserve Sakurajima Mai history | temporarily rewritten to include Toko identity | collective recognition overwrites first-person biography |
| RF | celebrity remains high | private, family, and professional recognition coexist | stable but not static | career demands continue; no magical permanent balance |

This matrix supports a central conclusion:

> **Mai's problem is never simply too little visibility. It is whether visibility remains answerable to the historically particular person.**


# 26. Mai's vocational self-authorship
Mai's acting career begins under maternal direction and childhood conditions she did not fully choose. The series nevertheless refuses the easy claim that all later performance is therefore false.

## 26.1 Withdrawal is a boundary, not hatred of acting

Mai's hiatus responds to:

- maternal control;
- objectification;
- exhaustion;
- loss of privacy;
- the sense that a public image is being substituted for her.

She does not simply discover that she never liked performance.

## 26.2 Return is meaningful because it is chosen

When Mai returns to work, the action is not restoration of the mother's old authority. It is a new relation to vocation.

She can:

- choose roles;
- negotiate schedules;
- involve a manager;
- continue education;
- refuse some projects;
- protect private life.

The work is socially constructed and still genuinely hers.

## 26.3 Professional discipline is part of her personhood

Mai repeatedly demonstrates:

- preparation;
- media fluency;
- emotional control;
- punctuality;
- awareness of production obligations;
- willingness to repair public disruption after crisis.

These are not merely “celebrity traits.” They are competencies developed through labor.

## 26.4 The finale preserves career friction

RF Mai continues major acting work and takes another university leave. The relationship does not magically remove:

- scheduling conflict;
- delayed graduation;
- geographic separation;
- public scrutiny.

This is important to the co-protagonist reading. A weaker romantic ending would convert Mai's future into a reward attached to Sakuta's career. Instead, both futures remain active.


# 27. Ordinary Mai: domesticity without domestic reduction
Mai's independent personhood is visible not only in professional scenes but in mundane relational life.

She:

- cooks;
- shops;
- drives;
- plans travel;
- studies;
- helps Hanakaede use a computer;
- works with Nodoka;
- returns to police/media duties after Sakuta is injured;
- discusses meals with Sakuta in RF.

These scenes matter because domestic capability can be misread in two directions.

## Misreading one

Mai's ordinary care proves her “true” self is private and domestic rather than public and professional.

## Misreading two

Domestic scenes are only romantic fan service and do not contribute to characterization.

The stronger reading is:

> **Mai's adult self contains both public vocation and private care. Neither sphere is the hidden truth that invalidates the other.**

Her ability to cook for Sakuta does not make acting superficial. Her acting success does not make domestic care a performance.


# 28. Mai's anger, jealousy, and capacity to refuse
Mai would be less convincing as a co-protagonist if she were only:

- patient;
- beautiful;
- competent;
- endlessly forgiving.

The novels preserve sharper traits.

## 28.1 Anger

Mai becomes angry when Sakuta:

- excludes her from decisions;
- makes himself disposable;
- treats danger as private jurisdiction;
- behaves carelessly with intimacy.

Her anger is often ethically informative.

## 28.2 Jealousy

Shoko produces real jealousy. So do other girls and Sakuta's provocative humor.

The emotion does not make Mai irrationally possessive by default. It reveals that relational security does not eliminate vulnerability.

## 28.3 Refusal

Mai can say:

- no;
- wait;
- tell me;
- do not decide alone;
- I do not accept your death as a gift.

This refusal capacity is one of the strongest differences between her and a rescue-reward heroine.

## 28.4 Teasing

Mai's teasing is often a way of retaining equality with a narrator who uses jokes to control emotional exposure. She can reverse embarrassment, expose his bluff, and make him answer the relational question beneath the gag.


# 29. Sisterhood after comparison
Mai/Nodoka should not be treated only as one body-swap case.

The sisters later develop a relation capable of:

- disagreement;
- practical support;
- professional comparison without total collapse;
- concern for each other's futures;
- honest acknowledgment of epistemic limits.

Nodoka's childhood letters are especially important. Mai says:

> `のどかだけは違ったの`

and that the letters gave her courage.

Nodoka's recognition is not valuable because it accurately predicts Mai's market worth. It is valuable because it arrives from a particular sister whose response matters independently of the audience.

By Volume 13, Nodoka can admit she does not fully know Mai's celebrity burden and still tell Sakuta:

> stay on her side.

Mature sisterhood does not require total access.


# 30. Co-protagonist test
Mai qualifies as co-protagonist under more demanding criteria than page count alone.

| Test | Evidence |
|---|---|
| independent conflict | celebrity, mother, vocation, Nodoka, public identity |
| independent agency | return to acting, public confirmation of romance, sacrifice, cooperation in Toko correction |
| capacity to alter Sakuta | challenges self-sacrifice, gives shared-future language, supplies ceiling-pattern metaphor |
| continuity outside romance | work, education, sisterhood, family integration, media responsibility |
| role in final resolution | trust against self-perception; adulthood metaphor; shared-scene endpoint |
| independent future | taiga work and continued education alongside relationship |

A recurring love interest may support the protagonist's growth.

Mai repeatedly does that.

A co-protagonist also possesses an independent line of personhood whose integrity the series must preserve.

Mai does that too.


# 31. Stronger critique: the limits of Mai's representation
The co-protagonist argument should not become idealization.

## 31.1 First-person access remains asymmetrical

Because Sakuta narrates, Mai's interiority is often inferred from:

- speech;
- gesture;
- action;
- Sakuta's interpretation.

She does not receive the same sustained first-person interior access as Sakuta.

## 31.2 Competence can become romantic idealization

Mai is extraordinarily capable across:

- career;
- academics;
- media;
- domestic life;
- emotional judgment.

The narrative sometimes uses that competence to position her as an almost impossibly desirable partner.

## 31.3 Sacrificial symmetry can romanticize danger

Mai's death proves Sakuta is not the only one capable of self-erasure. It also risks turning lethal substitution into proof of love.

The later `同じ景色` endpoint is therefore necessary. Without it, the relationship might remain trapped in reciprocal martyrdom rather than mutual life.

## 31.4 Public-sexual visual framing persists

The series critiques public gaze while continuing to market Mai through attractive costumes and illustrations. The bunny-girl image remains both critical device and market icon.

That tension should be retained rather than explained away.


# 32. Cross-document boundary and controlled Mai formulation
Document 03 is the primary home for:

- Mai's independent psychology;
- celebrity and vocation;
- public/private self;
- sisterhood;
- co-protagonist role.

The central romance belongs primarily to Document 04. Recognition mechanics belong to Document 09. Mai/Toko personhood belongs comparatively to Document 10. Sacrifice ethics belong to Documents 06 and 13.

The controlled Phase-4 formulation is:

> **Mai begins as a girl whose enormous visibility fails to protect personhood. Across the series she rebuilds a public vocation without surrendering private self-authorship, becomes Sakuta's equal critic and protector, and ultimately supplies the metaphor that lets him carry adolescent experience into adulthood. Her co-protagonist status rests not on being the most important heroine to Sakuta, but on possessing an independent line of work, family, recognition, refusal, and future that the romance must learn to respect.**


# 34. Final character synthesis
Mai begins as a girl everyone knows and no one recognizes.

Her image is public.

Her body and career have often been managed by others.

Her school treats her as air.

The first task is to become visible.

The larger task is to remain a person after visibility returns.

She does so by refusing every easy reduction.

She is not only:

- actress;
- daughter;
- older sister;
- Sakuta's girlfriend;
- public icon;
- Toko candidate.

Each role is real.

None has the right to consume the rest.

Her maturation is therefore less spectacular than some Syndrome arcs and more architecturally important. She builds a public vocation with greater authorship. She accepts a romance without becoming its reward. She protects Sakuta without making protection possession. She loves Nodoka without pretending comparison never hurt. She cares for Hanakaede without taking over. She trusts Sakuta against a rewritten identity without surrendering professional agency.

And when Sakuta no longer knows how to become an adult without betraying adolescence, Mai gives him the metaphor he needs.

The ghost can become a pattern.

The pattern remains.

Then she asks him for the adult thing she has wanted all along:

> one world;
> two persons;
> the same scenery.
