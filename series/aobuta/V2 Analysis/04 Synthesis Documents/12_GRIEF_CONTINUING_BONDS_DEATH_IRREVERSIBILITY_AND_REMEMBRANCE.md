# 12. Grief, Continuing Bonds, Death, Irreversibility, and Remembrance
## Loving the absent without forcing the present to become them

## Governing question

> **How does the series distinguish faithful remembrance from refusal of irreversibility?**

# 1. Executive thesis

AoButa repeatedly places its characters in situations where an absent person does not fit the ordinary grammar of loss.

Kaede is not dead, yet the autobiographical person Sakuta knew disappears when Hanakaede's memories return. Future Shoko is not an ordinary deceased person, yet Sakuta remembers a woman whose life, marriage, and relationship with him belonged to a possible history that no longer governs the present. Mai dies in one future and survives in another. Toko is unambiguously dead, yet her heart supports Shoko, her music survives through Miori's voice, her name becomes a public identity, and Miori lives among realities that never allow the loss to feel final.

The series therefore cannot treat grief as only:

> mourning a physically absent body after one settled death.

Its larger question is:

> **How should one relate to a person who remains real in memory, objects, possible histories, bodily consequence, art, and relationship—but who cannot simply be restored as the same living subject?**

The final answer is neither detachment nor magical restoration.

It is a form of continuing bond governed by two constraints:

1. **the absent person must remain particular rather than becoming a role someone else is forced to inhabit;**
2. **the present must be allowed to remain present rather than being endlessly rewritten to reproduce the absent person.**

This produces the document's governing thesis:

> **Continuing bonds become sustainable when the dead or absent can remain particular without requiring the living person, the present body, or the world itself to reproduce them.**

The negative forms of remembrance are therefore easy to identify.

- Hanakaede treating Kaede's dream as an obligation to become Kaede.
- Sakuta keeping R1 solely because it grants him an older Kaede.
- Shoko treating another person's death as a debt that authorizes self-erasure.
- Miori singing under Toko's name until grief becomes attached to a world that refuses finality.
- Nene or Mai being made to carry the public identity `霧島透子` as though recognition could return the dead friend by substitution.

The positive forms are quieter.

- Sakuta revisiting the zoo and saying he may come to see the panda and Kaede.
- Hanakaede asking to learn about Kaede `少しずつ`.
- Nodoka preserving a photograph Sakuta did not know existed.
- Shoko receiving Toko's heart without being treated as Toko.
- Miori accepting the exchange diary and saying `透子に出会えてよかった`.
- Sakuta listening to the completed song instead of mistaking a lookalike for Miori.
- Sakuta retaining future Shoko's kindness after the future person is no longer accessible.

The final series does not make memory a lesser substitute for presence.

It makes memory the ethical form presence must sometimes take.

# 2. Four kinds of absence

AoButa's grief architecture becomes clearer if the absences are distinguished.

## 2.1 Autobiographical absence inside bodily continuity

Kaede disappears while the body continues as Hanakaede.

The loss is real because the continuity Sakuta knew included:

- self-reference;
- voice;
- goals;
- habits;
- fears;
- relation to him;
- relation to the outside world.

The medical return of Hanakaede's autobiographical memory is therefore simultaneously:

- recovery under one clinical frame;
- bereavement under another relational frame.

## 2.2 Possible-person absence

Future Shoko, dead Mai, dead Sakuta, spouse Shoko, and other world-specific persons may not belong to the current stabilized history.

They nevertheless create:

- memories;
- obligations;
- grief;
- choices;
- changes to character.

Their absence is not ordinary death, but the ethical effects are not therefore imaginary.

## 2.3 Physical death with bodily aftermath

Toko dies.

Her heart is transplanted into Shoko.

Her body is absent, but the death leaves:

- Shoko's continued life;
- donor-family grief;
- Miori's guilt;
- music;
- photographs;
- diary;
- a public identity.

## 2.4 Social or relational absence inside physical presence

The series also repeatedly creates people who are bodily present but socially inaccessible:

- invisible Mai;
- unrecognized Sakuta;
- wrong-world Ikumi;
- Nene erased inside Toko;
- Miori invisible to everyone except Sakuta.

These are not all grief cases, but they train the series' central insight:

> physical presence alone does not guarantee that a relationship can reach the person.

This is why later remembrance depends so heavily on names, objects, stories, and recognition.

# 3. Kaede: bereavement without bodily death

The Kaede case supplies the series' first sustained grief problem because it deprives Sakuta of the social scripts that usually make grief intelligible.

He cannot say:

> my sister died.

The body remains.

Hanakaede wakes.

The family can reasonably describe this as recovery.

Yet the person who called herself `かえで`, wrote the notebook, depended on Sakuta, feared the outside world, and developed a particular little-sister relationship with him is no longer available in the same autobiographical form.

The series' achievement is to refuse both reductions.

### Reduction one

> Kaede was merely a symptom, so grief is sentimental confusion.

### Reduction two

> Hanakaede's return is a tragedy that should be resisted because Kaede was the preferable sister.

The first erases Kaede.

The second erases Hanakaede.

The sister arc instead establishes that:

> **recovery can be genuine and bereavement can be genuine at the same time.**

# 4. Kaede authors her own remembrance

Kaede does not simply disappear without anticipating the problem.

Her diary asks what will happen if Hanakaede's memories return:

> `かえではどこに行ってしまうんでしょうか？`

She understands enough of her condition to fear that another continuity may return and that her own current self may no longer remain available.

She also writes the kind of remembrance she hopes to receive:

> `笑いながら思い出してもらえたら`

This is one of the most ethically important requests in the series.

Kaede does not ask:

- that Hanakaede become her;
- that Sakuta refuse Hanakaede;
- that the family preserve permanent pain as proof of love.

She asks to be remembered with a smile.

That request anticipates the mature continuing-bond model later realized in *Animal Land*.

# 5. Sakuta's first grief response remains crisis-shaped

Immediately after Hanakaede returns, Sakuta's grief is inseparable from exhaustion, shock, caregiving burden, and the absence of a socially acknowledged death.

He has spent two years treating Kaede's life as his responsibility.

Then the relationship ends without a funeral, public ritual, or shared vocabulary.

This contributes to his tendency to experience grief as:

- private burden;
- proof of responsibility;
- something to survive alone.

The series later corrects this through distributed memory.

Kaede is not preserved only inside Sakuta.

She remains in:

- the notebook;
- annual zoo pass;
- Nodoka's photograph;
- Mai's memories;
- Nodoka's memories;
- places;
- habits learned during the Kaede period;
- the family history Hanakaede inherits.

The memory becomes social rather than monopolized by the brother.

# 6. Hanakaede's first relation to Kaede: inheritance as debt

Volume 8 shows that remembrance can become coercive even without anyone intending coercion.

Hanakaede inherits:

- Kaede's notebook;
- Kaede's educational learning;
- relationships Kaede helped create;
- Sakuta's changed life;
- Mai and the ensemble;
- Kaede's wish to attend Minegahara.

She interprets the inheritance through repayment:

> `もうひとりの私に……私は何もお返しできない……`

This is not yet sustainable remembrance.

Kaede has become:

> a creditor whose unfinished life Hanakaede must complete.

The problem is not gratitude.

The problem is jurisdiction.

Kaede's dream becomes capable of governing Hanakaede's future despite Kaede never coercing her.

Sakuta's decisive correction is:

> `『かえで』の願いを、花楓に叶えてほしいわけじゃない`

This separates:

- remembering Kaede;
- from obeying Kaede.

# 7. `Animal Land`: remembrance becomes safe after differentiation

The first bonus novella is one of the clearest continuing-bond texts in the corpus.

Its governing development is:

> Hanakaede can learn about Kaede because she no longer believes learning requires becoming Kaede.

The zoo is crucial because it is the same place and not the same event.

Kaede visited Ueno Zoo near the end of her continuity.

Hanakaede later returns using the annual pass bearing `梓川かえで`.

The place contains both histories.

It does not force one to overwrite the other.

## 7.1 The annual pass

The pass was originally future-oriented.

For Kaede, buying it meant:

> there can be another visit.

After her disappearance, the same object crosses continuity.

Hanakaede can use it without pretending the promised future belongs to exactly the same autobiographical person.

The pass therefore becomes:

> **a future object whose meaning survives even when the original future does not.**

## 7.2 The candid photograph

Nodoka unexpectedly possesses a photograph of Kaede at Shichirigahama.

Sakuta does not have it.

Mai does not have it.

No one individual owns the complete archive.

When Sakuta sees the image, the essential conclusion is:

> Kaede was there.

The photograph proves presence.

It does not explain personhood.

Stories remain necessary.

## 7.3 Hanakaede's request

Hanakaede says:

> `もうひとりの私のこと、もっと教えてほしい`

then specifies:

> `少しずつ話してほしい`

The pacing matters.

She requests biography under her own control.

She does not ask for total memory restoration or immediate identity fusion.

## 7.4 Sakuta's continuing bond

Sakuta says he may return to see:

> `パンダと『かえで』`

The phrase is metaphorical and relational.

Kaede is not established as a ghost at the zoo.

The place has become a site where Sakuta can access her memory without requiring maximum pain.

The novella's deepest grief proposition is therefore:

> **A memory does not become less loyal because it stops being unbearable.**

# 8. `Spring Days`: life after remembrance

The second bonus does not center grief explicitly, yet it completes the ethical movement.

Hanakaede works.

She earns wages.

She chooses gifts.

She moves between two homes.

She meets Kotomi without Sakuta managing the relationship.

This matters because sustainable remembrance must leave room for the successor's ordinary future.

A life dominated by the predecessor's memory would still be a life governed by absence.

Hanakaede's development demonstrates another principle:

> **The most faithful relation to an absent person may be to build a life the absent person does not control.**

# 9. R1 Kaede: grief becomes world-choice temptation

Volumes 14–15 make the Kaede problem radically harder.

R1 gives Sakuta what the earlier series treated as impossible:

- older Kaede;
- continued school life;
- club activity;
- breakfast;
- lunches;
- ordinary adolescence;
- Hanakaede still existing separately.

This is not merely a fantasy image.

R1 Kaede has:

- memories;
- preferences;
- relationships;
- fear;
- current agency.

The world therefore gives Sakuta a grief-driven temptation:

> keep the reality in which no sister has to be lost.

If the series simply called R1 fake, it would evade its own personhood commitments.

Instead, it gives Kaede standing.

# 10. Kaede's final choice and the limits of consent

R1 Kaede finds Hanakaede's diary and learns that the dissociative disorder eventually resolves.

She fears disappearance.

The narration names the fear.

Yet she says:

> `かえでは帰りたいです`

and:

> `かえでは、かえでの本当のお兄ちゃんのところに帰りたいです`

The scene matters because Sakuta does not decide for her.

Kaede's agency makes the transition ethically more defensible.

But consent does not remove every difficulty.

The novel does not explain exactly:

- what R1 Kaede subjectively experiences afterward;
- whether she regards return as continuity, death, integration, or something else;
- whether any R1 memory survives elsewhere.

The correct final judgment is therefore:

> **Kaede's choice matters decisively, but it does not make discontinuity metaphysically painless.**

# 11. Shoko: grief for possible persons

Shoko expands grief beyond the relation between body and autobiographical memory.

Sakuta encounters multiple future configurations involving:

- his own death;
- Mai's death;
- Shoko's survival;
- Shoko as spouse;
- Shoko as the person who relinquishes a fulfilled future;
- current Shoko who remembers possible futures;
- later current Shoko who grows into ordinary high-school life.

Some of these persons no longer belong to the stabilized current history.

The series nevertheless treats them as ethically consequential.

Sakuta remembers.

Shoko remembers.

Their choices were shaped by those relationships.

This creates a category best described as:

> **grief for possible persons whose lives were real enough to be remembered and lost, even if they are not ordinary deceased persons in one linear history.**

# 12. Mai's future death and the refusal of clean survival

Volume 7 begins in a future where Mai has died after saving Sakuta.

The grief is not hypothetical to the Sakuta living that state.

He has experienced:

- the hospital;
- confirmation of death;
- a world without Mai;
- his own desire to live emerging through the horror of losing her.

The later stabilized history does not erase that experience from Sakuta's moral development.

This is why the final memory solution is necessary.

If only the final world counted, then grief for dead-future Mai would be dismissed as a false event.

Instead, the experience remains part of the person Sakuta becomes.

# 13. Future Shoko as a relinquished possible life

One future Shoko appears to possess:

- an adult body;
- memory;
- relationship with Sakuta;
- possible marital future;
- a life she can relinquish.

Her disappearance from the current present is not narrated as an ordinary death.

Yet the series gives the possible life moral weight.

The important final distinction is:

> **A possible person may lose current actuality without losing the ethical significance of the relationship.**

Sakuta's later statement that future Shoko's kindness still helps make him is the mature version of this insight.

The possible person persists as:

- memory;
- language;
- moral disposition.

She does not need to remain physically accessible.

# 14. Ordinary Shoko is not a diminished remainder

The series resists a common temptation in speculative fiction:

> the mysterious adult version is the “real” or narratively valuable person, while the ordinary younger version is merely the origin.

Current Shoko's later life is the victory.

She:

- attends school;
- writes letters;
- makes friends;
- moves;
- grows;
- becomes animal-club president;
- considers medical knowledge and rare-disease support.

The miraculous future woman does not need to remain present because the child receives an ordinary future.

That ordinary future is not narratively smaller.

It is the thing the entire Shoko arc fought to make possible.

# 15. Survivor guilt and the danger of converting memory into debt

Shoko's survival depends upon donor histories.

This creates an obvious moral temptation:

> if somebody died and I lived, my life must repay the death.

The series repeatedly resists that logic.

Sakuta first treats his gratitude to Shoko as a reason to make his life disposable.

Later, Shoko can interpret Toko's death as something her survival caused.

Both forms of guilt confuse:

- causal history;
- moral debt;
- present authorship.

The mature response is not ingratitude.

It is:

> **the life saved becomes a life the survivor may author rather than a memorial project owned by the dead.**

This is why Shoko's later medical interest remains open rather than compulsory.

# 16. Toko: actual death and distributed aftermath

Toko is the series' clearest case of irreversible physical death.

She dies at sixteen on Christmas Eve.

Her death produces several continuities that must remain distinct.

## 16.1 Bodily continuity

Her heart supports Shoko.

This does not make Shoko Toko.

## 16.2 Artistic continuity

Her compositions survive.

Miori performs them.

The public artist becomes composite.

## 16.3 Documentary continuity

The exchange diary, photographs, computer, guitar, and preserved room remain.

## 16.4 Relational continuity

Her parents grieve.

Miori continues speaking to and about her.

Shoko corresponds with the donor family.

## 16.5 Social/public continuity

The name `霧島透子` becomes culturally powerful enough that other people disappear into it.

The series' task is to prevent these continuities from being collapsed into resurrection.

Toko remains dead.

# 17. Miori's failure to cry is not failure to grieve

Miori calls herself:

> `涙の一滴も出ないような薄情な女`

The self-judgment relies upon a culturally legible image of proper grief:

- tears;
- visible collapse;
- openness to consolation;
- recognizable need.

Miori does not perform that form.

She becomes angry at people who contact her with:

- `大丈夫？`
- encouragement;
- offers to listen.

She discards her smartphone.

That does not mean she lacks attachment.

Her later behavior is saturated with Toko:

- curry-bun counterfactual guilt;
- singing Toko's songs;
- preserving Toko's name;
- interpreting the final lyric as rejection;
- remembering many realities;
- wanting a Toko-alive world.

The correct conclusion is:

> **Failure to match the expected performance of grief does not establish emotional absence.**

# 18. Public remembrance and the composite Toko artist

When people stop talking about Toko, Miori begins singing the songs Toko left.

This is initially a continuing-bond act.

It fulfills Toko's repeated wish that Miori sing.

It also keeps strangers saying:

> `霧島透子`

But the act creates a new problem.

The public identity merges:

- Toko's name;
- Toko's compositions;
- Miori's voice;
- Miori's performance;
- audience recognition.

This composite is neither simple deception nor simple preservation.

It becomes unstable when the public begins seeking one living body who “is” Toko.

Nene, Mai, and the mass Santas show how remembrance can become substitution when the public role is detached from biography.

The tragedy is not that Toko is remembered.

It is that remembering the name begins erasing living people.

# 19. The final song: ambivalence rather than correction

The short known version of `Turn The World Upside Down` ends with:

> `君に出会わなければよかった`

Miori reads the line as Toko's final rejection.

Volume 15 does not erase it.

Instead, the completed song allows reverse movement toward:

> `君に出会えてよかった`

The song's literary achievement is to preserve both.

The painful line remains true as grief:

> losing you hurts enough to make encounter feel unbearable.

The grateful line also remains true:

> the relationship was worth having.

This is the final-series grief thesis in lyrical form:

> **regret over loss and gratitude for encounter can belong to one relationship without one exposing the other as false.**

# 20. `透子を返して`: grief rather than a technical demand

Miori's decisive cry is:

> `透子を返して`

Earlier in the final arc, the desire to get Toko back can be translated into world mechanics:

- find another reality;
- realize a possibility;
- rewrite the present;
- identify a substitute.

After Miori finally cries, the same words change category.

They become:

> an impossible protest against death.

No one can satisfy it.

The inability to satisfy it does not make the cry irrational noise.

It is the point where Miori stops converting loss into a solvable system and experiences the irreversibility itself.

# 21. The diary returns to Miori

The exchange diary passes through several ethical states.

### Private relation

Toko and Miori write to one another.

### Family archive

Toko's mother preserves it.

### Investigative evidence

Shoko carries it; Sakuta could read it.

### Relational restraint

Sakuta initially refuses because he wants Miori to tell him herself.

### Interpretive key

Shoko shows the final page when she believes Miori's reading must be challenged.

### Continuing-bond object

The diary is returned to Miori.

Miori embraces it and says:

> `わたし、透子に出会えてよかったよ`

The object no longer exists primarily to solve a mystery.

It returns to the person for whom it records a relationship.

# 22. Miori's second grief: discovering that she has continued living

After the public performance, Miori realizes:

> `透子が、思い出に変わってたことが`

and:

> `透子の死を、もう受け入れている自分がいたことが`

This is a different grief from the first.

The first is:

> Toko died.

The second is:

> I survived long enough for Toko to become memory.

Miori interprets her own functioning as betrayal.

She graduated.

Entered university.

Laughed.

Met Sakuta.

Sometimes lived without actively suffering.

This produces the fear that:

> healing proves insufficient love.

The series rejects that equation.

Memory changing temporal form does not mean attachment became false.

# 23. Friendship as the living structure of remembrance

Sakuta does not offer Miori resurrection or proof of innocence.

He offers:

> future listening.

> `透子のことを話したくなったら、いつでも僕を呼べ`

Why?

> `美東は友達だからだ`

This is the decisive relational alternative to serial possibility.

A world that may rewrite tomorrow cannot guarantee continuity.

A friend can promise:

> call me later.

The promise is ordinary and therefore profound.

Miori's smartphone becomes part of this.

The device she once discarded because other people imposed grief upon her becomes a chosen tool through which she can reach a friend when she wants to talk about Toko.

# 24. Art as presence without substitution

Three years later, Sakuta briefly mistakes a stranger for Miori.

He checks.

The woman lacks:

- Miori's presence;
- characteristic mole;
- relational recognition.

He accepts that she is not Miori.

He does not force resemblance into identity.

Instead, he plays the complete song Miori left.

This is a mature continuing bond.

The absent friend is encountered through:

- art;
- memory;
- chosen listening.

No body has to be recruited as substitute.

# 26. Remembrance and irreversibility

The series' deepest grief development is the move from:

> “If the relationship mattered, the person must remain recoverable”

into:

> “The relationship can remain meaningful after recoverability ends.”

This does not make irreversibility good.

Miori still wants Toko back.

Sakuta still wants Kaede.

Shoko still carries moral residue.

The point is not acceptance as cheerful agreement.

It is:

> **the present no longer has to be treated as provisional until the absent person returns.**

# 27. Strongest counterreadings

## 27.1 AoButa sentimentalizes grief by making every absence spiritually retrievable

There is evidence for this criticism.

The series repeatedly gives characters:

- dreams;
- future selves;
- alternate worlds;
- surviving songs;
- donor links;
- photographs;
- diaries.

Very little disappears without trace.

The strongest response is not that the criticism is false.

AoButa strongly prefers trace, memory, and relational residue.

But the finale preserves irreversible loss in the Toko case and refuses literal resurrection.

Its final answer is not that everyone remains recoverable.

It is that relationship can survive in changed form.

## 27.2 Kaede's R1 choice is a convenient device that lets Sakuta avoid responsibility

This counterreading has force.

By making Kaede choose `帰りたい`, the novel reduces the burden of Sakuta choosing a world that may end her current continuity.

The scene therefore serves the plot ethically as well as emotionally.

Still, removing Kaede's agency would be worse.

The controlled conclusion is:

> her choice is necessary but not sufficient to eliminate the ethical discomfort.

## 27.3 Future persons should not be grieved as though they were actual people

This objection depends upon a strict actuality boundary the series itself repeatedly destabilizes.

Sakuta and Shoko remember the future persons.

Those relationships alter choices.

The correct distinction is not:

> actual grief versus fake grief.

It is:

> grief for a current-world death versus grief for a possible person whose remembered life no longer governs the present.

## 27.4 Miori's final song interpretation is too neat

The complete song provides a powerful answer, but it risks making years of grief depend upon one clever formal reversal.

The novel partly avoids this by making the song only the trigger.

Miori still has to:

- cry;
- narrate Toko's biography;
- accept the diary;
- revisit Enoshima;
- recognize Toko as memory;
- form friendship.

The grief resolution is therefore larger than the lyric puzzle.

## 27.5 The series encourages “moving on” as the only legitimate grief outcome

Too strong.

The series does not ask characters to stop speaking about the absent.

It repeatedly legitimizes continuing bonds.

The distinction is between:

- memory that coexists with present life;
- and remembrance that requires the present to reproduce the absent person.

# 31. Final synthesis

AoButa's absent people never disappear cleanly.

Kaede leaves a notebook, a zoo pass, a photograph, a changed brother, and another continuity living with the consequences of her two years.

Future Shoko leaves kindness.

Dead-future Mai leaves Sakuta's first honest admission that he wants to live.

Toko leaves a heart, songs, a diary, parents, a friend, and a public name.

Miori leaves a complete song.

The danger is obvious.

If every trace becomes a command, then remembrance begins governing the living.

Hanakaede must become Kaede.

Shoko must repay a donor.

Miori must find a world where Toko returns.

Another body must become Toko.

R1 must remain because Kaede lives there.

The series gradually learns another form.

Remember the person.

Do not substitute.

Tell the stories.

Keep the objects.

Return to the place.

Let the successor choose.

Let the survivor live.

Let the dead remain dead and particular.

Let pain change without treating that change as betrayal.

The final song is therefore not an instruction to choose gratitude over regret.

It holds both.

> `君に出会わなければよかった`

and:

> `君に出会えてよかった`

The second line matters because the first is still intelligible.

The relationship caused vulnerability to loss.

The loss does not retroactively make the encounter worthless.

That is the series' mature continuing-bond ethic:

> **The person does not have to remain present in the old form for the encounter to remain part of the life that continues.**
