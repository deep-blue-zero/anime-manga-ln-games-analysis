## Archival source: Assistant Turn 20 — Original Episode 5 deep-dive response


# *Girls Band Cry* Episode 5 — Deep Dive

Episode 5 finally answers the question Nina has been pressing since Episode 1: **why did Momoka leave Diamond Dust?** The answer is much more painful than “Momoka remained authentic while everyone else sold out.”

Momoka did reject a commercial transformation she could not accept. But the original members’ deepest shared promise was not a particular sound or ideology. It was that they would remain together—even as old women. The others urged her to endure the compromise temporarily, gain influence, and reclaim artistic control later. Momoka left anyway.

She preserved one kind of integrity by violating another.

The episode’s central proposition is therefore:

> **There is no clean position outside compromise. Remaining can betray the work; leaving can betray the people. What matters is not proving oneself morally uncontaminated, but deciding what one will continue to affirm after learning that everyone involved has been wounded and implicated.**

Nina begins as a possessive fan demanding that Momoka validate the meaning she took from Diamond Dust. She ends by choosing Momoka after discovering that Momoka is not an unambiguous martyr. Momoka, meanwhile, is forced to acknowledge that her outward neutrality toward the new Diamond Dust is partly a lie she tells because condemning them would require defending the decision she still experiences as guilt.

The episode then transforms the trio’s stigmas—**school refusal, withdrawal, and lying**—into stage costumes. Their new band begins to define itself not by claiming purity, but by exposing the accusations already attached to its members.

---

## 1\. Narrative lens

### Nina has become frighteningly eager to perform

The trio visits the old live house Serbian Night, where Momoka has an established relationship with the owner. The venue is small, old, and financially precarious. Its audience is described as experienced enough to show open displeasure when a band is bad.

When Momoka asks whether they want to perform there, Nina answers immediately:

> 「やります」
> “We’ll do it.”

Momoka replies:

> 「ちょっとは悩め」
> “At least think about it a little.”

Only two episodes earlier, Nina had to be coerced into her first public performance. Now she accepts before considering:

-   the work required;

-   the audience;

-   the cost;

-   her study schedule;

-   or whether the trio is remotely ready.


The shift confirms how deeply Episode 3 changed her. Nina is no longer merely consenting to Momoka’s musical project. She wants the stage.

But this enthusiasm retains her usual absolutism. She moves rapidly from refusal to total investment. Music is becoming another area in which moderation feels intolerable.

---

### The ticket quota exposes the material economy beneath “authentic” music

Momoka gives them tickets to sell. Nina initially assumes that simply attracting people matters more than collecting money, so she gives one away.

Subaru explains why this is strategically foolish:

> 「人間っていうのは
> お金を出したものは無駄にしたくないって思う」
>
> “People don’t want something they paid for to go to waste.”

> 「けど　タダのものは無駄にしても全然惜しくないの」
> “But they don’t mind wasting something they received for free.”

She then reveals the more serious fact:

> 「このチケットって
> 私たちが買い取ったんだよ」
>
> “We bought these tickets ourselves.”

The live house receives money from the bands in advance through participation fees or a **チケットノルマ**, ticket quota. This guarantees some income even if no audience comes.

Nina calls the arrangement unfair:

> 「そんなのずるいじゃないですか」
> “That’s unfair.”

Momoka answers:

> 「そういう商売なんだよ」
> “That’s how the business works.”

This is a crucial addition to the series’ artistic argument. The girls cannot simply express themselves and wait for sincere listeners to appear. Performing requires:

-   venue rent or ticket commitments;

-   personal financial risk;

-   promotion;

-   selling to acquaintances;

-   converting social relationships into potential audience members.


The episode refuses the fantasy that independent music exists outside commerce. Diamond Dust’s major-label compromises and the trio’s ticket quotas are different in scale, but both emerge from the need to finance performance.

Authenticity does not abolish material structure.

---

### Momoka again protects the younger girls through concealed self-sacrifice

Nina realizes that Momoka personally covered the ticket expense without explaining it to them.

Subaru interprets the omission:

> 「桃香さんからしたら　子供なんだよ　私たち」
> “From Momoka’s point of view, we’re children.”

Nina objects:

> 「なんで」
> “Why?”

Subaru points out the obvious:

-   they are younger;

-   they have not entered the workforce;

-   they lack Momoka’s experience with the music business.


Momoka’s behavior is caring, but it repeats an established pattern. She gives things away and absorbs costs silently:

-   she surrendered the song;

-   gave Nina her guitar;

-   provided food and shelter;

-   purchased the live-house tickets;

-   refuses to make her vulnerability another person’s burden.


This generosity is also a form of control. By withholding the financial reality, she decides what Nina and Subaru are ready to know and prevents them from participating fully in the risk.

Nina’s later demand that Momoka speak honestly about Diamond Dust belongs to the same conflict. Momoka repeatedly protects herself and others by deciding which truths should remain unspoken.

---

### Nina learns strategic social performance almost immediately

After being corrected, Nina returns to the preparatory school and attempts to sell tickets. A classmate who previously received one for free asks for three more.

Nina now says:

> 「３枚で7500円です」
> “Three tickets will be ¥7,500.”

When the classmate protests that they were free before, Nina improvises an elaborate explanation:

-   the free distribution was a campaign;

-   that campaign ended;

-   the usual price is ¥3,000;

-   because of the previous interaction, she is offering a ¥500 discount.


The classmate calls her a liar.

Subaru calls it:

> 「努力」
> “Effort.”

This small joke continues Episode 4’s examination of performance and honesty. Nina is becoming capable of presenting information tactically rather than announcing every fact with total literalism.

The distinction remains ethically unstable. Nina is indeed lying. But she is also learning that sustaining a collective project requires persuasion, adaptation, and a willingness to ask others for something.

Her initial “purity”—give the ticket away because money is not the point—would only leave Momoka paying for everything.

Practical responsibility requires Nina to become slightly less pure.

---

### The Diamond Dust tickets bring Momoka’s past into the trio’s hands

The Serbian Night owner gives Nina and Subaru complimentary tickets to Diamond Dust’s major-label debut performance. The members had visited to thank the venue for its past support.

He also shares the industry rumor:

-   Diamond Dust was told that its music and appearance needed to become more commercially marketable;

-   Momoka alone refused;

-   the others remained.


This is the first apparently external confirmation of Nina’s preferred interpretation. Momoka did not leave because she lost interest or lacked talent. She rejected a commercial redirection.

Nina and Subaru decide to attend without Momoka.

Subaru is curious. Nina is emotionally compelled.

For Nina, Diamond Dust is not just a band she likes. Its older music is entangled with:

-   her survival;

-   her decision to leave Kumamoto;

-   her conviction that she was not wrong;

-   her emerging relationship with Momoka.


She is about to inspect what has happened to something she considers sacred.

---

### The scale of Diamond Dust’s success makes the dispute materially undeniable

Diamond Dust’s show is sold out. The venue is immense compared with Serbian Night:

-   coordinated light sticks;

-   giant LED displays;

-   polished lighting;

-   an elaborate stage;

-   a large, synchronized audience;

-   professional styling;

-   an immediately legible commercial image.


The performance demonstrates that the other members’ decision worked in one sense. Diamond Dust has gained the visibility and scale Momoka could not achieve independently.

This matters because the episode does not allow Nina or the viewer to dismiss their choice as simple cowardice. The commercial path produced real success, a real audience, and a technically accomplished show.

Momoka later acknowledges this:

> 「私がいた頃よりは売れてるみたいだし」
> “They seem to be more successful than when I was there.”

The cost is not that they became bad. The cost is that they became something Momoka could not remain inside.

---

### Hina collapses Nina’s private past into Momoka’s public past

The greatest shock is not merely the group’s new style. Nina recognizes its new vocalist:

> 「昔友達だった子」
> “A girl who used to be my friend.”

> 「絶交したけど」
> “Though I broke off the friendship.”

The episode does not yet establish the complete history between Nina and Hina. It would be premature to define Hina’s exact role in the bullying or school conflict. What we know is narrower but still potent:

-   they were once friends;

-   they are now estranged;

-   Nina experiences seeing Hina in this position as an intolerable collision.


The girl from Nina’s abandoned life is now singing the music connected to Nina’s escape from that life.

Nina shouts:

> 「お前が歌うな」
> “You don’t get to sing that.”

That sentence contains several simultaneous claims:

-   Hina does not have the moral right to carry these words.

-   This song belongs to the version of Diamond Dust that saved Nina.

-   The new group is wearing the identity of something it displaced.

-   Nina’s former friend has entered the sanctuary through which Nina tried to escape her.


Hina occupies the position Nina has recently begun occupying beside Momoka: the vocalist who gives Momoka’s musical world a new voice.

That makes her not only a figure from Nina’s past, but a dark mirror of Nina’s present.

---

### Nina’s fandom becomes openly possessive

After fleeing the concert, Nina erupts. She calls Diamond Dust:

> 「私が命の次に大事なもの」
> “The thing most important to me after my life.”

> 「宝物」
> “My treasure.”

Momoka responds with unusual severity. Nina attended the concert voluntarily, became angry at what she saw, and now behaves as if the band’s decisions must answer to her.

Momoka names the behavior:

> 「厄介って言うんだよ」
> “That’s what you call a nuisance—a problematic fan.”

Nina defends herself by asserting seniority and intensity:

> 「私はあそこに集まった人よりは
> ずっと前からダイヤモンドダスト聴いていて
> 愛してるんです」
>
> “I’ve listened to Diamond Dust far longer than the people gathered there, and I love them.”

Momoka’s answer is decisive:

> 「お前だけのものじゃないんだよ　ダイダスは」
> “Diamond Dust doesn’t belong only to you.”

This is an essential correction to any reading that treats Nina’s passion as inherently morally superior.

Her love is real. So is her entitlement.

Nina assumes that suffering through and loving the older music grants her interpretive ownership. New listeners, current members, and even the original artist become less authoritative than the meaning she personally received.

That is precisely the dangerous edge of fandom: the work becomes so important to the fan’s identity that the creators’ freedom to change it begins to feel like an attack.

---

### Nina asks Momoka to defend Nina’s life, not merely an old band

Nina repeatedly demands that Momoka say the current Diamond Dust is wrong:

> 「今のダイダスは違うって言ってくださいよ」
> “Tell me that the current Diamond Dust is different.”

> 「あんなの　ダイヤモンドダストじゃない」
> “Say that isn’t Diamond Dust.”

Momoka refuses. She argues:

> 「バンドも人間と同じで
> 成長もするし　変化もするんだ」
>
> “Bands are like people. They grow and change.”

> 「私はもうあそこにはいないんだ
> あとは自由だ」
>
> “I’m no longer there. What they do now is their freedom.”

This is a mature and defensible position. Momoka has left; she does not own the remaining members. Their commercial choices are theirs to make.

But Nina senses that Momoka’s neutrality is incomplete. She asks the question Momoka cannot answer abstractly:

> If change is natural and the current form is acceptable, why did you leave?

Nina then applies Momoka’s own moral language:

> 「自分が間違ってないって思ったから
> やめたんですよね」
>
> “You left because you believed you weren’t wrong, didn’t you?”

This is the core of Nina’s desperation.

Nina has built her life around the claim:

> I left because I was not wrong.

Momoka’s song gave that claim emotional legitimacy. If Momoka now says that the commercially altered Diamond Dust is simply another valid form and that her own departure was a mistake, Nina fears the song’s ethic may have been hollow.

She is demanding that Momoka validate not merely a musical preference, but Nina’s entire structure of resistance.

---

### Nina barges into Momoka’s interior in exactly the way she fears others doing to her

Momoka finally explodes:

> 「人ん中に土足でズカズカ入ってきやがって」
> “You come barging into other people’s insides with your shoes on.”

This is one of the episode’s most important reversals.

Nina has repeatedly feared that intimacy is an attempted invasion:

-   someone wants to decide how she should respond;

-   someone tries to define her;

-   someone expects access to her feelings;

-   someone imposes a role.


Here Nina does exactly that to Momoka.

She insists she knows:

-   what Diamond Dust meant to Momoka;

-   what Momoka’s soul was committed to;

-   why Momoka left;

-   what Momoka should feel now;

-   what verdict Momoka must issue.


Nina’s sensitivity to coercion does not automatically make her respectful of other people’s boundaries. Because she experiences her own interpretation as morally urgent, she can become exceptionally invasive.

---

### Momoka’s confession destroys the easy purity narrative

Momoka finally reveals the founding promise of Diamond Dust:

> 「ガールズバンドで
> ばあさんになっても
> オリジナルメンバーで続けようって」
>
> “We would remain a girls’ band and continue with the original members even when we became old women.”

She says that this was effectively all they had:

> 「音楽性も主張もない
> 目指していたのはそれだけ」
>
> “There was no particular musical doctrine or statement. That was the only thing we aimed for.”

When the label demanded compromise, the others did not simply surrender. They argued:

> 「今は耐えろ」
> “Endure it for now.”

> 「いつかのし上がって
> 自分たちのやりたいことができるようになる」
>
> “Someday we’ll rise high enough to do what we really want.”

They tried desperately to persuade Momoka.

She left anyway.

> 「だから　悪いのは私なんだ」
> “That’s why I’m the one who was wrong.”

Momoka’s guilt is not an admission that commercial compromise was artistically correct. It is an admission that she chose artistic self-consistency over the group’s most basic relational promise.

The other members chose:

-   temporary compromise;

-   collective continuity;

-   belief that future power might restore freedom.


Momoka chose:

-   immediate refusal;

-   departure;

-   preservation of a line she could not cross.


Neither position is morally clean.

Remaining might have hollowed out the music.

Leaving broke the promise that defined the group.

---

### Momoka and Nina have made structurally similar choices

The revelation brings Momoka and Nina even closer while also complicating Nina’s idolization.

Both girls experienced a system demanding that they endure something they considered intolerable for the sake of a future benefit.

Nina was effectively told:

-   remain within school;

-   absorb the social cost;

-   protect future educational options;

-   do not let the incident destroy your prospects.


Momoka was told:

-   accept the label’s direction;

-   remain with the band;

-   gain commercial power;

-   reclaim artistic agency later.


Both refused a promised future because the present compromise felt like self-betrayal.

Both then suffered:

-   damaged relationships;

-   uncertainty;

-   guilt;

-   financial or educational risk;

-   accusations that they had chosen badly.


But Momoka sees something Nina does not yet fully see: refusing to bend can hurt people who were not simply enemies.

That is why Momoka cannot comfortably tell Nina, “I was right.”

---

### Nina’s loyalty becomes more mature after the ideal collapses

Nina initially wanted Momoka to remain a flawless symbol. The confession makes that impossible.

Momoka may have been:

-   artistically principled;

-   emotionally stubborn;

-   unable to tolerate compromise;

-   responsible for breaking the group;

-   right in one sense;

-   wrong in another.


Nina responds:

> 「それでも　私は桃香さんが好きです」
> “Even so, I still love you, Momoka.”

> 「私の心を動かしたのは
> あの時のダイヤモンドダストだから」
>
> “Because it was the Diamond Dust from that time that moved my heart.”

> 「桃香さんだから」
> “Because it was you.”

This is a significant development in Nina’s attachment.

She no longer says:

> I love you because you were unquestionably right.

She says:

> Even if your decision contained guilt and contradiction, what you made reached me, and I choose the person who made it.

That does not fully cure her possessive fandom. But it begins to replace idealization with loyalty to a fallible person.

---

### Momoka’s drunken collapse reveals how badly she needed that loyalty

After the confrontation, Momoka drinks heavily and becomes physically dependent on Nina and Subaru to get home.

Outside, she repeatedly apologizes:

> 「ごめんね」
> 「ごめん」

The exact addressee remains emotionally ambiguous. She may be apologizing to:

-   Nina;

-   the former members;

-   herself;

-   everyone affected by the decision;

-   or all of them at once.


Subaru suggests:

> 「嬉しかったのかもね」
> “Maybe she was happy.”

Momoka denies it:

> 「違うよ」
> “No.”

But the denial is unconvincing.

Nina’s loyalty has given Momoka something she had denied herself: the possibility that leaving Diamond Dust did not render everything she made meaningless.

Momoka is still guilty. She is also relieved that someone can know the guilt and continue loving the music.

---

### Subaru converts emotional crisis into an artistic decision

Subaru’s role is less dramatic but structurally indispensable. She:

-   explains the ticket economy;

-   accompanies Nina to Diamond Dust;

-   recognizes that Nina’s deepest reaction concerns the new vocalist;

-   tries to mediate the argument;

-   manages the public embarrassment at the izakaya;

-   helps carry the intoxicated Momoka;

-   and finally converts the entire crisis into material.


She tells Nina:

> 「怒りも　喜びも　哀しさも
> 全部ぶちこめるのが
> バンドのいいところ」
>
> “The good thing about a band is that you can throw all of it in—anger, happiness, and sadness.”

Subaru does not attempt to settle the philosophical dispute. She asks what the band can make from it.

She then assigns Nina responsibility for the live costumes.

That is a subtle transfer of authority. Nina is not merely the emotionally volatile singer whom Momoka dresses and directs. She is asked to determine how the trio should present its wounds to an audience.

---

### The venue commodifies Momoka’s past despite her explicit boundary

Momoka had clearly instructed the live-house staff:

> 「元ダイヤモンドダストって宣伝はなしね」
> “Do not promote me as a former Diamond Dust member.”

The promotional page nevertheless announces:

> “Former Diamond Dust member Momoka appearing.”

The owner apologizes and claims the staff acted independently, but the result is financially successful: the show sells out.

This repeats the episode’s larger dilemma on a smaller scale.

Momoka does not want to exploit her former identity. The venue needs tickets sold. Her name has value whether she wants to use it or not.

The new band cannot make its debut before this audience as though Diamond Dust does not exist. The crowd has largely come because of Momoka’s past.

Nina therefore asks the correct artistic question:

> What should we show these people once they arrive?

They cannot control why the audience came. They can control what mark they leave.

## 2\. Japanese-language lens

### 「厄介」 identifies Nina as a recognizable type of fan

Momoka’s use of **厄介** carries more than the general meaning “troublesome.”

In fan and live-performance contexts, an **厄介** fan is someone whose attachment becomes disruptive, entitled, or invasive. Nina displays several features of that posture:

-   claiming superior legitimacy because she listened earlier;

-   treating personal emotional investment as ownership;

-   refusing the current group’s right to change;

-   policing who may sing the songs;

-   demanding that the former artist ratify her interpretation.


Momoka’s term is harsh because it accurately places Nina’s behavior inside a social pattern, not merely an individual emotional episode.

The series therefore does not romanticize every form of passionate fandom.

---

### 「お前が歌うな」 is a claim about moral voice

Nina does not shout merely:

> “I dislike your singing.”

She says:

> 「お前が歌うな」
> “You must not be the one to sing.”

The emphasis falls on the subject.

The song’s musical quality is secondary. Nina believes the singer’s life and moral position determine whether she has the right to embody the words.

This aligns with Nina’s understanding of authenticity: words cannot be separated from the person voicing them.

The series will likely continue testing that belief, because songs inevitably outlive and exceed their creators.

---

### 「命の次に大事」 reveals the severity of Nina’s attachment

Calling Diamond Dust the thing most important “after life itself” is hyperbolic, but Nina does not appear to experience it as exaggeration.

The phrase explains both the beauty and danger of her attachment. The music gave her a structure through which to survive. Any change to it therefore feels almost like an alteration of her own life-support system.

Momoka hears the entitlement.

Nina experiences threatened survival.

Both readings are true.

---

### 「お前だけのものじゃない」

Momoka’s line:

> 「お前だけのものじゃないんだよ」
> “It isn’t yours alone.”

establishes a crucial philosophy of art.

A song may belong differently to:

-   its writer;

-   its performers;

-   former members;

-   current members;

-   old fans;

-   new fans;

-   the label;

-   the listener whose life it changed.


No single relationship exhausts the work’s meaning.

Nina is correct that the song belongs to her emotionally.

She is wrong to infer that it therefore does not belong to anyone else.

---

### Nina demands 「好きか嫌いか」 instead of institutional neutrality

Momoka says she has no right to deny the current group’s freedom.

Nina responds:

> 「そんなことを聞いてるんじゃないんですよ
> 好きか嫌いかって話です」
>
> “That isn’t what I’m asking. I’m asking whether you like it or hate it.”

Nina rejects Momoka’s rights-based answer because it avoids affect.

Momoka can sincerely believe:

-   they have the right to continue;

-   they may be commercially successful;

-   she should not interfere.


But Nina wants the private judgment concealed beneath that mature posture.

This is one of Nina’s strengths: she detects when correct principles are being used to avoid emotional truth.

It is also one of her dangers: she believes another person owes her access to that truth.

---

### 「魂かけていた」 turns Momoka into a symbol

Nina calls Diamond Dust the band upon which Momoka had staked her soul:

> 「桃香さんが魂かけていたバンド」

Momoka responds:

> 「勝手に決めつけるな」
> “Don’t decide that for me.”

The phrase sounds admiring, but it is also coercive. Nina elevates Momoka into a figure whose choices must conform to an epic narrative of artistic purity.

Momoka objects not because Diamond Dust meant nothing, but because Nina is defining the meaning on her behalf.

Idealization can become another way of refusing a person’s complexity.

---

### 「土足でズカズカ」

Momoka’s image of Nina entering someone’s interior **with her shoes on** is deliberately visceral.

In Japanese domestic practice, entering an interior space with outdoor shoes violates a basic boundary between outside contamination and inside safety. **ズカズカ** adds the sense of shameless, heavy-footed intrusion.

Nina does not tentatively ask for access to Momoka’s pain. She marches in and demands that the contents conform to her expectations.

The metaphor is particularly powerful because Nina herself is intensely protective of her own interior.

---

### 「悪いのは私」 is relational guilt, not ideological surrender

Momoka’s:

> 「だから　悪いのは私なんだ」

should not be flattened into:

> “The label was right and I was wrong.”

Her explanation establishes the referent. She was “wrong” relative to the group’s foundational promise to remain together.

The line distinguishes several possible judgments:

-   Was the label’s proposed direction artistically desirable? Momoka evidently thought not.

-   Did the others have the right to accept it? Yes.

-   Did Momoka break the group’s promise by leaving? She believes so.

-   Was leaving therefore wholly wrong? The episode refuses a clean verdict.


Momoka’s guilt is morally specific but emotionally totalizing. Like Nina, she turns a complicated conflict into a judgment against her entire choice.

---

### 「それでも」

Nina’s response begins:

> 「それでも…」

“Even so” is the episode’s most important connective.

It acknowledges that the confession has changed the premise. Nina does not deny Momoka’s guilt or immediately reconstruct her as flawless.

She chooses attachment through the contradiction.

This is more mature than saying Momoka did nothing wrong. It allows love to survive knowledge.

---

### 「爪痕」

Nina becomes fascinated by the expression **爪痕を残す**, to leave a claw mark or lasting trace.

She describes it:

> 「必死であがいて　暴れまわって
> 自分が傷つきながら
> 相手も傷つけてやるって感じ」
>
> “Struggling desperately, rampaging around, hurting yourself while making sure you hurt the other side too.”

This is an unusually violent theory of art.

Nina says Momoka’s song:

> 「胸をえぐられた」
> “gouged into my chest.”

> 「爪痕残された」
> “left a claw mark on me.”

For Nina, meaningful music does not merely comfort. It wounds the listener into becoming someone else.

She wants their band to produce the same irreversible encounter.

---

### 「全部さらけ出して」

Before the performance, Nina’s declaration culminates in:

> 「全部さらけ出して」
> “Expose everything.”

> 「全部を晒して　生きてやる」
> “I’ll live with everything exposed.”

The language is aggressive. **生きてやる** is not simply “I will live.” It carries defiance: *I’ll live, damn it.*

The costumes literalize the statement. Each girl publicly wears a term that could be used to shame or dismiss her.

Exposure becomes both vulnerability and attack.

---

## 3\. Performance lens

### Diamond Dust’s performance is polished enough to make Nina’s anger harder, not easier

The major-label concert is not visually presented as incompetent.

The members:

-   play confidently;

-   occupy an elaborate stage;

-   move within coordinated lighting;

-   command a large audience;

-   have a coherent visual identity;

-   and possess the resources of a professional production.


The new vocalist is framed as charismatic and technically capable. Her pink styling and polished delivery distinguish her from Momoka’s darker, more weathered presence, but the sequence does not visually prove that she is fraudulent.

This matters. Nina cannot dismiss what she sees as obvious failure. She sees a successful version of the band that excludes Momoka and includes Hina.

The spectacle works.

That is part of what makes it unbearable.

---

### Nina’s body registers recognition before she can verbalize it

During the concert, Nina shifts from attentive fascination into shock:

-   her eyes widen;

-   her posture freezes;

-   the crowd’s movement continues around her;

-   the giant screens enlarge Hina’s presence;

-   Nina appears increasingly isolated despite standing among thousands.


Once recognition becomes undeniable, she does not remain to process it. Her body exits before her speech catches up.

Outside, the affect becomes explosive:

-   breathless complaint;

-   repeated questioning;

-   shouting;

-   physical agitation;

-   the attempt to expel obscenity through a supposedly meaningless scream.


As in Episode 2, Nina’s emotion becomes rhythmic and performative when ordinary speech fails.

---

### The scream fails to remain nonverbal

Nina tells Subaru:

> she is about to say something extremely filthy, so she will first scream meaninglessly and release the feeling.

She then ends the scream with:

> 「クソが　死ね」
> “Shit—die.”

Subaru observes:

> 「後半　言葉になっちゃってる」
> “The latter half turned into words.”

The joke is psychologically exact. Nina cannot keep affect and meaning separate. Even her attempt at pure discharge becomes an accusation.

This is also why music suits her. Sound rapidly becomes language, and language becomes moral claim.

---

### Momoka’s restraint deteriorates as Nina approaches the wound

Momoka initially responds to Nina with irritation and dismissive bluntness. She tries to maintain several protective positions:

-   Nina is behaving like a problematic fan.

-   The band does not belong to her.

-   Current Diamond Dust is free to change.

-   Success is preferable to failure.

-   Momoka is no longer involved.

-   She feels neither love nor hatred toward the current form.


As Nina presses, Momoka’s physical composure deteriorates:

-   her responses sharpen;

-   she drinks more;

-   she turns her body defensively;

-   pauses become longer;

-   her anger increasingly resembles pain rather than annoyance.


Nina’s accusation that Momoka never truly cared finally pushes her through the barrier.

The confession is not delivered as a triumphant revelation of integrity. It is weary, ashamed, and almost self-punitive.

---

### Nina’s crying changes the relational balance

Nina openly cries while telling Momoka that the old Diamond Dust changed her life and that she cannot bear Momoka declaring herself defeated.

The tears are not elegant. Momoka points out that Nina’s nose is running.

Nina responds:

> 「バカですよ」
> “I am an idiot.”

> 「バカじゃなければ
> こんなところでこんなことしてません」
>
> “If I weren’t an idiot, I wouldn’t be doing this here.”

The exchange turns the argument into intimacy without pretending that either girl has won.

Nina admits that her attachment is irrational.

Momoka responds not by rejecting it but by teasing her bodily mess.

The scene allows affection to survive humiliation.

---

### Momoka physically collapses into the care she usually provides

Momoka is normally the practical caretaker:

-   she cooks;

-   pays;

-   arranges;

-   secures venues;

-   teaches;

-   lends equipment;

-   pushes the others into action.


After the confession, Nina and Subaru must carry her.

Momoka’s head rests against Nina. Nina supports her weight, while Subaru manages the logistics. The physical arrangement externalizes the emotional one:

> Momoka has allowed the two younger girls to see enough of her past that she can no longer remain entirely self-supporting.

Her repeated apology becomes almost childlike.

Nina, who usually resists dependency, does not reject Momoka’s weight.

---

### The final performance shows a more deliberate Nina

Episode 3’s Nina appeared to be overtaken by the experience of singing. Episode 5’s Nina enters the stage with a concept.

She has chosen:

-   the clothes;

-   the symbolic vocabulary;

-   the emotional target;

-   the effect she wants on an audience that came primarily for Momoka.


Her movement remains volatile, but it is increasingly directed:

-   she points outward;

-   opens her body toward the crowd;

-   occupies the front of the stage;

-   uses the microphone as an instrument of confrontation;

-   repeatedly turns back toward Momoka and Subaru.


She is not merely surviving exposure now. She is trying to impose an experience upon the room.

---

### Momoka appears most alive when “withdrawal” becomes something she can play through

Momoka’s shirt reads:

> **脱退**
> “Withdrawal” or “departure from the group.”

The word names the action around which her guilt has organized itself.

Wearing it does not resolve that guilt. It prevents her from hiding it behind mature neutrality.

During the performance, her body is more forceful and openly engaged than during the farewell-like solo in Episode 1:

-   broader guitar movement;

-   stronger forward posture;

-   visible enjoyment;

-   interaction with Nina’s energy;

-   less sense of narrating a finished past.


The accusation becomes part of the performance rather than the conclusion of her identity.

---

### Subaru’s “lie” becomes stable rhythm

Subaru’s shirt bears:

> **嘘**
> “Lie.”

Her role in the episode repeatedly involves socially useful forms of falsification:

-   helping Nina improvise sales language;

-   mediating conversations;

-   understanding performance;

-   turning private tensions into artistic presentation.


Behind the drum kit, the “liar” provides the most stable structure in the group.

That is not accidental. Subaru’s ability to regulate presentation, timing, and response is precisely what allows Nina and Momoka’s less regulated emotions to coexist musically.

---

### The final musical block is the episode’s densest sonic space

Within the bundled audio, the Diamond Dust concert segment measures approximately **−17.2 LUFS**, while the concluding live-house sequence is slightly denser at roughly **−16.3 LUFS** and has a broad dynamic range of about **12.6 LU**.

Those figures should not be treated as an objective judgment that one band is musically “more powerful”; they are part of the episode’s final mix. But they support the perceptual contrast:

-   Diamond Dust is enormous, controlled, and spatially polished.

-   The trio’s performance is closer, more abrasive, and built to feel as though it is expanding beyond a room barely able to contain it.


---

## 4\. Music lens

### 「空の箱」 becomes the contested body of Diamond Dust

Diamond Dust performs the song that Nina associates most intensely with Momoka. Its lyrics ask how to move forward at an unmapped intersection and reject prefabricated answers.

In the new commercial setting, the song acquires an unavoidable contradiction.

Its core statement is:

> 「正解は無いんだ」
> “There is no single correct answer.”

Yet Nina is demanding a correct answer about who has the right to sing it and which version of the band is legitimate.

Momoka’s own history likewise resists a single correct answer:

-   compromise might have preserved the group;

-   compromise might also have destroyed the music Momoka believed in;

-   leaving preserved Momoka’s boundary;

-   leaving also violated the members’ promise.


The song Nina treats as proof of moral certainty actually speaks from a world without clean solutions.

---

### Hina’s performance threatens Nina because songs carry embodied memory

Nina does not perceive a song as transferable content. The singer’s body and history become part of what the words mean.

Hina singing Momoka’s song therefore feels to Nina like a false occupation of emotional territory.

But the episode also shows why that position cannot remain absolute. Momoka herself no longer owns Diamond Dust, and Nina has already sung Momoka’s songs through her own experience.

Nina’s own legitimacy as a vocalist depends upon the possibility that a song may pass into another life and acquire another voice.

Hina represents that same possibility in a form Nina cannot accept.

---

### Momoka’s confession reveals that the original band’s central composition was relational

Momoka says Diamond Dust had no elaborate musical doctrine. Its real project was:

> remain together.

Their most important “work” was therefore the group itself.

This reframes the split. The dispute was not only over music versus commerce. It was over which part of the band constituted its identity:

-   Momoka treated the artistic boundary as non-negotiable.

-   The others treated collective continuity as non-negotiable.


Each side believed it was preserving the band.

Each side believed the other was abandoning it.

---

### The old song returns under the confession

As Nina insists that Momoka’s original work changed her life, lines from the older song recur:

> 「コタエはだいたい
> カタチばかりの常識だろう」
>
> “Answers are usually little more than conventional forms.”

> 「指先が震えようとも」
> “Even if your fingertips tremble…”

The placement binds Momoka’s past conviction to Nina’s current demand.

But it also complicates Nina’s request for an explicit verdict. The song resists ready-made answers, while Nina wants Momoka to provide one.

The episode honors Nina’s belief in the song while showing that she has not yet fully accepted its uncertainty.

---

### The final song abandons individual purity for compromised plurality

The closing song repeatedly uses **僕ら**, “we.”

Its opening imagery describes an intersection where people continually mix:

> 「絶え間なく混ざる交差点で
> 僕らは何を手にしていた」
>
> “At an intersection where everything mixes without end, what were we holding?”

This is the episode’s exact moral space. Nina, Momoka, Diamond Dust, Hina, the venue, the audience, and the music industry have become entangled.

The song then attacks:

> 「上辺だけの言葉達が
> こだまして削り合った」
>
> “Surface-level words echoed and wore one another down.”

That describes much of Nina and Momoka’s argument:

-   “They are free.”

-   “They are successful.”

-   “I feel neither love nor hate.”

-   “You were right.”

-   “They were wrong.”


Correct-sounding statements grind against each other without reaching the pain beneath them.

---

### The song indicts the desire to remain innocent

Its most important passage is:

> 「僕ら手が汚れないように」
> “So that our hands would not become dirty…”

> 「気づかないフリして
> 聞こえないフリして」
>
> “We pretended not to notice; pretended not to hear.”

Momoka’s detached posture toward Diamond Dust is partly an attempt to remain ethically clean:

-   she left;

-   they are free;

-   she will not condemn them;

-   she will not exploit her past.


But withdrawal does not remove her from the consequences. Her name still sells tickets. Her songs still circulate. Nina still lives through them. The former members still exist inside the choice she made.

Nina’s posture of absolute loyalty also seeks innocence. She wants a morally pure Momoka against a corrupt new Diamond Dust.

The episode denies both forms of cleanliness.

---

### 「正しさの渦」 answers Nina’s obsession with being right

The song describes:

> 「正しさの渦と　流れてく街を
> プカプカ浮かんでどこにゆこう」
>
> “Where will we drift, floating through the city and its vortex of righteousness?”

Nina’s life is organized around **正しさ**, being right:

-   she was not wrong at school;

-   Momoka was not wrong to leave;

-   current Diamond Dust must be wrong;

-   Hina must not sing;

-   compromise must be surrender.


The image of a vortex suggests that correctness can become a current which carries people away rather than guiding them.

The alternative offered is not surrendering moral judgment. It is:

> 「手を繋ごう　夢じゃない」
> “Let’s hold hands; this is not a dream.”

Connection does not require one uncontested verdict first.

---

### The song asks what they will hold up rather than what they will prove

The ending asks:

> 「僕ら　何を浮かべようか」
> “What will we set afloat?”

> 「何を掲げようか」
> “What will we raise?”

The trio’s literal answer is written across their bodies:

-   **不登校** — school refusal/nonattendance;

-   **脱退** — withdrawal;

-   **嘘** — lie.


They do not raise a claim that they are innocent. They raise the social charges against them and turn those charges into a collective image.

That is the band’s first genuine visual manifesto.

---

## 5\. Formal and directorial lens

### Two venues create the episode’s central spatial argument

Diamond Dust performs in a vast hall. The trio performs in a basement live house.

The contrast is comprehensive:

The direction does not simply declare the basement more authentic. It makes clear what the larger venue offers:

-   reach;

-   resources;

-   stability;

-   spectacle;

-   recognition.


The trio’s smaller space offers something else: the possibility of defining themselves before the machinery becomes too large to resist.

---

### The episode moves repeatedly downward

Serbian Night is reached through a descending stairway into a red, narrow basement.

Diamond Dust occupies a monumental hall with a huge elevated stage.

The new band’s path is literally downward—into:

-   the underground scene;

-   financial precarity;

-   cramped corridors;

-   unglamorous labor;

-   Momoka’s buried past.


But “down” is not synonymous with failure. The basement is where suppressed things can become audible.

---

### Tickets become symbols of attention, debt, and ownership

Tickets circulate throughout the episode:

-   the trio must buy and resell their own;

-   Nina mistakenly gives one away;

-   Momoka secretly covers costs;

-   Diamond Dust gives complimentary tickets through the venue owner;

-   the major concert sells out;

-   Momoka’s former identity later sells out the trio’s event.


A ticket is not merely access. It represents the conversion of interest into measurable commitment.

The episode asks who pays for attention:

-   artists pay the venue;

-   fans pay the artists;

-   Momoka pays for the younger members;

-   the new band draws upon the capital of Momoka’s former identity.


No audience is economically innocent.

---

### Diamond Dust is primarily encountered through screens

The new group first appears in promotional media. At the concert, Hina and the members are repeatedly enlarged on giant displays.

Nina therefore confronts Diamond Dust both as musicians and as a manufactured image.

The enormous screens produce distance:

-   Hina’s face becomes unavoidable;

-   the group appears larger than life;

-   Nina remains one anonymous spectator among thousands;

-   her personal history is dwarfed by public branding.


By contrast, the final live-house performance places the bodies close to the audience. There is less space between person, image, and sound.

---

### The red pedestrian signal externalizes Nina’s blocked movement

After leaving the concert, Nina is repeatedly framed in transitional urban space:

-   station passages;

-   exterior walkways;

-   traffic signals;

-   nighttime crowds.


A red pedestrian figure appears as she struggles with the collision between Hina, Diamond Dust, and Momoka.

The image fits her state. She has reached another intersection and cannot proceed using the map she previously trusted.

The song that helped her choose a direction now belongs to a situation she cannot interpret cleanly.

---

### The izakaya turns private conflict into public spectacle

Nina and Momoka conduct their most painful argument in a cramped restaurant while other patrons are nearby.

Subaru repeatedly attempts to regulate the scene and tells those around them not to worry.

This setting matters because neither girl knows how to keep the conflict private:

-   Nina’s fandom concerns a public work;

-   Momoka’s past is publicly branded;

-   their argument affects the band;

-   emotion spills into the surrounding social space.


The scene resembles an unplanned live performance. Nina demands truth; Momoka resists; bystanders become an involuntary audience.

The difference is that no artistic form initially contains the damage.

---

### Alcohol acts as an instrument of de-armoring

Momoka drinks increasingly heavily as Nina presses her. The liquid is not simply comic adult characterization.

Momoka’s usual defenses include:

-   irony;

-   physical looseness;

-   rough language;

-   practical competence;

-   emotional understatement.


After the confession, those defenses collapse. She can no longer stand independently and repeats apologies without controlling their meaning.

Nina’s tears and Momoka’s intoxication produce parallel forms of bodily exposure.

Neither can remain the composed interpreter of the other.

---

### The costumes turn labels into chosen visibility

Episode 3 placed Nina in clothing she hated in order to disrupt her self-consciousness. Episode 5 lets Nina choose.

Her choice is not flattering. Each shirt bears a blunt social accusation:

-   Nina: **不登校**

-   Momoka: **脱退**

-   Subaru: **嘘**


The clothing is artificial, planned stage presentation. Yet it makes them more truthful.

This advances the series’ argument about performance:

> Authenticity is not dressing “naturally.” It is choosing a form capable of carrying what one is otherwise compelled to hide.

Nina tells Momoka:

> 「一緒に裸になってもらいますから」
> “You’re going to get naked with us.”

They remain fully clothed. The nakedness consists of making shame readable.

---

### The shirts prevent the members from becoming generic rebels

Each word is specific.

Nina is not merely “angry.” She is the girl who ceased attending school and carries the stigma of educational failure.

Momoka is not merely “independent.” She withdrew from the band she promised never to leave.

Subaru is not merely “socially skilled.” She lies and performs in order to protect herself.

The trio’s identity emerges through their distinct failures, not an abstract punk image applied equally to everyone.

---

### The other band’s hostility gives Nina a target

A male performer tells Momoka that he hates the atmosphere surrounding girl bands and asks whether he may destroy it.

Before Momoka can answer, Nina says:

> 「心配しなくても
> 私たちがぶっ壊しますんで」
>
> “Don’t worry. We’ll destroy it ourselves.”

This is a meaningful change from Nina’s earlier defensiveness.

She does not ask the hostile performer to respect them. She does not insist that their femininity or sincerity be correctly understood. She claims the right to disrupt the category before he can impose his contempt upon it.

The trio will not preserve a comfortable “girls’ band” atmosphere for either admirers or detractors.

---

### The crowd’s calls for Momoka define the performance’s initial problem

Before the song, the audience calls Momoka’s name and declares love for her.

The crowd has arrived carrying the exact history Momoka wanted excluded from the promotion.

Nina stands at center stage, but public desire is directed toward the guitarist’s former identity.

The performance must therefore accomplish two things:

-   honor the real significance of Momoka’s past;

-   prevent the new group from becoming merely a Diamond Dust nostalgia vehicle.


Nina’s declaration about leaving claw marks gives the trio a new purpose at the precise moment the audience tries to define them through the old one.

---

### The visual register of the final performance is intimate and abrasive

Diamond Dust’s concert relies on enormous scale, coordinated cool colors, and polished display architecture.

The trio begins in deep blue darkness, then erupts into close, warm, overexposed golds and reds. The camera repeatedly enters the performers’ physical space:

-   fingers on strings;

-   drum impacts;

-   Nina’s strained face;

-   bodies crossing one another’s visual fields;

-   lights flaring into the lens;

-   the audience compressed against the stage.


The result is less a perfected image than an encounter.

They do not yet possess Diamond Dust’s reach. They compensate by reducing the distance through which the audience can remain untouched.

---

## 6\. Series-level lens: revised provisional theses

### 1\. The series rejects the simple “sellout” narrative

The remaining Diamond Dust members were not merely weak or commercially greedy. They believed staying together mattered more than refusing one compromise immediately.

Momoka’s choice was not simply heroic. It preserved her boundary while breaking the group’s central promise.

The conflict is:

-   artistic continuity versus personal integrity;

-   strategic compromise versus immediate refusal;

-   loyalty to the collective versus loyalty to one’s present convictions.


No option preserves everything.

---

### 2\. Nina’s fandom is both pathological and life-giving

Momoka is correct that Nina behaves like an **厄介** fan.

Nina:

-   claims ownership;

-   polices legitimacy;

-   barges into private history;

-   demands emotional testimony;

-   treats her personal salvation as an obligation imposed upon the artist.


Yet Nina’s devotion also does something Momoka desperately needs. It proves that her supposedly failed period created meaning beyond commercial success and beyond the breakup.

The series does not ask us to declare Nina’s fandom good or bad. It shows how the same intensity can:

-   save;

-   possess;

-   preserve;

-   distort;

-   and return an artist to herself.


---

### 3\. Nina and Momoka need one another’s refusal validated

Nina needs Momoka’s old music to confirm that leaving Kumamoto was not defeat.

Momoka needs Nina’s response to confirm that leaving Diamond Dust did not make her music meaningless.

This creates profound intimacy but also danger.

Each girl is becoming evidence in the other’s moral case.

That can sustain them. It can also make disagreement feel existential.

---

### 4\. Hina unifies two unresolved pasts

Hina now occupies:

-   Nina’s former friendship;

-   Momoka’s former band;

-   the vocalist position within Diamond Dust;

-   the song through which Nina encountered Momoka.


She is the first figure to connect Nina’s Kumamoto history directly with Momoka’s professional history.

The episode gives us only Nina’s shocked perspective. It does not yet establish Hina’s motives or moral responsibility.

That restraint should be preserved. The emotional violence of her appearance is established; the full ethical judgment is not.

---

### 5\. Subaru is becoming the group’s emotional arranger

Momoka arranges the music. Subaru often arranges the relationships.

She:

-   identifies what Nina is actually reacting to;

-   explains social and economic systems;

-   interrupts destructive escalation;

-   converts painful experience into stage concepts;

-   helps others use performance rather than merely survive it.


Her “lie” is not incidental. Her ability to frame, translate, and regulate may be what keeps Nina’s and Momoka’s intensities from destroying the group.

---

### 6\. Commercial exploitation is not located only at the major label

The major label asks Diamond Dust to change.

But the underground venue also:

-   imposes ticket quotas;

-   expects bands to sell to friends;

-   uses Momoka’s former affiliation against her instructions;

-   converts her pain into promotion.


The difference between major and independent music is not commerce versus purity. It is scale, leverage, and the degree of control artists retain within commercial necessity.

The trio will not escape the industry merely by remaining small.

---

### 7\. The band’s identity begins with reclaimed stigma

The trio’s first coherent visual statement consists of accusations:

-   dropout;

-   quitter;

-   liar.


This suggests that the group will not define itself through aspirational perfection. Its foundation is closer to:

> We know how respectable society reads us. We will use those words ourselves before you can use them to silence us.

That is not uncomplicated empowerment. The labels still hurt.

But performance changes who controls their visibility.

---

### 8\. Nina’s understanding of Momoka matures from icon to person

Before Episode 5, Momoka was still partly:

-   the voice of Nina’s theme song;

-   the artist who proved refusal was right;

-   a wounded but admirable rebel.


Now Nina knows that Momoka:

-   broke a promise;

-   hurt people who loved her;

-   remains guilty;

-   conceals her feelings;

-   can be self-righteous and self-erasing;

-   does not fully believe her own public neutrality.


Nina stays.

This may be the first point at which their bond becomes grounded in knowledge rather than projection alone.

---

### 9\. Music is being defined as a wound that travels

Nina describes Momoka’s song as something that gouged her chest and left a claw mark.

The metaphor reverses ordinary therapeutic language. Music does not heal by making pain disappear. It enters another person, creates a new wound, and changes how that person lives.

Nina now wants to continue the chain:

-   Momoka was wounded by Diamond Dust.

-   Momoka transformed the wound into music.

-   The music wounded Nina.

-   Nina carries it onto another stage.

-   Their new band seeks to mark an unknown audience.


Art becomes transmitted injury converted into form.

---

### 10\. “No correct answer” is becoming the series’ hardest demand

Nina loves the phrase because she initially hears it as liberation from society’s answer.

Episode 5 reveals the harder implication:

> There may be no answer that proves Nina completely right, Momoka completely innocent, or Diamond Dust completely false.

Living without a single correct answer does not mean treating all choices as equivalent. It means accepting that a choice may be necessary and still cause real harm.

Momoka has reached that knowledge through experience.

Nina is only beginning to encounter it.

---

# The characters after Episode 5

## Nina

Nina is now unquestionably invested in the band. She:

-   wants live performance;

-   sells tickets;

-   studies the music economy;

-   shapes the trio’s visual identity;

-   defines what she wants their audience to experience.


Her discovery of Hina intensifies her unresolved past, but the episode’s greater development is her relationship with Momoka.

Nina sees Momoka’s guilt and remains loyal.

At the same time, she remains possessive, invasive, and prone to treating her personal meaning as universal moral authority. Momoka’s accusation that she barges into others is deserved.

Nina’s artistic power and relational danger remain inseparable.

## Momoka

Momoka’s detachment toward Diamond Dust is exposed as a defensive posture.

She sincerely respects the others’ right to continue and acknowledges their success. She also left because she could not accept their chosen path and remains deeply wounded by the split.

Her defining guilt is not that she cared too much about music. It is that she broke the promise to remain together.

Nina’s love does not absolve her, but it allows her to imagine that the choice produced something besides loss.

The final performance shows her beginning to carry “withdrawal” as history rather than treating it as a verdict that ended her musical life.

## Subaru

Subaru remains the least emotionally destabilized member, but that does not make her secondary.

She is the person most capable of:

-   recognizing context;

-   translating hidden motives;

-   regulating public behavior;

-   accepting contradiction;

-   and turning emotional debris into a usable artistic decision.


Her shirt, **嘘**, acknowledges that her strength is built partly from performance and concealment.

She does not deny it. She gives it rhythm.

## Hina

Episode 5 establishes Hina as Nina’s former friend and Diamond Dust’s new vocalist.

It does not yet give enough evidence to determine:

-   why the friendship ended;

-   how she understands Nina;

-   how she joined Diamond Dust;

-   whether she sees herself as replacing Momoka;

-   or what personal meaning the songs hold for her.


The direction places her at the center of Nina’s shock, but Nina’s perspective should not yet be mistaken for a complete account of Hina.

## The current Diamond Dust

The group is commercially successful, professionally polished, and apparently committed to its chosen path.

Its members previously tried to persuade Momoka to remain rather than simply expelling her. Their decision to compromise may have been pragmatic, relational, ambitious, or all three.

They now function as more than antagonistic “sellouts.” They represent a path Momoka could not follow and a question the new band cannot escape:

> Can a group enter the industry, change, and still remain meaningfully itself?

---

# Overall assessment

Episode 5 is the first episode in which *Girls Band Cry* fully dismantles the comforting purity of rebellion.

Nina wants Momoka to declare:

-   I was right.

-   They were wrong.

-   The old music was true.

-   The new form is false.

-   Your faith in me was justified.


Momoka cannot honestly give her that answer.

She rejected a compromise she found intolerable, but in doing so she broke the promise at the heart of Diamond Dust. The others accepted a compromise that may have altered the band, but did so partly because they wanted to remain together.

The series does not resolve the conflict through a verdict. It resolves the episode through a choice:

> Nina learns the contradiction and still chooses Momoka.

That “even so” becomes the basis of the new band.

Their stage clothing then gives the episode its final argument. They do not appear as three people who have escaped accusation:

-   the girl who stopped attending school;

-   the musician who left;

-   the actress who lies.


They expose those words and play through them.

> **Episode 5 defines the emerging band as a place where shame is not denied, guilt is not purified, and contradiction is not solved before expression begins. The members take the labels that could end their stories, wear them publicly, and turn them into the first language of a collective identity.**

Diamond Dust’s new form asks whether a band may change and remain itself.

Nina, Momoka, and Subaru answer with a different possibility:

> A band may begin precisely because people who can no longer defend clean versions of themselves decide to expose the mess and continue together anyway.
