## Archival source: Assistant Turn 36 — Original Episode 10 deep-dive response


# *Girls Band Cry* Episode 10 — Deep Dive

Episode 10 turns Nina’s declaration of independence into a question of return.

She has quit preparatory school, rejected her family’s financial support, committed herself to professional music, and insisted that she has no path of retreat. Yet she remains unable to write the lyrics Momoka has entrusted to her. The obstacle is not lack of emotion. It is that the feeling she most needs to articulate remains bound to the father she refuses to face.

The episode’s central proposition is:

> **Reconciliation does not require Nina to admit that her family was right, nor her father to approve every choice she has made. It requires them to separate love from control, protection from ownership, and returning home from returning to the person Nina used to be.**

The resolution is deliberately incomplete. Nina’s father never gives a clean verbal apology for failing to support her when the school tried to erase the bullying. Nina does not reverse her decision, resume her studies, or accept parental authority over her career. They remain stubborn and only partially capable of speaking to one another.

What changes is more modest and more important:

-   her father publicly calls Nina the victim;

-   he listens to the song that helped her leave;

-   Nina explains that she likes the person she has become;

-   her family tells her that she is loved;

-   her father gives back the key to her apartment;

-   Nina invites him to hear the song she is writing;

-   and their next separation is expressed through **「行ってきます」 / 「行ってらっしゃい」**—the language of leaving with an expected return.


Episode 10 therefore does not send Nina “back” to her family. It allows her to visit home without surrendering the self she found after leaving it.

---

# 1\. Narrative lens

## The agency offer immediately tests what Momoka learned in Episode 8

The episode opens with Togenashi Togeari operating a merchandise booth and conducting cheki-style fan interactions.

Their new name is still not an obvious commercial success:

-   merchandise sells unevenly;

-   Rupa remains the most naturally popular member;

-   Momoka complains that Nina chose a strange name;

-   Nina tries to market “TogeToge” goods with uncertain results.


Then a woman named Miura presents a business card from the talent agency Golden Archer.

Miura is not a neutral industry representative. She is a fan of the old Diamond Dust and explicitly dislikes the current group:

> 「ダイヤモンドダストが好きです」
> “I like Diamond Dust.”

> 「でも　今のダイヤモンドダストが好きではないのです」
> “But I do not like the current Diamond Dust.”

> 「あれは　元のバンドの良さが完全に失われています」
> “It has completely lost what was good about the original band.”

Miura resembles Nina’s earlier possessive fan position. She believes that the authentic Diamond Dust existed with Momoka and that the present version has betrayed it. She wants Momoka’s intended music properly realized.

Episode 8 has prepared Momoka to answer this without either self-erasure or contempt for her former bandmates:

> 「それを間違っているとは思ってません」
> “I don’t believe their choice was wrong.”

> 「実際　今のダイダスはファンに受け入れられました」
> “In fact, the current Diamond Dust has been accepted by its fans.”

Momoka does not allow Miura to recruit them through a simplistic “true Diamond Dust versus false Diamond Dust” narrative. She acknowledges that:

-   the industry’s assessment of her former sound was not necessarily irrational;

-   the current group found a public;

-   their commercial transformation worked;

-   and their choice deserves recognition.


She then defines Togenashi Togeari positively:

> 「ここにいる４人は　それでも勝負したい」
> “The four people here still want to compete.”

> 「私のやりたかった歌で挑戦してみたいと」
> “They want to try challenging the world with the songs I wanted to make.”

> 「その言葉に乗っかってみようと思っています」
> “I’ve decided to go along with what they’re saying.”

This is substantial growth.

Momoka no longer speaks as though:

-   her uncompromised sound possesses automatic moral superiority;

-   Nina is an innocent singer whom she must protect from ambition;

-   or she alone determines the band’s future.


She credits the other four members with drawing her into the challenge. Her commitment is relational rather than proprietary.

The agency’s interest is real, but it carries a warning. Miura may want to preserve Momoka as much as Momoka once wanted to preserve Nina. A professional relationship based on nostalgia for old Diamond Dust could recreate the same imprisonment Episode 8 dismantled.

---

## The festival becomes a negotiation over artistic leverage

Miura explains that the upcoming festival will function as a final audition.

A strong performance would give the agency evidence that Togenashi Togeari already possesses:

-   audience demand;

-   a distinctive sound;

-   public attention;

-   and enough momentum to justify resisting externally imposed changes.


Miura puts the industry logic plainly:

> If the band attracts enough fans, the agency will have a stronger argument for preserving its current musical direction.

This is not freedom from commercial pressure. It is the attempt to acquire bargaining power within it.

Episode 5 and Episode 6 established that there is no uncontaminated space outside commerce:

-   live houses impose ticket quotas;

-   venues exploit Momoka’s former affiliation;

-   agencies look for numbers;

-   marketable images attract attention;

-   professional continuation requires money and promotion.


Episode 10 offers a more mature strategic possibility:

> **Artists may not be able to avoid negotiation, but visible audience commitment can alter who has leverage inside that negotiation.**

This is the first plausible path by which Momoka’s music might enter the professional world without simply repeating Diamond Dust’s experience.

---

## Momoka transfers authorship to Nina

Momoka then asks Nina to write lyrics for the song they were developing in Episode 9:

> 「この前の曲の詞
> 仁菜が考えてくれないか」
>
> “Would you write the lyrics for that song?”

Her explanation is deliberately recursive:

> 「見てみたいんだ
> 『私の歌』が書いた私の歌を」
>
> “I want to see the song about me written by ‘my song.’”

In Episode 2, Momoka called Nina **「私の歌」**—“my song”—because Nina embodied the defiance and emotional truth Momoka had forgotten.

Now Momoka asks that “song” to become an author.

The circuit is:

1.  Momoka turns her experience into music.

2.  The music reaches Nina.

3.  Nina organizes her life through the meaning she receives.

4.  Nina returns that meaning to Momoka through her voice.

5.  Momoka asks Nina to create new words.

6.  The listener becomes one of the writers of what comes next.


This is the clearest sign that Momoka has stopped asking Nina to remain a preserved version of young Momoka.

She no longer wants only to listen beside Nina.

She wants to discover what Nina herself will say.

---

## Nina’s difficulty with lyrics is a fear of externalized truth

Nina insists that she has something she wants to express, yet cannot write it.

She explains:

> 「言葉ってさ　外にあるんだよ」
> “Words exist outside you.”

> 「言葉にした瞬間
> 自分の中にあったものが外に出て
> 形になっちゃうんだよ」
>
> “The moment you put something into words, what was inside you comes outside and takes on a shape.”

The phrasing is unusually insightful.

Nina is not empty. She is frightened of form.

An unspoken feeling can remain:

-   contradictory;

-   private;

-   changeable;

-   immune from external interpretation.


Once written, it becomes:

-   an object;

-   something others can judge;

-   something that may fail to match what she felt;

-   something that can be repeated apart from her;

-   and something whose implications she must own.


This is precisely the problem underlying her family conflict.

Nina has repeatedly said:

-   her father did not take her side;

-   the family cared more about appearances;

-   the school erased the bullying;

-   she refuses to return.


But the deeper feeling has remained less fully articulated:

> She wanted her father to believe her and defend her.

Momoka later identifies that this—not the examination and not even the bullying itself—is what Nina continues to carry.

The lyric cannot be written while Nina is still pretending that this desire does not matter.

---

## The father’s arrival makes Nina literally hide

Nina’s father travels to Kawasaki and searches for her.

He goes to:

-   her workplace;

-   her apartment;

-   people connected to her;

-   and the places he believes she might be found.


At work, Nina sees him and ducks behind the counter. She wears a cap and mask, turning herself into someone almost unrecognizable while he asks after a girl named Iseri.

The imagery is direct:

> Nina has constructed an independent life, but the moment her father enters it she becomes a hiding child again.

She later runs to Subaru’s apartment and argues that speaking is pointless:

> 「通じないんだよ　話」
> “Conversation doesn’t get through to him.”

> 「価値観が違うっていうか」
> “Our values are completely different.”

Nina uses Subaru’s grandmother as an analogy. Some adults seem to inhabit systems of value so distant from the younger person’s that explanation cannot bridge the gap.

But Subaru hears evasion. Nina says she has no time for family conflict because:

-   she must write lyrics;

-   the festival is close;

-   everyone’s future is at stake.


The argument is revealingly circular.

Nina claims that her professional responsibility prevents her from addressing the family wound. Yet that wound is exactly why she cannot complete the professional work.

---

## Nina’s sister refuses the letter as sufficient communication

Nina’s sister calls and reprimands her for sending a letter that rejects further financial support.

She tells Nina:

> 「本当に言いたいことがあるんなら
> 逃げてないで直接言いなっせ」
>
> “If you really have something to say, stop running and say it directly.”

The sister is not necessarily demanding obedience or reconciliation. She is demanding that Nina apply her own ethic of honesty.

Nina has repeatedly condemned:

-   institutional euphemism;

-   concealed feelings;

-   false politeness;

-   people deciding privately and announcing conclusions afterward.


Yet she attempted to end the family’s material relationship through a letter and then hid when her father came for an answer.

The accusation is deserved.

Her letter was sincere, but sincerity delivered at a distance protected Nina from hearing any response.

---

## The band refuses to become a hiding place

Nina returns to her apartment and discovers that Momoka has changed the lock. Nina indignantly points out:

> 「住んでるのは私ですよ」
> “I’m the one who lives here.”

> 「新しい鍵をください」
> “Give me the new key.”

The moment initially looks comic, but the key is becoming a major symbol of autonomy and access.

Nina insists that the apartment is her home and that Momoka cannot determine whether she may enter it.

Then the entire band coordinates to send her to Kumamoto:

-   Rupa has her work shift canceled.

-   Tomo and Rupa give her the round-trip ticket.

-   Subaru tells her to talk.

-   Momoka refuses to let her use Momoka’s home as refuge until she faces her father.


Their methods are intrusive. Nina is again being maneuvered by people who believe they understand what she needs.

But the intent differs from the coercive systems she fled.

They do not demand that Nina:

-   reconcile;

-   forgive;

-   return to school;

-   accept her father’s authority;

-   or abandon the band.


They insist only that she stop using them to avoid an unfinished conversation.

Episode 8 allowed Nina to say:

> “Don’t use me to run away.”

Episode 10 turns the sentence back toward her.

The band refuses to let Nina use professional urgency and found-family intimacy as her own means of escape.

---

## Rupa gives Nina a theory of finite opportunity

Rupa explains that she once planned to call her family.

Work ran longer than expected. She thought:

> Tomorrow will be fine.
> I can always talk to them later.

That later opportunity never came.

Her conclusion is restrained:

> 「思っているより
> 時間やきっかけはないものですよ」
>
> “There is less time—and fewer opportunities—than you think.”

Rupa does not tell Nina that family love overrides harm. Her own history would make such reassurance dishonest.

She tells Nina something narrower:

> You cannot assume that an unresolved relationship will remain available indefinitely.

That is the ethic produced by Rupa’s bereavement.

Tomo punctures the solemnity with:

> 「自分語りはうざいよね」
> “Talking about yourself is annoying, right?”

The interruption is characteristically abrasive but also protective. It prevents Rupa’s loss from being turned into sentimental leverage. Rupa shares only enough to make the point.

---

## Momoka names Nina’s actual wound

Momoka says:

> 「お前が一番引きずってんのはさ
> 受験でも　いじめでもないだろう」
>
> “The thing you’re carrying most isn’t the exam or even the bullying.”

> 「父親が味方してくれなかったことじゃないのか」
>
> “Isn’t it that your father didn’t take your side?”

Nina denies it.

Momoka answers:

> 「ないんだったら　会えるだろう」
> “If that isn’t true, then you should be able to face him.”

This is similar to the way Nina exposed Momoka’s fear in Episode 8. Momoka now sees through the principle Nina uses as armor.

Nina’s anger toward the school is morally clear.

Her pain toward her father is more dangerous because it contains love and expectation.

The school’s failure proves that an institution was cowardly.

Her father’s failure means the person whose support she wanted most did not recognize her reality.

---

## The journey reverses Episode 1

Nina returns to Kumamoto by train.

Episode 1 began with her arrival in the Tokyo region:

-   disoriented;

-   lost;

-   uncertain how to navigate;

-   without a stable sense of home.


Episode 10 sends her in the opposite direction.

Yet she is not “going back” in the existential sense. She is traveling to the geographic home of a self she no longer wishes to resume.

This distinction becomes the episode’s organizing tension:

-   Can she return to the house without surrendering the life she built after leaving?

-   Can she accept that her family loves her without conceding that their decisions were justified?

-   Can the family remain home when Kawasaki has also become home?


The episode ultimately answers yes—but only after redefining home as a relationship to which one may return, not a place entitled to determine who one must be.

---

## The initial family argument repeats the failure of guardianship

Nina’s father finds her and brings her home.

He is furious that she:

-   left under the premise of preparing for university;

-   pursued a band without reporting it;

-   rejected financial support unilaterally;

-   and made decisions affecting the entire family.


He reminds her that her independence was materially supported:

-   the family processed her school withdrawal;

-   arrangements were made for Tokyo;

-   her mother’s acquaintance helped secure the apartment;

-   a parent remains the guarantor;

-   Nina is legally still a minor.


Then he uses highly institutional language:

> 「大人の庇護のもとで
> 管理下にあるということだ」
>
> “It means that you remain under adult protection and management.”

The pairing is revealing:

-   **庇護** — protection;

-   **管理** — management or control.


For the father, responsibility and authority are inseparable. If adults bear legal and financial risk, he believes the child must accept their decisions.

Nina hears the implied conclusion:

> 「だから言うこと聞けって言うんだ」
> “So you’re telling me to obey.”

And immediately reconnects it to the original betrayal:

> 「いじめなんかなかったことにしろって言うんだ」
> “You’re telling me to act as though the bullying never happened.”

Her father is discussing:

-   housing;

-   money;

-   guardianship;

-   university;

-   and family communication.


Nina collapses all of it into the one unresolved moral question:

> When I needed you to acknowledge what happened, you made continuation more important than truth.

Until that is addressed, every offer of protection sounds like an attempt to control the narrative again.

---

## The school admits the facts but resists the word “bullying”

The next day, Nina’s father takes her back to the school.

He has investigated and pressured the institution to confirm what happened. The other student has acknowledged the underlying facts. The school apologizes for failing to understand the situation.

But when the father asks about responsibility, the staff retreats:

> 「学校としては
> いじめという認識は…」
>
> “As a school, we do not necessarily recognize it as bullying…”

This is a precise demonstration of institutional self-protection.

The school is willing to admit:

-   specific conduct occurred;

-   information was missed;

-   Nina suffered;

-   procedural mistakes were made.


It resists the category that would give those facts moral and institutional meaning.

“Bullying” would establish:

-   asymmetry;

-   sustained victimization;

-   duty of protection;

-   and institutional failure.


Without the word, the school can treat the case as an unfortunate series of incidents.

Nina previously experienced this language as the erasure of her reality.

Now her father argues against it.

---

## 「お前は被害者なんだ」 is the recognition Nina needed

Nina begins to speak, perhaps to press the school herself or explain what happened.

Her father stops her:

> 「仁菜がそんなことを言う必要はない」
> “Nina, you should not have to say that.”

> 「お前は被害者なんだ」
> “You are the victim.”

This is the decisive paternal act.

He does not tell her to:

-   compromise;

-   accept mutual responsibility;

-   preserve appearances;

-   shake hands;

-   or consider future recommendations.


He names the asymmetry.

The father who once privileged institutional management now confronts the institution and says that his daughter should not have to argue for the legitimacy of her own victimhood.

Nina then tells everyone to stop.

This does not mean the recognition is unwanted. It may mean that once her father says it, she no longer needs the school to complete the sentence.

The institutional verdict has ceased to be the only possible proof.

Her father has finally taken her side.

---

## The father’s action cannot undo the lateness of his support

The scene should not be treated as complete absolution.

Nina needed this defense when:

-   she was still at school;

-   the bullying was ongoing;

-   her future appeared to depend upon the institution;

-   and she had not yet been forced to leave.


The father acts after:

-   Nina has dropped out;

-   moved away;

-   rejected his support;

-   and reconstructed herself through music.


He cannot return her old life.

The episode therefore frames his intervention as repair, not restoration.

It matters because it is late.

It hurts because it is late.

---

## Going home does not mean returning to the old self

Back at the family house, Nina’s sister asks whether she truly intends to return to Kawasaki and continue with the band.

Nina explains what Momoka’s song did for her.

Everyone had told her:

-   endure;

-   accepting the situation is intelligent;

-   resistance is pointless;

-   the future matters more than present injustice.


The song told her something else:

> 「間違ってないんだったら
> 我慢なんかするなって」
>
> “If you aren’t wrong, then don’t endure it.”

> 「先のことなんか考えるなって」
> “Don’t think only about what comes later.”

> 「思いっきり飛べって」
> “Leap with everything you have.”

Nina does not romanticize the result:

> 「それが奈落の底に落ちてるのか
> 大空へ向かって飛んだ瞬間なのかは分からないけど」
>
> “I don’t know whether I’m falling into the abyss or whether this was the moment I flew into the open sky.”

This is one of the series’ clearest statements about Nina’s decision.

Music did not guarantee that she chose correctly.

It made choosing possible.

Nina cannot know whether leaving school and pursuing music will become:

-   liberation;

-   disaster;

-   career;

-   poverty;

-   flight;

-   or fall.


What she knows is that the action belonged to her.

---

## 「帰る地面はない」 rejects safety as a neutral return point

Nina continues:

> 「私は　あの歌で飛べたの」
> “That song allowed me to fly.”

> 「だから帰る地面はないし
> もう戻りたくもない」
>
> “So there is no ground for me to return to, and I don’t want to go back anymore.”

The ground is:

-   school;

-   family expectations;

-   the bargain of endurance for future security;

-   the version of Nina who could survive by suppressing herself.


She can return to Kumamoto.

She cannot return to that ground.

This distinction allows the episode to reconcile family affection with irreversible personal change.

---

## 「自分を殺そうとしてた」 deliberately approaches literal life-and-death language

Nina says:

> 「あの時の私は
> 自分を殺そうとしてた」
>
> “At that time, I was trying to kill myself.”

The expression can refer to suppressing or annihilating one’s authentic self. In context, Nina had been trying to endure by eliminating:

-   anger;

-   resistance;

-   desire;

-   and the conviction that she was not wrong.


But the series has also repeatedly given her relationship to Momoka’s song genuine existential force:

-   Nina had no place where she belonged.

-   She said someone chose to live because of Momoka’s music.

-   Her mother later thanks her for remaining alive.


The line should therefore not be reduced to a harmless metaphor. The episode does not establish a specific suicide attempt, but it deliberately leaves self-erasure adjacent to suicidal implication.

Nina’s survival involved both:

-   preserving a psychologically recognizable self;

-   and continuing to live at all.


---

## Nina’s central statement evolves from righteousness into self-love

Nina concludes:

> 「でも　今の私が一番好き」
> “But I like who I am now best.”

> 「胸張って言える
> これが本当の私だって」
>
> “I can say with pride that this is the real me.”

This is a crucial evolution from:

> 「私は間違ってない」
> “I am not wrong.”

“Not wrong” depends upon a verdict. It remains organized around:

-   the bully;

-   the school;

-   the father;

-   Diamond Dust;

-   the people who said resistance would destroy her future.


“I like who I am now” is more internally grounded.

Nina is not claiming:

-   success;

-   safety;

-   complete maturity;

-   or certainty about the future.


She is saying that the self produced through her choices is one she can inhabit without self-contempt.

That is more durable than proof.

---

## The family meal transforms an earlier symbol of control

Episode 2 described the family dinner through legalistic household rules:

-   everyone must attend;

-   no phones;

-   Sunday family meetings;

-   the household must function properly.


In Episode 10, Nina eats with her family without the compulsory apparatus being invoked.

Her sister jokes about the old **家訓**, family precepts, and says the household has felt strange since Nina left.

The dinner is no longer evidence that the family is exemplary.

It becomes food offered to a daughter who is leaving again.

This is a small but meaningful transformation:

> care ceases to require that the household perform correctness.

---

## Nina learns that love and failure can coexist

Her sister tells her that their father has been profoundly unsettled:

-   he drank despite being poor at handling alcohol;

-   asked others what he should do;

-   sought advice despite his status as a well-known educator;

-   and tried to understand the situation rather than relying solely upon his professional certainty.


Her mother secretly arranged the return ticket through a friend.

Then the sister says:

> 「あんたはさ　愛されてるの」
> “You are loved.”

> 「あんなだけど
> お父さんたちは間違いなく
> あんたのことを愛してる」
>
> “They’re difficult, but there is no question that Mom and Dad love you.”

> 「それだけは忘れないで」
> “Please don’t forget that.”

This does not mean:

-   the father was right;

-   love excuses control;

-   Nina misread the past;

-   or a loving family cannot cause severe harm.


The episode’s more difficult proposition is:

> **Her family loved her and failed her.**

Nina’s earlier binary logic made those claims difficult to hold together. If her father loved her, perhaps she was required to accept his decisions. If he failed her, perhaps his love was false.

Episode 10 allows both to remain true.

---

## The mother’s line answers the episode’s deepest fear

Nina’s mother says:

> 「ありがとう　生きててくれて」
> “Thank you for staying alive.”

> 「私も愛してるよ　仁菜」
> “I love you too, Nina.”

The phrasing **私も**—“I love you too”—suggests an ongoing family emotional exchange, not a sudden discovery.

The mother does not debate Nina’s career. She does not defend the father. She responds to Nina’s survival.

Nina then remarks:

> 「辛いな」
> “It’s hot/painful.”

> 「こんな辛かったっけ
> お母さんのカレー」
>
> “Was Mom’s curry always this spicy?”

**辛い** carries both “spicy” and “painful.”

The joke allows Nina to cry without having to name the tears directly. Food, grief and affection become inseparable.

---

## The father listens to 「空の箱」

Later, Nina finds her father listening to Momoka’s rendition of 「空の箱」.

He asks:

> 「好きなのか　この曲」
> “Do you like this song?”

Nina assumes he dislikes it:

> 「嫌？」
> “You hate it?”

> 「嫌だよね」
> “Of course you do.”

He answers:

> 「いい曲だな」
> “It’s a good song.”

Then repeats:

> 「いい曲だ」
> “It is a good song.”

The repetition feels like effort. He is making the acknowledgment explicit enough that Nina cannot dismiss it.

He does not claim to understand:

-   rock;

-   the band;

-   Nina’s emotional reading;

-   or the entire life she has chosen.


He listens to the object that reached her when he could not.

That is an important form of humility.

The father cannot become the person who saved Nina at that moment. He can recognize the song that did.

---

## Nina’s apology is not a retraction

Nina says:

> 「言うことを聞かない
> わがままばっかりの娘で
> ごめんなさい」
>
> “I’m sorry for being a daughter who never listens and is always selfish.”

This can initially sound troublingly submissive. The daughter appears to apologize while the father never says a direct 「ごめん」 for his original failure.

But Nina does not say:

-   leaving was wrong;

-   the bullying should have been ignored;

-   she will return to school;

-   her father’s authority was justified;

-   or the band is a mistake.


She apologizes for the pain and difficulty of being in relationship with her.

This parallels Episode 9’s distinction between specific criticism and total judgment.

Nina can acknowledge:

> I have been difficult, unilateral and hurtful.

without accepting:

> Therefore my choices were false.

She then asks her father to reduce his smoking. The request restores relational reciprocity. She is not kneeling before an authority. She is worrying about someone she expects to remain in her life.

The asymmetry nevertheless remains important. His apology is primarily behavioral:

-   confronting the school;

-   listening to the song;

-   returning the key;

-   and changing how he sends her away.


The absence of a direct verbal apology leaves the repair partial.

---

## 「行ってきます」 changes the meaning of Nina’s departure

When Nina first left home, the defining language was:

> 「勝手にしろ」
> “Do whatever you want.”

> 「勝手にする」
> “I will.”

Their separation was framed as mutual defiance.

Now Nina says:

> 「行ってきます」
> “I’m leaving—and I’ll be back.”

Her father initially stops her, creating one last moment of fear that he will reassert control.

Instead, he says:

> 「行ってらっしゃい」
> “Go safely—and come back.”

This may be the episode’s quietest resolution.

The conventional household exchange contains an assumption:

> departure is not severance.

Nina does not need to remain physically in the family home for the relationship to continue. Her father does not need to approve every choice before allowing her departure to remain connected to home.

---

## The apartment key recognizes Nina’s autonomous home

Nina’s sister gives her the key to the Kawasaki apartment:

> 「お父さんから」
> “From Dad.”

Earlier, the father had invoked his position as guarantor as evidence that Nina remained under adult management.

Returning the key reverses that logic.

The apartment may have been secured through family support, but it is Nina’s home.

The key signals:

-   relinquishment of direct access;

-   recognition of privacy;

-   acknowledgment that Nina will live elsewhere;

-   and trust that family connection no longer requires surveillance.


The key also answers the changed lock earlier in the episode. Nina demanded control over who could enter her space. Her father ultimately acknowledges that demand.

---

## Nina invites her father into her chosen future

Nina runs back and says:

> 「私の作った歌
> 今度歌うから」
>
> “I’m going to sing a song I wrote.”

> 「もしよかったら
> 聞きに来て」
>
> “Come listen, if you’d like.”

This is not a request for permission.

It is an invitation.

The change from Momoka’s song to **私の作った歌**, “the song I made,” completes the episode’s authorship arc.

Nina does not demand that her father understand the band in advance. She invites him to encounter the self she has created through the work itself.

Her father answers:

> 「わかった」
> “All right.”

The family relationship will now be tested not by whether Nina resumes the planned future, but by whether they can show up for the future she has chosen.

---

## Tokyo has become the place to which Nina returns

Arriving in Tokyo, Nina says:

> 「帰ってきた」
> “I’m back.”

An older woman at the station recognizes her from before:

> 「あら　またあんた」
> “Oh—it’s you again.”

The echo of Episode 1 is unmistakable.

The city that once represented:

-   unreadability;

-   wrong stations;

-   loneliness;

-   and failed arrival


now possesses continuity and recognition.

Nina has become someone who can return to it.

---

## Togenashi Togeari waits for her

The group is waiting in the public square.

They explain that Momoka refused to believe Nina might remain in Kumamoto. Had Nina returned one day later, Momoka apparently intended to drive everyone there—possibly driving herself, to everyone’s horror.

The teasing exposes how afraid they were:

-   Momoka insisted Nina would return.

-   The others nevertheless prepared for the possibility that she would not.

-   Rupa would likely have had to rescue them from Momoka’s driving.

-   They watched the station rather than merely waiting for a message.


Nina cries and says:

> 「ありがとう　みんな」
> “Thank you, everyone.”

Then, back inside:

> 「ただいま」
> “I’m home.”

> 「おかえり」
> “Welcome home.”

The episode does not make the birth family and the band mutually exclusive.

Nina has two forms of home:

-   the family that finally allows her to leave while remaining loved;

-   the band that trusts her to return after facing them.


---

## The lyrics become possible after Nina speaks

Momoka asks:

> 「ちゃんとできたか」
> “Did you do it properly?”

Nina answers:

> 「はい　できました
> いい歌が」
>
> “Yes. I did. A good song.”

The statement refers to the lyrics, but also to the entire journey.

Nina said that words externalize the interior and give it a shape. She could not write because she had not yet found a form capable of holding:

-   anger at her father;

-   love for her family;

-   gratitude toward Momoka’s song;

-   uncertainty about whether she is flying or falling;

-   and the declaration that she likes who she is now.


By returning home and speaking, Nina discovers language that does not require those feelings to become mutually exclusive.

The completed song is the artistic form of that new complexity.

---

# 2\. Japanese-language lens

## Momoka’s 「乗っかってみよう」 reduces her authority deliberately

Momoka says she will **乗っかる** on the other four members’ desire to compete.

The phrase is casual: to get on board, ride along or take someone up on an idea.

She does not present herself as:

-   visionary leader;

-   sole author;

-   or adult deciding the younger members’ future.


The understated wording rebalances the group after Episodes 8 and 9.

---

## 「私の歌が書いた私の歌」

The phrase is intentionally paradoxical.

Nina is simultaneously:

-   a person;

-   a vocalist;

-   the result of Momoka’s earlier music;

-   and the “song” through which Momoka recovered herself.


Momoka now wants this living song to write another song.

Authorship becomes circular rather than hierarchical.

---

## 「外にある」

Nina’s claim that language exists **outside** emphasizes that words are public material.

Feeling may originate inside, but language belongs to:

-   culture;

-   other speakers;

-   conventional meanings;

-   and listeners who may interpret it differently.


This explains why Nina can be verbally direct while remaining afraid of lyric writing. Argument allows spontaneous reaction. Lyrics require choosing words that must continue representing her after the immediate feeling has passed.

---

## 「庇護」 and 「管理」

Nina’s father pairs protection with control:

> 「大人の庇護のもとで
> 管理下にある」

The language sounds administrative or legal rather than familial.

His concern is real:

-   Nina is a minor;

-   he remains responsible;

-   her housing and finances involve adults.


But the register reveals why Nina cannot hear love in it. She is described as someone located within a system of management.

Her objection is not merely adolescent rejection of rules. She has already experienced institutional management as a way of denying her account of herself.

---

## 「認識」

The school avoids saying that no bullying occurred. Instead, it says that it did not have the **recognition** or classification of the situation as bullying.

This is bureaucratically careful.

The institution can admit facts while protecting itself from the implications of the category.

The father’s insistence and Nina’s rejection expose how official vocabulary controls moral reality.

---

## 「被害者」

The father calls Nina:

> 「被害者」
> “the victim.”

The word is stark and asymmetrical.

It rejects the earlier formulation of mutual conflict and restores the difference between:

-   the person who inflicted or organized harm;

-   and the person upon whom it was inflicted.


For Nina, this is more important than a generalized expression of parental love. It means her father finally accepts her version of the event.

---

## 「戻る」 versus 「帰る」

The episode repeatedly distinguishes two verbs that English often renders similarly.

**戻る** suggests returning to a previous place or condition.

**帰る** suggests coming or going home.

Nina does not want to **戻る** to the person or path she abandoned.

She can nevertheless **帰る** to Kumamoto and later **帰ってくる** to Tokyo.

The episode’s reconciliation depends upon separating those movements.

---

## 「自分を殺す」

The phrase can mean suppressing the self, but the verb remains **殺す**, to kill.

The intensity should be retained.

A translation such as “I was denying myself” captures one layer but weakens the episode’s existential stakes. “I was trying to kill myself” risks implying a documented attempt the episode does not show.

A fuller interpretive rendering would be:

> “I was trying to destroy myself—the person I really was.”

The Japanese productively holds both senses together.

---

## 「辛い」

Nina’s reaction to the curry uses the polysemy of **辛い**:

-   spicy;

-   painful;

-   difficult to bear.


The family meal physically burns while the emotional reconciliation hurts.

The joke permits Nina to speak about one without naming the other.

---

## 「行ってきます」 and 「行ってらっしゃい」

These are routine household expressions, but here they carry structural significance.

**行ってきます** literally combines going with returning.

**行ってらっしゃい** sends the person away while awaiting that return.

After a story organized around cutting off retreat and severing home, these ordinary phrases reestablish continuity without confinement.

---

## 「ただいま」 and 「おかえり」

Nina uses the paired homecoming expressions with the band.

The repetition across family and band contexts suggests that home is no longer singular.

Belonging is not a competition in which chosen family must replace biological family completely.

---

# 3\. Performance lens

## The father initially appears as an impersonal authority

When he searches Kawasaki, reflections often obscure his eyes behind his glasses. His posture is rigid and his speech formal.

Nina sees the office of “father” before she can see the worried person inhabiting it.

At home, his body remains controlled, but the performance gradually changes:

-   he searches rather than summons;

-   sits through music rather than dismissing it;

-   confronts the school;

-   returns the key;

-   and uses the language of expected return.


He never becomes emotionally fluent. His care becomes visible through action.

---

## Nina physically regresses when he appears

At work Nina crouches, hides and holds her breath.

This is the same Nina who:

-   confronted Diamond Dust;

-   stood in front of Momoka’s vehicle;

-   announced her career choice onstage;

-   and screamed before strangers.


Her father’s presence reaches a younger and less empowered layer of her body.

The contrast shows that professional courage does not automatically resolve family fear.

---

## The band’s intervention distributes care among five people

No single member delivers the whole lesson.

-   Subaru encourages conversation.

-   Tomo listens and participates despite her usual impatience.

-   Rupa offers the ticket and her personal history.

-   Momoka names the paternal wound.

-   Everyone clears Nina’s schedule and expects her return.


Their methods are intrusive, but the group is beginning to function collectively rather than as Momoka managing Nina.

---

## The school scene changes Nina’s position in the room

In the earlier flashback, Nina was the student expected to participate in a scripted reconciliation.

In Episode 10, her father stands between her and the institution.

When Nina begins to speak, he tells her she should not have to defend herself.

The change is bodily as well as verbal. Nina is no longer being positioned as one half of a dispute.

Someone finally takes the role of advocate.

---

## The flight sequence makes liberation and destruction visually indistinguishable

As Nina describes what the song did, the direction leaves realistic space.

She appears:

-   falling;

-   flying;

-   suspended against bright sky;

-   surrounded by fragments;

-   reaching into open blue.


The visual does not clarify whether she rises or descends.

That is faithful to her statement:

> she cannot know whether she is headed toward the sky or the abyss.

The act of choosing is visually real.

The safety of the choice is not guaranteed.

---

## The family’s affection remains indirect until the meal

Much of the family’s care travels through:

-   apartment arrangements;

-   train tickets;

-   school procedures;

-   advice sought from others;

-   food;

-   keys;

-   and third-party mediation through the sister.


The mother’s direct declaration therefore has unusual force.

This family has tended to express love through administration and logistics. Nina needed the feeling named.

---

## The father’s listening is a performance of humility

He sits alone with Nina’s phone and listens rather than interpreting immediately.

He allows another person’s art to occupy the room.

For someone whose professional identity is built around explanation, educational authority and correct guidance, listening without first correcting is a significant change.

---

## Nina’s return produces collective emotional release

The waiting band initially protects itself through teasing:

-   Momoka was certain.

-   They almost drove to Kumamoto.

-   Momoka might have attempted to drive.

-   Rupa would have had to save them.


Nina’s tears puncture the comedy.

The others had feared that the family might reclaim her or that Nina might decide not to return. Their jokes are the social form in which they admit relief.

---

## The episode’s quietest sound carries its largest emotional change

The father-and-song sequence is the quietest major passage in the bundled audio, at approximately **−25.2 LUFS**, but has the episode’s widest measured loudness range, roughly **19.3 LU**.

That combination fits the scene:

-   long quiet intervals;

-   the song entering the house;

-   sparse speech;

-   emotionally decisive short lines.


The episode does not require a live-performance climax. Its largest transition occurs through a father listening in a nearly silent room.

---

# 4\. Music lens

## The opening performance presents the band as professionally visible

The episode’s opening song is energetic, polished and built around the completed five-person sound.

Its lyrics include:

> 「この手は　自分自身のものさ」
> “This hand belongs to you.”

and:

> 「変わらないはずはないよ
> 手を伸ばして」
>
> “Things cannot remain unchanged—reach out your hand.”

Those lines become thematically important once Nina’s father describes her as being under adult management.

The performance attracts Miura because Togenashi Togeari has become legible not merely as emotional chaos but as a viable act.

The industry is responding to something the members have already built.

---

## Nina’s first lyric assignment completes her movement from receiver to creator

She began the series as someone preserved by a song.

Now her professional future depends partly upon whether she can write one.

This is not only a talent-development milestone. It is the series’ theory of artistic transmission:

> Art saves a listener not by keeping her forever in the position of listener, but by enabling her to answer.

---

## 「空の箱」 mediates where direct family language failed

The song originally traveled from Momoka to an unknown Nina.

Now it travels from Nina to her father.

He hears:

-   the voice that reached his daughter;

-   the ethic that contradicted his advice;

-   and the emotional object around which Nina reconstructed herself.


The father does not need to endorse every interpretation to acknowledge that the song is good.

Music creates a narrow point of agreement without erasing disagreement.

---

## The song’s “no correct answer” acquires a family meaning

While the father listens, the lyrics state:

> 「正解は無いんだ
> 負けなんて無いんだ」
>
> “There is no single correct answer. There is no defeat.”

This is not a declaration that every parental choice was equally valid.

It suggests that Nina’s life cannot be judged only by whether:

-   she reaches university;

-   the band succeeds;

-   she becomes financially secure;

-   or the family’s original plan proves safer.


Her father’s greatest failure was trying to convert an unmanageable moral situation into the administratively correct answer.

Listening to the song means briefly entering a worldview in which Nina may choose without being guaranteed a conventional proof of success.

---

## Nina’s new song is born from an unresolved but livable relationship

The episode does not present the completed lyrics in full.

What matters is that Nina can now say:

> 「いい歌が」
> “A good song.”

She has not solved:

-   the father’s control;

-   the family’s indirectness;

-   her own recklessness;

-   or the uncertainty of her career.


She has found a form capable of carrying them.

Artistic completion does not require emotional completion.

---

# 5\. Formal and directorial lens

## Access and keys govern the episode

The episode is filled with controlled entry:

-   Nina hides at work.

-   Her father searches her apartment.

-   Nina changes the lock.

-   Momoka refuses her access to a refuge.

-   Nina returns to the family house.

-   The father enters the school’s institutional space.

-   The father returns the apartment key.


The key becomes the episode’s material definition of autonomy.

Love no longer means possessing access to Nina’s private home.

It means trusting that she can leave and return voluntarily.

---

## The structure moves from public future to private past and back again

The episode begins with:

-   merchandise;

-   fans;

-   professional performance;

-   an agency offer;

-   festival strategy;

-   lyric authorship.


It then descends into Nina’s unresolved family history.

After the family journey, she returns to:

-   Tokyo;

-   the band;

-   the song;

-   the professional future.


The structure argues that career ambition cannot simply bypass the self from which the art emerges.

Nina has to revisit the past not in order to resume it, but in order to keep writing beyond it.

---

## Bright Kumamoto refuses the visual grammar of a dark underworld

Nina’s painful return occurs under:

-   saturated blue sky;

-   summer sunlight;

-   green residential spaces;

-   familiar roads;

-   ordinary family interiors.


The place that hurt her is not visually monstrous.

It is home.

That makes the conflict harder. Nina is not escaping an obviously evil environment. She is separating from people and spaces containing real warmth as well as real failure.

---

## The school is visually preserved as an institution

The office contains:

-   trophies;

-   formal furniture;

-   documents;

-   carefully ordered space;

-   staff performing institutional politeness.


The school appears respectable.

That is precisely the problem. Its legitimacy rests partly upon its ability to convert individual suffering into manageable paperwork.

The father’s confrontation disrupts the space without physically destroying its order.

---

## The meal revises the compulsory family-table motif

Episode 2 associated family dinner with bureaucratic household rules.

Episode 10 returns Nina to the table, but the direction emphasizes:

-   food;

-   faces;

-   tears;

-   remembered habits;

-   and imperfect warmth.


The family does not become healthy because everyone follows the rules.

It becomes momentarily real because Nina can speak and still be fed afterward.

---

## The route back to Tokyo repeats arrival imagery with changed meaning

Stations, announcements, train platforms and crowds once emphasized Nina’s displacement.

Here they mark competence and return.

The older woman’s recognition provides a tiny proof that Nina has acquired a history in the city. She is no longer the anonymous rural arrival who belongs nowhere.

---

## Multiple homecomings prevent found family from becoming an exclusive replacement

The episode uses the language of home with both families.

This is important because found-family narratives can become as absolutist as the biological structures they oppose:

> the old family failed, therefore the new one must replace it entirely.

Togenashi Togeari does the opposite. The band sends Nina home and trusts that she will come back.

Their love does not require exclusive possession.

---

# 6\. Series-level lens: revised provisional theses

## 1\. Nina’s governing statement is shifting from “I was not wrong” to “I like who I am”

This is one of her most important developments.

Righteousness remains important, especially because institutions falsely distributed blame.

But self-worth can no longer depend entirely upon proving others wrong.

Nina can acknowledge uncertainty while affirming the person produced through her choice.

---

## 2\. Love does not retroactively make control acceptable

The episode clearly establishes that Nina’s parents love her.

It does not establish that their earlier decisions were justified.

The father’s love contributed to:

-   overmanagement;

-   concern for future outcomes;

-   inability to accept her school refusal;

-   and the treatment of protection as authority.


The series preserves the distinction:

> love explains some harmful behavior; it does not erase the harm.

---

## 3\. Reconciliation is possible without consensus

Nina’s father does not become a rock enthusiast or explicitly endorse leaving education.

Nina does not agree to return to university preparation.

They reach a relationship through smaller acts:

-   advocacy;

-   listening;

-   apology for relational pain;

-   returned access;

-   invitation;

-   and farewell language.


This is more credible than ideological conversion.

---

## 4\. The father’s greatest growth is from institutional authority toward listening

He initially speaks through:

-   guardianship;

-   responsibility;

-   management;

-   educational planning.


His meaningful actions occur when he:

-   asks other people for advice;

-   challenges the school;

-   listens to the song;

-   and accepts an invitation rather than demanding compliance.


He does not abandon adulthood. He stops assuming adulthood grants interpretive supremacy.

---

## 5\. The family’s exemplary image had concealed emotional incompetence

The Iseri household’s rules created the appearance of coherence.

Nina’s departure exposed that the adults did not know how to respond when:

-   the daughter rejected their plan;

-   love could not be expressed through provision;

-   and professional educational expertise failed inside the family.


The father’s search for advice is an act of humility precisely because he is publicly known as an educator.

---

## 6\. Togenashi Togeari is a found family that refuses to become an escape fantasy

The band gives Nina:

-   shelter;

-   recognition;

-   music;

-   work;

-   and a place to return.


It does not validate every avoidance strategy.

The members send her to Kumamoto because a chosen family capable only of confirming that the birth family is evil would reproduce Nina’s binary thinking.

---

## 7\. Rupa’s relationship to time is shaped by irreversible loss

Rupa knows that “tomorrow” is not a guaranteed resource.

Her intervention does not deny Nina’s pain. It denies Nina the fantasy of indefinite postponement.

Rupa’s calm increasingly appears not as natural detachment but as a discipline formed after the permanent disappearance of her own family.

---

## 8\. Momoka now trusts Nina enough to ask for authorship

This is the artistic consequence of Episode 8.

Momoka no longer defines Nina as the pure vocalist who should remain unchanged.

She asks Nina to take responsibility for words that may change:

-   the song;

-   the band;

-   Momoka;

-   and whoever later hears them.


---

## 9\. Miura may represent another form of nostalgic possession

Her admiration creates a professional opportunity.

It may also create pressure to reconstruct old Diamond Dust through Togenashi Togeari.

Momoka’s response establishes an essential boundary: the new band will not justify itself by declaring the current Diamond Dust false.

Whether the agency respects that boundary remains unresolved.

---

## 10\. Nina can return home without restoring a fallback route

Earlier, return was synonymous with defeat.

Episode 10 distinguishes:

-   emotional return;

-   geographic return;

-   material dependence;

-   and existential retreat.


Nina can remain a daughter without making the family plan her route of escape.

This is a more mature autonomy than total severance.

---

## 11\. The song changes the family without winning an argument

「空の箱」 does not persuade the father that every Nina decision is correct.

It allows him to recognize:

> this mattered enough to keep my daughter alive and help her become someone she can love.

That may be the strongest case art can make.

---

## 12\. Nina’s new role is lyricist of the life she previously could only shout

Her early emotional vocabulary consisted largely of:

-   accusation;

-   refusal;

-   screaming;

-   and physical action.


Lyrics require:

-   selection;

-   structure;

-   recurrence;

-   responsibility for form.


Her development into a writer may be the next stage of turning anger from discharge into art.

---

# The characters after Episode 10

## Nina

Nina confronts the wound beneath her moral argument: her father’s failure to take her side.

She does not forgive everything or retract her choices. She accepts that:

-   her family loves her;

-   their love did not prevent them from harming her;

-   she can return without becoming her old self;

-   and she no longer needs to sever every relationship in order to preserve autonomy.


Her most important statement is no longer that she was correct. It is that she likes who she has become.

She also crosses an artistic threshold. She returns not merely as vocalist or fan, but as the writer of a new song.

## Momoka

Momoka demonstrates that Episode 8 changed her.

She:

-   rejects Miura’s simplistic condemnation of current Diamond Dust;

-   describes herself as following the other four members’ ambition;

-   entrusts lyrics to Nina;

-   identifies the wound Nina is avoiding;

-   and remains certain Nina will return.


Her confidence in Nina is no longer based upon freezing her in the past. It includes trusting her to leave, confront change and come back with something new.

## Rupa

Rupa’s family loss directly informs her care for Nina.

She does not weaponize it melodramatically. She offers a principle:

> meaningful conversations do not remain available forever.

Her intervention is one of the episode’s ethical foundations.

## Subaru

Subaru initially encourages Nina to speak and recognizes avoidance beneath Nina’s claim of professional urgency.

She continues functioning as a relational interpreter, though Episode 10 lets Rupa and Momoka carry more of that work.

## Tomo

Tomo participates in the plan to send Nina home and softens Rupa’s disclosure through abrasive humor.

Her line about self-narration also echoes Episode 7, when she stopped Nina from turning the whole band into Nina’s public autobiography. Here she prevents Rupa’s grief from becoming sentimental spectacle.

## Nina’s father

He remains rigid, controlling and poor at direct emotional language.

He also changes meaningfully:

-   he searches for Nina;

-   seeks advice;

-   confronts the school;

-   names her as the victim;

-   listens to the song;

-   returns the apartment key;

-   and tells her to come home safely.


His repair is real but incomplete. He never clearly apologizes for not standing beside her when it mattered most.

## Nina’s mother

The mother’s presence is quieter and largely expressed through logistics and food:

-   arranging the ticket;

-   maintaining the household;

-   preparing the curry.


Her direct lines—thanking Nina for being alive and declaring love—give emotional language to care that the family previously communicated mainly through management.

## Nina’s sister

The sister remains the family’s most effective translator.

She can say simultaneously:

-   their father was wrong;

-   his practical concerns were real;

-   Nina is avoiding direct speech;

-   and the parents unquestionably love her.


She does not demand that Nina choose between condemnation and affection.

---

# Overall assessment

Episode 10 is not an episode in which Nina admits that her parents were right.

It is an episode in which she learns that accepting their love does not require doing so.

Her father’s failure was not that he lacked concern. It was that concern became management, and management became more important than recognizing his daughter’s reality. His repair begins when he stops asking Nina to fit the institution’s story and tells the institution:

> **She is the victim. She should not have to argue this herself.**

Nina’s reciprocal growth is not obedience. It is allowing her family’s love to exist beside their failure and allowing herself to return without interpreting return as surrender.

The episode’s deepest transformation is contained in its ordinary household vocabulary.

Nina once left through:

> Do whatever you want.
> I will.

She now leaves through:

> I’m going.
> Go safely. Come back.

And when she reaches Togenashi Togeari, she says it again in another form:

> I’m home.
> Welcome home.

The trip does not reveal which home is the “real” one. It makes the demand unnecessary.

> **Episode 10 defines mature autonomy not as having nowhere to return to, but as being able to return without giving up ownership of the person who leaves again.**

Nina goes to Kumamoto unable to write because her feelings toward her family have no form beyond rejection. She returns able to say that she was hurt, that she was loved, that she may be falling, that she may be flying, and that she likes who she is now.

That complexity becomes the song.
