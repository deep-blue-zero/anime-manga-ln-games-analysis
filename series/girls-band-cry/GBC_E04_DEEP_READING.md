## Archival source: Assistant Turn 18 — Original Episode 4 deep-dive response


# *Girls Band Cry* Episode 4 — Deep Dive

The Episode 4 bundle contains 748 retained frames, 38 contact sheets, 473 subtitle cues, and the complete audio track. The episode has no full-scale musical climax comparable to Episode 3, but that absence is meaningful: it transfers the series’ questions about performance and authenticity from music into acting, family, and ordinary conversation.

Its central proposition is:

> **Telling the truth is not automatically the same thing as acting truthfully. A truth can become cruel when delivered without regard for timing, while a performance can provide the safest available form for saying something real.**

Nina begins the episode believing that honesty means immediate disclosure and that concealment is weakness. By the end, she helps Subaru postpone a confession because she finally understands that Subaru’s “false” role is sustained by genuine love, guilt, gratitude, and fear.

This is not Nina abandoning her commitment to truth. It is her first serious encounter with the ethics of **how, when, and to whom truth is spoken**.

---

## 1\. Narrative lens

### Nina’s post-performance intoxication

Episode 4 opens with Nina still exhilarated by the previous street performance. In a guitar shop, she imagines herself playing like Momoka and asks whether she could learn.

Momoka immediately discourages her:

> 「やめとけ」
> “Don’t.”

> 「歌がおろそかになるぞ」
> “Your singing will suffer.”

Momoka’s answer is practical rather than possessive. Nina has only just discovered her voice, and her impulse is already to add another means of expression before mastering the first.

Nina’s accompanying internal monologue explains why she is so energized. Singing publicly allowed her to do something she had never done before:

> 「自分が思っていることを　思い切り叫ぶ」
> “Shout what I’m thinking with everything I have.”

> 「人の顔色を気にして
> いつも何かが喉につかえながらしゃべっていた」
>
> “I was always watching people’s expressions, always speaking with something caught in my throat.”

This retrospectively clarifies Nina’s aggression. She appears incapable of restraint when angry, but she experiences most ordinary social life as extreme restraint. Much of her hostility comes from accumulated speech that has remained lodged in her throat until it erupts.

The concert did not merely make her happy. It gave her a bodily experience of existing without monitoring everyone else’s reaction first.

---

### The band becomes labor rather than revelation

The recording session that follows is deliberately unglamorous.

Momoka stops takes, listens critically, asks Nina for more expressive variation entering the chorus, and dismisses one musical idea as too obvious:

> 「なんか当たり前すぎてつまらない」
> “It feels too obvious—it’s boring.”

Subaru is told to record the drums again despite having an early morning.

This is an important correction to the ecstatic ending of Episode 3. Emotional truth is not enough by itself. The band requires:

-   repetition;

-   criticism;

-   technical focus;

-   tolerance for inconvenience;

-   the ability to hear that sincere expression may still be artistically commonplace.


Momoka’s standards are demanding, but they establish that she is not merely exploiting Nina’s raw emotional force. She expects craft.

The scene also shows that the trio is beginning to function as a working unit. Nina may still think of the band primarily as an emotional escape, but Momoka is already treating it as something that must survive revision.

---

### Nina’s interest in Diamond Dust is really an interest in abandonment

Nina notices that Subaru has been searching for Diamond Dust and asks Momoka what she thinks of the current group.

Momoka says she does not resent them:

> 「今のダイダスは　売れてくれればいいって思ってるよ」
> “I hope the current Diamond Dust succeeds.”

Nina immediately asks:

> 「じゃあ　なんでやめたんですか」
> “Then why did you leave?”

Momoka refuses to explain and tells Nina to concentrate on singing. Nina continues pressing until Momoka eventually shows her old material and recounts the group’s history:

-   they met in their high-school light music club;

-   they were unusually capable from the beginning;

-   they shared passion and artistic goals;

-   Momoka believed the meeting was destiny;

-   their school performance drew an overflowing crowd;

-   they dropped out to eliminate their path of retreat;

-   they moved to Tokyo;

-   they released CDs and performed independently;

-   they were approaching a major-label debut;

-   and then Momoka left.


The episode provides the shape of the history but still withholds the cause of the rupture.

Momoka describes the early Diamond Dust with language of wonder:

> 「これは運命だと感じた」
> “I felt it was destiny.”

> 「こんな奇跡ないって　鳥肌が立ったよ」
> “I got goosebumps thinking there could be no miracle like it.”

Nina sees footage or photographs of Momoka smiling openly and says:

> 「こんな顔で笑うんですね」
> “So you smile with a face like this.”

The line is quietly devastating. Nina has seen Momoka amused, caring, teasing, irritated, and musically absorbed, but this earlier smile appears unfamiliar: unguarded joy inside a band she believed would define her life.

The episode strongly suggests that Nina’s questioning is not just curiosity. If Momoka could leave a group she called destiny at the threshold of success, then Nina has reason to fear that Momoka might also leave this new group.

That fear immediately transfers to Subaru.

---

### Subaru’s “quitting” activates Nina’s worst fear

When Subaru suddenly leaves and sends:

> 「バンドやめます　ごめん！」
> “I’m quitting the band. Sorry!”

Nina refuses to accept Momoka’s relatively calm reaction.

Momoka speculates comically that Subaru may be involved with a man and advises Nina not to interfere too aggressively. Nina insists that the trio had decided to continue together:

> 「この３人でやっていこうって決めたじゃないですか」
> “We decided that the three of us would do this together.”

> 「そんな簡単に諦めていいんですか」
> “Can we really give up that easily?”

Nina has known Subaru only briefly, but the band already carries enormous emotional weight. She experiences Subaru’s departure not as a logistical change but as a betrayal of a declaration.

The language of “quitting” matters especially because Nina has just heard that Momoka once quit Diamond Dust. The two withdrawals become emotionally linked:

-   Momoka once left a supposedly destined group.

-   Subaru now claims she is leaving this one.

-   Nina cannot yet tolerate the idea that people can care deeply and still withdraw.


For Nina, departure retroactively casts doubt on everything that preceded it.

---

### Subaru’s grandmother makes performance hereditary

Nina drags a reluctant Momoka to confront Subaru. They discover her accompanying her grandmother, the famous actress Awa Tendō.

Subaru’s demeanor transforms instantly.

Around Nina and Momoka, she can be teasing, rough, theatrical, and openly irritated. Around her grandmother, she becomes:

-   meticulously polite;

-   physically composed;

-   deferential;

-   attentive;

-   addressed through rules of posture and conduct.


Her grandmother corrects her with a single word:

> 「姿勢」
> “Posture.”

Subaru immediately responds:

> 「はい」
> “Yes.”

Tendō explains:

> 「芝居には普段の言動
> 立ち居振る舞いがしみ出てくるもの」
>
> “One’s everyday words, conduct, and bearing seep into one’s acting.”

This is not merely acting advice. It describes how Subaru has been raised. Performance is not limited to the stage; her entire daily body is being trained as preparation for a role already selected for her.

Tendō’s control is extensive:

-   she monitors Subaru’s posture;

-   she expects physical and mental discipline;

-   she assumes Subaru’s acting trajectory;

-   she arranges a television role;

-   she has even given Subaru a name taken from the role that made her famous.


Subaru has not simply been encouraged toward acting. Her identity was artistically pre-scripted before she was old enough to choose.

---

### The grandmother is not a simple antagonist

At first, Tendō resembles precisely the kind of adult authority Nina instinctively opposes: elegant, controlling, indirect, and certain of what is best for a younger person.

Subaru warns them:

> 「表面上はね」
> “On the surface.”

> 「言ったこと額面通りに受け取ると
> 痛い目を見るわよ」
>
> “If you take what she says at face value, you’ll regret it.”

But the episode later complicates Subaru’s own interpretation.

Tendō is controlling, yet her feelings are not insincere. She tells Nina and Momoka that appearing in the same production as Subaru has been a long-held dream.

She then reveals why Subaru’s interest in acting matters so much to her.

Tendō’s daughter resented her career:

-   she was rarely home;

-   she was self-important;

-   she could not manage ordinary domestic life.


Subaru, by contrast, appeared to love her work:

> 「あの子だけは笑ってくれたの」
> “That child alone smiled for me.”

> 「私の仕事　あの子だけが好きだって」
> “She alone said she loved my work.”

> 「やってみたいって」
> “She said she wanted to try it herself.”

Subaru’s supposed acting ambition became more than a career plan. It became Tendō’s proof that her life had not been rejected completely by her family.

This creates the real trap.

If Subaru says, “I never wanted to become an actress,” she is not merely declining a profession. She risks telling her grandmother:

-   the affirmation you treasured was misunderstood;

-   the continuation you imagined does not exist;

-   the one family member you believed loved your life’s work was performing that love.


Tendō’s affection is genuine. That makes its pressure harder to resist.

---

### The acting exercise turns fiction into confrontation

Tendō asks Nina, Subaru, and Momoka to participate in an improvisational exercise. The scenario is melodramatic: one woman confesses her love while an ex-partner attempts to reclaim her.

Nina is initially horrified. She has little romantic experience and dislikes artificial social display. Yet the scene becomes one of the episode’s clearest demonstrations of her latent acting ability.

She begins with the assigned romantic premise:

> 「やっぱり　自分の気持ちに嘘をつきたくない」
> “After all, I don’t want to lie about my feelings.”

That sentence is pure Nina. It may belong to the fictional role, but it also states her governing ethic.

She then redirects the scene toward Subaru:

> 「私は最初に付き合った時から本気だった」
> “I was serious from the moment we first started dating.”

> 「絶対別れないって思ってたのに」
> “I thought we would never break up.”

> 「どうしてあんな簡単に別れるなんて言ったの」
> “Why did you say we should break up so easily?”

> 「あの時私に本気だって言ったのは嘘なの」
> “Was it a lie when you said you were serious about me?”

The ostensible subject is a romantic breakup. The actual subject is Subaru’s message quitting the band.

Nina cannot comfortably say:

> I believed you had chosen us.
> Your departure frightened me.
> I already care enough for this to hurt.

The fictional romantic frame gives her permission.

The scene does not establish that Nina’s feelings are literally romantic. It does, however, deliberately equate the emotional intensity of band commitment with the language of romantic abandonment. For Nina, the distinction may be less important than the underlying question:

> **Did you mean it when you chose me?**

---

### Subaru uses performance to make Nina speak honestly

Episode 3 positioned Subaru’s acting ability as evidence of possible falseness. Episode 4 reverses that assumption.

Subaru recognizes that Nina will not express her hurt directly. She uses the improvisation to draw it out. When Nina tries to refuse, Subaru provokes her:

> 「やって　バンドやりたいんでしょ」
> “Do it. You want to be in the band, don’t you?”

The acting exercise becomes relational mediation. It allows Nina to speak from inside a role while addressing Subaru unmistakably.

This confirms a major series thesis:

> **A performed statement can be emotionally more honest than unmediated conversation.**

The role does not conceal Nina’s truth. It creates the conditions under which she can bear to say it.

---

### Subaru did not truly intend to leave

Afterward, Subaru admits that the quitting message was an extreme attempt to conceal the band from her grandmother:

> 「敵を欺くには　まず味方からというか」
> “To deceive your enemies, first deceive your allies—or something like that.”

The proverb is offered as a comic excuse, but it exposes Subaru’s habits. When threatened, she protects herself by controlling information—even from people who are supposed to be on her side.

She insists she does not want to quit. She was frightened because her grandmother’s unexpected arrival clearly had a purpose. Tendō does not act spontaneously, and Subaru correctly suspects that something has already been arranged.

The group soon discovers the television production.

Subaru has been cast in a small role and will be unavailable for rehearsal for approximately a week.

Nina reacts as though this temporary absence may become permanent.

---

### Nina mistakes nonintrusion for indifference

Momoka accepts Subaru’s temporary absence. Nina becomes angry:

> 「すばるちゃんいなくなっちゃうかもしれないんですよ」
> “Subaru might disappear.”

Momoka responds:

> 「バンドじゃよくあることだ」
> “Things like this happen all the time in bands.”

Nina accuses her:

> 「桃香さんが思いやりがないからです」
> “Because you have no consideration.”

Subaru reverses the accusation:

> 「私からしたら　思いやりがないのはニーナの方」
> “From my perspective, you’re the inconsiderate one, Niina.”

She asks Nina to trust her promise to return.

Momoka then explains:

> 「人には触れられたくない事情ってのがあるんだよ」
> “People have circumstances they don’t want others touching.”

> 「お前だって　どうしてこっち来たか聞かれたくないだろ」
> “You don’t want people asking why you came here either.”

This is one of Momoka’s most mature interventions so far.

Nina defines care as pursuit:

-   demand the explanation;

-   prevent the departure;

-   force the issue into the open;

-   make the other person declare her allegiance.


Momoka understands that care can also mean restraint:

-   acknowledge that someone has private circumstances;

-   accept a provisional answer;

-   allow the person to return voluntarily;

-   resist turning intimacy into entitlement.


Nina is correct that Subaru is in danger of being absorbed into her family’s plan. But she has not yet learned that accurately identifying danger does not grant her unlimited access to another person.

---

### Subaru’s home reveals the burden encoded in her name

Subaru later invites Nina to her home and shows her an old film. The title character—Tendō’s breakout role—was named Subaru.

Her grandmother chose the same name for her:

> 「このタイトルから取ったんだよ　私の名前」
> “My name was taken from this title.”

The name is a blessing only if Subaru becomes an actress. Otherwise it becomes evidence that her life was designed as an extension of another person’s career.

Subaru explains that she dislikes:

-   being treated specially because of her grandmother;

-   the reflected fame implied by 「七光り」;

-   acting itself;

-   the embarrassment of performing.


Yet she cannot ignore one image: her grandmother’s genuine smile when Subaru says she wants to pursue acting.

> 「その時だけ　本当に笑うんだよね」
> “That’s the only time she really smiles.”

> 「作った笑顔じゃなくて」
> “Not a manufactured smile.”

This is a crucial reversal.

Subaru, the skilled performer, is held captive by a moment she believes was unperformed.

She can reject artificial smiles. She finds it much harder to reject the desire that produces her grandmother’s real one.

---

### Subaru tried to solve the conflict through passive failure

Nina tells Subaru that if she does not want to become an actress, she should say so quickly.

Subaru agrees in principle. But her previous strategy was:

> 「役者の学校行けば　才能ないって分かって
> 諦めてくれると思ったんだけどな」
>
> “I thought that if I went to acting school, they would realize I had no talent and give up.”

Subaru hoped objective failure would make the decision for her.

This is the opposite of Nina’s method. Nina would rather create open rupture than permit others to misunderstand her. Subaru would rather remain misunderstood until circumstances remove the need for confrontation.

Neither strategy is fully free.

Nina’s direct refusal can destroy relationships before they have a chance to change.

Subaru’s passive compliance allows other people’s plans to become increasingly entrenched.

---

### Drumming has become more than rebellion

In Episode 3, Subaru called the band a small act of resistance against her assigned future.

Episode 4 advances that motivation:

> 「この前ライブで叩いてて思ったんだ」
> “While I was playing at the last concert, I realized something.”

> 「私　意外とこれ本気で好きかもって」
> “I might genuinely like this more seriously than I expected.”

This is decisive.

The band is no longer valuable only because it is not acting. Subaru has discovered a positive desire within it.

That distinction matters because rebellion alone cannot define a sustainable life. If she chooses music merely to oppose her grandmother, her future remains controlled by the same relationship in negative form.

Drumming begins to become something she wants independently.

She is not yet ready to call it her complete vocation. Later, when asked when she will tell her grandmother, she says:

> 「やりたいことがちゃんと見つかったら…」
> “When I properly find what I want to do…”

Her commitment is real but still developing.

---

### Nina learns that truth can be mistimed

At the filming location, Subaru finally prepares to tell Tendō that she does not want the acting future planned for her.

Before she can do so, Nina and Momoka overhear Tendō’s sincere confession about her daughter and Subaru.

Subaru begins:

> 「あの　私ね…」
> “Um, I…”

Nina abruptly interrupts with a false emergency, claiming Tendō has forgotten something and must hurry to catch her train. She and Momoka physically pull Subaru away.

This is Nina’s first deliberate act of protective dishonesty.

Earlier, she told Subaru:

-   lying is wrong;

-   if she does not want acting, she should say so;

-   delay will only worsen the situation.


Then Nina hears the emotional context and changes course.

Subaru accurately accuses her:

> 「言動不一致も甚だしい」
> “The contradiction between your words and actions is outrageous.”

But Nina now repeats the lesson Momoka taught her:

> 「人にはそれぞれ事情があるんだよ」
> “Everyone has their own circumstances.”

> 「簡単じゃないんだよ」
> “It isn’t simple.”

> 「そういうところに気を遣えるようにならなきゃ」
> “You have to learn to be considerate about things like that.”

Her language is comically borrowed maturity. She has learned the principle only minutes earlier and now lectures Subaru with it.

Nevertheless, the change is genuine.

Nina has recognized that truth is not morally pure simply because it is true. A confession can become an act of self-liberation that transfers all its pain onto someone else at the worst possible moment.

Tendō is about to leave. She has just described the shared appearance as a dream. Subaru has not yet developed a clear alternative life plan. The confession would be honest, but honesty alone would not make the scene humane.

---

### Subaru’s final “performance” is her most honest speech

After being dragged away, Subaru explodes:

> 「私のことは私が決める」
> “I decide what happens to me.”

> 「ほっとけよ」
> “Leave me alone.”

She attacks both girls:

-   Nina is a 「正論モンスター」, a monster of technically correct arguments who reverses herself at the decisive moment.

-   Momoka watches with a knowing expression.

-   Both interfered after Subaru had prepared herself to confess.


The outburst seems entirely genuine.

Then Subaru asks:

> 「どう　私の演技」
> “How was my acting?”

> 「すっきりした」
> “I feel better.”

This is not a reveal that the anger was false. The relief proves the opposite.

Subaru used acting to speak an authentic grievance with enough framing that it would not permanently rupture the relationship. The performance creates emotional safety:

-   she can say “leave me alone” without actually leaving;

-   she can accuse them without abandoning the band;

-   she can express anger while signaling that the relationship survives it;

-   she can regain agency after they overrode her decision.


Her acting is simultaneously controlled and sincere.

Nina sings truth.

Subaru acts truth.

They are less opposite than they initially appeared.

## 2\. Japanese-language lens

### 「喉につかえる」 makes voice bodily

Nina says she normally speaks with something caught in her throat:

> 「いつも何かが喉につかえながらしゃべっていた」

The image converts social inhibition into a physical obstruction. Singing does not simply give Nina better vocabulary; it clears a blocked passage between interior feeling and the world.

This helps explain why Nina experiences performance so intensely. It is almost respiratory freedom.

---

### 「余計なことは気にしないで　歌ってればいい」

When Nina presses Momoka about Diamond Dust, Momoka says:

> 「余計なことは気にしないで
> 歌ってればいいんだよ」
>
> “Don’t concern yourself with unnecessary things. Just sing.”

The sentence can be heard as reassurance, evasion, or control.

Momoka is protecting painful history, but she is also defining Nina’s proper role: vocalist, not investigator. The phrasing anticipates her instruction not to learn guitar yet.

Momoka sees Nina’s expressive function clearly, but sometimes manages her by narrowing what Nina is permitted to know or do.

---

### 「こんな顔で笑うんですね」

Nina does not say simply that Momoka “looked happy.”

> 「こんな顔で笑うんですね」

The phrasing expresses discovery: *So you are capable of smiling in this way.*

It suggests that Nina regards the photographed or recorded Momoka almost as another version of her—one who existed before resignation.

The line is intimate because Nina is studying not merely Momoka’s career history but her emotional range.

---

### Subaru’s proverb turns deception into comic strategy

> 「敵を欺くには　まず味方から」

The conventional idea is that deceiving an enemy may require deceiving one’s allies first. Subaru uses it to justify sending a distressing quitting message to her new bandmates.

The joke works because her “enemy” is her grandmother, whom she also loves.

Subaru’s world is not actually divided into enemies and allies. The proverb imposes a clean strategic structure on a deeply entangled family relationship.

---

### 「姿勢」 compresses an entire hierarchy into one word

Tendō does not need to explain herself. She says:

> 「姿勢」

Subaru’s body responds before any argument occurs.

The one-word correction contains:

-   professional authority;

-   family hierarchy;

-   years of training;

-   the demand that public form be maintained at all times.


Subaru’s automatic 「はい」 shows how deeply the instruction has been incorporated.

---

### The grandmother’s polished language and emotional sincerity

Tendō uses elevated, refined forms such as:

-   「〜かしら」

-   「〜ですわ」

-   「お二人ともご一緒によろしいかしら」


Her register creates distance, but the episode refuses the easy conclusion that formal language equals emotional falseness.

Her later confession remains composed, yet is clearly personal. This is another challenge to Nina’s instinctive equation between polish and deception.

---

### 「額面通り」 and the limits of Subaru’s interpretation

Subaru warns Nina not to take her grandmother’s words 「額面通り」—at face value.

She is partly right. Tendō’s polite statements can carry hidden force.

But Subaru also fails to read everything beneath the surface. She knows her grandmother wants an acting successor; she does not fully grasp how much that hope is tied to the pain of being rejected by her daughter.

The episode therefore applies Subaru’s warning to Subaru herself: surfaces can conceal coercion, but they can also conceal vulnerability.

---

### Nina weaponizes the vocabulary of romance

During the improvisation, the repeated language is:

-   「本気」 — seriousness;

-   「嘘」 — lie;

-   「別れる」 — break up;

-   「どうして」 — why?


The romantic frame intensifies the accusation while giving Nina deniability.

Her repeated 「どうして」 gradually strips away the artificial scenario. The language becomes less like scripted melodrama and more like the raw questioning of someone frightened by arbitrary abandonment.

---

### 「七光り」

Subaru calls attention to being treated specially through her grandmother’s 「七光り」—reflected prestige inherited from an influential relative.

The expression is important because Subaru’s privilege and oppression coexist.

She has access to:

-   elite training;

-   professional opportunities;

-   a famous family name;

-   a large and comfortable home.


But those same advantages make it difficult to distinguish her ability, desire, and identity from her grandmother’s influence.

The series does not treat privilege as evidence that Subaru’s conflict is unreal. It makes the conflict inseparable from the privilege.

---

### 「作った笑顔じゃなくて」

Subaru distinguishes her grandmother’s smile from a 「作った笑顔」—a constructed smile.

Because Subaru herself is highly skilled at constructing expressions, she places enormous moral value on the smile she believes is unmade.

The irony is that the episode repeatedly demonstrates that constructed expressions may still carry truth. Subaru’s binary between made and genuine smiles is more emotionally understandable than philosophically secure.

---

### 「正論モンスター」

Subaru’s term for Nina is unusually precise:

> 「正論モンスター」

**正論** is a correct or logically defensible argument. But in ordinary use, it can imply a truth deployed without sensitivity to context.

Nina is monstrous not because she is factually wrong. She is monstrous because she uses correctness as though it settles every ethical question:

-   If Subaru does not want acting, she must say so.

-   Concealment is dishonest.

-   Delaying the conversation makes things worse.


Then, when the emotional cost becomes visible, Nina reverses herself.

The phrase identifies both Nina’s strength and immaturity. She sees contradictions others avoid, but can mistake moral clarity for complete understanding.

---

### 「私のことは私が決める」

Subaru’s declaration:

> 「私のことは私が決める」

is the episode’s most direct statement of autonomy.

Notably, she delivers it as part of an “act.”

This dissolves the simple opposition between performed speech and self-determined speech. The performance is the form in which Subaru can temporarily possess the sentence without yet reorganizing her entire life around it.

---

## 3\. Performance lens

### Nina’s body remains in the afterglow of the concert

In the opening, Nina is more expansive than before:

-   she moves through the guitar shop with visible excitement;

-   she imagines herself occupying Momoka’s instrumental position;

-   her face is open and animated;

-   she responds immediately when Momoka calls her to practice.


The concert has altered her posture toward the world. She no longer looks only like someone navigating danger. She looks like someone searching for more ways to act upon her environment.

---

### Momoka’s old smile reveals a lost bodily state

The Diamond Dust footage matters because the difference is not solely verbal.

The younger Momoka’s expression is broad and unguarded. Her body appears to belong naturally inside the group rather than hovering at its edge.

Present-day Momoka narrates the memory with a degree of warmth, but also with the controlled distance already evident in her Episode 1 singing.

Nina’s observation recognizes that the past band did not merely give Momoka commercial ambition. It gave her a bodily ease that Nina has not yet seen in the present.

---

### Subaru changes voice and body in her grandmother’s presence

The transition is immediate:

-   her spine straightens;

-   her gestures narrow;

-   her face becomes composed;

-   her language becomes deferential;

-   irritation survives mainly in brief private reactions behind Tendō’s back.


This is not necessarily conscious deception in each moment. The role has become habitual.

Tendō’s claim that everyday bearing seeps into acting is therefore reversed: acting discipline has seeped into Subaru’s everyday body.

---

### The improvisation reveals Nina’s natural performance mechanism

Nina begins the exercise stiffly and with obvious embarrassment. She appears to treat the scenario as something externally imposed.

Then she finds the real grievance inside it.

Her posture becomes more direct, her gaze fixes on Subaru, and the repeated questions acquire force. The fictional role stops being an obstacle and becomes a channel.

Nina is most compelling when she cannot fully separate performance from personal necessity. That is also true of her singing.

Her weakness as a conventional actor may be her inability to fabricate a feeling she does not possess. Her strength is that once she locates a real feeling, the performance becomes difficult to dismiss.

---

### Subaru’s home self is deliberately loose

At home, Subaru lounges, jokes, and occupies space with an ease absent around her grandmother. The expensive, spacious apartment contrasts sharply with Nina’s sparse room, but it does not appear emotionally warm.

The enormous screen displaying Tendō’s old film dominates the space. Even in Subaru’s private home, her grandmother’s image occupies architectural scale.

Subaru can relax physically, but the inherited narrative remains present.

---

### Tendō’s production makeup makes transformation visible

At the television shoot, Tendō’s exaggerated makeup foregrounds acting as transformation. The person underneath is not erased; she uses technical artifice to become legible as a character.

This visually rebuts Nina’s earlier assumptions about performance. Makeup, role, costume, and scripted behavior are not inherently fraudulent. They can be skilled forms through which a person works.

The same applies to Subaru, even if she does not want that work to define her future.

---

### The quietest performance is Tendō’s private confession

Tendō’s most emotionally revealing scene occurs outside the official dramatic performance. She speaks calmly about:

-   her daughter’s resentment;

-   her own domestic failures;

-   Subaru’s apparent admiration;

-   her dream of appearing with her granddaughter.


The controlled delivery does not reduce the feeling. It makes the confession more painful because Tendō is not trying to manipulate the girls theatrically. She is explaining something she believes is joyful.

Her inability to see Subaru’s reluctance is real. So is her gratitude.

---

### Nina’s intervention is bodily before it is intellectual

When Subaru begins to confess, Nina does not produce a philosophical argument. She invents an excuse and physically removes Subaru from the encounter.

Her body reaches the ethical conclusion before her language catches up:

> This is not the moment.

Only afterward does Nina develop a vocabulary about circumstances and consideration.

This mirrors her musical development. Nina often acts, sings, or erupts before she can explain what she knows.

---

### Subaru’s final outburst is controlled catharsis

Subaru’s apparent anger is physically vigorous:

-   forceful pointing;

-   roughened posture;

-   accusatory rhythm;

-   direct verbal attack;

-   rejection of both girls’ interference.


Then she relaxes and identifies it as acting.

The emotional relief is unmistakable. She has discharged anger without turning it into abandonment.

Acting gives Subaru something similar to what music gives Nina:

-   a protected container;

-   permission for intensity;

-   a way to say socially dangerous things;

-   a route back into relationship after saying them.


---

## 4\. Music lens

### The episode begins by limiting Nina in order to develop her

Momoka’s refusal to let Nina immediately pursue guitar is not anti-creative. It establishes role discipline.

Nina’s tendency is to pursue every available expressive outlet at maximum intensity. Momoka understands that musical development also requires choosing what not to do yet.

This is another early lesson that restraint need not equal suppression.

---

### The recording scene distinguishes expression from composition

Nina can feel a line intensely, but Momoka still asks for more deliberate contour entering the chorus.

The lyric:

> 「生きていく理由ってやつを誰か教えて」
> “Someone tell me this thing called a reason to live.”

is emotionally compatible with Nina, but Momoka calls the treatment too obvious.

The implication is not that the feeling is false. It is that art must transform feeling rather than merely announce it.

That distinction is central to the entire series:

-   raw anger can become street violence;

-   raw confession can become cruelty;

-   raw sincerity can become artistically banal;

-   form makes emotion communicable without necessarily weakening it.


---

### Momoka’s Diamond Dust memory preserves collective transcendence

Momoka’s account repeats the language of miraculous ensemble experience:

-   shared goals;

-   exceptional ability;

-   an overflowing audience;

-   goosebumps;

-   destiny.


The story prevents the new band from claiming exclusive ownership of authentic musical community.

Diamond Dust was real to Momoka. Its later fracture does not make the earlier connection fraudulent.

This matters for Nina, who tends to believe that an ending invalidates the sincerity of everything that came before it.

---

### Drumming becomes Subaru’s first chosen art

Subaru’s earlier acting activity is inherited, supervised, and publicly validated. Drumming begins as hidden resistance but becomes something she may love.

The difference is not that one form is artificial and the other authentic. Both acting and music involve technique, performance, and audience.

The difference is authorship:

-   acting was selected for her;

-   drumming was selected by her.


That is why the band matters.

---

### Acting temporarily replaces the episode’s musical climax

Episode 4 does not culminate in a song because its central expressive breakthrough belongs to Subaru.

Her art form is acting.

The improvisational breakup scene and her final mock-angry monologue perform the structural role that a song would normally play:

-   ordinary dialogue fails;

-   a formal performance begins;

-   concealed emotion becomes audible;

-   the participants understand one another differently afterward.


The episode therefore expands the series’ theory of expression beyond music. What matters is not the medium itself but whether a form allows otherwise impossible truth to circulate.

---

### The final rehearsal is more important than a concert

The episode ends with Subaru at the drum kit, Nina and Momoka nearby, and the trio returning to rehearsal.

This is not a triumphant public spectacle. It is routine continuation.

Subaru’s commitment is expressed through showing up to work.

The band becomes slightly more real because it survives:

-   a false resignation;

-   family pressure;

-   an argument;

-   temporary absence;

-   competing interpretations of care.


The resolution is not that Subaru has solved her life. It is that the band remains one of the places from which she may eventually solve it.

---

## 5\. Formal and directorial lens

### Screens and recorded images structure the episode

Episode 4 repeatedly presents people through mediated images:

-   Diamond Dust material on a phone or screen;

-   Tendō’s old film projected in Subaru’s home;

-   online information revealing the television production;

-   the television shoot itself;

-   photographs connecting past and present.


The episode asks whether an image captures or distorts a person.

Nina sees Momoka’s old smile through recorded media and discovers something real.

Subaru watches her grandmother’s performance and understands both the constructed character and the authentic joy connected to it.

Mediation is not treated as inherently false. Sometimes a recording preserves a truth that present-day conversation cannot access.

---

### Smiles become a visual motif

Several kinds of smiles appear:

-   Momoka’s old, open smile with Diamond Dust;

-   Subaru’s polished social smile;

-   Tendō’s professional smile;

-   Tendō’s supposedly genuine smile when Subaru speaks of acting;

-   Subaru’s smile after using acting to vent;

-   the trio’s playful expressions in the rehearsal room.


The episode does not provide a simple method for separating real smiles from false ones.

Instead, it asks what each smile is doing:

-   concealing discomfort;

-   maintaining social order;

-   expressing genuine pleasure;

-   rewarding another person’s compliance;

-   creating temporary relief.


A smile can be authentic and still exert pressure.

---

### Posture is both theme and visual discipline

Tendō’s correction of Subaru’s posture gives the episode a bodily motif.

Subaru’s uprightness around her grandmother contrasts with:

-   Nina’s impulsive leaning and lunging;

-   Momoka’s relaxed slouch;

-   Subaru’s lounging posture at home;

-   her freer body behind the drum kit.


Posture becomes a visible index of who currently governs Subaru’s body.

Around Tendō, she performs readiness for the inherited role.

At home, she attempts private relaxation.

At the drums, her body serves a rhythm she has chosen.

---

### The episode contains several nested stages

There are at least four distinct performance spaces:

1.  **The rehearsal studio**, where music is recorded and criticized.

2.  **The acting classroom**, where fictional romance becomes genuine band conflict.

3.  **The television set**, where Tendō and Subaru professionally perform family continuity.

4.  **The post-shoot confrontation**, where Subaru stages anger to express real frustration safely.


Each stage produces a different relationship between artificiality and truth.

The episode’s structure itself argues that “acting” is not one thing.

---

### Subaru’s home is spacious but narratively occupied

The apartment is visually impressive, modern, and materially comfortable. Yet its largest visual object is the screen containing Tendō’s past.

This gives form to Subaru’s situation. She possesses considerable physical space but limited symbolic space.

Her future, name, and public interpretation are occupied by a predecessor.

---

### The park set blurs natural and manufactured space

The television production takes place amid greenery and daylight, but it is filled with:

-   cameras;

-   monitors;

-   makeup;

-   marks;

-   crew;

-   repeated takes;

-   props.


The “natural” setting is visibly constructed for performance.

At the same time, Tendō’s most personal admission occurs between takes in that manufactured environment.

Once again, the direction refuses to locate authenticity outside artifice. The honest conversation emerges inside the production apparatus.

---

### Nina’s reversal is staged as comedy to avoid moral grandstanding

Nina’s sudden invention of the forgotten-item emergency is frantic and ridiculous. Momoka joins her, and they drag Subaru away while everyone protests.

The comedy prevents the episode from presenting Nina’s new empathy as solemn moral perfection.

She has learned something, but awkwardly:

-   she overcorrects;

-   she interferes physically;

-   she becomes hypocritical;

-   she borrows language of consideration immediately after ignoring Subaru’s choice.


Growth remains messy and partially self-serving.

---

## 6\. Series-level lens: revised provisional theses

### 1\. Authenticity now requires timing

The first three episodes repeatedly privileged the refusal to falsify oneself.

Episode 4 introduces an essential qualification:

> **A person can be truthful in content and still irresponsible in delivery.**

Subaru must eventually confront her grandmother. The episode does not argue otherwise.

It argues that “eventually” and “right now” are not morally identical.

---

### 2\. Performance can be the route to authenticity

Subaru does not merely wear a false social mask.

Her acting skills allow her to:

-   manage dangerous family expectations;

-   provoke Nina into honest speech;

-   voice anger without destroying a relationship;

-   experiment with autonomy before fully living it.


The series is moving away from any crude contrast between Nina’s honesty and Subaru’s falseness.

Both need performance.

Nina simply calls hers singing.

---

### 3\. Nina’s first major growth is learning not to force disclosure

Nina remains intrusive and absolutist, but she demonstrates something new: she can revise a moral judgment after learning another person’s emotional context.

Earlier Nina would have treated the grandmother’s pain as irrelevant to Subaru’s right to choose.

Now she recognizes both at once:

-   Subaru has the right to refuse the inherited future.

-   Tendō’s love and vulnerability are real.

-   The conversation cannot be avoided forever.

-   This particular moment may still be wrong.


That capacity to hold multiple truths is a meaningful step beyond Nina’s earlier binary thinking.

---

### 4\. Momoka’s restraint is a form of leadership

Nina jokingly or insistently assigns Momoka the position of leader, which Momoka denies. Functionally, however, she already behaves like one.

She understands:

-   members have lives outside the band;

-   absences happen;

-   pressure can backfire;

-   private circumstances do not automatically belong to the group;

-   trust sometimes means accepting incomplete explanations.


Her calm is not indifference. It is partly experience purchased through Diamond Dust’s collapse.

---

### 5\. Tendō embodies loving coercion

The grandmother is not an abusive caricature or cynical stage parent. She loves Subaru, admires her, and has attached genuine hope to their shared profession.

That is precisely why her expectation is powerful.

The series recognizes a difficult truth:

> People can restrict another person’s life through sincere love rather than malice.

Subaru’s task is not defeating an enemy. It is separating herself from a person whose happiness she does not want to destroy.

---

### 6\. Subaru’s rebellion is becoming a positive identity

The band began as something she did against acting.

After the first live performance, it becomes something she may genuinely want.

This is a necessary transition. A life built only as resistance remains dependent on the authority being resisted.

Drumming may become the first place where Subaru is not:

-   Tendō’s granddaughter;

-   a future actress;

-   a bearer of inherited fame;

-   or the socially perfect girl.


She is simply the person creating the rhythm.

---

### 7\. Nina and Subaru are parallel performers

Nina’s strongest truth emerges in song.

Subaru’s strongest truth emerges in acting.

Both girls initially mistrust the other’s method:

-   Nina thinks performance means falseness.

-   Subaru may see Nina’s bluntness as socially destructive.


Episode 4 shows that each can teach the other.

Subaru teaches Nina that framing can enable honesty.

Nina teaches Subaru that a performed role cannot permanently substitute for choosing her own life.

---

### 8\. Momoka’s departure from Diamond Dust remains the unresolved central precedent

Momoka still has not explained why she left a band she considered miraculous just before major success.

That gap now matters even more because every member is confronting the possibility of departure.

Nina’s fear of Subaru leaving cannot be separated from the evidence that Momoka once did precisely that.

Until Momoka explains the difference between:

-   abandoning people;

-   protecting oneself;

-   preserving artistic integrity;

-   and accepting that a group has become unlivable,


Nina will likely continue interpreting every absence as potential betrayal.

---

# The characters after Episode 4

## Nina

Nina remains a “correct-argument monster,” but she is no longer completely governed by correctness.

She has begun learning:

-   that people may conceal things without rejecting her;

-   that care does not grant unlimited access;

-   that a true statement can still be badly timed;

-   that social performance may communicate genuine feeling;

-   that another person’s love can be both real and restrictive.


Her decision to stop Subaru’s confession is hypocritical, intrusive, and empathetic at the same time. That combination is precisely what makes it credible growth for Nina.

## Subaru

Subaru’s conflict is now fully established.

She does not want the career attached to:

-   her family name;

-   her grandmother’s legacy;

-   her own given name;

-   her polished social ability.


But she loves the person imposing it and understands the emotional need underneath the expectation.

The band has become more than evasion. Drumming is beginning to feel like a genuine desire.

Her final performed anger demonstrates that her acting ability is not merely the instrument of her imprisonment. It may also become part of how she liberates herself.

## Momoka

Momoka continues to demonstrate the best interpersonal judgment of the three:

-   she recognizes Subaru’s right to privacy;

-   she does not catastrophize a temporary absence;

-   she accepts that band members have other obligations;

-   she understands Nina’s questions but maintains boundaries;

-   she joins Nina’s intervention once she hears Tendō’s confession.


At the same time, she still withholds her own defining history. She asks Nina and Subaru to trust the limits around her past while building a band whose emotional center increasingly depends on that past.

## Tendō

Tendō is loving, proud, controlling, professionally disciplined, and emotionally dependent on the idea of Subaru as successor.

She is not consciously trying to erase Subaru. She may genuinely believe she is offering the granddaughter she loves a magnificent inheritance.

But she has mistaken Subaru’s childhood affection for permanent vocational consent.

Her tragedy is that the smile Subaru wants to protect is connected to a future Subaru cannot inhabit honestly.

---

# Overall assessment

Episode 4 is the series’ strongest treatment so far of the difference between **truth and authenticity**.

Nina assumes authenticity means saying the true thing immediately.

Subaru demonstrates that people sometimes require roles, scripts, and controlled performances before they can approach what is true.

Tendō demonstrates that completely genuine emotion can still impose an identity upon someone else.

Momoka demonstrates that respecting another person may mean not forcing entry into every concealed part of her life.

The episode’s deepest conclusion is not that Subaru should continue lying. It is:

> **Subaru must eventually choose her own life, but that choice will become meaningful only when it is more than an escape from her grandmother’s dream. Until then, the band gives her somewhere to practice being the person who can one day speak.**

Nina previously believed performance was the opposite of honesty.

Episode 4 teaches her that sometimes performance is where honesty waits until a person is ready to live it.
