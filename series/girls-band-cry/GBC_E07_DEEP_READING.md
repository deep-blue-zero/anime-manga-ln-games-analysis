## Archival source: Assistant Turn 24 — Original Episode 7 deep-dive response


# *Girls Band Cry* Episode 7 — Deep Dive

Episode 7 is where the five-person group finally receives a name and Nina makes her first unmistakably affirmative declaration about what she wants. The episode refuses to make either development clean.

The name is not carefully selected through consensus. It is taken impulsively from another musician’s T-shirt.

Nina’s commitment to music is not expressed through a balanced plan. She announces that she is quitting preparatory school.

Momoka, meanwhile, responds to the prospect of a serious professional band by trying to leave again.

The episode’s central proposition is:

> **The fear of failure becomes most severe when failure might settle a question one desperately wants to remain unanswered. Momoka would rather call herself defeated than risk receiving a second verdict. Nina responds by deciding that the band’s purpose is not to guarantee victory, but to continue acting as though the music deserves belief.**

The newly named **Togenashi Togeari** is built around that contradiction. At the level of its component words, the name combines the ideas of being **without thorns** and **having thorns**. That is already an excellent description of these girls:

-   they are defensive and abrasive;

-   they injure people who approach them;

-   they are also frightened, needy, and terrified of abandonment;

-   they tell others to leave them alone while asking not to be left alone.


The final song makes this contradiction explicit.

---

## 1\. Narrative lens

## They have merchandise before they have an identity

The opening is almost a satire of premature professionalization.

The group is selling:

-   T-shirts;

-   towels;

-   improvised merchandise bearing the temporary name **新川崎（仮）**;

-   goods for a band that has still not chosen its actual name.


Tomo complains:

> 「バンドの名前も決まってないのに
> なんで作ったの」
>
> “Why did you make merchandise when we haven’t even decided on a band name?”

The answer is practical:

> 「少しでも活動の足しになればと思って」
> “I thought it might help fund our activities, even a little.”

This continues Episode 6’s movement from expressive necessity into professional infrastructure. The group now needs:

-   transportation;

-   rehearsal space;

-   equipment;

-   merchandise;

-   ticket revenue;

-   audience cultivation.


But the order is revealing. They have begun producing a marketable surface before defining the collective underneath it.

Rupa is immediately the most naturally popular at the booth. Fans approach her, ask for photographs, and promise to attend the next show. Her social ease and striking appearance already have promotional value, even though she is one of the newest members.

The group’s public identity is emerging unevenly:

-   Rupa is effortlessly attractive to fans.

-   Nina remains awkward but earnest.

-   Subaru understands presentation.

-   Tomo wants the naming problem solved seriously.

-   Momoka still has no clear commitment to the group’s future.


They can sell a shirt more easily than they can say what they are.

---

## Momoka accepts another live opportunity without answering the professional question

An older musical contact invites Momoka to perform in Suwa, Nagano. The schedule is demanding:

-   travel the previous day;

-   rehearse;

-   perform the following night;

-   return immediately afterward.


Momoka offers to go alone, particularly because Nina supposedly has to study.

Nina immediately says:

> 「行きます」
> “I’m going.”

This mirrors her instantaneous acceptance of the Serbian Night show. Nina no longer needs Momoka to force her onto a stage. She actively seeks opportunities to perform.

Rupa supports her:

> 「場数踏んだほうが」
> “It would be better to gain more experience.”

Tomo reluctantly accepts because Rupa does.

But beneath this apparent momentum, Nina and Momoka are in a cold war. Nina has declared that she wants to pursue professional music, while Momoka has refused to answer.

Subaru describes Momoka’s behavior:

> 「何かってニーナに嫌味言ってくるよね」
> “She keeps finding reasons to make snide comments at Niina.”

Momoka is not merely annoyed by Nina’s ambition. Nina has begun forcing her to confront a question she had tried to close permanently:

> Was leaving Diamond Dust the end of Momoka’s professional musical life, or only the end of one attempt?

---

## The failed naming session reveals five incompatible fantasies

The girls try to choose a band name over food.

Three proposals emerge:

-   Tomo: **ウンディーネ / Undine**

-   Subaru: **ドリームガール / Dream Girl**

-   Nina: **ちょこれーと / chocolate**, specifically written in hiragana


Each proposal reveals something private.

### Tomo: 「ウンディーネ」

The foreign, fantastical name suits Tomo’s aesthetic seriousness. It sounds like the name of a carefully designed artistic project rather than an accidental group formed from interpersonal chaos.

It reflects her desire to create something elevated and controlled.

### Subaru: 「ドリームガール」

Subaru’s suggestion sounds polished, accessible, and commercially legible. It resembles a name already prepared for entertainment branding.

Even her private imagination of the band passes naturally through presentation.

### Nina: 「ちょこれーと」

Nina insists that it must be in hiragana rather than katakana.

The suggestion is almost absurdly soft and sweet for the person who most aggressively defines the band’s confrontational spirit. It exposes a part of Nina that her anger normally conceals:

-   childishness;

-   sentimentality;

-   a desire for something cute and uncomplicated;

-   perhaps the ordinary adolescence she has not been able to inhabit.


The others mock every proposal. Nothing survives.

That failure is appropriate. Each name describes the private band one member imagines. None describes what they have become together.

Their eventual name will not be the winner of an argument. It will emerge from a shared experience.

---

## Tomo sets an early boundary against becoming trapped in another collapsing group

Tomo warns:

> 「揉めるんなら　私らは抜けるからね」
> “If you’re going to keep fighting, Rupa and I are leaving.”

> 「時間がないんだから」
> “We don’t have time.”

This establishes that Tomo has not merged with the trio unconditionally.

She has:

-   an ambitious goal;

-   limited tolerance for emotional chaos;

-   prior experience with projects that failed to move forward;

-   a belief that time itself is an adversary.


Tomo was attracted to the trio because they seemed capable of confronting one another honestly. But conflict is valuable to her only if it produces work. She has no interest in becoming collateral damage in Nina and Momoka’s unresolved relationship.

That boundary will become important. Nina has begun speaking as though the band exists to vindicate Momoka. Tomo and Rupa have joined for reasons of their own.

---

## Nina’s sister brings the family conflict into the new musical life

Nina returns home late and finds her older sister waiting.

The scene is not a simple confrontation with a family enforcer. Her sister is affectionate, teasing, observant, and worried. She initially suspects:

-   a boyfriend;

-   suspicious employment;

-   some hidden social life.


Then Nina admits:

> 「バンド」
> “A band.”

Her sister is genuinely astonished. Nina shows her photographs, including the conspicuous outfit from the earlier performance. Asked what it is supposed to be, Nina answers:

> 「ロックだよ」
> “It’s rock.”

The certainty is funny because Nina herself only recently learned what Momoka meant by “rock.” She has already begun using the term as an identity.

Her sister then reveals that Nina’s grades have fallen sharply and that the preparatory school has contacted the family. Nina has also been missing classes.

This confirms that Nina’s divided life is already collapsing. She has not successfully balanced university preparation and music. Her obsession has made the decision before she has formally acknowledged it.

---

## The sister complicates the moral account of Nina’s father

The sister says their father was wrong:

> 「確かにさ
> お父さんは悪かったと思う」
>
> “Certainly, I think Dad was wrong.”

She explains that he was unable to accept his own daughter’s school refusal because of his public identity as a celebrated teacher and author.

That gives the father’s failure a particularly ugly dimension:

-   he had professional authority concerning education;

-   he had published an image of himself as an exemplary teacher;

-   Nina’s inability to attend school contradicted his public self-conception;

-   preserving that conception took precedence over fully supporting her.


Yet the sister also understands his strategy.

Had the family agreed not to pursue the bullying publicly, the school would have owed them:

> 「学校に貸しは作れた」
> “They could have created a debt the school owed them.”

With Nina’s strong grades, that leverage might have secured an easy recommendation.

Nina calls that:

> 「ズル」
> “Cheating.”

This directly parallels the Diamond Dust dispute.

### The family’s proposal

-   tolerate injustice now;

-   preserve institutional relationships;

-   acquire leverage;

-   secure Nina’s future later.


### Diamond Dust’s proposal to Momoka

-   tolerate commercial direction now;

-   remain with the group;

-   gain industry power;

-   recover artistic control later.


Nina and Momoka both reject strategic compromise because accepting it would require living inside something they regard as false or unjust.

Their refusals are morally understandable. They also abandon future advantages and damage relationships.

Episode 7 places the two decisions in clear structural alignment.

---

## Nina’s original bargain with her family is breaking down

Nina apparently justified moving away by promising to enter university through her own effort:

> 「大学は自力で受かる
> そう言ってこっち来たんじゃないの」
>
> “Didn’t you come here saying you would enter university on your own?”

Her sister argues that abandoning preparatory school and formally leaving the family arrangement would make it impossible to support Nina’s position.

The sister warns that “leaving home” would mean more than living elsewhere:

> 「もう二度と家に帰れないってことになるよ」
> “It could mean never being able to return home again.”

Nina is not deciding merely whether to attend classes. She is considering severing the framework through which her family still financially and socially supports her.

This makes her final announcement much more consequential than a triumphant “I choose music.”

She is breaking the agreement that made her escape possible.

---

## The road trip gives the five girls their first ordinary collective life

The trip to Suwa is one of the first extended sequences in which the five members exist together outside rehearsal or crisis.

It is full of unglamorous logistics:

-   packing light;

-   carrying instruments;

-   renting a manual-transmission vehicle;

-   Momoka’s poor driving;

-   Rupa taking over;

-   highway rest stops;

-   snacks;

-   games;

-   cheap sleeping arrangements;

-   moving heavy equipment.


The competence hierarchy becomes clearer.

### Rupa

Rupa is the most broadly capable adult presence:

-   she drives smoothly;

-   monitors fatigue;

-   handles logistics;

-   works;

-   drinks without collapsing;

-   manages Tomo;

-   and relates naturally to strangers.


### Momoka

Momoka organizes the band but is comically bad at certain practical tasks, especially driving. Her authority is real but not universal.

### Subaru

Subaru maintains social momentum through games, jokes, and group photographs.

### Tomo

Tomo complains, judges, and resists embarrassment, but participates.

### Nina

Nina is eager, literal, socially awkward, and increasingly delighted by belonging to the group.

The road trip matters because a band is not made only in performance. It is also made through the ability to survive hours together in a vehicle.

---

## The “few friends” game quietly names their common condition

Subaru suggests a game in which someone asks a question and wins if everyone raises a hand.

Nina asks:

> 「友達が少ない人」
> “People who have few friends.”

Everyone responds.

Nina wins immediately.

The joke is affectionate, but it identifies the group’s shared social condition. They are not necessarily alike in temperament, background, or ambition. What they share is difficulty inhabiting ordinary social structures.

The band is not formed from naturally harmonious extroverts. It is becoming a collective of people whose previous relationships have been:

-   coercive;

-   absent;

-   broken;

-   strategically performed;

-   or difficult to sustain.


Their lack of ordinary friendship is becoming the basis for an unusually intense alternative.

---

## Tomo’s school withdrawal was a survival calculation

At the rest area, Nina asks Tomo why she left school.

Tomo says:

> 「親はいないも同然だから」
> “My parents were as good as nonexistent.”

She describes seeing her mother with an unfamiliar man:

> 「別の生き物みたいだった」
> “She looked like a different creature.”

That sight triggered a realization:

-   no adult was going to construct a future for her;

-   after graduation she would have to survive alone;

-   performers beginning at eighteen or twenty were already common;

-   if she was going to pursue music, she needed to begin immediately;

-   school consumed time she could not afford.


Tomo’s withdrawal was not framed primarily as rebellion or trauma response. It was a ruthless career calculation made by someone who believed she had no safety net.

She concludes:

> 「それだけ」
> “That’s all.”

The brevity closes the subject. Tomo gives the logic, not an invitation to comfort her.

---

## Rupa’s loss is stated indirectly and without spectacle

Tomo adds that Rupa had to think similarly when her family died in an accident.

Rupa’s history is not delivered through:

-   a tearful confession;

-   a flashback sequence;

-   a dramatic request for sympathy.


It appears almost incidentally within Tomo’s explanation.

That restraint fits Rupa’s characterization. Her composure and competence are now revealed to exist beside catastrophic loss, but the episode does not reduce her to that loss.

It also helps explain why Rupa can tolerate uncertainty better than the others. She has already encountered a form of rupture that no argument, achievement, or accusation can reverse.

---

## Nina begins ranking the legitimacy of suffering

After hearing Tomo and Rupa’s histories, Nina says:

> 「私　全然だ」
> “I’m nothing compared with that.”

She had previously treated leaving school as a central proof of her own seriousness and injury. Tomo’s practical urgency and Rupa’s bereavement make Nina feel sheltered, immature, or insufficiently committed.

This is another form of Nina’s absolutism. She turns suffering into a hierarchy:

-   if someone endured more, perhaps her own decision was self-indulgent;

-   if someone had fewer options, perhaps her own refusal was not courageous;

-   if Tomo and Rupa already committed fully, perhaps Nina’s university plan proves she is not serious.


That emotional comparison likely contributes to her eventual decision to quit preparatory school.

But the episode does not establish that pain must be ranked to justify art. Nina’s experience does not become unreal because someone else’s history is harsher.

Her tendency is to seek a verdict even where no competition is necessary.

---

## The group photograph precedes the group’s name

At the rest stop, Rupa arranges a photograph of all five.

She asks everyone to smile:

> 「はい　Say cheese, please」

Tomo and Momoka resist. Nina and Subaru participate more readily.

Someone remarks:

> 「アイドルみたい」
> “We look like idols.”

Tomo immediately says:

> 「黙れ」
> “Shut up.”

The photograph is significant because it is their first clear image as a five-person unit. They have not agreed on a name, professional plan, or even whether Momoka will remain. Yet they are already generating memories and images of belonging.

The photograph temporarily creates the group before language can define it.

It also continues the series’ complication of performance. The smiles are staged. The photograph is nevertheless evidence of a real shared trip.

---

## Mine reveals Momoka as “Momo”

At the venue, they meet Mine, an older musician who knew Momoka during her earliest period in Tokyo.

Mine calls her:

> 「モモ」
> “Momo.”

The nickname surprises the younger girls. It reveals a Momoka who existed before she became:

-   Nina’s idol;

-   the disillusioned former Diamond Dust member;

-   the group’s experienced organizer;

-   the person carrying professional failure.


Mine treats her as a younger musician she once knew and encouraged.

A member of Mine’s band still has a sticker Momoka gave her. The small object confirms a history of affection and artistic exchange that predates Nina.

Subaru observes that Momoka seems different around them:

> 「もともとはあんな感じだったのかもしれませんね」
> “Perhaps she was originally more like that.”

Momoka becomes lighter, more embarrassed, and less guarded. Mine gives Nina access to a version of Momoka not defined by the Diamond Dust rupture.

---

## Mine is part of Momoka’s artistic lineage

Mine invites Momoka and the others to join her for one song.

Momoka explains that when she first moved to Tokyo, she saw Mine perform in an early opening slot:

> 「前座のおまけみたいな出番で
> 一歩も引かずに
> 歌で戦ってるみたいでさ」
>
> “She had a slot that was almost an afterthought for an opening act, but she didn’t give an inch. It looked as though she were fighting through her song.”

Momoka remembers thinking:

> 「ああ　ロックだって」
> “That is rock.”

This expands the series’ lineage:

1.  Mine fought through song on a minor stage.

2.  Momoka saw her and developed part of her understanding of rock.

3.  Momoka wrote the song that reached Nina.

4.  Nina now sings with the five-person band.

5.  Their future audience may receive the same force.


Momoka did not invent her ethic in isolation. She inherited it from another woman who persisted on small stages.

---

## Momoka tries to turn another performance into a funeral

After describing how much Mine’s song meant to her, Momoka says:

> 「私の最後のライブには　ぴったりだ」
> “It is perfect for my final live.”

She then announces:

> 「やめるよ　このバンドは」
> “I’m ending this band.”

> 「明日の本番で終わりだ
> 私は抜ける」
>
> “Tomorrow’s show will be the end. I’m leaving.”

This repeats her Episode 1 pattern.

Momoka repeatedly uses music to create graceful endings:

-   one final performance;

-   one final song;

-   a farewell through something beautiful;

-   withdrawal staged as completion.


Nina repeatedly refuses to accept those endings.

But this time Momoka gives a different reason:

> 「私はこのバンドを背負って
> プロを目指す気にはなれない」
>
> “I cannot bring myself to carry this band and aim for the professional world.”

> 「私の作る曲じゃ
> プロの世界では通用しないからだ」
>
> “The songs I write won’t work in the professional world.”

Momoka is no longer saying only that she does not want professional ambition.

She is saying she is not good enough.

---

## Momoka is afraid that a second failure will become a verdict

Mine later explains Momoka’s fear:

> 「嫌なんだろうね
> 結論が出るのが」
>
> “She probably hates the idea of reaching a conclusion.”

> 「１度目はうまくいかなくても
> 運が悪かったで済むけど」
>
> “If it goes badly once, you can say it was bad luck.”

> 「２回やってダメだったら
> さすがに実力って言われる」
>
> “If it fails twice, people will say it was your actual ability.”

This is one of the most important explanations of Momoka so far.

Leaving Diamond Dust preserved ambiguity.

Perhaps:

-   the label was wrong;

-   the other members compromised;

-   timing was bad;

-   Momoka’s music could have succeeded under different conditions;

-   her songs still possessed unrealized value.


If she builds another band around her music and fails again, she fears the conclusion will become unavoidable:

> The problem was not the label, the timing, or Diamond Dust.
> The problem was Momoka.

Her withdrawal is therefore not lack of ambition. It is an attempt to preserve the possibility that her music might have been good enough.

---

## Gratitude becomes Momoka’s farewell weapon

Momoka tells Nina:

> 「仁菜が　私の歌を好きでいてくれたのは
> 嬉しい」
>
> “I was happy that you loved my songs.”

> 「本気になってくれたことにも
> 感謝してる」
>
> “I’m grateful that you became serious about this.”

> 「ありがとう」
> “Thank you.”

The language is affectionate, but functionally final. Momoka acknowledges Nina’s devotion while refusing the future Nina wants from it.

She treats Nina’s belief as a beautiful thing that happened, not as evidence requiring another attempt.

This is why Nina cannot answer immediately. Momoka’s gratitude is harder to fight than contempt. It recognizes Nina’s feelings while denying them any power over the decision.

---

## Mine offers an adult theory of professional fear

Nina asks Mine whether she has ever wanted to quit.

Mine does not answer with heroic confidence.

She says fear persists:

> 「今でも眠れなくもなれば
> 食べられなくもなる」
>
> “Even now, there are times when I cannot sleep or eat.”

Nina asks:

> 「今でも？」
> “Even now?”

Mine replies:

> 「もちろん」
> “Of course.”

Then:

> 「本当に好きなものを仕事にするって
> そういうこと」
>
> “That is what it means to make what you truly love your work.”

Mine once considered becoming a teacher and singing as a hobby:

> 「それが一番賢いやり方なんだろうなあって」
> “I thought that was probably the smartest way.”

Nina asks why she did not choose it.

Mine says she has forgotten the original reason. What remains is simpler:

> 「私にとってステージは
> 言いたいことが言える場所」
>
> “For me, the stage is where I can say what I want to say.”

> 「大好きな場所」
> “It is a place I love.”

> 「だからいくら怖くても苦しくても
> しょうがない」
>
> “So however frightening or painful it is, it cannot be helped.”

Mine does not promise success. She does not say fear can be conquered.

Her mature position is:

> Fear is not evidence that the path is wrong.
> It may be the continuing cost of caring enough for failure to matter.

Momoka sees fear as a reason to prevent a verdict.

Mine sees fear as part of remaining available to the stage.

---

## Nina’s walk to the lake is the first time she decides without confronting someone

After speaking with Mine, Nina leaves the others and walks alone through the night.

She repeatedly says:

> 「決めた」
> “I’ve decided.”

The repetition grows increasingly energetic:

> 「決めた　決めた　決めたぞ」
> “I’ve decided. I’ve decided. I’ve decided!”

She moves toward the lake while fireworks fill the sky.

This is a different decision-making process from Nina’s usual pattern. She does not immediately:

-   accuse Momoka;

-   demand a confession;

-   force the group into a debate;

-   insist upon a morally explicit verdict.


She leaves, thinks, and returns with an act prepared.

The decision is still impulsive, but it is not purely reactive. Nina has listened to:

-   her sister;

-   Tomo;

-   Mine;

-   Momoka;

-   and her own experience of the band.


---

## The name is discovered rather than invented

Mine finishes her set and introduces the group she loves.

Backstage, someone asks whether their band name was ever decided.

Nina sees the text on Mine’s shirt:

> **トゲナシトゲアリ**

She walks onstage and announces:

> 「私たちは
> トゲナシトゲアリです」
>
> “We are Togenashi Togeari.”

The name resolves the failed naming session because it does not correspond to one member’s idealized self-image.

Its internal contradiction fits all of them.

They are simultaneously:

-   thornless and thorned;

-   wanting contact and repelling it;

-   fragile and aggressive;

-   sincere and performative;

-   terrified and ambitious;

-   desperate to be free and desperate not to be abandoned.


It is a name capable of holding incompatibility without resolving it.

That is a better name for this group than any harmonious aspiration could have been.

---

## Nina’s stage speech moves from private joy to moral proof

Nina begins by admitting that she still does not completely understand why she started:

> 「なんでやっているのか
> どうして歌っているのか
> よくわからなかったんですけど」
>
> “I did not really understand why I was doing this or why I was singing.”

Then she says:

> 「でも　楽しくて」
> “But it was fun.”

> 「生まれて初めて
> 何かに夢中になってて」
>
> “For the first time in my life, I became completely absorbed in something.”

This is an enormous development.

Until now, Nina has justified major choices negatively:

-   she did not want to lose;

-   she was not wrong;

-   she wanted to defeat Diamond Dust;

-   she wanted to prove Momoka’s song had not lost.


Here she first names simple positive desire:

> **I enjoy this.
> I am absorbed in it.**

She then returns to proof:

> 「その歌が間違ってないって
> 証明したいんです」
>
> “I want to prove that the song was not wrong.”

> 「その歌で人の心を動かして
> どうだ　すごいだろうって言いたいんです」
>
> “I want to move people’s hearts with that song and say, ‘See? Isn’t it incredible?’”

Nina still asks artistic success to provide a moral verdict. But the speech now contains something beyond vindication: she wants to transmit what she received.

---

## Nina names every member—and almost speaks for them

Nina continues:

> 「すばるちゃんと
> ルパさんと
> 智ちゃんと
> 桃香さんと」
>
> “With Subaru, Rupa, Tomo, and Momoka…”

> 「みんなきっと
> 似たようなこと思ってるんです」
>
> “I’m sure everyone feels something similar.”

This is moving and presumptuous.

Nina publicly turns her personal interpretation into a group manifesto. But the other members do not necessarily share her precise motivation:

-   Subaru’s struggle concerns inherited identity.

-   Tomo’s concerns survival, ambition, and control.

-   Rupa’s reasons remain more private.

-   Momoka is actively trying to leave.

-   Nina wants to vindicate a song and defeat Diamond Dust.


Nina is right that all of them are working despite fear.

She is not necessarily entitled to define what each is trying to prove.

Tomo interrupts:

> 「うざいですよ
> 自分語りは」
>
> “This self-narration is annoying.”

Her polite harshness punctures the speech before Nina can turn the whole band into an extension of Nina’s story.

Then they play.

The music becomes the only form capable of including everyone without requiring identical explanations.

---

## Momoka’s reversal occurs through action, not confession

Momoka had declared that the show would be her last.

She does not deliver a long counter-speech after Nina’s declaration. She complains:

> 「こんなめちゃくちゃな始まりあるか」
> “What kind of absurd beginning is this?”

Someone answers:

> 「知らないよ」
> “Who knows?”

Then Momoka plays.

This is the episode’s true answer.

She has not solved:

-   her fear of a second failure;

-   the guilt surrounding Diamond Dust;

-   her belief that her songs may not survive professionally;

-   the unequal responsibility she feels toward the younger members.


But she participates in the beginning Nina has created.

The band’s continuation is enacted before Momoka can defend it intellectually.

---

## Nina’s final announcement is both liberation and warning

After the performance, Nina tells the others:

> 「予備校やめます」
> “I’m quitting preparatory school.”

This is the practical decision underlying her repeated 「決めた」.

It represents genuine growth:

-   Nina is no longer pretending university is her affirmative dream.

-   Music has become the first thing she has pursued with deep voluntary absorption.

-   She is choosing a path rather than only fleeing one.


But it also preserves her most dangerous pattern:

-   uncertainty becomes intolerable;

-   divided commitment feels false;

-   the answer must therefore be total;

-   she proves seriousness by removing the alternative.


Momoka and Tomo also left school to pursue music. Nina may interpret their sacrifices as the standard required for legitimacy.

The comic reaction—rather than uncomplicated celebration—helps preserve the ambiguity.

Nina has chosen something.

Whether she has chosen wisely remains unresolved.

# 2\. Japanese-language lens

## The candidate names expose registers of self

The contrast among the proposed names is enhanced by their written and phonetic forms.

### 「ウンディーネ」

Tomo’s proposal is entirely katakana, foreign-sounding, elegant, and slightly grandiose. It belongs to a self-consciously artistic register.

### 「ドリームガール」

Subaru’s name is English-derived, aspirational, and readily commercialized. It sounds like a title designed for public consumption.

### 「ちょこれーと」

Nina insists:

> 「カタカナじゃないよ
> ひらがなでちょこれーと」
>
> “Not in katakana. ‘Chocolate’ in hiragana.”

Writing the foreign-derived word in hiragana makes it softer, more childlike, and less sleek.

Nina, the most verbally combative member, privately proposes the gentlest name.

The written form reveals an emotional register her normal speech suppresses.

---

## 「新川崎なら本気でやめる」

Tomo warns:

> 「新川崎なら
> 本気でやめるからね」
>
> “If the name is Shin-Kawasaki, I am seriously quitting.”

The recurring temporary name embodies accident and amateurism:

-   Nina initially went to the wrong place;

-   the group formed through improvised circumstances;

-   its identity was provisional;

-   no one had chosen it.


Tomo wants a name that signals intention. Her rejection of Shin-Kawasaki is therefore also a rejection of remaining an accidental project.

---

## 「冷戦」

Subaru calls Nina and Momoka’s situation:

> 「冷戦ですね」
> “A cold war.”

The phrase is apt because neither directly addresses the central disagreement. Instead:

-   Momoka makes barbed remarks;

-   Nina complains to others;

-   both wait for the other to disclose her real position;

-   the band proceeds around the unresolved hostility.


The problem is no longer open explosive conflict. It is withheld declaration.

That is progress only in the narrowest sense.

---

## 「貸しを作る」 reveals the family’s strategic morality

Nina’s sister says overlooking the bullying could have made the school owe the family.

The expression **貸しを作る** treats moral injury like a social account:

-   accept a loss now;

-   create obligation;

-   collect advantage later.


Nina calls this cheating because it converts the original wrong into bargaining power rather than demanding that the wrong be acknowledged.

The same logic appears in professional compromise:

> Accept an intolerable present so that future leverage may become possible.

Nina’s ethics reject delayed justice because she does not trust that the future repayment will ever arrive.

---

## Tomo’s 「それだけ」 shuts down emotional interpretation

After describing:

-   absent parents;

-   her mother’s behavior;

-   fear of future instability;

-   the urgency to begin her career,


Tomo concludes:

> 「それだけ」
> “That is all.”

The phrase attempts to reduce a deeply personal history to rational explanation.

Tomo prefers to present herself as someone who calculated correctly, not someone who was frightened or abandoned.

Her technical control extends into autobiography.

---

## 「背負う」 reveals Momoka’s understanding of leadership

Momoka does not merely say she cannot join a professional band.

She says:

> 「このバンドを背負って」
> “Carry this band on my back.”

The verb implies:

-   responsibility for the members;

-   responsibility for the songs;

-   responsibility for professional outcomes;

-   responsibility for another possible collapse.


Nina sees Momoka primarily as a songwriter whose work deserves vindication.

Momoka sees the decision as accepting the weight of five lives and ambitions.

Her fear is not only personal failure. It is failing people who now trust her.

---

## 「通用しない」 turns a fear into an objective verdict

Momoka says:

> 「私の作る曲じゃ
> プロの世界では通用しない」
>
> “The songs I write will not work in the professional world.”

**通用する** means to pass, function, or be accepted within a particular sphere.

Momoka presents her judgment as though professional inadequacy were already established fact. She does not say:

-   “I’m afraid they may not succeed.”

-   “I’m uncertain about the market.”

-   “I don’t know whether I want the cost.”


She says the songs do not qualify.

This is self-protection through premature judgment. If Momoka declares the verdict herself, she does not have to wait for the industry or audience to issue it.

---

## 「結論が出る」

Mine diagnoses Momoka as fearing:

> 「結論が出る」
> “A conclusion being reached.”

The passive structure matters. The conclusion seems to emerge from events rather than from a specific judge.

Who decides?

-   the audience;

-   follower counts;

-   the industry;

-   the band’s survival;

-   Momoka herself?


The ambiguity makes the fear total. Any second failure could be interpreted as confirmation.

Momoka is not merely avoiding an outcome. She is avoiding the transformation of uncertainty into identity.

---

## 「実力って言われる」

Mine says that after two failures:

> 「さすがに実力って言われる」
> “People will say it was your actual level of ability.”

Again, the agent is generalized.

The sentence captures how public artistic judgment works psychologically. A person does not need one explicit authority to condemn her. She anticipates a collective verdict:

> You had two opportunities.
> The consistent variable was you.

This is precisely the kind of social atmosphere—**空気**—that Nina also distrusts.

---

## Mine’s 「しょうがない」 is acceptance without resignation

Mine says the fear and pain cannot be helped:

> 「しょうがない」

Momoka also uses realistic language to justify withdrawal. But Mine’s realism points in the opposite direction.

For Momoka:

> Fear is evidence that continuing risks unbearable harm.

For Mine:

> Fear is the unavoidable condition of continuing to do the work she loves.

The same apparently resigned expression can support retreat or endurance.

---

## 「間違ってないって証明したい」

Nina’s formulation remains revealingly impersonal:

> She wants to prove that **the song** was not wrong.

Songs are not ordinarily morally right or wrong. Nina treats the song as a proposition:

-   refusing surrender is valid;

-   the self need not bend;

-   no external answer has absolute authority.


If the song moves other people, Nina believes its worldview has been vindicated.

This is emotionally coherent but logically impossible to settle completely. Every new listener can become another piece of evidence, but never final proof.

---

## Tomo’s 「自分語り」

Tomo dismisses Nina’s speech as:

> 「自分語り」
> “Talking about yourself” or “self-narration.”

The criticism is precise.

Nina has begun with a public introduction of the band, but gradually transforms it into a story about:

-   the song that saved her;

-   her desire to prove it;

-   her understanding of everyone else.


Tomo’s interruption protects the group from becoming Nina’s autobiography.

She does not dispute the emotion. She insists that the performance begin.

---

## 「トゲナシトゲアリ」

The name itself is a contradiction:

-   **トゲナシ** — without thorns

-   **トゲアリ** — with thorns


Its rhythm also makes it sound like a phrase correcting itself:

> No thorns—actually, thorns.

That resembles the emotional speech of the band:

-   Leave me alone—do not leave me.

-   I do not care—I need you to understand.

-   I am quitting—play one final song.

-   I am afraid—therefore I must try.

-   I will not bend—I want to create with others.


The name does not solve contradiction. It publicly adopts it.

---

## 「予備校やめます」

Nina’s final announcement is characteristically negative in grammatical form.

She does not say:

-   “I will devote myself to music.”

-   “I have chosen the band.”

-   “I want to become a professional singer.”


She says:

> “I am quitting preparatory school.”

Even when Nina makes an affirmative choice, she expresses it through refusal.

Her positive desire is real, but the linguistic structure shows how much of her identity remains organized around what she will no longer accept.

---

# 3\. Performance lens

## Momoka becomes visibly younger around Mine

Mine’s use of “Momo” changes Momoka’s physical presence.

Around Nina and the others, Momoka is usually:

-   loose but authoritative;

-   teasing;

-   physically dominant;

-   the experienced person organizing the room.


Around Mine, she becomes:

-   more embarrassed;

-   less verbally in control;

-   more visibly pleased;

-   closer to the younger musician who once looked upward for inspiration.


This is important for Nina, who has mostly known Momoka as either idol or mentor. Mine reveals Momoka as someone who was also once the inexperienced recipient of another woman’s example.

---

## The road trip distributes intimacy through physical proximity

The vehicle compresses all five bodies into one moving space.

The animation uses:

-   shifting seats;

-   glances between rows;

-   sleeping postures;

-   snacks passed around;

-   complaints about driving;

-   reactions to personal questions.


No one can leave an uncomfortable conversation immediately.

That forced proximity produces more ordinary intimacy than the emotionally explosive confrontations in Kawasaki. Tomo can disclose her history because the group is moving together and the conversation does not require a formal face-to-face confession.

---

## Rupa’s competence is performed without fanfare

Rupa’s driving scene is significant because she does not announce herself as the reliable adult.

She simply:

-   takes the wheel;

-   reassures everyone;

-   plans rest stops;

-   tells them they may sleep;

-   manages the vehicle smoothly.


Her competence is made visible through the relief of everyone around her.

This distinguishes her from Momoka’s style of leadership. Momoka directs, argues, and pushes. Rupa stabilizes conditions so that other people can function.

---

## Momoka’s quitting speech is physically controlled self-amputation

When Momoka announces her departure, she does not collapse.

She:

-   lowers or averts her gaze;

-   maintains a relatively flat delivery;

-   thanks Nina;

-   states her judgment as settled;

-   turns the emotional issue into professional assessment.


The controlled manner makes the scene more painful. She is not deciding in the moment. She has prepared a clean cut.

Her body says:

> I will leave before anyone can persuade me to hope.

---

## Nina cannot directly challenge Momoka at first

In previous episodes, Nina would ordinarily confront Momoka immediately.

Here she asks Mine questions instead:

-   What music does Mine like?

-   Has Mine ever wanted to quit?

-   Is Mine afraid?


Nina temporarily approaches Momoka through another adult’s experience.

This is evidence that Subaru’s lessons about conversational conditions have affected her. Nina remains intrusive, but she recognizes that direct attack may only deepen Momoka’s retreat.

---

## Mine embodies fear without physical retreat

Mine’s stage presence and ordinary speech align.

She is not presented as:

-   invulnerable;

-   commercially spectacular;

-   free from anxiety;

-   or artistically purified.


She performs with the bodily assurance of someone who has repeatedly chosen the stage while remaining aware of its cost.

Her steadiness offers an alternative to both:

-   Nina’s unsustainable total expenditure;

-   Momoka’s preemptive withdrawal.


---

## Fireworks convert Nina’s private decision into physical momentum

Nina walks and then moves with increasing determination as fireworks appear over the lake.

The repeated:

> 「決めた」

is accompanied by an expanding posture and quicker movement.

She does not look calm. The decision energizes her rather than settling her.

That is characteristic of Nina: certainty does not make her quiet. It gives her direction.

---

## The speech begins with Nina alone and gradually constructs the ensemble

Nina initially stands alone at the microphone under restrained lighting.

As she names:

-   Subaru;

-   Rupa;

-   Tomo;

-   Momoka,


the direction gives each member visual acknowledgment.

The band is being assembled grammatically and cinematically.

But when Nina begins speaking for everyone, Tomo interrupts. The transition into music then allows each member to contribute through her own instrument rather than through Nina’s account.

Formally:

> Nina can name the members.
> She cannot completely narrate them.

---

## Momoka’s participation is the real reply

Momoka does not verbally retract her decision.

During the performance, however, she:

-   engages fully with the guitar;

-   responds to the enlarged five-person arrangement;

-   interacts visually with the others;

-   ceases behaving like someone merely completing an obligation.


The difference between a final performance and a new beginning lies less in what she says than in how she plays.

Once again, Momoka’s musical self makes a commitment her defensive self cannot formulate.

---

## The full ensemble changes Nina’s performance body

Nina is no longer singing over the comparatively open architecture of the trio.

With Tomo and Rupa added:

-   bass gives her body a firmer ground;

-   keyboard creates additional harmonic and melodic pressure;

-   the arrangement surrounds her more completely;

-   her gestures occur inside a denser collective field.


She remains the most volatile body onstage, but the volatility is now contained by four distinct sources of structure.

The visual language repeatedly alternates:

-   Nina’s face and reaching hands;

-   Momoka’s guitar;

-   Rupa’s bass;

-   Subaru’s drum impacts;

-   Tomo’s keyboard;

-   the crowd;

-   Mine watching.


The performance is not merely Nina expressing herself with accompaniment. It is a five-way system.

---

## Tomo’s interruption is a form of intimacy

Calling Nina’s speech annoying might sound cruel in another context.

Here it demonstrates that Tomo has already entered the group’s internal language. She is willing to:

-   challenge the vocalist in public;

-   refuse sentimental overreach;

-   trust that the relationship will survive blunt correction;

-   and immediately support Nina musically afterward.


Tomo does not offer warm reassurance. She offers continued participation.

For this band, that is often the more meaningful form of affection.

---

# 4\. Music lens

## Mine completes a female musical lineage

Mine is not merely a guest musician.

She is the artist who gave Momoka an image of what rock could be:

> occupying a marginal slot and refusing to perform as though it were marginal.

Momoka later gives Nina a song that plays a comparable role.

The lineage is not about imitating a musical style. It is about transmitting an ethic:

-   the stage remains meaningful even when the industry has not granted status;

-   expressive force does not require institutional permission;

-   a small audience does not make the performance small;

-   fear does not invalidate the act.


This is an alternative form of inheritance to the one Subaru receives from her grandmother. Mine does not prescribe Momoka’s career. She offers an example Momoka chooses to remember.

---

## Momoka repeatedly converts beloved songs into farewell objects

In Episode 1, Momoka performs 「空の箱」 as part of her intended departure from music.

In Episode 7, she chooses a song associated with Mine’s example as the ideal conclusion to her new band.

The pattern suggests that Momoka uses music to dignify retreat.

She cannot simply stop. She must create:

-   a meaningful final performance;

-   a song that expresses what she cannot confess;

-   an ending beautiful enough to feel complete.


Nina’s recurring role is to deny that beauty the authority to declare finality.

---

## Mine separates fear from evidence of unfitness

Momoka interprets fear as diagnostic:

> If attempting this again terrifies me, perhaps I know I am not good enough.

Mine interprets fear as relational:

> It frightens me because I love it and therefore have something to lose.

The difference is profound.

Fear can mean:

-   danger;

-   unpreparedness;

-   lack of desire;

-   or the magnitude of desire.


No emotional sensation carries one fixed verdict.

---

## The final song is the first true Togenashi Togeari manifesto

The song begins with bodily and psychological fragmentation:

> 「いつだって傷ばっか
> カラダもココロもバラバラになっていっちゃって」
>
> “It is always wounds; body and heart breaking apart.”

It describes a city filled with lies and noise:

> 「吐きそうな嘘ばっか
> 次から次へと飛び交う街」
>
> “One nauseating lie after another flying through the city.”

That language belongs to all five members differently:

-   Nina distrusts institutional narratives.

-   Momoka distrusts commercial promises and her own hopeful impulses.

-   Subaru lives through performed identities.

-   Tomo distrusts industry formatting and familial reliability.

-   Rupa’s composure exists within a world already altered by irreversible loss.


---

## The song rejects consolation while pleading for it

The lyric imagines a voice saying:

> 「怖くないから隠れてないで
> 一緒に僕と踊ろう」
>
> “There is nothing to fear, so stop hiding and dance with me.”

The response is disgust:

> 「気色悪い世界から逃げたい」
> “I want to escape this repulsive world.”

Then comes the contradiction:

> 「散って散って散って」
> “Scatter—go away.”

followed immediately by:

> 「そっと　そっとひとりにしないで」
> “Quietly, gently—do not leave me alone.”

This is the emotional structure of the band’s name.

They have thorns because approach feels dangerous.

They are thornless because beneath the aggression is an almost unbearable need for continued presence.

Nina’s conflict with Momoka, Subaru’s performed withdrawal, Tomo’s refusal to add members, and Momoka’s repeated farewells all enact the same contradiction:

> Go away before you can leave me.

---

## 「愛を証明できるまで」

The lyric promises continuation:

> 「ずっとずっとずっと
> 愛を証明できるまでずっと」
>
> “Forever, forever, forever—until love can be proven.”

This directly echoes Nina’s stage speech.

Nina wants to prove:

-   Momoka’s song was right;

-   Momoka’s choice had meaning;

-   the band’s effort deserves belief;

-   the connection among them is real.


But the formulation is dangerous because proof may never be complete.

How does one prove love?

-   by staying?

-   by succeeding?

-   by suffering?

-   by reaching Budokan?

-   by moving an audience?

-   by refusing to leave?


The lyric captures Nina’s tendency to make affection into an endless trial. She will continue until evidence becomes conclusive, but emotional life rarely provides that kind of conclusion.

---

## The five-person arrangement makes Momoka’s exit musically implausible

Episode 6 demonstrated the greater potential of bass and keyboard. Episode 7 gives that sound a public stage.

The arrangement now has:

-   Rupa’s live low-end foundation;

-   Tomo’s harmonic and melodic layering;

-   Subaru’s rhythmic structure;

-   Momoka’s guitar attack;

-   Nina’s voice at the center.


Momoka can claim that her songs are professionally inadequate in conversation. The fuller arrangement allows the band to answer through realization rather than reassurance.

The music does not prove commercial viability.

It proves that the artistic possibility is not exhausted.

---

## The audience is inherited through trust rather than brand alone

Mine introduces them as:

> 「私の大好きなグループ」
> “A group I love.”

That introduction transfers relational trust.

The audience does not know Togenashi Togeari. It knows Mine. Her endorsement grants the group an opening.

This is another professional pathway distinct from follower counts and major-label machinery:

-   artistic lineage;

-   local reputation;

-   venue relationships;

-   older musicians giving younger ones a stage.


The series continues to show that independent music is social infrastructure, not isolated authenticity.

---

# 5\. Formal and directorial lens

## The episode begins and ends with clothing as identity

At the opening, the group attempts to sell its own merchandise before possessing a settled name.

At the climax, Nina reads a phrase from Mine’s shirt and turns it into the band’s name.

This creates an elegant circular structure.

### Opening clothing

-   manufactured for promotion;

-   bears a temporary identity;

-   attempts to create value before meaning has settled.


### Mine’s shirt

-   already belongs to a lived person and context;

-   is encountered during a formative musical experience;

-   contains a contradiction Nina recognizes intuitively;

-   becomes meaningful before it becomes marketable.


The group begins with merchandise searching for an identity.

It ends with an identity discovered on clothing.

---

## The road-movie structure allows the band to become a social unit

Travel removes them from their usual individual spaces:

-   Nina’s sparse apartment and preparatory school;

-   Momoka’s working life;

-   Subaru’s acting context;

-   Tomo’s controlled technological room;

-   Rupa’s restaurant.


Inside the car, no member has complete environmental control.

The road trip therefore functions as a temporary collective household.

The group:

-   argues;

-   sleeps;

-   eats;

-   reveals family history;

-   takes photographs;

-   transports shared equipment.


Before they receive a name, the episode lets them live as a band.

---

## Suwa’s open landscape contrasts with Kawasaki’s compression

Kawasaki has repeatedly been shown through:

-   stations;

-   apartment corridors;

-   bridges;

-   commercial streets;

-   enclosed rehearsal rooms.


Suwa gives the episode:

-   open roads;

-   mountains;

-   lake surfaces;

-   broad skies;

-   fireworks.


Nina’s decision occurs in this expanded environment.

The physical openness does not automatically solve her conflict, but it creates visual space outside the constant pressure of urban judgment.

The fireworks also anticipate stage lighting. Nina watches an immense public display and then returns to create a smaller, human-made eruption onstage.

---

## The service-area photograph creates a family image before the family knows itself

The photograph stages the five girls as a unified group:

-   bodies compressed into one frame;

-   smiles requested;

-   resistance visible;

-   the result preserved anyway.


It resembles an idol photograph, which Tomo rejects, but it also resembles a family photograph.

That is especially important after the episode’s discussions of:

-   Nina’s family;

-   Tomo’s absent parents;

-   Rupa’s deceased family;

-   Momoka’s lost band;

-   Subaru’s inherited family role.


The group image does not replace those histories. It creates an additional structure beside them.

---

## The older venue turns the episode into a meeting between generations

The venue’s dark, worn environment is not a glamorous professional destination. Its value lies in accumulated musical memory.

Mine and Momoka’s history is embedded in:

-   stickers;

-   instruments;

-   nicknames;

-   backstage recognition;

-   old songs;

-   the invitation to share a stage.


The setting shows artistic continuity at a scale smaller than Diamond Dust’s commercial spectacle but longer than Nina’s brief experience.

Mine is evidence that one can remain frightened, imperfect, and active over time.

---

## Momoka is repeatedly framed at thresholds

During her quitting declaration, Momoka often appears:

-   near doorways;

-   turned partly away;

-   at the edge of the rehearsal space;

-   under restrained lighting;

-   between the old band and the new one.


She is once again attempting to cross from participation into absence.

The final performance repositions her fully inside the ensemble frame. The direction does not solve her internal threshold through dialogue; it places her body back among the instruments.

---

## Nina’s speech begins as a solo image and is dismantled into ensemble editing

Nina occupies the central light during the introduction.

As her speech continues, the direction alternates among:

-   the audience;

-   each member;

-   Momoka’s reactions;

-   Mine watching;

-   the dark room around them.


When the music begins, the solitary framing gives way to rapid circulation.

The form itself corrects Nina’s self-narration. No single face can contain the band once the arrangement starts.

---

## Fireworks and stage particles connect private decision to public expression

The fireworks at the lake are distant and untouchable. Nina watches from below.

During the performance, colored lines, sparks, flares, and abstract particles appear around the band. The direction converts external spectacle into subjective musical force.

Nina goes from witnessing light to helping generate it.

The transition gives her decision visual form:

> She will no longer remain only the person moved by someone else’s spectacle. She wants to become one of the people creating the experience.

---

## The final ordinary scene prevents the performance from becoming complete resolution

After the musical climax, the girls return to ordinary space.

Nina announces that she is quitting preparatory school.

The effect is abrupt because the audience has just witnessed a successful emotional performance. The practical consequences arrive immediately afterward.

The series repeatedly insists:

> A song can clarify desire.
> It cannot eliminate rent, education, family, career risk, or future regret.

The performance creates a decision.

The next episodes will have to deal with what that decision costs.

---

# 6\. Series-level lens: revised provisional theses

## 1\. Momoka’s deepest fear is not failure but confirmation

Momoka has survived one professional rupture by telling herself that the conditions were wrong.

A second attempt could produce a much more personal verdict.

This explains why she can:

-   work obsessively on music;

-   recognize talent;

-   produce strong arrangements;

-   enjoy the stage;

-   and still insist that she has no professional future.


She is protecting uncertainty.

---

## 2\. Nina is finally pursuing something she wants, but still proves desire through destruction

Her stage speech contains the clearest positive statement she has made:

> The band is fun.
> She is absorbed in it.

That matters enormously.

Yet her concrete response is to quit preparatory school.

Nina still experiences commitment as requiring the destruction of alternatives. She does not yet know how to choose something without turning the choice into an ultimatum.

---

## 3\. The father’s educational compromise and Diamond Dust’s commercial compromise are parallel dilemmas

Both ask a younger person to:

-   endure a wrong now;

-   trust an institution;

-   accept contamination;

-   preserve future opportunity.


Nina and Momoka reject those bargains.

The series does not simply say they were correct. It asks what happens after refusing the only route that powerful institutions were willing to offer.

---

## 4\. Mine provides the first sustainable adult model of artistic commitment

Momoka has treated adulthood as the knowledge that dreams fail materially.

Mine offers another adulthood:

-   fear remains;

-   the work still causes insomnia and loss of appetite;

-   wise alternatives exist;

-   success never becomes emotionally safe;

-   the stage remains worth choosing.


She does not romanticize suffering. She integrates it into a life that continues.

---

## 5\. The band name gives contradiction legitimacy

Before this episode, contradiction was mostly treated as a personal problem:

-   Nina is timid and arrogant.

-   Subaru is sincere and performative.

-   Momoka is resigned and ambitious.

-   Tomo is desperate for collaborators and rejects them.

-   Rupa is gentle and forceful.


**Togenashi Togeari** makes contradiction their collective identity.

They no longer need to resolve every opposing impulse before acting.

---

## 6\. Tomo and Rupa prevent the band from remaining Nina and Momoka’s private drama

Tomo warns that she will leave if the conflict wastes time.

She interrupts Nina’s stage monologue.

Rupa supports forward movement while maintaining practical stability.

Their presence means that the band cannot indefinitely function as:

> Nina proving Momoka’s past correct.

It now includes people with:

-   separate ambitions;

-   different histories;

-   their own musical contributions;

-   and the right to reject Nina’s narrative.


---

## 7\. Rupa’s calm should not be mistaken for absence of stakes

The episode reveals:

-   catastrophic family loss;

-   significant practical experience;

-   a desire to move forward with music;

-   the ability to manage both Tomo and the wider group.


Rupa’s emotional life is not yet foregrounded, but her stability has a history. She is not simply “the mature one” by nature.

Her calm appears to be a practiced way of continuing after irreversible loss.

---

## 8\. Momoka repeatedly tries to make music carry her farewells

This has now happened twice.

Momoka does not stop playing because she no longer loves music. She tries to perform a final song precisely because she loves it enough to need an ending with meaning.

Nina repeatedly turns those intended farewells into beginnings.

That dynamic is beautiful, but it may also become coercive if Nina never permits Momoka the possibility of choosing withdrawal on her own terms.

---

## 9\. The band’s first public manifesto is interrupted before it becomes ideological doctrine

Nina attempts to explain what everyone believes.

Tomo calls it annoying.

Then the song expresses a more complicated truth:

-   go away;

-   do not leave me;

-   I distrust love;

-   I need to prove it;

-   I want to escape;

-   I continue calling out.


The song is a more accurate manifesto than Nina’s speech because it preserves contradiction.

---

## 10\. Professional commitment is now producing real sacrifices

The members’ educational histories are accumulating:

-   Momoka left school for Diamond Dust.

-   Tomo left because she believed delay was unaffordable.

-   Nina now quits preparatory school.

-   Subaru’s formal training belongs to a career she does not want.


The series is not presenting school withdrawal as a universal mark of authenticity. The different contexts matter.

Nina may be imitating the visible sacrifices of people she admires before fully understanding whether her own situation requires the same action.

---

## 11\. Togenashi Togeari is founded through artistic lineage rather than institutional approval

The group receives:

-   its opportunity through Momoka’s old contact;

-   its model of endurance from Mine;

-   its name from Mine’s clothing;

-   its audience through Mine’s endorsement.


Before an agency recognizes them, another working musician does.

That form of recognition is not enough to guarantee a career. It is enough to make the band’s beginning socially real.

---

## 12\. Nina’s desire to prove the song right is becoming a desire to become the singer who once saved her

Nina says she wants to move people and declare the song’s greatness.

She is no longer satisfied with having been saved.

She wants to occupy the other side of the relationship:

-   become the unknown voice;

-   reach the stranger;

-   leave the claw mark;

-   make someone else continue.


That is the most generative form her fandom has taken so far.

# The characters after Episode 7

## Nina

Nina has made the strongest positive discovery of her life:

> She loves being in this band.

She names it, publicly defines its purpose, performs with the full lineup, and quits preparatory school afterward.

But she remains dangerous to herself and others because she equates seriousness with totality. She has not yet learned that commitment can coexist with contingency, planning, or self-preservation.

Her choice is authentic.

It may also be reckless.

## Momoka

Momoka’s claim that she lacks ambition is now fully exposed as fear.

She does not merely fear another difficult experience. She fears that another failure will make her inadequacy conclusive.

Mine gives the clearest explanation:

> Momoka is afraid because she genuinely loves music.

Nina does not refute the fear. She creates a beginning too emotionally and musically compelling for Momoka to complete her planned farewell.

## Subaru

Subaru continues to function as the group’s social composer.

She:

-   identifies the cold war;

-   understands why direct confrontation fails;

-   creates group games;

-   stages photographs;

-   reads shifts in Momoka’s behavior;

-   maintains collective movement.


She is increasingly the person who helps separate feeling from the most destructive way of expressing it.

## Tomo

Tomo’s history clarifies her urgency.

She left school because she believed she had no reliable family support and no time to waste. Her ambition is not romantic leisure; it is tied to survival and the need to build an adult life quickly.

Her interruption of Nina also establishes that she will not let emotional grandstanding replace work.

She may be abrasive, but she is already protecting the band from becoming ideologically centered upon one member.

## Rupa

Rupa emerges as the practical foundation of the expanded group.

She handles:

-   fans;

-   driving;

-   travel;

-   Tomo;

-   alcohol;

-   logistics;

-   and shifts in group mood.


The brief revelation of her family’s death gives this competence greater gravity. Rupa appears to have learned how to continue without expecting the world to restore what was lost.

## Mine

Mine is the episode’s adult moral center.

She does not tell Nina that dreams always succeed or that fear disappears with experience.

She says:

-   professional love remains frightening;

-   failure remains possible;

-   wise alternatives exist;

-   she chooses the stage because it is where she can speak.


Mine is the person Momoka once needed to see.

In Episode 7, Nina needs to see her too.

## Nina’s sister

The sister occupies an uncomfortable but important position.

She recognizes that their father failed Nina, yet understands the practical logic behind his choices. She is neither a defender of the family system nor a simple ally in Nina’s rebellion.

She asks whether Nina is prepared for the permanent consequences of severing the agreement that brought her to Kawasaki.

The episode does not answer her concerns merely by giving Nina a successful performance.

# Overall assessment

Episode 7 is the point at which *Girls Band Cry* turns the group from a musically promising arrangement into a named collective.

It accomplishes that not by resolving their incompatibilities, but by making incompatibility the name.

Momoka is without ambition and full of ambition.

Nina wants to be left free and desperately needs Momoka to remain.

Tomo wants collaborators and threatens to abandon them.

Subaru performs identities in order to approach the truth.

Rupa appears soft but applies some of the group’s firmest pressure.

They are thornless and thorned.

Mine’s lesson gives the episode its mature emotional center:

> **Fear does not prove that an artistic path is false. Sometimes fear persists precisely because the work remains capable of defining, wounding, and disappointing the person who loves it.**

Momoka tries to avoid a second verdict by declaring the show her last.

Nina answers by giving the group a name and a public beginning.

The final song then expresses what the spoken declaration cannot:

> Go away.
> Do not leave me alone.
> I do not trust love.
> I will continue until I can prove it.

That is Togenashi Togeari’s first true statement.

And Nina’s final decision—to quit preparatory school—makes clear that the name is no longer symbolic play. The band has begun reorganizing the members’ actual lives.
