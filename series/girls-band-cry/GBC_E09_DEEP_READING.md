## Archival source: Assistant Turn 34 — Original Episode 9 deep-dive response


# *Girls Band Cry* Episode 9 — Deep Dive

Episode 8 gave Togenashi Togeari its most dramatic declaration of commitment. Momoka stopped treating the band as a temporary refuge, Nina publicly chose music, and Diamond Dust became a living rival rather than a closed wound.

Episode 9 immediately asks the less glamorous question:

> **Can these people withstand the ordinary experience of being told that they are not good enough?**

Its answer centers on Tomo.

Tomo is not simply the group’s caustic prodigy. She is someone who previously drove collaborators away by demanding that their declared seriousness become visible in their work. After those relationships collapsed, she learned the wrong lesson: not how to criticize better, but how to conceal criticism until someone forces it out of her.

Nina, meanwhile, wants to expand from vocalist into guitarist because she no longer wants merely to depend upon Momoka. She wants to become someone who can carry part of the band’s musical weight. Her intention is sincere. Her playing is not yet close to professional level.

The resulting conflict clarifies the episode’s central proposition:

> **A serious band is not one in which everyone is praised for trying. Nor is it one in which the most talented member’s standards rule without qualification. It is a group capable of hearing “this is bad,” answering “then I’ll improve,” and continuing to work together.**

Episode 8 made Nina refuse to become Momoka’s memory.

Episode 9 makes Tomo discover that telling the truth does not always cause everyone to leave.

---

# 1\. Narrative lens

## The opening shows Tomo choosing dishonesty to preserve the band

The episode begins during rehearsal. Momoka is dissatisfied with the new song and summarizes the problem as:

> 「一言で言うと　ありきたり」
> “In one word: it’s conventional.”

She asks Tomo what she thinks.

Before Tomo answers, the episode flashes through a phone conversation associated with her earlier band. A former collaborator condemns the “teacher’s self-indulgence” and quits; another follows.

Then Tomo gives her present answer:

> 「いいんじゃないの　別に」
> “It’s fine, I guess.”

The line is plainly evasive. Tomo has already heard the same weakness Momoka identified. She had previously implied that something about the song bothered her. But the moment she is asked to commit that criticism to speech, memory intervenes.

The old breakup has taught her:

1.  She hears problems other musicians may not hear.

2.  When she names those problems, her standards become oppressive.

3.  When she presses harder, people withdraw.

4.  Therefore, preserving a group may require pretending not to hear.


This is the episode’s founding contradiction:

> **Tomo’s dishonesty is produced by her commitment to musical honesty.**

She wants the work to improve, but fears that expressing that desire will destroy the relationship required to make the work.

Nina recognizes the lie almost immediately. After rehearsal, she asks why Tomo said nothing when Tomo had previously admitted that the song felt wrong.

Tomo again says that it is fine.

Nina does not yet understand the history beneath that answer, but she accurately detects that Tomo’s social withdrawal is not indifference.

---

## Broken air conditioning forces the band into domestic intimacy

The episode’s comic engine is an escalating heat problem.

Nina’s air conditioner breaks. She invades Tomo and Rupa’s apartment for relief. Their own aging unit is then overloaded, leading the group toward Subaru’s apartment. Momoka eventually arrives after work. Refrigerated drinks, the last train, cards, sleeping arrangements and Tomo’s pet snake transform what was supposed to be a brief visit into a chaotic overnight gathering.

This is the first episode where the expanded five-person lineup spends substantial time together without:

-   performing;

-   negotiating membership;

-   confronting Diamond Dust;

-   or resolving a dramatic career crisis.


They simply inconvenience one another.

That matters because a functional band requires ordinary social endurance:

-   tolerating noise;

-   negotiating personal space;

-   adapting to one another’s habits;

-   sharing food and rooms;

-   seeing embarrassing fears;

-   and remaining present after the exciting musical moment has ended.


Episode 8 established an emotionally monumental bond. Episode 9 tests whether that bond can survive a malfunctioning appliance and a snake.

---

## Nina barges in because practical boundaries still barely exist for her

Nina arrives at Tomo and Rupa’s home with almost no embarrassment:

> 「こんばんは　仁菜でーす」
> “Good evening—it’s Nina!”

Tomo’s immediate answer is essentially:

> “We don’t need anything.”

Nina then steps into the cool air before she has been invited.

This is consistent with her entire relational pattern. Nina has become more capable of understanding emotional context, but she still experiences access to people she cares about as something that can be asserted bodily.

Her reasoning is simple:

-   their air conditioner exists;

-   cool air is not visibly depleted by sharing it;

-   she needs relief;

-   therefore refusal appears arbitrary.


Tomo experiences the same interaction as an invasion of a highly controlled private space.

The episode repeatedly places them in this opposition:

-   Nina moves toward people before obtaining permission.

-   Tomo waits at a distance and hopes people will infer what she wants.


Each finds the other’s method infuriating.

Each also needs something the other possesses.

---

## The sleepover reveals Tomo’s ambivalence toward ordinary friendship

Nina realizes:

> 「みんなでこういうことしたことないなって思って」
> “I realized we’ve never done something like this together.”

She has brought cards and wants the gathering to become a social occasion.

Tomo refuses and withdraws to another room.

At first glance, this looks like straightforward antisocial hostility. But when Rupa follows and calmly agrees to leave her alone, Tomo almost immediately asks why Rupa is not complaining.

That small exchange exposes Tomo’s actual desire.

She does not merely want solitude. She wants someone to challenge the withdrawal without demanding too much from her. She wants evidence that her presence matters enough to provoke pursuit, while retaining the right to reject that pursuit.

This closely resembles Nina’s earlier structure:

> Do not approach me as though you can decide we are friends.
> But if you stop approaching, I will experience that as confirmation that I am unwanted.

Subaru later identifies this explicitly.

Tomo watches from a distance because she fears being disliked. When someone comes close, she attacks.

The “thorned and thornless” structure is not only the band’s name. It is the intimacy pattern of its members.

---

## Momoka’s fear of the snake temporarily reverses the hierarchy

Momoka enters as the group’s older, experienced organizer, then panics over Tomo’s harmless pet snake.

The others are comparatively untroubled. Nina teases Momoka’s reaction as “old-fashioned,” while Momoka insists that four girls being unafraid of a snake is abnormal.

The comedy serves several functions.

First, it removes Momoka from the stable adult role she has occupied. She can:

-   navigate the music industry;

-   book performances;

-   compose and arrange;

-   manage Nina’s volatility;


but a small reptile sends her behind a closed door.

Second, the snake resembles Tomo’s position within the group in a loose but useful way. It is:

-   small;

-   contained;

-   described as calm;

-   not venomous;

-   but immediately interpreted as threatening by someone unfamiliar with it.


Tomo’s speech can bite. The episode gradually shows that the danger lies less in malicious intent than in everyone’s fear of what contact with her severity might do.

The scene should not be reduced to a one-to-one symbol, but the juxtaposition is apt: something perceived as dangerous must be handled rather than simply expelled.

---

## Nina asks Tomo for help because she takes Tomo’s ability seriously

Later, Nina asks Tomo to watch her practice guitar.

Mine has given her an older acoustic instrument and told her to practice if she genuinely wants to play. Tomo immediately notices a problem with the second string and corrects the tuning.

Nina responds with admiration:

> 「さすが智ちゃん」
> “As expected of you, Tomo.”

Tomo tries to minimize the skill:

> 「このくらい　誰だって分かる」
> “Anyone would notice something like this.”

That response is typical of highly trained prodigies. Tomo experiences capacities developed through years of musical education as self-evident. She therefore has difficulty estimating the distance between:

-   what is obvious to her;

-   what is learnable by a committed beginner;

-   and what cannot reasonably be expected yet.


Nina’s request also demonstrates trust. She does not ask Tomo because she expects comfort. She asks because Tomo can hear what is wrong.

That distinction becomes crucial.

---

## Nina’s desire to play guitar is relational rather than merely self-expressive

At the next rehearsal, Nina plays for the group and proposes using guitar during a live performance.

Momoka initially responds positively. A second guitar could thicken the arrangement, and Nina has improved.

Tomo immediately refuses:

> 「その腕でライブなんて　冗談じゃない」
> “Playing live with that level of skill is out of the question.”

Nina asks why she wants to play:

> 「桃香さんに頼るんじゃなく
> 力になって　一緒にやっていきたい」
>
> “I don’t want simply to rely on Momoka. I want to become a source of strength and continue together.”

This is a meaningful continuation of Episode 8.

Nina had been:

-   the fan receiving Momoka’s song;

-   the vocalist supported by Momoka’s guitar;

-   the younger girl repeatedly protected and directed by her.


She now wants to redistribute the relationship. Learning guitar represents a desire to become musically useful rather than only emotionally central.

She also adds that she wants to contribute to everyone, not solely Momoka. The clarification is important. Nina is trying to let the band exceed the Nina–Momoka dyad.

Her motivation is admirable.

Tomo’s technical judgment remains correct.

---

## Tomo turns accurate criticism into personal annihilation

Nina asks Tomo to name what needs improvement.

Tomo initially resists. Nina persists.

Finally Tomo says:

> 「そんなままごとギター
> 一生かかったってまともにならないわよ」
>
> “With that playing-house guitar, you wouldn’t become decent even after a lifetime.”

This is not useful criticism.

Tomo does not identify:

-   timing;

-   fingering;

-   rhythm;

-   posture;

-   chord transitions;

-   tone;

-   or an achievable practice priority.


She converts a present skill deficit into a permanent judgment of the person’s potential.

The phrase **おままごとギター** is especially severe. It characterizes Nina’s playing as a child imitating real musicians, not a beginner undertaking real practice.

Tomo is technically perceptive but pedagogically disastrous.

Her fear of relational damage produces the very form of criticism most likely to cause it. Because she has delayed speaking until she is cornered, everything arrives as accumulated contempt.

---

## Rupa rejects Tomo’s private standard as the universal measure

Rupa intervenes:

> 「智の物差しで話さないでよ」
> “Don’t speak using Tomo’s yardstick.”

She reminds Tomo that someone who won repeated piano competitions from childhood cannot treat her own developmental history as the normal baseline for everyone else.

The metaphor of a **物差し**, ruler or measuring stick, is central.

Tomo’s assessment contains two separate judgments:

1.  Nina is not ready to play guitar professionally onstage.

2.  Nina will never become capable of doing so.


The first is supported.

The second is Tomo extending her personal ruler into a law of human possibility.

Rupa’s objection is not that standards are unfair. She is the person who originally challenged Tomo to take down the Budokan goal if she refused the risk needed to pursue it. Rupa understands ambition.

Her objection is that standards must account for:

-   starting point;

-   time;

-   motivation;

-   role;

-   and the distinction between present inadequacy and permanent incapacity.


---

## Tomo’s harshness repeats the breakup she fears

Rupa’s reaction includes:

> 「やってらんない」
> “I can’t deal with this.”

The expression recalls the old accusation appearing at the episode’s beginning: the former collaborator who could no longer tolerate “Sensei’s self-indulgence.”

Whether Rupa consciously intends the echo or not, Tomo hears the structure she fears:

-   she says what she really thinks;

-   the other person condemns her manner;

-   the group becomes unstable;

-   abandonment approaches.


Tomo’s withdrawal after this point is therefore not self-satisfied. It is frightened.

The episode shows that her cruelty and fear are causally connected without suggesting that fear excuses the cruelty.

---

## The night air changes when artificial control fails

After the argument, Rupa stands with Tomo beside an open window or balcony.

Rupa says:

> 「少し涼しくなってきましたね」
> “It’s becoming a little cooler.”

> 「空気が少しずつ変わるのが
> 肌で感じられます」
>
> “You can feel the air gradually changing against your skin.”

This is a quiet thematic statement.

The entire episode has been organized around artificial climate control:

-   Nina cannot tolerate her hot apartment.

-   The girls invade one another’s cooled spaces.

-   Machines fail under pressure.

-   Everyone tries to control the atmosphere immediately.


The night breeze cannot be forced. The temperature changes gradually and must be felt rather than commanded.

The relational implication is strong:

> Tomo cannot make people improve instantly.
> Nina cannot make Tomo open instantly.
> The group’s atmosphere changes through continued exposure, not perfect control.

Rupa is the member most able to tolerate this graduality.

---

## Subaru recognizes Tomo as another version of Nina

Nina later brings the guitar problem to Subaru.

Subaru does not flatter her. She agrees that Nina is not ready for a professional stage:

> 「一応プロレベル目指してんだから」
> “We are, at least in principle, aiming for a professional level.”

She points out that one or two years may not be enough to close the gap.

Nina objects that Subaru’s drumming has become usable.

Subaru replies that Tomo probably has many criticisms of her as well. The difference is:

> 「ニーナみたいに聞きに行かないからね」
> “I don’t go asking her the way Niina does.”

Subaru then diagnoses Tomo:

> 「智って　たぶん臆病でさ」
> “Tomo is probably timid.”

> 「嫌われたくないから
> いつも離れたところで　こっちを伺ってて」
>
> “She doesn’t want to be disliked, so she stays at a distance and watches us.”

> 「でも　こっちから近づくと…ガーッ」
> “But when we approach her—gaaah!”

Nina recognizes the resemblance:

> 「ちょっと似てるかも」
> “We may be a little alike.”

This is one of Episode 9’s major relational discoveries.

### Nina

-   fears being controlled;

-   attacks approaching intimacy;

-   cannot conceal her disagreement for long;

-   interprets withdrawal as rejection;

-   seeks truth aggressively.


### Tomo

-   fears being disliked;

-   watches intimacy from a distance;

-   conceals disagreement until directly approached;

-   interprets conflict as the beginning of abandonment;

-   releases truth as aggression.


They are not identical. Nina externalizes nearly everything; Tomo compresses it.

But both convert fear of losing people into behavior that makes them difficult to remain near.

---

## Nina detects Tomo’s false encouragement

When Tomo later hears Nina practicing again, she changes her answer:

> 「弾きたければ　ステージで弾けば」
> “Play onstage if you want to.”

Instead of accepting the permission, Nina asks:

> 「どうして嘘つくの」
> “Why are you lying?”

This is an important sign of Nina’s development.

Earlier in the series, she often treated any criticism as total rejection. Here, she recognizes that Tomo’s positive answer is emotionally safer but musically false.

Nina would rather hear:

> You are bad.

than:

> Do what you want.

when the second statement means Tomo has given up believing that criticism can help.

Nina wants evaluation because she wants growth. The praise she seeks is not unconditional reassurance but evidence that effort has produced real change.

This does not make Tomo’s earlier insult appropriate. It establishes that suppressing every negative judgment is not care either.

---

## Rupa reveals the history behind Tomo’s silence

Rupa explains how she met Tomo.

Tomo came to Tokyo, recruited a drummer and guitarist online, and formed a band supposedly aimed at becoming professional. Tomo believed that the others shared her level of commitment. Because she trusted that seriousness, her demands escalated:

> 「これじゃダメだ
> これじゃダメだって」
>
> “This isn’t good enough. This isn’t good enough.”

The group dissolved.

Tomo then claimed she would continue alone, but she was still underage. Rupa became involved as a guarantor and began living with her. They formed **beni-shouga**, attracted professional interest more than once, and repeatedly lost those opportunities through Tomo’s collisions with other people.

Rupa’s conclusion is:

> 「思ったことを口にするのが
> 怖くなってるんだと思います」
>
> “I think she has become afraid to say what she thinks.”

> 「自分の本気をぶつけると
> ダメになっちゃうんじゃないかって」
>
> “She fears that if she throws her seriousness at others, everything will fall apart.”

This gives Tomo’s behavior tragic coherence.

Her deepest artistic virtue—refusal to pretend inferior work is good—became associated with relational catastrophe.

She has not stopped hearing the defects.

She has stopped believing that people can survive being told about them.

---

## Rupa’s relationship with Tomo began as material responsibility

The guarantor detail matters.

Rupa did not merely tell a lonely prodigy that she believed in her. She entered the practical structure of Tomo’s survival:

-   housing;

-   legal or contractual responsibility;

-   daily life;

-   continued musical work.


Rupa’s calm toward Tomo is therefore not abstract patience. It has been tested through repeated failure.

She understands that Tomo:

-   wants an enormous future;

-   is genuinely capable;

-   is socially destructive;

-   fears dependency;

-   and may still require someone willing to remain after saying, “You are wrong.”


Rupa does not cure Tomo by agreeing with her.

She creates situations in which Tomo must act differently.

---

## Momoka confirms that Nina is not ready

Nina asks Momoka directly whether her guitar is good enough to perform together.

Momoka’s reaction makes the answer clear. Nina responds:

> 「ですよね」
> “Right.”

This is another important correction to any interpretation in which Tomo is merely the cruel person obstructing Nina.

Tomo’s delivery is indefensible.

Her underlying musical assessment is shared by the group’s most experienced professional.

Nina’s task is not to prove that Tomo was secretly wrong about her current ability. It is to make the present judgment temporary through work.

---

## Rupa engineers a confrontation at a safe distance

Rupa takes Tomo for a walk.

Tomo insists:

> 「間違ってないから」
> “I wasn’t wrong.”

> 「ダメだからダメって言っただけ」
> “I said it was bad because it was bad.”

> 「あれじゃ通用しないから」
> “That won’t pass at the level we’re aiming for.”

Rupa does not deny the technical point. She asks why Tomo later told Nina that she could play onstage.

Tomo admits that Nina expects encouragement because she wants to perform beside Momoka.

Rupa answers that Nina asked Tomo precisely because she wants to improve.

Tomo generalizes from past experience:

> Everyone says they are serious.
> Everyone says they want to get better.
> What they really want is attention, praise, or the pleasure of singing comfortably.

This is Tomo’s wound becoming a philosophy.

Former collaborators claimed professional ambition but would not accept the demands Tomo associated with it. Tomo therefore distrusts the verbal statement **本気**, seriousness.

Rupa has brought her within hearing distance of Nina practicing alone.

Nina’s struggling fingers become the counterargument.

---

## Rupa defines seriousness as embodied continuation

Rupa says that when she saw the original trio perform, she understood immediately:

> 「この人たち　本気だ」
> “These people are serious.”

Then:

> 「うまさとか　内容とか関係ない」
> “It had nothing to do with skill or content.”

> 「ただ　本気でステージに立ってる」
> “They were simply standing onstage in complete seriousness.”

This should not be read as saying technical skill does not matter. The entire episode demonstrates that professional skill matters greatly.

Rupa is identifying something prior to skill:

> **the willingness to remain exposed to judgment and continue working after judgment arrives.**

That is the trait Tomo actually wants.

She does not need musicians who are already equal to her technical history. She needs people whose ambition will survive the phrase:

> You are bad.

Nina is uniquely suited to answer that challenge.

---

## 「ヘタクソ」 becomes a form of recognition

From a distance, Tomo shouts:

> 「ヘタクソー！」
> “You suck!”

Nina answers:

> 「絶対うまくなってやる！」
> “I’m absolutely going to get good!”

Tomo repeats:

> 「ヘタクソ！」
> “You suck!”

The words have not changed. Their relational function has.

Earlier:

> 「一生かかったってまともにならない」
> “You’ll never become decent.”

closed the future.

Now:

> 「ヘタクソ」
> “You’re bad.”

names the present.

Nina’s answer creates the future herself.

The exchange works because Tomo finally directs criticism at a condition that can be changed, while Nina refuses to interpret criticism as expulsion.

It becomes call-and-response:

-   Tomo recognizes Nina’s deficiency.

-   Nina asserts continued effort.

-   Tomo remains present to witness it.

-   Nina remains present to answer.


Rupa says:

> 「私　こういうの大好きなんです」
> “I love this kind of thing.”

This is the relationship she believed Tomo needed: not people who never resist her standards, but people who throw something back.

---

## The ending returns to the opening question

The episode returns to rehearsal.

Tomo hesitates. The memory of her old band still exists. The possibility of hurting the group has not disappeared.

Then she addresses Momoka:

> 「この曲
> 今のままだとつまんないと思うんだけど」
>
> “I think this song is boring as it is now.”

Momoka answers:

> 「だよな」
> “Yeah.”

Nothing collapses.

No one quits.

The song becomes available for revision.

This is the episode’s real climax—not Nina becoming a competent guitarist, because she has not. Not Tomo becoming kind, because she remains abrasive. Not the band eliminating disagreement, because disagreement is precisely what it needs.

The breakthrough is:

> **Tomo states a negative musical judgment, directs it toward the work, and receives agreement rather than abandonment.**

The circular structure gives the episode its complete meaning.

At the beginning:

-   Tomo hears the problem.

-   She remembers loss.

-   She lies.


At the end:

-   Tomo hears the problem.

-   She speaks.

-   The band continues.

# 2\. Japanese-language lens

## 「ありきたり」 means conventional rather than merely incompetent

Momoka calls the song:

> 「ありきたり」

This suggests:

-   predictable;

-   familiar;

-   lacking distinctive necessity;

-   too close to something already heard.


The song may be structurally competent. Its problem is that it does not yet sound as though only this band could have made it.

That is an increasingly important professional standard. Togenashi Togeari cannot survive solely through emotional sincerity if the music communicates that sincerity through generic forms.

---

## 「センセーの自己満」

The former collaborator’s phrase can be rendered:

> “I can’t keep indulging the teacher’s self-satisfaction.”

Calling Tomo **センセー** is sarcastic. It characterizes her not as a bandmate sharing creative ownership, but as a superior lecturing inferior students.

**自己満** abbreviates **自己満足**, self-satisfaction. The accusation is that Tomo’s standards serve Tomo’s ego rather than the music or group.

The phrase helps explain why Tomo now fears criticism. She does not merely worry that people dislike harshness. She worries that her artistic seriousness is fundamentally narcissistic.

---

## 「別に　いいんじゃないの」

Tomo’s opening answer is extremely noncommittal.

**別に** creates distance: nothing in particular, not especially, whatever.

**いいんじゃないの** is weaker than direct praise. It means roughly:

> “Isn’t it fine?”

The phrasing avoids ownership. Tomo does not say:

> “I think it is good.”

She produces the minimum social answer required to stop the question.

Nina correctly hears the absence behind it.

---

## 「おままごとギター」

**おままごと** means children’s make-believe or playing house.

Calling Nina’s playing **おままごとギター** does more than call it amateurish. It denies that the practice belongs to the same category as real music.

This is especially cruel because Nina is attempting to become a fuller collaborator. Tomo recasts that desire as childish imitation.

The insult reveals Tomo’s inability to distinguish between:

-   respecting professional standards;

-   and withholding legitimacy from anyone who has not already reached them.


---

## 「智の物差し」

Rupa’s phrase literally asks Tomo not to speak using Tomo’s ruler.

A ruler performs two functions:

-   measures distance;

-   establishes a standardized scale.


Tomo’s scale was formed through exceptional early training. Applying it universally causes her to mismeasure others—not because the measurement is wholly inaccurate, but because it ignores where the measurement begins.

---

## 「本気」

The episode repeatedly interrogates **本気**, genuine seriousness.

Nina says she seriously wants to improve.

Former collaborators said they seriously wanted to become professional.

Tomo has repeatedly been disappointed by people whose effort did not match the word.

Rupa therefore moves seriousness from speech into behavior:

-   practicing alone;

-   accepting criticism;

-   returning after humiliation;

-   standing onstage without assurance of praise.


The episode’s position is not that words are worthless. It is that **本気** becomes legible over time.

---

## 「通用しない」

Tomo says Nina’s playing will not **通用する** at the required level.

The word means to pass, function, hold up or be accepted within a sphere.

It is less absolute than her earlier “never become decent.” Properly understood, it means:

> This performance does not currently survive contact with the professional standard we claim to pursue.

That is a legitimate band judgment.

The problem is not the standard. It is Tomo’s tendency to transform a present failure to pass into a permanent identity.

---

## 「ちやほやされたい」

Tomo suspects that people who claim to want improvement really want to be **ちやほやされる**:

-   fussed over;

-   flattered;

-   praised;

-   treated as special.


She also mentions wanting to sing pleasantly or enjoyably.

This echoes Momoka’s earlier attraction to Nina as someone without the “ugliness” of wanting praise.

Both Momoka and Tomo are suspicious of recognition as an artistic motive.

The series is gradually complicating that suspicion. Wanting an audience or affirmation is not necessarily fraudulent. The relevant question is whether the desire can survive criticism, labor and inconvenience.

---

## 「どうして嘘つくの」

Nina’s question to Tomo is striking because it reverses the expected ethical complaint.

Tomo’s permission is kinder on the surface than her condemnation.

Nina calls the kinder answer a lie.

She is not demanding gratuitous cruelty. She is demanding that Tomo remain engaged enough to evaluate her honestly.

For Nina, false approval means:

> You have stopped taking my ambition seriously.

---

## 「ヘタクソ」

**ヘタクソ** is stronger and rougher than simply “not very good.” It can mean lousy, hopelessly unskilled or a terrible performer.

Yet the elongated shouted form across the river becomes almost playful.

Context does not erase the insult. It transforms it into a recognized challenge.

Nina hears:

> You are bad now.

not:

> You do not belong here.

That distinction allows the relationship to continue.

---

## 「今のままだとつまんない」

Tomo’s final criticism is carefully bounded:

-   **この曲** — this song;

-   **今のままだと** — if it remains in its current form;

-   **つまんない** — it is boring.


She does not say:

-   Momoka is boring;

-   the band lacks talent;

-   the song can never work.


The grammar preserves change.

This is the constructive form Tomo’s honesty needs.

---

# 3\. Performance lens

## Tomo’s silence is physically crowded by memory

When Momoka asks Tomo’s opinion in the opening, Tomo’s face becomes still and guarded. The episode overlays the old phone exchange before returning to her present answer.

Her silence is not empty. It is filled with anticipated consequences.

The performance suggests that Tomo experiences criticism as a branching catastrophe:

> Say the truth → become the domineering teacher → someone quits.

Her muted “fine” is therefore an active act of fear.

---

## Domestic comedy makes the band’s bodies ordinary

The episode spends long stretches on:

-   sweat;

-   heat;

-   sprawled bodies;

-   cold drinks;

-   sleeping;

-   bare feet;

-   cramped rooms;

-   air-conditioner controls;

-   the physical handling of the snake.


This matters after Episode 8’s heightened confrontation. The members are not only symbols of artistic integrity or wounded ambition. They are people who become irritable when overheated and sleepy.

The ordinary physicality makes their emerging closeness credible.

A band capable of producing emotional transcendence must still decide who sleeps where.

---

## Tomo isolates herself where she can still be found

When Tomo withdraws during the sleepover, the framing repeatedly places her behind thresholds and in cool blue darkness.

She is removed from the group but not truly unreachable. Rupa can follow. Nina’s noise remains nearby. The group occupies the same apartment.

Tomo’s preferred distance is relationally precise:

> far enough not to be exposed;
> close enough not to be abandoned.

---

## Nina’s fingers become evidence of seriousness

The direction repeatedly emphasizes Nina’s hands:

-   tuning;

-   stiff chord positions;

-   hardened fingertips;

-   pain;

-   fingers refusing to obey;

-   continued repetition.


Nina comments that practicing really does make the fingers harder.

Her body records labor before the sound becomes good.

This contrasts with Tomo’s early-trained ease. Tomo hears the inadequate result. The camera shows the process through which that result may eventually change.

---

## Tomo’s old band appears behind her current judgment

During the harsh rehearsal confrontation, spectral or desaturated images associated with Tomo’s former collaborators crowd the composition.

The present band moment is therefore visually haunted.

Tomo is not only looking at Nina.

She is looking at:

-   the former musicians who claimed seriousness;

-   the arguments she began;

-   the people who left;

-   and the possibility that the same sequence has restarted.


This does not absolve her statement. It explains why the statement arrives with such disproportionate finality.

---

## Subaru interprets Tomo through spatial behavior

Subaru’s description—watching from a distance, then attacking when approached—is reinforced by the staging.

Tomo frequently appears:

-   at the edge of rooms;

-   behind another person;

-   framed through doors;

-   seated apart;

-   looking rather than entering.


Subaru, trained in social performance, reads the blocking as character.

Her caricatured “gaaah” gesture turns Tomo’s hostility into a frightened animal response rather than a sovereign judgment from above.

This allows Nina to recognize kinship instead of only insult.

---

## Rupa narrates painful history without dramatizing herself

Rupa delivers Tomo’s backstory calmly. She does not center:

-   how much responsibility she assumed;

-   how difficult living with Tomo may have been;

-   what she sacrificed;

-   or how many failed opportunities she endured.


That restraint is characteristic but also revealing. Rupa often turns her own emotional investment into practical narration.

She is not outside Tomo’s history. She is the person who stayed through it.

---

## The riverside creates a noninvasive form of confrontation

Rupa and Tomo stand together while Nina practices at a distance.

This is an ideal arrangement for Tomo:

-   she can hear Nina;

-   she does not initially have to face her;

-   the open space reduces claustrophobic social pressure;

-   she can shout rather than formulate an intimate apology;

-   Nina can answer without physically approaching.


Distance, which usually symbolizes alienation, becomes productive.

The episode does not insist that all honest communication must occur face-to-face. It finds a form suited to the people involved.

---

## Nina’s answer transforms humiliation into fuel

When Tomo calls her a lousy player, Nina’s face moves through pain and anger into determination.

She does not deny the assessment.

She does not say:

> I am already good.

She says:

> I will become good.

That response is exactly what Tomo has been unable to believe other people mean when they claim seriousness.

Nina’s defiance is finally directed toward practice rather than moral victory.

---

## The audio contracts around the most vulnerable conversations

The bundled track reflects the episode’s movement.

The overnight comedy is comparatively dense and volatile, around **−20.1 LUFS** with peaks close to full scale and a broad dynamic range.

The quieter interpretive scenes are substantially more restrained:

-   Subaru’s discussion of Tomo: approximately **−23.5 LUFS**;

-   Rupa’s account of Tomo’s past: approximately **−23.6 LUFS**.


The post-critique night and riverside sections have much larger dynamic ranges—roughly **16.9 LU** and **15.2 LU**—because the episode moves between quiet hesitation and sudden emotional calls.

The sonic architecture makes honesty feel difficult to initiate but forceful once released.

# 4\. Music lens

## Episode 9 distinguishes musical taste from actionable criticism

Momoka hears that the new song is conventional.

Tomo hears it too.

The episode’s question is not whether they have taste. It is whether their perception can become collective work.

At the beginning, Tomo’s judgment remains private and therefore useless.

At the end, it becomes a statement Momoka can answer.

Musical collaboration requires not only hearing what is wrong, but speaking in a form that allows revision.

---

## Nina wants instrumental competence as a more equal form of love

Her guitar ambition continues the Episode 8 relationship shift.

Nina no longer wants to be:

-   the listener saved by Momoka;

-   the vocalist carried by Momoka;

-   the person whose purity Momoka protects.


She wants to help make the sound.

The instrument becomes a material answer to:

> 「私はあなたの思い出じゃない」
> “I am not your memory.”

A memory can receive a song repeatedly.

A collaborator must learn how to contribute.

---

## The episode refuses the fantasy that sincerity produces immediate competence

Nina’s reason for learning guitar is emotionally compelling.

It does not make her ready.

This is one of the series’ strongest recurring commitments:

> genuine feeling matters, but genuine feeling does not substitute for craft.

Earlier examples include:

-   Nina injuring her voice by singing with total force;

-   Momoka criticizing an emotionally appropriate line as too obvious;

-   Subaru needing actual drum practice despite her enthusiasm;

-   the group needing arrangement and equipment rather than only conviction.


Episode 9 applies the principle directly to Nina’s hands.

---

## Tomo supplies the critical faculty the band lacked

The five members now contribute different musical functions:

-   Nina: emotional and vocal force;

-   Momoka: songwriting, guitar and professional arrangement experience;

-   Subaru: rhythmic stability and social coordination;

-   Rupa: low-end foundation and mature continuity;

-   Tomo: harmonic architecture, technical hearing and uncompromising evaluation.


Tomo’s role is not merely keyboard.

She is the person most likely to say:

> This does not work yet.

Without that voice, Momoka could remain the sole musical authority, and the others could mistake emotional identification with her songs for adequate creative collaboration.

Tomo creates a second center of musical judgment.

---

## Rupa identifies the prerequisite beneath technical excellence

Rupa does not say skill is irrelevant in the final product.

She says skill did not create her initial conviction that the trio was worth joining.

What she saw was total commitment to the stage.

That gives the band a two-stage standard:

1.  **Seriousness determines whether the work is worth undertaking together.**

2.  **Craft determines whether that seriousness can become durable professional music.**


Nina passes the first test.

She has much further to go on the second.

---

## The riverside exchange turns criticism into rhythm

Tomo’s “You suck” and Nina’s “I’ll get good” form a primitive musical pattern:

-   call;

-   response;

-   repetition;

-   escalation;

-   continued presence.


The content remains conflict. The form creates coordination.

This is a miniature model of Togenashi Togeari’s music:

> different emotional forces do not cease opposing each other; they become rhythmically organized.

---

## The final critique makes Tomo part of the song’s authorship

When Tomo finally tells Momoka the song is boring, she steps beyond instrumental execution.

She claims responsibility for the composition’s quality.

Momoka’s immediate agreement confirms that Tomo’s judgment is not an intrusion. It is a needed creative contribution.

The group is becoming less like:

> Momoka’s songs performed by younger musicians

and more like:

> music produced through five conflicting sensibilities.

---

## The ending theme answers the episode’s dilemma

The ending lyrics return to several core ideas:

-   inability to become someone else;

-   ordinary conversation causing pain;

-   lies crossing between people;

-   being immature and unchanged;

-   wanting to sing together anyway;

-   having been disliked repeatedly;

-   trusting an unmistakable self.


This is almost a direct commentary on Tomo.

She cannot become a socially easy collaborator by imitating someone else. But remaining “herself” cannot mean preserving every destructive habit.

The possibility offered is:

> discard the lies, retain the self, and keep singing with others.

---

# 5\. Formal and directorial lens

## The episode is circular

It begins and ends with rehearsal.

### Opening

-   Momoka asks Tomo’s opinion.

-   Tomo remembers abandonment.

-   Tomo lies.

-   The song remains unresolved.


### Ending

-   Tomo raises the issue herself.

-   She limits the criticism to the current song.

-   Momoka agrees.

-   Revision becomes possible.


The plot between those scenes is the emotional work required to move from one answer to the other.

---

## Climate control is the episode’s governing metaphor

Broken air conditioners repeatedly force the characters out of controlled spaces.

Artificial cooling represents a managed atmosphere:

-   each person in her own room;

-   discomfort suppressed;

-   boundaries maintained;

-   emotional temperature regulated.


When the machines fail, people move into one another’s homes and become impossible to manage cleanly.

Later, Rupa notices the natural air becoming cooler without mechanical intervention.

The contrast suggests:

> a group cannot permanently engineer itself into frictionless comfort; it must learn to endure and perceive gradual changes in atmosphere.

---

## Doors and rooms map Tomo’s relational position

Tomo is repeatedly shown:

-   behind doors;

-   inside separate rooms;

-   at edges;

-   through frames within frames;

-   watching others from a protected interior.


Nina tends to cross these boundaries.

Rupa tends to stand at them.

Subaru interprets them.

This visual grammar distinguishes three forms of care:

-   invasion;

-   patient availability;

-   and social understanding.


The episode does not declare one universally correct. Tomo needs different combinations at different moments.

---

## The snake and terrarium echo controlled vulnerability

Tomo’s pet lives inside a carefully maintained enclosure.

When the air-conditioning crisis disrupts that environment, the snake must be moved and temporarily entrusted to others.

Tomo’s own life has followed a similar pattern:

-   she built a controlled musical environment;

-   the first band collapsed;

-   Rupa assumed responsibility;

-   new collaborators now enter the enclosure.


The parallel should remain suggestive rather than definitive, but the visual repetition of containers, rooms and temporary homes supports it.

---

## Cool interiors give way to warm exterior light

Much of Tomo’s isolation is presented through:

-   deep blue;

-   gray;

-   black;

-   cool apartment light;

-   desaturated memory.


The resolution occurs outdoors in warm late-day light.

This is not simply “warm color equals healing.” The external space allows Tomo’s criticism to travel without the oppressive closeness of an interior confrontation.

The visual warmth accompanies a new possibility:

> honesty that does not immediately terminate belonging.

---

## The former band is visually superimposed upon the present one

During Nina’s guitar conflict, past collaborators appear in desaturated or spectral form around Tomo’s current perception.

The formal choice prevents the viewer from reading her words as a response to Nina alone.

Nina is being judged through an accumulated category:

> another person who says she is serious and may leave when seriousness becomes unpleasant.

The episode’s resolution requires Nina to separate herself from that category through action, not argument.

---

## The final rehearsal is deliberately understated

There is no concert climax.

No crowd validates the group.

No industry figure praises Tomo’s ear.

No polished version of the revised song appears.

The conclusion is a sentence in an ordinary rehearsal room:

> “This is boring as it is.”

> “Yeah.”

That modesty is the point. Professional bands are built as much through such exchanges as through emotionally transcendent performances.

---

# 6\. Series-level lens: revised provisional theses

## 1\. Episode 9 defines the band as a structure capable of surviving negative judgment

Until now, the members have survived:

-   trauma disclosures;

-   quitting threats;

-   family pressure;

-   professional fear;

-   rivalry;

-   public exposure.


Episode 9 introduces a more routine but equally essential danger:

> One member thinks another member’s work is bad.

The band becomes more real when that fact no longer automatically means the relationship is ending.

---

## 2\. Tomo and Nina are parallel sincerity absolutists

Both distrust socially convenient reassurance.

Both are highly sensitive to falsehood.

Both fear being rejected.

Both attack when intimacy exposes them.

But their directions are opposite:

-   Nina forces truth outward.

-   Tomo traps truth inside.


Their relationship may become one of the band’s richest because each possesses the corrective the other needs.

Nina can teach Tomo that people may remain after criticism.

Tomo can teach Nina that conviction does not make unfinished work professionally sufficient.

---

## 3\. Tomo’s standards are both indispensable and dangerous

Without Tomo, the band risks:

-   generic composition;

-   emotional self-indulgence;

-   weak technical judgment;

-   excessive deference to Momoka.


With an unchecked Tomo, it risks:

-   permanent judgments;

-   contempt for beginners;

-   unequal artistic ownership;

-   repeated relational collapse.


Her development cannot consist of becoming less discerning.

It must consist of learning how to make discernment usable.

---

## 4\. Rupa is an active architect of the band

Rupa does not simply absorb conflict.

In Episode 9, she:

-   challenges Tomo’s universalized standards;

-   explains Tomo’s history to the others;

-   creates a safe-distance confrontation;

-   positions Tomo where she can hear Nina practicing;

-   identifies the seriousness connecting the group;

-   and invites Tomo to speak again.


She is not neutral.

She actively engineers a future in which Tomo’s talent can remain connected to people.

---

## 5\. Subaru understands hidden selves through performed behavior

Subaru again sees what direct speakers miss.

She reads Tomo’s:

-   distance;

-   silence;

-   watchfulness;

-   sudden aggression;


as fear rather than simple arrogance.

This does not mean Subaru excuses her. It means Subaru can distinguish the behavior’s social function from its surface appearance.

Her acting background continues to make her an expert on how people reveal themselves while apparently concealing themselves.

---

## 6\. Nina’s guitar marks a move from dependence toward contribution

Nina does not merely want another spotlight.

She wants to help carry Momoka and the band.

That is a meaningful maturation of her love.

But contribution requires accepting:

-   apprenticeship;

-   slowness;

-   role limits;

-   technical humiliation;

-   and the possibility that desire may take years to become useful.


Episode 9 prevents the guitar from becoming an effortless symbol of Nina’s growth.

---

## 7\. Professionalism is being defined as the union of seriousness and craft

Nina has seriousness.

Tomo has craft.

Momoka has experience.

Rupa has continuity.

Subaru has relational and rhythmic adaptability.

The band will fail if any one principle claims complete authority.

-   Seriousness without craft becomes self-indulgence.

-   Craft without relational tolerance becomes domination.

-   experience without renewed risk becomes resignation.

-   adaptability without a chosen position becomes self-erasure.

-   continuity without confrontation becomes stagnation.


The five-member structure is valuable because these weaknesses can potentially correct one another.

---

## 8\. Honest criticism becomes care only when it preserves possibility

Tomo’s first critique says Nina will never become decent.

Her final critique says the song is boring **in its current form**.

That grammatical difference contains the episode’s ethical development.

Good criticism names what is inadequate without declaring the person incapable of change.

---

## 9\. False reassurance can also be abandonment

Tomo believes saying “play if you want” is kinder.

Nina hears it as disengagement.

When someone genuinely asks for help improving, refusing to give an honest judgment can mean:

> I no longer consider your ambition worth investing in.

The episode therefore rejects both extremes:

-   cruelty disguised as rigor;

-   and indifference disguised as kindness.


---

## 10\. The expanded band is beginning to decentralize Momoka

Momoka remains the experienced songwriter and guitarist, but Tomo now challenges her composition directly.

Nina wants to add guitar rather than remain only the voice Momoka supports.

Rupa and Subaru interpret and redirect conflicts Momoka does not control.

This is necessary if Togenashi Togeari is to become a five-person band rather than a vehicle for resolving Momoka’s past through Nina.

---

## 11\. Ordinary cohabitation is becoming a form of band formation

The overnight gathering does not produce a dramatic pact.

It produces:

-   shared cards;

-   teasing;

-   broken appliances;

-   beer;

-   sleeping bodies;

-   pet care;

-   embarrassment;

-   and the discovery that Momoka fears snakes.


These experiences make departure harder in a different way from grand declarations. The members begin accumulating ordinary memory.

A band survives not only because everyone vowed to reach Budokan, but because they have become part of one another’s daily texture.

---

## 12\. Episode 9 revises “conflict is good” into a more demanding principle

Momoka and Tomo both value collision.

But not every collision is productive.

Productive conflict requires:

-   a specific object;

-   a changeable horizon;

-   reciprocal response;

-   continued participation;

-   and enough trust that negative judgment is not identical to exile.


The final exchange possesses all five.

Tomo’s earlier attack on Nina does not.

---

# The characters after Episode 9

## Nina

Nina is beginning to convert emotional dependence into practical contribution.

Her desire to play guitar is sincere and generous. She also badly underestimates the time required to perform at a professional level.

Her major development is not guitar skill. It is her response to criticism.

She is hurt, loses motivation temporarily, seeks other opinions and learns that Tomo’s technical verdict is substantially correct. She then continues practicing.

Nina still reacts intensely, but she no longer needs every painful judgment to be morally illegitimate.

## Tomo

Episode 9 establishes Tomo as a prodigy traumatized by the social consequences of her own standards.

She is not secretly wrong about the music. She is wrong about:

-   how to speak;

-   how quickly others can develop;

-   what criticism entitles her to conclude;

-   and whether everyone who dislikes her method lacks seriousness.


Her shouted “You suck” becomes progress because it leaves Nina a future and because Tomo remains to see what Nina does with it.

Her final criticism of Momoka’s song is the episode’s true act of trust.

## Rupa

Rupa becomes indispensable as Tomo’s longest-standing witness and most skilled challenger.

She knows when to:

-   protect;

-   confront;

-   explain;

-   wait;

-   and arrange an encounter rather than dictate an emotional conclusion.


Her history with Tomo includes concrete responsibility, not merely fondness. She helped make Tomo’s continued life and music materially possible.

## Subaru

Subaru refuses both empty encouragement and Tomo’s annihilating language.

She agrees that Nina is not ready while helping Nina understand why Tomo behaves as she does.

She is increasingly able to translate hostile behavior into relational knowledge without denying the harm it causes.

## Momoka

Momoka’s role changes subtly.

She confirms the professional standard Nina has not yet reached, but she also accepts Tomo’s critique of her own composition.

That acceptance matters after Diamond Dust. Momoka is no longer the isolated artistic conscience who must either control the music or leave. She is becoming capable of allowing another strong musical intelligence to alter her work.

---

# Overall assessment

Episode 9 may appear lighter than Episode 8 because it revolves around air conditioners, a snake and beginner guitar practice. Structurally, however, it performs essential work.

Episode 8 proved that the members want a future together.

Episode 9 asks whether their methods of pursuing that future are compatible.

Tomo’s past tells her that musical seriousness destroys groups. Nina’s response gives her another possibility:

> You can tell me I am bad.
> I can hate hearing it.
> I can continue anyway.
> You can remain to judge what happens next.

The episode does not domesticate Tomo into a gentler archetype. It finds a relationship capable of answering her severity.

Likewise, it does not reward Nina’s sincerity by instantly making her talented. It forces her to accept the humiliating duration between wanting to contribute and becoming capable of contributing.

The final rehearsal supplies the decisive proof:

> Tomo says the song is boring.
> Momoka agrees.
> Nobody leaves.

That small exchange is one of Togenashi Togeari’s most important achievements so far.

> **Episode 9 defines a band not as people who never wound one another, but as people who learn how to make a wound specific, answerable and survivable—so that “you are not good enough yet” can become the beginning of work rather than the end of belonging.**
