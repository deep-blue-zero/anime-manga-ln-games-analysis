# Volume 11 — the same cards

Volume 11 takes the heroine abroad. She skates first of thirty in Bangkok, lands the jump that has failed her for a year, earns the top level for her steps again, and sits in the leader's chair for the first time in her life. Then the reigning champion of the series skates the same three jumps and beats her by eight points. The free skate is not shown. A chapter opens on the result: fourth, again.

The volume's subject is the difference between holding a card and having trained it. Junior rules give nearly every strong skater the same short program. The quadruple she learned as a novice cannot be used there, and in the free skate the step sequence that made her name has no slot. What separates skaters is execution, and behind execution the conditions under which a body is kept and a program rehearsed. So the volume spends its length on conditions: a trainer who tapes her knees and rations her jumps, a coach who learns to tell when her assent is empty, a rink that five clubs share, and an Olympic champion who offers to take both of them to Tokyo.

It also gives the heroine's own account of what has been driving her, and it is not the one the adults proposed in Volume 10. Floating in a hotel pool she tells an older skater that she is ashamed to live in a world from which hardship has been cleared away for her, and that she fears she is taking things from her coach that she does not even know about. The older skater, whose parents are cruel to her and pay her fees, answers that the capacity to comfort someone comes from having been treated kindly. A head coach tells her much the same over tea. The reading takes these as the volume's correction of the last one's hypothesis.

The closing pages move the rival. Hikaru tells the man who has housed and fronted for her that she is leaving his club for Tokyo. In the chapter in which the heroine's coach refuses to move her merely to prove that she is serious, the other side moves.

## Developments in disclosure order

**Score 41** begins by settling a question the last volume left loose. The two girls are in the same school year, but the skating year turns in July; Inori was born in April and Hikaru in January, so Inori is a first-year junior while Hikaru is in her second year as a novice. They will not meet in the same class this season. (`MD-EV-V11-0002`.)

Over lunch Tsukasa explains how six finalists are chosen from more than a hundred entrants: places in two events convert to points, fifteen for a win, and the route to the final is two golds or a gold and a silver. Kōhei protests that the final is too high an aim for a first season. Inori corrects him. The aim is to win it. Tsukasa says his part is to get her to the goal she has chosen. Kōhei gives way on the goal and holds on the method. After his last pupil's collapse he thinks even his cautious layout was not cautious enough; he wants to know by what conditions a layout will be judged ready. Tsukasa proposes that they work those out together. Then the assignment arrives: the first event, in Thailand, with Iruka, who won the series last year. (`0003`.)

Two weeks before the event she is on a bench with her knees wrapped. He sends her home to rest. She has noticed that he looks worn, carries difficult books and asks strange questions in lessons about what she feels just before a movement. She concludes that he is working on something for her and that she must not get in his way. (`0004`.)

A signboard falls in the wind and is caught above her head by a tall woman with a braid. That evening Hitomi introduces her: Kanayumi Miho, a medical trainer and licensed judo therapist, who will attend the club's night sessions twice a week. She weeps with relief that the girl is unhurt. She knows her, it turns out, from Kyoto. (`0005`.)

Her account goes back to Volume 3. At the West Japan meet she saw the girl dancing in a car park, noticed that she was landing so as to spare the arch of her foot, and recognized the early signs of shin splints. She went to tell her. A small boy swinging a flask by its strap caught her in the face, and she woke in hospital. One of the later skaters, she heard, had fallen and hurt her leg. She has dreamed about it since. (`0006`.)

So when a coach turned up at her club in Fukuoka and proved to be that girl's teacher, she was moved, and he was certain he had found the right person. She refused: she was that club's trainer and wanted to see its children through. He went down on his knees. She threw him, and told him a prostration burdens the person it is aimed at. He came back the next day, and the next. She relented when her own feeling changed. (`0007`.)

Her first advice is unwelcome. Taping is not treatment; treatment is rest; with a competition close, the number of jumps should be limited. Inori's answer is that she is already doing very little. Hitomi tells her something to steady her: Hikaru, too, is resting, with a sore back, and will use her seeded entry to skip the block competition. It does not steady her. If she cannot practise, how is she to make an effort? She is, she says, not working hard at all. (`0008`.)

Tsukasa is appalled and tells her how hard she works, and pours paper medals over her. She thanks him flatly. Kanayumi says, as an outsider, that her effort cannot be called insufficient, and that the pain in her legs is itself the evidence of how much she has practised. Inori lights up. He is baffled: he said the same thing. Hitomi explains that inside the girl's head he has become the person who praises everything. (`0009`.)

He then says something that does reach her. Cutting practice is not standing still. Effort is not only long hours and a body worked hard. Making practice efficient, finding ways not to tire, and the patience to wait for an injury to heal are effort too. Reducing needless sacrifice is effort. The trainer adds a maxim from archery about treating each arrow as the only one, and points out that a junior short program allows three jumps. Inori is given four attempts a day at a new combination, and instead of repeating a failure at once she stops to work out its cause. Her caption is that she is going to face a body she does not know. (`0010`.)

The combination, a triple flip and triple toe loop, is landed three times in three under the test Kōhei brought from Niigata. He consents to the goal, and wonders privately whether she always grew at this rate. (`0011`.)

**Score 42** is the journey. Four skaters are sent, two girls and two boys. Tsukasa has never flown. Gori's wife travels as Iruka's chaperone, and the trainer travels with Inori. The chapter's doctrine is that the competition begins at boarding and that the real barrier abroad is not language but everything to do with the body: food, water, plumbing, sleep. (`0012`.)

One of the boys, a pupil of Riley Fox named Hiyodori Suzaku, cannot bear the smell of the food court and berates himself for it. Inori gives him the sweets she had packed against the same trouble. (`0013`.)

Iruka pulls her to another table. Inori asks her how she thinks the event can be won, and when Iruka objects to being asked by a rival, explains that adults leave out the unkind things and she wants to hear them. Iruka's reply is a tease with an edge: the girl has a complex about being treasured. Then she gives one piece of advice. In the junior short program, holding the strongest card does not win. (`0014`.)

The rink is on the fifth floor of a shopping mall. Iruka's practice jump goes over Inori's head and the foreign skaters at the boards gasp. The other boy, Kamomeda Hayato, has not slept for excitement. Inori sleeps soundly, and her coach concludes that every problem he had worried about is solved. She then draws first in the starting order. (`0015`–`0016`.)

He recasts it at once: skating first abroad is a rare experience, and she may have to do it at the Olympics one day. In the warm-up a Thai girl of her own age, frightened by the size and speed of the older skaters, watches the small Japanese skater cut through them and jump, and decides not to lose. At the boards Inori does the trainer's eye exercises and follows her coach's instruction to breathe until the air inside her is the air of this hall. (`0017`–`0018`.)

**Score 43** is her short program. There are nine judges, about twice as many as she has faced, and one of them is Japanese. His thought sets the terms: for outsiders with no national medal, the only means of making themselves known is skating skill. The music is from a film about a timid boy who trains with his own double, risen from a lake. (`0019`.)

The combination is landed with its second jump a little short. The Lutz is landed from a held outside edge. A flying sit spin with a difficult variation draws applause. The step sequence fills its checklist and is called at the top level; her own thought is that a clean twizzle is the pride of an ice dancer's pupil. A double Axel comes late, out of a turn, with a twizzle straight after it. Two more spins at the top level. (`0020`–`0021`.)

The commentators know who she is: the girl who landed two quadruples at home and came fourth. They infer how deep the Japanese field must be. Her score is 65.28, against a target of something over 65. She has to ask whether that is good. (`0022`.)

She sits at the top of a pyramid of chairs and vows to stay there. Then she remembers Iruka's remark and asks what it meant. Her coach's face goes oddly calm. He explains, and lets the next skaters demonstrate. Under this season's rules the short program must contain a solo triple Lutz and a solo double Axel, the triple Axel is not permitted, and the combination cannot reuse the solo jump. Skaters with all five triples therefore arrive at the same three passes. The Mongolian skater has them, and a stage presence that makes Inori judge her own performance a school recital. She falls and scores 55.21. A French skater falls and scores 54.72. Rare cards mean nothing here; what wins is the card that has been trained. As he says it she realizes that he has told her all of this before, more than once, since February, and that it never went in. She reads his calm as the face of a man who knows he was not heard. (`0023`–`0024`.)

She apologizes. He tells her that noticing one has not understood, and asking, are both acts of growth, and that teaching is his job. Privately he worries that his disappointment showed. (`0025`.)

Twenty-nine skaters remain. She discovers that being chased is worse than chasing, and that without confidence in her own result she is close to wishing the others would fail. She stops herself with a thought of Hikaru, who she is sure would never think it, and concludes in tears that she had imagined herself Hikaru's equal while knowing nothing of this world. Iruka, passing in costume, tilts her chin up: one cannot see the ice looking down. (`0025`–`0026`.)

**Score 44** is Iruka's program and the night after it. Her music is from a circus show about a mermaid driven into the deep and reborn as a witch. Her Lutz, off a spread eagle, is high enough that a spectator thinks it will clear the boards. Her double Axel lands into a butterfly. Tsukasa's eye goes to her neck. Skaters fix the head, the heaviest part of the body, to keep their balance; she swings it freely, which means her field of view must be streaming, and still her tracings do not slip. (`0027`.)

The page then goes inside her. She shuffles the world on the ice and finds herself by lines of light. Under the words are images of a dark, cluttered home, and then a memory: an older girl telling a small child that she has a talent for skating, that she should aim at the Olympics, and that they will skate together every day. Then the same child alone on an empty rink, waiting. She no longer needs that, she thinks. The ice gave her speed and height in its place. (`0028`.)

She puts her combination last, for the bonus, and adds a flourish after it to outdo the one Inori showed. Her coach, at the boards, sees that she changed it herself. A commentator calls her a model of what the phrase for execution marks means. Inori leaps up cheering. Hearing her name shouted, Iruka thinks that she may not need to be angry any longer; as the people she holds dear increase, the one who left stops being the special one. 73.83. (`0029`–`0030`.)

Tsukasa's assessment is plain. His pupil had this season's advantages, a light body and freshness. Against Iruka there is a large difference in stamina, technique and carriage. (`0030`.)

In her hotel room, with the door open and the trainer present, he teaches her to read a protocol. One judge gave her double Axel a four; the highest and lowest marks are discarded, so it does not count, but someone saw it. She calls herself lucky, and the weakest of those who compete on quality. Asked how a passage of Iruka's went, he stands up and performs it. She says that it takes her more than a week to learn choreography. His unspoken reply is that this is exactly what he wants to give her and does not yet know how. Then he hears her say yes and recognizes the voice: it is the yes that means nothing has gone in. He ends the session. (`0031`–`0032`.)

In the corridor the trainer asks his leave. She has observed that his policy is never to apply the brake, and has kept her own counsel so as not to confuse the athlete; may she caution the girl when she hurries for results? He is glad to be asked. He wants to be the adult who always stays beside her wish to be first, he says; he has only decided to look for the best method, and is not attached to speed; he wants her to learn that there is more than one way to approach a contest. (`0033`.)

Alone on the balcony she thinks of a lake at night. The lack of results is not what hurts, she thinks. What she hates is that while she cannot do a thing, she may be making him do something. (`0034`.)

Below, Iruka is swimming. Inori jumps in, finds it deeper than she thought, and is hauled out. They float. Iruka notices that the girl's mother packed her school swimsuit for her, and remarks that she is loved. Inori says that Iruka put into words something she had left vague. She can skate; she was chosen; her teachers and her family are all kind. A self that lives in a world from which unhappiness has been removed seems to her a shameful thing, and she feels she is hurting someone by it. She is probably taking a great deal from her coach without knowing. She can fight only in a place where she could not stand alone, and wonders if that is cheating. She sometimes wants to be someone who knows what it is to have no help. (`0035`.)

Iruka's answer is about who people are actually angry at: those who take advantage for merit, who pretend to know hardship, or who throw away what they have. Then she says what she has not said to anyone on the page. Her parents call her ugly and tell her to die, and consider that paying for her skating cancels it. She cannot refuse the money. Her coach spent years putting distance and arrangements in place until she could skate without being hurt. So the girl need not feel guilty. If she wants to be convinced, she should learn with what feelings the adults are protecting them. Inori takes her hand. Iruka, crying, tells her that knowing to do that is something she has because she was treated kindly. Then she tells her to jump a good quadruple tomorrow. (`0036`.)

**Score 45** does not show tomorrow. It opens years earlier, on a sixteen-year-old American landing a quadruple flip to win the Olympics and announcing at her press conference that she is retiring to coach in Japan. Riley Fox, in the present, reflects on the rule that made it possible: after the Games at which Yodaka retired the senior age limit was lowered to fifteen, and it has since gone back to seventeen. Iruka, born in August, is in her last junior season; under the old rule she could have gone to last year's Olympics. Then a caption gives the women's result. Iruka first, an American second, a French skater third. Inori fourth. (`0038`.)

The Thai girl comes to find her. She says that Inori's skate gave her the nerve to show everything she had, and asks to be friends. Her nickname means shrimp. So, Inori admits, does hers. (`0039`.)

The trainer prescribes a day of markets and presents. Iruka observes that the girl is taking this very differently from the last time someone beat her. Inori agrees. She is angry enough to want to rage, and it would only trouble people. But this time she can see what she lacked, so it feels as if it could not be helped. The All-Japan Novice was different: she was fully prepared and gave everything, and that is why it is still unbearable. Iruka says that most juniors learn to hold something back, and that this one competes at full strength. She also buys a present for her own mother, who cheered for her yesterday for the first time. Inori's father has joined the party. (`0040`.)

After dinner Riley Fox invites Tsukasa to her room, with Jakuzure for propriety. Among bouquets and attendants she makes an offer: a post as assistant coach at her club in Tokyo, with Inori to come too. She gives her reasons. He adapts fast, learns fast, and praises in a way she finds original and would like to receive herself; she sensed at the camp that he was her own kind. Jakuzure, watching, cannot tell how much is artlessness and how much design. Tsukasa says nothing to the offer and asks what she noticed about his pupil. (`0042`.)

Through her the free skate is reported. The plan had been one quadruple, to spare the knees, with the newly stable Lutz to carry the execution marks. The quadruple Salchow was landed. The Lutz was a fall. The flip in a combination was called for its edge. And the choreographic sequence, which in junior free skating replaces the step sequence and has no level, scored poorly; her movement had gone stiff. He names the cause himself and she agrees: too little practice with music across a whole rink, because in Nagoya several clubs share the ice. Then the answer is simple, she says. Come to us. Her club has its own rink, with ice reserved for competitors, trainers and ballet and classes in house, and lower costs overall. Lux has a second coach now. If he truly puts the girl first, is the answer not obvious? (`0043`–`0044`.)

In the hotel laundry at the same hour her pupil is saying it to Inori. Everyone who is serious has moved house and changed schools for skating. She is serious, isn't she? (`0044`.)

Jakuzure cannot fault the case. Then Riley, meaning to be gracious about Lux, calls fourth place at a first event a wonderful result, and Tsukasa breaks. He does not think it wonderful. She herself won both her events and the final in her first season, and his pupil is that kind of skater; she had a real chance. He had been holding in his own disappointment because the girl was holding in hers for everyone's sake. This is the second time he has failed to get her to first. The Olympic champion goes down on her knees to apologize for speaking as someone who expects too little of others. (`0045`.)

When the offer is restated sincerely he answers it. He cannot reply without the girl and her family. A move to Tokyo would change a great many things in her life; inside the skating world relocating looks like earnest effort, but a child who does not settle carries a heavy load, and the image beside his words is of a schoolgirl alone among chattering classmates. The diagnosis is still a guess. If a change of environment were certain to make her stronger he would make it. He will not have her pay a single needless sacrifice whose only function is to prove that she is serious. Downstairs she is telling the boy that she is glad to be asked, because it means she has become strong, and that she will decide a turning in her life only when she is convinced, by her own strongest feeling. ## What the junior class takes away

`MD-OQ-V10-02` asked what her first events abroad would show. The answer has a structure the series has not used before: her distinctive assets are removed by rule, one from each segment.

In the short program the quadruple is barred, the Lutz she skipped is compulsory, and so is a double Axel; a triple would be a violation. The result is that the field skates one layout. Volume 6 won a regional title on a strategy of added marks that was unusual among novices. Here the same strategy is everyone's. In the free skate the loss is quieter and worse. The step sequence, in which she has twice done what no novice had done in eleven years, is not an element. Its replacement has no level. A skater cannot be better at it by rule, only by effect.

Her short program was nonetheless a success by any measure the page offers. She landed the Lutz in competition for the first time, repeated the top level for her steps before a foreign panel, and drew a top mark from one judge for a double Axel. The combination's second jump was called a quarter short. Her score met the target.

What the eight-point gap to Iruka measures is therefore not content. Iruka's three passes are the same. Hers are higher, come out of harder entries, are placed for the bonus and are followed by ornament; her spins bend further; and she carries herself through all of it with a freedom her coach's rival can describe anatomically. `MD-CL-0008@10` records the conclusion. The foundation that the heroine's coach built is still what distinguishes her, and it is now a necessary condition and no longer a sufficient one.

The free skate is given only as a list by someone with no reason to soften it. One quadruple, by plan, landed. The Lutz, on which the plan leaned, fallen. The flip's edge called, which is a new weakness on the record, anticipated by a mark on her novice protocol. And the sequence that should have suited her, stiff. She is fourth by a margin the page does not print.

## A rink that five clubs share

The volume offers three explanations of why she does not yet skate as well as she can, and the third is new.

Volume 10's adults looked inside her: a child who watches faces, a concentration that pares her down. This volume's first chapters look at her body and its management. Its last chapter looks at the ice. A choreographic sequence is skated across a whole rink to music, and a club that shares its rink can rarely rehearse one that way. Both coaches reach this independently, and one of them owns a rink.

That is what makes the offer serious. Riley Fox is not flattering him. Her club has competitor-only ice, in-house trainers, ballet and classes, and by her account lower total costs. Jakuzure, who came to defend Lux, can find nothing to say against it, since his own club advertises the same strengths. The patron economy that carried the pair through Volumes 1 to 8 and the earned economy that designated her in Volume 9 are joined here by a third: recruitment by a rich club that wants the coach as much as the skater.

His answer keeps to the method he has used at every decision since Volume 5, and adds a ground from Volume 9. He will not answer for her. He counts her life outside skating as a cost, and the picture that accompanies the thought is the isolated schoolchild of the first volume. He treats the diagnosis as unproven. And he refuses the argument that relocation is what serious people do. Under the bridge he called sacrifice the self-harm of someone who feels inferior. Here the same doctrine governs an institutional choice: no payment whose only function is to demonstrate commitment.

He does not refuse on loyalty, and says he would move if it were certain to help. His positive case for Lux is that a small club gives one child several adults' attention, and that it has done the hard thing once already. `MD-CL-0007@10` treats the offer as open.

Downstairs, without consulting him, she gives an answer of the same shape. `MD-CL-0027` has insisted on what the two have not said to each other. This is a counter-instance worth marking. On a question neither has discussed, each defers to her judgment, and she names him as the source of the habit.

## Who is allowed to say no

Volume 10 found the heroine's coach unable to make his words arrive. Volume 11 shows him learning why, and rearranging the adults round her.

He learns two things about her. Her assent can be empty; after three years he knows the sound of it, and when he hears it he stops instead of repeating. And his praise has lost its value through abundance. The proof is brutal and comic. The trainer says exactly what he said and the girl glows. Hitomi tells him what he has become to her.

He learns one thing about himself from the trainer, who has watched him and concluded that his policy is never to brake. He does not dispute it. He tells her what he wants to be: the adult who stays beside the girl's wish to be first. Having chosen that, he lets others say what he will not. Kōhei is the brake on layouts and asks for conditions of success. The trainer is the brake on pace and may tell her to slow down. Hitomi, in the side story, is the adult to whom she can be a child. The criterion from Volume 5, that he never overrules her stated wish, is unbroken, and this volume shows it as a deliberate division of labour.

It works where it is tried. The new combination is admitted on a test with a cautious examiner. The jump quota produces, in her own caption, the resolve to face a body she does not know, and the habit of stopping after a failure to think. That is the first sign of the thing he resolved in Volume 10 to give her, and it comes from the trainer's rationing more than from his questions. His own project is stalled by his own account: he shows her what he can do with a single viewing, wants to hand it over, and does not know how.

His outburst at Riley Fox shows what his refusal to brake rests on. He does not think fourth place is a good result for her. He measures her against an Olympic champion's first season. He says, of himself, that this is the second time. `MD-CL-0010@11` records that on this point there is no distance between them, and that he sees her composure for what it is.

## What she says she is afraid of

`MD-CL-0028@1` registered, at low confidence, the hypothesis of two adults that her gifts were formed by lonely years. This volume gives her own account of her present state three times, and none of them is that.

On the balcony she separates two things. Slow results do not hurt in themselves. What she cannot bear is that her not being able may be costing him. In the pool she generalizes it. She is ashamed of a life from which hardship has been removed; she is afraid of what she takes; she suspects that fighting only where she cannot stand alone is a kind of cheating; being protected is painful. In the side story she hides her homesickness from him, and her wish, months earlier, was to stay out of his way.

So the mechanism on the page for her haste, her concealment and her deafness to his praise is guilt at being cared for. That is close to the reverse of deprivation. It is continuous with Volume 9, where she believed she had ended his dream, and with Volume 10's creed that she is on the ice by someone's belief and must keep proving she belongs. `MD-CL-0029@1` registers it on the strength of her own words. `MD-CL-0028` is downgraded: nothing here disproves what two men guessed about her childhood, but the volume supplies a better-attested account of her conduct now, and two speakers who contradict the hypothesis's premise.

Those two are Iruka and Hitomi, and they say the same thing from opposite positions. Iruka, who was not treated kindly at home, tells her that her readiness to take a hurt person's hand is the product of kindness received. Hitomi tells her that adulthood is built from memories of having been cherished and that one may go back for more. Neither says she must earn it. Against her creed that her place must be proved, and against the adults' theory that hardship made her, the volume sets the claim that care did.

She has an answer to her question about what the fortunate owe, too, and it is not gold. It is knowledge: not to mistake advantage for merit, not to pretend to hardship, not to throw away what one has, and to learn what the adults who protect her feel.

Two further things qualify the darker readings. Her account of the two defeats is exact. A loss in which she can see what she lacked she can absorb, and does, in a day. The loss at full strength is the one she cannot forgive, ten months on. And in the leader's chair she catches in herself the wish that others would fail, and refuses it.

## Iruka

The series has not given a supporting skater this much since Volume 8, and the profile page and two scenes make her the volume's second centre.

Her skating began as cover for a lie her mother had told. A girl at the rink told her she had talent and promised to be there every day, and then was not. Her parents made trouble at one club; she was abandoned at a shopping centre, walked into the rink next door, and was taken in by a man who was there to discuss founding a club. She became its first pupil. He and his wife have since built the distance that lets her skate without being hurt. She wears a necklace bought with her own wages, as a promise to herself to skate one day without her parents' money.

Read with this, Volume 10's rudeness about a sister who was called a genius, and this volume's remark about a complex over being treasured, are the observations of someone who knows precisely what being treasured is worth. Her program is the story of a mermaid made a witch. Her advice is unsentimental. And in the middle of the best skate of the volume she decides that she may stop being angry at the girl who left.

She is also a case for the series' concern with windows of age. She was born in August; a rule that was changed and changed back kept her out of last year's Olympics; this is her last season as a junior. The skater who benefited from the rule's earlier form is the woman now offering Tsukasa a job.

`MD-CL-0022@7` carries this. It also notes that the pattern of careers oriented by someone who stopped, registered at Volume 10, has its strongest first-person instance here, and its first sign of release.

## Hikaru goes to Tokyo

Three facts are on the page. She rested for her back and has returned to full training. The man who shelters her has collapsed and blames himself. She has told him, alone and without his son present, that she is leaving his club, and the place is Tokyo.

Two more are juxtaposed and not stated. Yodaka is at Riley Fox's door. Riley, who has just failed to recruit one coach and pupil, laughs that everything she wants comes to her. The inference that Hikaru is to train at Riley's club is strong, and the reading holds it as an inference until a page says so.

What it would mean is clear either way. In Volume 10 a former coach said that Yodaka changed coach every year and that the talent needed no adult though the child did, and an old teacher asked whether he was making her repeat his path. Sonidori said he could not hold her if she chose to go. Each of those is now enacted. `MD-CL-0016@10` and `MD-CL-0003@10` record it. Whether she leaves his household as well as his club is not said, though the last panel's uniform suggests a new school.

For the contest between the two coaches the timing is pointed. One refuses to relocate a child as proof of seriousness. The other relocates his. `MD-CL-0025@6` notes that the opposition of Volume 9 has acquired an institutional form, and that the woman who told Tsukasa he was her own kind appears to have acquired Yodaka's pupil.

## Written Japanese

The first chapter's title joins 騎士, a knight, with 柔, the first character of judo, read here as softness; the trainer is both. Her maxim is from archery and concerns a single arrow.

Tsukasa's sentence about effort turns on 犠牲, sacrifice, qualified as needless, and 努力, effort; in Volume 9 the first word was paired with self-harm. His phrase to Riley Fox for a pointless payment is a sports idiom for a swing that connects with nothing, and what it would prove is 本気, being in earnest, the word her pupil uses to Inori.

The chapter in the chair is titled for 一番上, the very top. Yō's fan word for a name that stays first is a pickling stone. The rule lesson uses the vocabulary of card games, with a rarity grade for cards that may not be played, and returns to the first volume's たい焼き for a plan built on quality.

Iruka's chapter is 傷と鰭, a wound and a fin. Her word for what Inori has a complex about is 大事にされる, to be treasured or handled with care; Hitomi's sentence in the side story uses the same verb for what a child needs in order to grow up. Inori's word for herself in the pool is ずるい, unfair or sly, and for her situation 恥ずかしい, shameful.

The last chapter is 善意の策動, a manoeuvre made in good will. The Thai girl's nickname and Inori's are both shrimp.

None of this establishes how any line sounds.
