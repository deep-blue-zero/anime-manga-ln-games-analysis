# Volume 5 — the wager

Volume 4 ended with entry to the qualifier fixed. Volume 5 is the qualifier, and its protagonist does not skate in it. Fifteen girls contend for five places at the All-Japan Novice. Thirteen have skated when the volume stops. Inori is fourteenth. What the volume does with the pages this frees is its subject.

It gives them to everyone else. The girl who drew first and cried about it, the girl who moved her combinations to the end of the program against her coach's nerves, the girl driven for hours from a prefecture with no rink, last year's champion, and above all a sixth-grader named Yagi Yūna, each get an interior and a stake. The one skater who is not there shapes all of them. Hikaru has been seeded past this event and is in St Petersburg. The field calls itself her generation, and treats her absence as the one chance it will get.

A rink clerk supplies the volume's thesis to a stranger in the stands: on ice there is no such thing as certain success, and what a spectator watches is a wager. The reading's argument is that the volume then tests that sentence from three sides. It shows what wagering does to children and what their coaches do about it. It lets a rival state, more sharply than anyone has, the case against the story's own heroine: a late starter has hope in front of her, and the child who started early and was overtaken has only the gap. And it ends with Tsukasa telling his pupil, after the leader has scored above last year's winning mark, that today he will make her a gold medalist. Whether that sentence is a wager or a promise is the question the volume leaves, and this reading does not settle it.

## Developments in disclosure order

**The prologue** is in a girl's first person over images of many skaters and of Inori among them. Others are dazzling and out of reach: a sense she lacks, technique piled up, each one's dream beautiful. Her own talent looked tiny. How can she shine? Will she go on waiting for someone to light her? The second page answers: among all these glittering stars she will prove that her own light shines brightest. Six girls hold cards marked with jump abbreviations; hers reads S. (`MD-EV-V05-0001`.)

**Score 16** opens a month after the test, on the day. Her mother brings her; her father is there too, for the first time on the page, and comically orders the coach to make her win. Tsukasa tells Hitomi that being here is a feat for Inori and the bare minimum for the strong clubs. Fifteen entrants, five places. He says the coach's ability is what will be tested from here. Hitomi thinks it hard to believe this is the man who did not want to coach. Coaches, a caption notes, do not attend opening ceremonies. (`0003`.)

So Inori is alone. At earlier meets clubmates sat with her. She does not know where to sit, sees a cluster of Novice A girls who all know one another, and resolves not to be timid, "like Tsukasa-sensei". A caption labels what follows: possessed by an imaginary Tsukasa. She announces herself at a shout, compliments a ribbon, flexes. A second caption gives her inner model of him: an energetic, muscular big brother who praises even small things. Elsewhere her coach feels a presence like his own. The girls are kind. They ask whether this is her first Novice; for them it is the fourth, and they are friends from the Nagano camp. Still in his voice, she says she had taken becoming a Novice to mean becoming the same kind of being as these girls, and is ashamed. (`0004`.)

The girls rank the field among themselves and agree that none of it matters, since the strongest is Hikaru, in a different dimension; only one girl in Kyoto could trouble her. One of them, pale-haired, says they should stop this before a competition. Another checks the entries. Hikaru is not on the list. (`0005`.)

The page cuts to a dark rink. Hikaru lands something and a man applauds: pass; as expected of Jun-kun's student. He asks what magic a girl who is already Cinderella expects of him, and says he can do nothing unless she shows him her ideal. She answers that she does not want to be Cinderella. She wants to be the magician. She does not want "good at ballet for a child skater"; she wants to know the highest standard in ballet itself, and that is why she had herself brought to Russia. A caption: St Petersburg. Yodaka smokes on a shore at dusk. (`0006`.)

Rio, in a jacket, explains to Inori. Hikaru was made a seeded skater at the summer camp in Nagano, so her place at the All-Japan is fixed. This competition was always a game of musical chairs for the five other seats. Seeded skaters usually enter anyway, for the experience. Inori remembers "at the All-Japan" and understands it differently now: on the day of the test Hikaru already believed she would get through this. (`0007`.)

Cameras turn. Three older skaters come in, and a caption explains the federation's designated skaters: about twenty in women's singles across senior, junior and Novice A, in three ranks, with practice slots and subsidies. Okazaki Iruka, sixteen, rank A. Kuriobe Marika, eighteen, rank B. And at the top, drawn from last season's All-Japan podium, Kojō Riina, nineteen, of Hikaru's and Yūna's club, who skated at the last Olympics. (`0008`.)

Inori blurts a question at her: how do you get to the Olympics? Iruka answers first, coldly. Who does she think she is asking; does she think the Games are a hurdle one clears by asking the way; is she taunting people who have trained half to death? Inori realizes that this winter is an Olympic winter and that for the seniors today is the first stage of selection. She apologizes at once. Marika offers her a lap to sit on. (`0009`.)

Riina is kind about it. What Inori means, she says, is what practice gets one there, and she does not know; whether what one piled up was right cannot be known until one competes. But she does know a certain way. In an Olympic season, win the All-Japan. Four years ago she was a new senior with no record and did exactly that. She declares for the cameras that she will do it again. Inori, scarlet, says that she will too, someday, surely. Riina laughs, and then removes the "someday". Novice A's required elements are stricter than the seniors': Lutz, flip and loop must all be there, a rule that exists only at this level, to raise skaters who can compete abroad. Novice A skaters are already counted as future national-team material. Prove today that you are of that calibre. Inori's thought: I am already on the road to the Olympics. (`0010`.)

The athletes' oath is taken, the draw announced, and Kago arrives with his daughter Yō, remarking that a rink is like the vegetable drawer of a refrigerator. (`0011`.)

**Score 17** goes back one night. Kago finds Tsukasa on the floor among past score sheets, checking the layout for a possibility he may have missed, delighted by how each strong club scores in its own way. Kago warns that too much preparation makes small errors frightening. Then Kago remembers, and the reader sees it: Tsukasa in a black suit and tie, head down, apologizing that he could not make it come true though they had supported him, that he could give nothing back. Kago closes the door and stands a moment. (`0012`.)

The draw puts Inori fourteenth, in the second group. A girl from the leading club draws first, again. A spread names all fifteen with their clubs and school years. (`0013`.)

In a corridor Inori slips on spilled ice and Kago catches her. He asks where he might find a coach called Akeuraji, who used to do ice dance. She takes him for a fan and announces that this is her coach. His face changes. So you are Inori; I wanted very much to meet you; thank you. Tsukasa appears and cannot believe his eyes. Kago says he has come to see Tsukasa's work, because in the end he never once saw him skate in person. (`0014`.)

He explains while Inori changes. He had been cheered by the sight of Tsukasa working at ice dance; not only Meiko, he too. Watching it live had become their dream. The page shows a hospital room, a woman in bed, a small television, Kago beside her having the rules explained; and then the same room with the bed stripped. Yesterday, he says, Tsukasa was shining, on his way to a new dream, and at the household altar he finally thought that he too could fulfil the old one in another form. Your passion let me move forward again. Tsukasa covers his face. What he manages to say is the fullest statement he has made of what he thinks a coach is. He will do his best today. His work cannot be seen. Inori's performance is all of it. Watch over her skate as you have always watched over me. (`0015`.)

On his way to the rink he finds the first starter crying into a telephone because, skating first, her mother and someone called Tsukasa will not arrive in time. A young assistant coach from her club, Kijita, collects her brusquely. (`0016`.)

In the stands Kago and Yō understand nothing. Yō says that without knowing how one wins it is boring; she had wanted to know the sport Tsukasa loves and thinks she cannot. Kago asks the man beside them how one watches figure skating. The man is Sekoma, the clerk from Inori's home rink, who by the editorial page has attended every competition she has entered. He thinks, and answers that apart from its artistry it is a sport of watching wagers. Put crudely, the bet is how well one can avoid falling on ice. A competition is not a recital. On an unstable surface there is no certain success. He lays out the game with cards: ten elements at this level, six of them jumping passes; each cleared element earns, each fall or shortfall costs; harder elements earn more; one presents a layout chosen so that the expected score is highest; the jump one is surest of goes first, so the hardest usually does. (`0017`.)

The first starter, Rinna, opens with a triple Lutz and lands it. Sekoma names her club's hallmark: finishing high-difficulty jumps in competition. She falls later on a jump she never misses in practice. In the kiss-and-cry, which Inori sees for the first time, Sonidori tells the weeping girl exactly what was fine: that opening jump, first of the whole event, under that pressure, and what it did to everyone who follows. Kijita hands her a bouquet he has prepared, one for each of his "princesses". 70.04, first. Her thought closes the chapter over an image of five gilt chairs on a dais: the game in which everyone is in earnest has begun. (`0018`.)

**Score 18** gives the next four skaters one line each before they start. Today Hikaru is not here. We are already called the Kamisaki Hikaru generation; will there ever be another day like this? I have watched the genius sit in the medalist's seat; am I going to watch someone else take it too? As I am, it may be impossible; but if a miracle might happen. (`0019`.)

Then it runs their four free skates as one. Captions belong to all of them: the hard jump it took so long to make one's own, the one jump that must succeed, placed first for that reason. Four take-offs are labelled by edge. Base values are printed: triple Lutz 5.90, triple flip 5.30, triple loop 4.90, a triple Salchow and double loop 6.00. Two land. Two fall, and a deduction of 0.50 appears. Coaches are named at the boards. The fallen get up telling themselves to reset, not to give up; one falls again at the next jump; another answers with a clean combination. (`0020`.)

Their shared interior continues. With the first jump gone there are not enough points; what place will this be; the strength drains away. But this fingertip, this angle, the way the arm comes down here, a kick put in as an accent: every second of the program holds their days, and none can be done carelessly. One can tell the result from the number of mistakes while still skating. The seconds built with one's teacher are to be guarded to the end. Captions give the three required spins and the features that raise a spin's level, the broken-leg position among them. (`0021`.)

Scores follow with printed protocols: 69.98, 63.26, 62.43, and 53.77 with three falls. (`0022`.) Kago, who has never watched before, says what he sees. These children skate from morning to night, and it is still this hard; they are sent out to a place where failing is the premise and try to do the usual there. Sekoma notes that last year's winner scored 76. Inori thinks the Novice girls are amazing: nearly all have a difficult triple. Tsukasa's reading is that every skater so far has attempted a high-difficulty jump she has never landed in competition, and that the girl who is not here has set them all alight. (`0023`.)

The sixth skater, Roba Manaka of a rising club, does not open with her best jump. Sekoma explains the second strategy: the last two of the six jumping passes are multiplied by 1.1, so she has moved her combinations there. With first and second 0.06 apart, that matters; a failure there cannot be recovered. Her coach, Gori, is frantic. She proposed it; he agreed on one condition, that the moment she felt unsure she would jump the combination at once. She nearly falls on a loop, then falls on a triple toe loop that was meant to carry a combination. Two passes remain and both must be combinations. She is terrified and furious with herself. What comes back to her is Gori after an earlier reckless failure: have you forgotten that those attempts are how you grew; you, who can take something on, are cool. She lands a double Lutz and double Axel in sequence, then a triple toe loop with two doubles after it. Her line, drawn full-height: someone who cannot bet on herself cannot beat fate. 72.62, first. The five chairs are re-seated. (`0025`.)

Two are eliminated. Yotsuha weeps into her bouquet. Umitsuki Waka, fifth grade, scores 49.86. Her coach thinks about the handicap of having no rink nearby, and about how more ice time would cost the parents too much. Waka can hardly speak. Shizuoka has no rink; he drove her to Nagoya again and again; she passed grade 6 and wanted to go to the All-Japan Novice with him. He tells her that driving her was no trouble and that she must not mistake it for a debt. (`0026`.)

Kago calls out her name, and the two men discover they have come for the same skater. (`0027`.) In the corridor a girl tells Sonidori not to worry. She has been on the ice with him longer than anyone, longer than Hikaru, who came later. She will take this gold home and prove that his club is the strongest in Nagoya. ## The absent queen

Hikaru appears in this volume for four pages, in another country. She is nonetheless on most of its pages. The girls at the ceremony dismiss their own rankings because of her. Four skaters name her before they start. One says the cohort is already called by her name. Last year's champion wanted a second title so as not to disappear behind her. Six children at her own club stopped skating after she arrived, and gave talent as the reason. The older girl who was that club's number two has spent the years since trying to get the place back.

`MD-CL-0001@4` has held that recognition in this work can enable, select and burden, and noted as a limit that selection had still produced the strongest skater shown and that Hikaru showed no cost on the page. Volume 5 moves the cost. It falls on the children near her. That is a different claim from saying she is harmed, and the volume is careful about it: nothing here shows Hikaru as unkind, or even present, and the one time she speaks it is about wanting a craft at its highest standard. The burden is structural. A prodigy turns a regional qualifier into a contest for the places she does not need, and turns a club's ladder into a ranking with one fixed rung.

The volume then asks what children do under that condition, and its answer is uniform enough to be a finding. They raise the stakes. Tsukasa says so in so many words: everyone is attempting a jump she has never landed in competition, and the absent girl lit the fire. Sonidori says he counted on it. The leader with one skater's best jump opens with it and falls later; the leader after her moves her combinations into the bonus zone; the champion does the same and loses everything on one take-off; Yūna abandons a winning plan in mid-air for the one combination that would put her level with Hikaru. `MD-CL-0023@1` registers this as the volume's design: the prodigy as a condition others live under, and escalation as their common response.

Two cautions. The first is that the pattern is partly the genre's. A qualifier with five places is built for risk whoever is absent, and Sekoma's account of the sport as a wager applies in any year. What is specific to this field is that the skaters themselves attribute their choices to her. The second is that the volume shows escalation rewarded more often than punished among those it follows closely, and that selection of cases is the author's. The two eliminated girls are shown falling on first jumps they had staked the program on.

## A wager on ice

Sekoma's speech is the first time the series has explained its sport to an outsider, and the outsider is well chosen. Kago has paid for a skater's career without understanding the rules, and his daughter wants to like what Tsukasa likes and cannot. The explanation is pitched at them and is honest about what it leaves out. Artistry is set aside in a clause. What remains is expected value: a hand of ten cards, each with a probability of success, arranged for the highest likely total.

Two things follow that bear on earlier claims.

`MD-CL-0005@3` has said that results and grades are weak evidence of level, and that practice, warm-up and program diverge. Volume 5 supplies the densest evidence yet. Printed protocols carry marks for under-rotation and edge on jumps the page shows landed. A skater falls on a jump she never misses in practice. Last year's champion is seventh with two skaters to come. A coach judges that the fastest-improving entrant cannot be assessed from data at all. Kago's summary, that these children are sent to a place where failure is the premise, is the lay version of the same point.

`MD-CL-0008@3` has described Inori's foundation-first strategy as having a competitive result and a clear ceiling: at grade 1 it lost only to content two levels harder that was also clean. The three highest scores printed in this volume do not fit that ceiling. 80.03 was scored with a counted fall and a hand down. 72.62 was scored with a fall. 70.04 was scored with a fall. Each carried content the cleaner skaters below lacked. On this evidence, at Novice A, difficulty with errors has outscored lower content, and the stated mechanics explain why: a triple Lutz and triple loop is worth more than twice a triple Salchow, and a fall costs half a point plus the element's negative mark. The claim is revised to say so. It is not overturned, because Inori's own result at this level does not exist yet, and because Sonidori, who has reason to know, expects her to compete on exactly the quality the strategy built.

The mechanics themselves are in-story statements and are recorded as such. The base values printed agree with the season Volume 1's rule references pointed to; no external document was consulted.

## The other side of a late start

Yagi Yūna is the volume's main addition and its sharpest instrument. She is built point for point as a counterpart to Inori, and the under-jacket page confirms that the pairing is intended. Each is the first pupil of her coach, and Tsukasa notes the parallel himself. Each has vowed to prove her coach by winning: Inori to Rio in Volume 3, Yūna to Sonidori in a corridor. Each calms herself by producing her coach's voice when he is absent. They differ in one respect. Yūna began at four.

`MD-CL-0022@1` set four answers to unrecoverable time side by side: Inori's acceptance and thanks, Tsukasa's revaluing of wasted years, Rio's paralysis without a deficit, Ryoka's resolve to win until the choice was right. All four are positions on time one lacks or mis-spent. Yūna's is the missing fifth, and it is addressed to Inori by name. A late starter has hope in front of her, since the gap is one that experience might close. An early starter who is overtaken has no such prospect. Her years were not lost; they were spent well, and someone else's were worth more. What she knows is the helplessness of being passed and the sadness of filling a gap that opened above her.

This is the first time the work has let a character argue that Inori's position is the easier one, and the argument is not weak. Everything Inori has been told since Volume 1 treats her late start as the deficit to be made good. The series' sympathy has run that way, and so has this reading's. Yūna's speech does not refute it. Inori's obstacles were real, and Yūna knows nothing of them. It does show that "behind" is not one condition. A child can be behind a start date or behind a person, and the second cannot be worked off. Rio said something close to this in Volume 4 from a position of privilege. Yūna says it from a position of effort, which makes it harder to set aside. `MD-CL-0022@2` records the fifth answer and the fact that it is hostile to the first.

Her other line cuts at `MD-CL-0001`. The series has argued, case after case, that what moves a child is being accurately seen and believed in by an adult. Yūna has that. Her coach chose her first, praises her precisely, and trusts her; she has turned his praise into a ritual. On the approach to her opening jump she rejects it. What she wants is not the kindness of being believed in but the strength to make him turn round. The complaint is not that Sonidori neglects her. Nothing on the page shows that he does, and his response to her disobedience is warm. It is that belief given to her is no longer the scarce thing, since another child holds his astonishment. Recognition, on this evidence, has a comparative dimension that the earlier formulation did not allow for, and a child can be fully believed in and still feel unseen. `MD-CL-0001@5` adds that, with its limit: this is one child's account of her want at the most heated moment of her season, and her coach's actual division of attention is not shown.

Whether the volume endorses her is left open by its own structure. She is given the title jump, the ovation and the lead. She is also given, in her last words on the ice, a vow whose object is to stop another child. And her premise about Inori is one the reader knows to be partial. The honest statement is that the volume makes her case as strongly as it can and stops before the answer.

## Coaches and broken plans

Five coaches handle a child's risk in this volume, and the series has shown Tsukasa do so twice before. Set together they make a range, and `MD-CL-0024@1` registers it.

Gori's pupil proposes the dangerous layout herself. He accepts it and attaches a rule that limits the damage, and when the rule fails to save her what she draws on is his earlier praise of attempting at all. Sonidori sets a conservative plan for sound tactical reasons and has it broken in the air. His response has three parts and no more: he names the broken promise in one sentence, accepts her apology with "that is all", and at once begins planning around the jump she chose. Kijita is rude to a crying child and has a bouquet ready for each of his pupils. Kumano refuses to let a ten-year-old convert her defeat into a debt to him. And Tsukasa, in Volume 3, refused an escalation in advance and gave his reasons, and in Volume 4 vetoed added training on his own authority.

What these share is that none of the adults commands the child's ambition out of existence, and none punishes a risk that failed. Where they differ is in who originates the plan and what the adult does when it is departed from. The volume's sympathy seems to lie with the coaches who let the layout be partly the child's. That is a reading of tone; the outcomes it shows are too favourable to count as a test, since both pupil-initiated gambles it follows end in first place.

Sonidori gains most as a character. Volume 4 showed a humble father. Gori's testimony adds a competitor whose attachment to winning was "beyond the ordinary", and the volume shows the tactician directly: he reads the draw, predicts how the first skater's jump will move the field, and identifies the one entrant he cannot model. He is also the adult whom the public credits with "drawing a winning child", and at whose club six children quit. Nothing ties him to their leaving. It is recorded as a fact about the institution he heads.

Then there is the last page. `MD-CL-0007` has followed Tsukasa's use of certainty since Volume 1, when he promised a mother victory. At Volume 4 the reading described a progression in his words before a performance: we will win; you may fall and I will teach you again; nothing can send us back. Volume 5 ends with "today I will make you a gold medalist". On its face this is a return to the promised result, and the claim's criterion has always been that a win cannot validate such a promise.

Three features distinguish it from the Volume 1 promise, and none of them decides the matter. Its grammar makes him the agent: the verb is his, and what he commits is his own work. It answers an aim she stated first, twice, in public, and so it ratifies her target and does not set one for her. And it comes from a man shown the night before studying every rival's score sheets, and said by the advertisement that follows to be about to stake more than anyone. Against that: it is unqualified; it names an outcome that depends on judges and on two other skaters; it is said to a twelve-year-old who has just told herself she knows the gaps; and the leader's score is four points above last year's winning mark. A reading that calls it a regression and a reading that calls it the coach taking the wager onto himself are both supported. `MD-CL-0007@5` records the line as breaking the tidy progression and holds the evaluation until the page shows what the sentence was attached to and what he does with the result.

## What Kago came to see

`MD-CL-0010` has asked since Volume 1 what Tsukasa's own failed aspiration does in his coaching, and has stayed unresolved through four versions. Volume 4 added Hitomi's observation that he can act for others and not for himself. Volume 5 supplies the history under that observation.

Kago's support was not a patron's whim. By the editorial page it was his wife's wish. By his own account she followed Tsukasa's skating from a hospital bed, explaining the rules to a husband who did not know them, and seeing him skate in person was something the two of them wanted. She died first. Tsukasa's career ended without either of them in the building. And at some point, in mourning dress, he apologized for failing to make it come true and for giving nothing back. The reading infers that the apology was at or soon after her funeral. The page shows the clothes and the words and does not caption the occasion.

This explains a good deal at once. His refusal of Kago's money in Volume 1 and his sense of being ineligible for help were the conduct of someone who believed he had already defaulted on a debt to the dead. His inability to move on his own behalf has an origin that is not vanity or timidity. And his reply in the corridor is consistent with it to the point of being troubling. Told that he has given something after all, he defines his work as invisible and wholly contained in a child's performance, and asks his patron to watch her as he once watched him. He has moved the hope onto Inori. Kago's own words allow a healthier reading: he says that Tsukasa's passion itself, shown yesterday on the floor among score sheets, is what let him move, before any result. Whether Tsukasa heard that half is not shown.

`MD-CL-0010@5` therefore gains a mechanism and stays open on what it produces. `MD-CL-0011@5` gains its clearest case of care that asks nothing: Kago's, and beside it Kumano's refusal of a child's sense of debt and Kijita's bouquets. The Volume 4 question of what happens when Tsukasa learns that Inori has been hurrying to shield him is now sharper. He is a man who has just been released from one debt and has, within the minute, described his whole work as someone else's performance.

## Inori before she skates

She is on the ice in this volume only in practice. What can be said about her comes from a ceremony, two corridors and the boards.

The possession gag is comic and is also evidence. Faced with a social fear and no adult, she produces her coach's manner and gets through. Volume 3 showed her supplying her own encouragement in his absence on the ice. This is the same resource used off it. The caption's description of her inner Tsukasa, a big brother who praises small things, is what a child has taken from a year and a half of being coached by him. The pairing with Yūna's puppets then makes a distinction the under-jacket page names. Inori becomes her coach. Yūna calls hers up and stays herself, and by the same page has been living on one compliment. The reading does not build on the labels, which are a joke; the observation that two of the volume's children manufacture an absent adult's approval, and that neither adult knows, is the page's own.

Her blunder with Riina repeats something. In Volume 3 she made a vow in anger to a boy who had insulted her coach. Here she asks an Olympian, before her qualifier and in front of cameras, how one gets to the Olympics, and a few minutes later vows before the same cameras to win the All-Japan. By the afternoon she has told Rio and a rival that she is skating for gold. Three public commitments under strong feeling, across two volumes, each to someone above her, is enough to call a habit of conduct. What it rests on is less clear. She corrects the rudeness at once and without prompting. The vow is half-withdrawn as she makes it ("someday, surely") and it is the adult who strikes out the hedge. `MD-CL-0002@5` records the habit and that an elite senior, like her coach before, answered it by taking her seriously.

`MD-OQ-V04-02` asked what would drive her once she was no longer behind. The volume's answer is in two registers that do not quite agree. The prologue and her thoughts at the ceremony speak of longing and proof: the front line she admired, the wish to show that her light is the brightest. That is appetite, as Volume 4 forecast. But the prologue also says that her talent looked tiny, and at the boards she tells herself that she understands the difference in talent and in time. The deficit has not gone. It has changed its measure, from a start date to the people around her, and for the first time includes talent. She meets it with a "but" and a grip on his drawstrings. `MD-CL-0015@4` says that the driver at this boundary is a wish to prove herself among people she admits are stronger, and that whether this is appetite or the old shame in new clothes cannot be told before she has skated and been scored.

`MD-CL-0019@2` held that her acceptance of the unrecoverable gap survived a season in which nothing went wrong. Here she restates the acceptance in her own words at the worst possible moment for it, having just watched 80.03 go up. That is evidence that it is still her view. The same day she raised her aim from qualifying to winning, on an older skater's dare. The two are not in contradiction, but they have not yet met a result. `MD-CL-0019@3` keeps the claim and moves its test to the next volume.

What she kept from her coach in Volume 4 she still keeps. Nothing in Volume 5 shows him learning it.

## Hikaru in St Petersburg

Five panels of dialogue are the most Hikaru has said since Volume 1, and they change what can be claimed. Asked what she wants, she refuses the role offered, the girl on whom magic is worked, and asks for the role of the one who works it. She rejects being judged good for her age or for a skater. She had herself taken abroad for this, in the week of a qualifier she did not need.

`MD-CL-0003@3` recorded her warmth toward Inori and called her interior thin. It is thicker by one clear statement, and the statement is about mastery and agency; it does not mention rivals. Inori's inference that Hikaru believed in her on test day is reasonable and is Inori's. It fits "at the All-Japan" and the earlier "I thought so". Hikaru has not confirmed it.

For `MD-CL-0016@3` the additions are modest. A third party abroad calls her Jun-kun's student, so the arrangement that is hidden at home is known in at least one professional circle. Yodaka takes her out of the country for training during the season and stands apart, smoking, while she is taught. The editorial page restates the public fiction and the private fact. Purpose and promise are no clearer.

## Written Japanese

The closing line is 「今日 俺はあなたを金メダリストにする」. The construction XをYにする makes the speaker the one who brings the change about, and 俺, his private pronoun, replaces the careful register he normally uses with her while あなた keeps her at the respectful distance he has always given her. Riina's dare turns on one adverb, set off with emphasis dots: 「いつかじゃなくて今日」. Inori's own vow carries its hedges inside it: いつか… きっと….

Yūna's two key sentences are built as a rejection and a replacement across a page turn: 「信じてくれる優しさじゃない」 and then 「振り向かせる強さだ」. The verb is causative, to make someone turn round, and its object is unstated. The nearest candidate is her coach. The reading takes him as primary and notes that the text leaves room for a wider audience. Her address to Inori is in the plain form, with あなた, and ends on a causative negative, させない: she will not let it happen.

Sekoma's thesis is 「「絶対の成功」はあり得ないのです」, with 絶対 in quotation marks. The word has a history in this work. It was Tsukasa's in Volume 1, when he promised victory, and his alone on a ridge in Volume 4. Here a bystander puts it in quotation marks and denies it, forty pages before Tsukasa makes a promise that does not use it.

Hikaru's sentence sets two loanwords and a native compound against each other: シンデレラじゃなくて魔法使いになりたい. Sonidori's 「それだけです」 closes a reprimand in four syllables, and his 「やんちゃだなあ」 is the only informal thing he says in three volumes.

Inori's borrowed voice is marked by sentence endings she never otherwise uses (だぜ, the rough masculine particle) and by ムキムキ, "muscly", applied to everything. Kijita speaks in a drawl (っすか, ですケドぉ〜, だりー). Iruka's rebuke uses お前 and a mock-sweet お仕置きしちゃうぞ. Kago's speech is soft and trailing throughout, and his gravest disclosure ends in a laugh, ワハハ.

Names continue the work's convention of animals: goat, donkey, squirrel, badger, cow, chicken, piglet, reindeer, crab, flying squirrel, sea lion, jellyfish among the skaters; orca, dolphin and sea angel among the seniors; pheasant, gorilla and bear among the coaches. Readings are from furigana on the page except where noted above. None of this establishes how any line sounds.

## Manga form

**Withholding the protagonist.** The volume's largest formal decision is to end before its heroine skates. A reader arrives at page 169 having watched or been told the result of thirteen programs and knowing every rule that will apply to the fourteenth. The effect is to make the field the subject and to load the last page.

**Cards and chairs.** A hand of jump cards and a dais with five gilt chairs organize the volume from the colour pages on. Each chapter's title art deals the cards differently. The chairs are redrawn after scores with skaters seated and unseated, so that the standings are a picture before they are a number.

**The explainer and the lay viewer.** Sekoma and Kago in the stands are a device the series has not used before: a man who knows the sport and a man who has paid for it without understanding it. It licenses exposition, and it does more, because each has a history with one of the pair on the ice below and neither knew the other existed. Their discovery that they are there for the same child is played as a fan's joke.

**Four programs as one.** Orders two to five are cut together with shared captions, so that four girls from four clubs think one thought. Their opening jumps are labelled with edge diagrams and prices. The ten-square element row introduced in Volume 2 runs under each, and a counter of jumps and spins remaining appears in the later programs.

**The printed protocol.** Scores arrive as boards with element codes and error marks, set beside a child's face. The form insists on the difference between what the reader saw and what was called.

**A room shown twice.** Kago's account of his wife is two panels of the same hospital room, one with her in it and one with the bed stripped. No caption says she died.

**Two interruptions.** Yūna's approach to her opening jump is broken by panels of Hikaru, and her step sequence by a panel of Inori at the ceremony. The same device, a remembered sentence entering a performance, carried Yodaka's verdict into Inori's skate in Volume 3.

**Black gutters** mark Yūna's flashback, as they have marked retrospection since Volume 1.

**Spreads.** The naming of the field is a two-page composition and is the first place in the run where facing-page geometry could be verified.

**Echoes.** The drawstrings are in her fists again. The broken-leg spin she improvised in Volume 2 appears in a caption as a catalogued feature. A child watching an Olympic skate on television, as Tsukasa once did.
