# Volume 7 — an ordinary leap

Volume 6 ended two weeks before the All-Japan Novice with its heroine two triples short of what a rival coach called the condition for a national podium. Volume 7 supplies both in one evening and treats the fact as a joke. A travelling jump specialist straps her into a harness, and by the end of the session she has landed a triple flip and a triple Lutz. Her coach protests that this is too fast. The specialist says it happens all the time. A caption announces that the special-training arc is over.

Having disposed of the problem it set, the volume spends its length on what the solution costs and what it cannot reach. The coach buys a harness of his own, falls while protecting her from it, and cracks his ribs. The Lutz she gained disappears: the sight of him on the ice has joined the memory of her sister in a cast, and her body will no longer do what she tells it. Nine days out he drives her through the night to another prefecture. Beside a lake that freezes in winter he tells her it is all right if she cannot jump tomorrow, and she, half asleep on his back, tells him he was born to skate.

Then the specialist offers a choice, and the volume's argument turns on how each person in it decides. Restore the triple Lutz in the hour that is left, or use the hour to begin a quadruple Salchow. Inori asks to have both branches explained and chooses the quad. A girl ranked far above her is offered a quad the same morning and hands the decision to her coach, who declines it. The girl's rink is closing and she will stop skating when the season ends. Tsukasa, told by an old clubmate that neither of them knows a quad's risk, hears the champion's verdict in the sentence and decides that making the quad succeed is his task.

The reading's argument is that Volume 7 answers Volume 6's question about proof with a different question about attention. Wanting the result hard enough does not produce it; the coach says so in as many words, at the moment the genre would have him say the opposite. What produces it is how finely she can feel what is happening. And the same volume shows a sport in which leaps like hers are ordinary for anyone the specialist reaches, and rinks vanish under children who did nothing wrong.

## Developments in disclosure order

**Score 24** opens on an archive stream. Tsukasa is watching Hikaru's free skate from this summer's 名港杯, her first in Novice A: 101.16. Against Inori's 81.20 that is about twenty points, and the block layout cannot reach it. A diagram explains why the flip and Lutz are hard: the toe goes in with the right foot, against the direction of rotation, so the axis is difficult to form. Inori falls and says she cannot get to the middle of the coffee cup, her old image for the axis. (`MD-EV-V07-0001`.)

Then Tsukasa takes off his jacket and jumps a single Lutz to show her the timing. He has started demonstrating jumps since the competition. The page plays what follows for comedy. She thanks him flatly and he is crushed, taking it for disappointment and resolving to become a better model. She is in fact so delighted that she is keeping a straight face on purpose: if she makes a fuss he may be embarrassed and stop. She thinks that he has changed how he teaches for the All-Japan, and that she must change too. (`0002`.)

The specialist has given a visit to their club priority, he tells her, because the Chūbu champion is there. She glows at the reward. He adds, drooping, that a teacher who can actually jump will look after her now. He goes to meet the man at a harbour at night and finds what he takes for a schoolboy landing an enormous fish. It is the specialist. (`0003`.)

魚淵翔, Uobuchi Kakeru, is thirty-two and looks fourteen. He brings a harness on a pole and explains what it is for. He does not lift the skater to lengthen her time in the air. He corrects her axis in the air in time with her own jump, into the middle of the coffee cup. What he sells is the knowledge he keeps renewing by doing this for skaters all over the country. Inori lands a triple flip in the harness, then a triple Lutz, then both without it. A banner reads that the high-difficulty special-training arc is complete. Tsukasa asks whether two such jumps in a day is rare. In his teaching, Uobuchi says, it happens often. He makes his living on this alone, and if he could not produce that much in a day it would not be a job. (`0004`–`0005`.)

An hour later every pupil in the club has got past the jump she was stuck on. Uobuchi names the structure behind his trade. Abroad it is normal to have separate coaches for jumps and for skating. In Japan many clubs cannot run unless one coach teaches everything. So he stays a neutral freelance and tries to raise the floor everywhere, and admits that skaters with a big event ahead end up first in the queue. Inori counts her cards: all five triples now, the two new ones among them, the jumps her sister could not land. Leaving for Fukuoka, he says that whether or not one can jump greatly sways a skater's path, and that they should not hold back from calling him. (`0006`.)

A few days later Tsukasa arrives with a harness he has bought with his own money, since he might not be able to use it and could not ask the club to pay. He has learned the timing by watching. He hoists an older clubmate, Yuki, who has been stuck on a double Axel since before the camp, and she lands it and bursts into tears. She is in her examination year, has not passed grade 6, and had decided that if she could never enter a junior competition she would quit. Tsukasa takes the specialist's sentence and sharpens it: whether one can jump a jump at the time it is needed sways a skater's life. He cannot jump difficult jumps himself. With this he can turn what he does have into teaching. (`0007`.)

Then Inori's turn. Her jump travels, which drags the pole forward, and his blade catches a rut. In the instant of falling he sees that if he lets go to break the fall, the pole will swing with her rotation and hit her. He holds on and goes down full length. She lands on her feet and turns to find him face down and not moving. (`0008`.)

**Score 25** opens with three colour pages out of sequence: a child waiting in pyjamas in her front hall, headlights, a coach at the wheel at night. An author's note explains that the chapter leaves out how the adults, with her father away, her mother unable to leave work and the head coach committed elsewhere, talked it over seriously and arrived at a drive with Tsukasa alone and a night in the car. (`0009`.)

The story returns to the fall. He was winded. His first words to her are to ask whether she is hurt; his nose is bleeding; he tells her to finish her practice and leaves waving. Next morning it is cracked ribs. He may drive and may not exert himself. He tells her not to worry about anything. (`0010`.)

Her jumps collapse. She can land the Lutz with one rotation. His ribs stab as he watches. He stops her checking whether she can land and has her attend to the arm and the placing of the toe. A countdown runs from fifteen days to ten. Through a window she sees him bowing low to her mother. At nine days he has gone to ask the specialist about "the jump I can no longer do", and comes back running: Uobuchi can see her tomorrow morning, in Niigata, and leaves for Canada after that. (`0011`–`0012`.)

In the van she is giddy. Her mother telephones to check and scolds her about a meat bun. She will not sleep. Asked why she is so lively, she says it may be because this is the first time she has talked with him about anything other than skating. (`0013`.)

Later she wakes to find the driver's seat empty. He is sitting on a wall by a lake with a can of coffee, and his face, which he has kept bright for her since the fall, is lowered and grave. She watches from the dark van. When she opens the door he sprints to catch her, a hand to his side. (`0014`.)

At the rail she begins a vow: she will absolutely get back to how she was tomorrow. He talks across it. They say this whole lake freezes. He looked it up; one is not allowed out on it; he would like to take her some day to a lake where one can skate, because she would look very fine jumping on one. From now on they should go to rinks all over the country. It is all right if she cannot jump it tomorrow. They will try every method and build it up together. He offers to carry her to the car, to prove that he is sturdy. (`0015`.)

On his back she says he is warm, and asks if he has a fever. His normal temperature is high, he says. Half asleep, she decides that he must have been born with a warm body so that he could skate a great deal on the ice. Fragments of older voices cross the page: clubs that would not take him, the question why it had to be skating, and his own summary of his career as changing the shape of the dream and somehow clinging on to this one sport. He stops on the path and cries with his teeth clenched. Under a page of stars he tells the sleeping child that he, too, will make her a skater of whom people all over the world say she was born to skate. (`0016`.) A pencil page adds a question she asked on the road, what kind of person his own teacher is, and his flinch. (`0017`.)

**Score 26** begins elsewhere. In a dark studio Hikaru has stayed behind in secret, folded in a split, watching footage of Yodaka's old programs. A man who made her choreography finds her and calls her a beast pretending to be a girl. She says she is not watching because she likes it. She hates remaining ignorant of the world her coach saw, because she wants to make everything hers and not only what she is given. (`0018`.)

The Niigata rink is old, and a private session there is so cheap that Inori has the ice to herself for the first time in her life. A local coach in the corridor knows Tsukasa by his given name. Uobuchi straps her in, and she starts: she has not worn the harness since the fall, and tells herself that saying she is frightened would be selfish. He does not ask her to jump. He asks about the drive. She says the harness feels a little like a seatbelt, and does a triple Salchow where he wanted a single. (`0019`.)

Then he hands the pole to Tsukasa. The cause of the lost Lutz is already clear from the video. If it is to be mended by the competition the harness must be used every day, and he will be abroad. Inori protests that her coach broke a bone on this very thing. Uobuchi knows. The harness has no course and no licence; whoever uses it bears the whole risk and learns by feel; cracked ribs are common, and he breaks his own. Jumps that travel are hard to hoist and cause accidents. So Tsukasa must give himself to her movement, and she must jump upward. Each of them, comically rigid, vows not to let the other be hurt. A double Lutz lands. (`0020`.)

The diagnosis is that she has stopped tightening. A jump needs the body drawn in on the way up and opened to land, and she is letting go of the drawing-in a moment before take-off, without knowing it. Is she afraid of injury? Tsukasa thinks that she never used to fear height or falling. Uobuchi names the condition with a sportsman's word for a body that will not obey, and declines to pursue its origin: whatever the trigger, what is needed is technique, and a habit is corrected by repetition. They should be in time. (`0021`.)

There is time left, and he proposes another way of jumping, a higher swing of the arms that uses the pull she has always fought. On her best jump, in the harness, it spins her faster than she has ever turned. She can see nothing. The page spells one word across itself, a syllable at a time: scared. He shows them the clip. This is how a quadruple is jumped. (`0022`.)

His reasoning is plain. The Lutz invites deductions for edge even when it is landed. It is more rational to turn once more on an easy take-off. A triple Lutz is worth 5.9 and a quadruple Salchow 9.7. And she already has the precision of step and the speed of rotation a quad needs, which he puts down to her coach's strict, dance-bred training. In the hour left he can restore the triple Lutz, or teach the quad. Which do they want? (`0023`.)

Tsukasa sees the arithmetic: a jump worth more than Hikaru's triple Axel. Inori leans the other way, toward the jump she has landed before and the safe plan she has called by a food name since Volume 1. Then she asks him for something. This is one of the things she has to choose, as he taught her. Would he tell her what follows from each, as he did at her first competition? He explains, offers to explain again in other words as often as she likes, and says he means to make her win whichever she chooses. She thinks for three panels and asks to be allowed to jump the quadruple Salchow. (`0024`.)

A quad, Uobuchi says, is a matter of rotating faster in the same time in the air, and of two moments: tightening at the one best instant, and opening at the very last. Open too early and the rotation is short. Fail to open and the leg takes the shock and breaks. Concentration and courage are the conditions. She opens early, again and again, and says it aloud this time. She used to be fine coming down from a height at speed. Now she is afraid of falling, because she remembers her sister and her coach breaking bones. She does not think she should jump slowly or stop. Why will her body not move? Many children are stopped by exactly that, he says; look for a landmark that tells you this one will work. He and her coach will keep her from injury. (`0025`.)

With fifteen minutes left it is not working. Everything is too fast to grasp. A full page gives her state: she must jump, she cannot bear not to, she does not want it to be now or never, please. (`0026`.)

Tsukasa kneels. She must not wish for success too much. This is not a magic that works by strength of feeling. What she needs is not to be frantic. He asks what words are in her head, and she says only that she has to jump. Then, in place of that, would she think about how to teach him what a quad feels like? Which muscle tightens. How fast the light passes. What wind is on her cheek. Jump to find one more word, not to succeed. How finely she can feel the world is the key to control. (`0027`.)

The next page is a frozen lake under an enormous sky with a child standing on it. She notices that this rink is a little colder than her own and the ice harder. The harness is unclipped and left on a bench. She runs an inventory on the approach, the pitch of the ice, the fold of a thumb, the weight of air on her face, and aims at the instant when everything lines up. The page tells her to hold four times and then to open. She lands on one blade. Uobuchi says it is the first successful landing and congratulates her. (`0028`.)

**Score 27** widens the frame. A television feature introduces the All-Japan Novice: six regional qualifiers, the last on the 24th. It lingers on a Tokyo club four years old whose coach, Riley Fox, won an Olympic title for the United States; off camera she is eating cup noodles and is being scolded by her twelve-year-old block champion. In the north an eleven-year-old dreams of a grandfather who once flooded the garden for her to skate on; the championship will be held near her home, in Aomori, and he will come. (`0030`.)

At the Kinki qualifier Ema, last seen winning at grade 1 in Volume 3, skates last in Novice A. One fall, a triple Lutz, 80.01, seventh. Six advance. Her coach cannot make sense of it and will not tell her that the others were too strong. She tells him he need not cheer her up. The medal from that first meet, which she got by not giving up however often it went wrong, is her charm. Starting over is her speciality. At the ceremony she admits to herself that she still gets a little sad, and watches the winner, the clubmate who brought her into skating, take the block by ten points with more than a hundred. The nearer she gets, the better she understands how far away that girl is. (`0031`.)

A week later their head coach, a man with a monk's manner, refuses to fix a layout. He will decide after seeing four named skaters at official practice. He is curious about one more, and it is not the Chūbu champion. It is the girl from Niigata. (`0032`.)

That girl is at the next bench. 亜昼美玖, Ahiru Miku, lands a triple Lutz in Uobuchi's harness while Inori does her homework. Tsukasa explains the six seeded skaters and shows a table he has compiled of every block score in the country. One skater has 101.23. Miku, unseeded, is second with 91.10. Inori's name is thirteenth. He tells her that if she lands the quad she too can pass a hundred. (`0033`.)

Uobuchi offers Miku a quadruple toe loop. She says she cannot judge that and will have her coach decide; this competition is not for her memories but is the match of three people, and strategy has an owner. Her coach, who has known her since she was four and calls her by name as she calls him, says they do not need it. They will compete with their own weapons. (`0034`.)

**Score 28** introduces him properly. 鴨川洸平, Kamogawa Kōhei, was Tsukasa's senior at his old club and an ice dancer; he now runs a tiny club here, one gifted girl and a handful of small boys recruited from the rink's hockey team. The third of the three is 白鳥珠那, Shiratori Juna, another clubmate, now a television personality and Miku's choreographer, who arrives in a goose costume. Tsukasa turns curt and small in front of him. Inori notices and glowers at the man on her coach's behalf. (`0035`.)

Miku tells Inori her goal. It is not to beat anyone. It is to place high enough to be entered in the junior championship, because then she can skate for one month longer. She is stopping when this season ends. Kōhei tells Tsukasa why. The rink closes this year. It is also the reason she is so good: nobody comes, so she has had the ice to herself every day, more time than any skater at a strong club. It is a common story, he says. (`0036`.)

He has seen that Inori means to use the quad, and offers a test. Three jumps in a row, moving on after each without stopping; if she lands all three it goes in. Tsukasa sees at once that three quads in a row cannot be done. Juna corrects the rule. As their teacher taught it, the first two may fail; what is watched is the third, because landing that one shows she can jump at a chosen moment. And even that is unnecessary: in his day he landed a thing once and took it to competition. There is no right answer on the ice. Kōhei calls that recklessness. Juna says an Olympic medalist decides the same way, that Kōhei turned down the quad behind his back, and that adults keeping to the safe zone is all ego. Kōhei says the child told him to decide. (`0037`.)

Then Kōhei gives his own creed, and it is the one Tsukasa won a title with a month ago. As a former ice dancer he wants to win by completeness and not by the difficulty of jumps. The top skaters all have triple Lutzes, so avoiding deductions decides it. And neither of them has ever jumped a quad, so neither knows its risk. Tsukasa hears another voice under that: you do not know, so your dream will not come true. He thanks them both. He has learned what he wanted to know. Putting the quad in and making it succeed is his task as a coach this time. A flashback shows him years younger and gaunt, telling these same two that he would stake all the skating money he had left on the championship at which his partner planned to retire, and become a medalist, and so make himself a reason to go on. Kōhei smiles: he has not changed, and they can fight again. (`0038`.)

Back in Nagoya she lands the quad on home ice, the first of three, and strangers begin to stare. In the van on the way home from Niigata he had warned her: once she practises it at home she will be the centre of attention, grown-ups' manner will change, and she should not mind it, and he will not. She answers that she chose the quad on purpose, knowing that. A person who is not beaten by that attention is the one who can become the best in the world. (`0039`.)

Sonidori telephones Yodaka to report that Hikaru's layout is settled, and mentions a rumour. Yodaka says she will be jumping a quadruple Salchow. It is about the only card in her hand that could beat Hikaru. He watches the club's children practise and wonders, in a black panel, what he will do. (`0040`.)

One week later, a hotel ballroom in Aomori. Girls in the rows ask which one is the skater with the quad who was not at the selection camp, and whether she might beat Hikaru. A barefoot girl in a tailored suit walks in with her shoes in her hand, stops beside a child in a plaid skirt, and says her name. I have been waiting. (`0041`.)

## An ordinary leap

`MD-OQ-V06-02` asked whether two triples could be had in the time and whether the plan Tsukasa called a tightrope would survive its second half. The first part is answered in a page. `MD-CL-0008@5` and `MD-CL-0004@5` therefore need care about what the page shows and whom it credits.

It credits the specialist. The tool is his, the knowledge is his, and the remark that such a day is ordinary is his. Tsukasa is the one who is astonished. Two things qualify that. Volume 6 had a rival's son explain that blade skill built by skating practice makes jumps come faster, and in this volume Uobuchi says that Inori already has the precision and rotation speed a quadruple needs and attributes it to her coach's training. So the same expert who supplies the leap names its precondition. And the profile states that he does this for every club. Whatever advantage the champion's title bought, it was a place in a queue for something her rivals can also have. The girl at the next bench landed the same jump in the same harness that morning.

What follows from "ordinary" is the volume's first reversal of Volume 6. There the missing triples were a ceiling and the time spent on completeness was the charge against her coach. Here the ceiling lifts in an evening, for a child prepared by exactly that work, and the hard problem turns out to be something no specialist's tool can reach. `MD-CL-0008@6` records the first landings and their status. By the model her coach gave her in Volume 6, a first landing is step one; a jump may enter a layout only when its rate has been raised. The flip is never seen again in this volume. The Lutz is lost within days.

Yuki's scene is the same fact from the floor of the sport. A pupil of no competitive consequence had been on the point of quitting because one jump stood between her and a grade, and with the grade a class of competition. Tsukasa draws the general statement himself. It bears on how the earlier volumes should be read. The foundation-first method was right about what makes jumps possible, and it does not remove the fact that the system gates children by jumps, on a schedule.

## The harness

He buys it with his own money. Every earlier volume has shown what that means for him: a second job overnight, borrowed equipment, and by his remark in this one a competitive career during which he could not choose what he ate. He buys it for the club, uses it first on other children, and learns it by watching, as he learned skating and a backflip.

Then he is hurt, and how he is hurt belongs with what `MD-CL-0010@6` has been tracking. The decision takes one panel. To save himself he would have to let go, and letting go would endanger her, so he does not. Afterwards he asks whether she is injured, tells her to finish practising, and reports cracked ribs with instructions not to worry. Hitomi said in Volume 4 that he moves for others and cannot move for himself. Here that reaches his body. It is the same disposition that made him offer away the coach's seat in Volume 6, with the comedy removed.

The specialist's frankness about the tool matters for the institutions ledger and for `MD-CL-0024@2`. There is no training and no licence. The operator carries the risk. Rib fractures are routine. He asks an injured man to work through pain because there is no one else to do it before the event, and the man agrees, and the child consents only once she is told that he has agreed knowing all of it. This is the first case in the run where the adult's risk, and not the child's, is what a child has to accept.

The gain is also real. He ends the volume able to hoist his own pupil, taught by the best practitioner in the country, with a problem neither could have solved alone.

## The lost Lutz

The regression is the volume's most carefully evidenced event, and its cause should be stated in the order the page establishes it.

First, conduct: after the fall she can land the jump with one rotation. Second, a technician's observation from video, confirmed in person: she releases the body's tightening just before take-off, unconsciously. Third, his question, her echo of one word, and her coach's thought that she never used to fear falling. Fourth, her own statement, made aloud to two adults in the middle of failing at something else: she is afraid of falling because she remembers her sister and her coach breaking bones, she does not want to hold back, and her body will not move.

That is as complete an account as this work has given of anything inside her, and it comes from her. It joins two things. One is the family history disclosed in Volume 6, a sister's skating ended by a fracture in jump practice. The other is new and belongs to this relationship. In Volume 4 she told a friend, and kept from him, that she was afraid he would be blamed for her failures. She has now seen him physically hurt while working on her jump, and has watched him bow to her mother. The thing she feared for him has happened in a form she did not imagine, and what gives way is the jump.

`MD-CL-0019@4` named the first strain on her vow against haste and asked whether it would recur. It does, at the worst moment, as a page of compulsion: she must, she cannot bear not to, please. Her own word for what she renounced on the train in Volume 3 is the word her coach uses when he tells her it is what she does not need. He is the one who holds the vow. `MD-CL-0019@5` records both the recurrence and that it was met.

`MD-CL-0002@6` had her withholding one thing from him for his sake. In this volume she withholds three more and each is of that kind. She hides her delight at his demonstrations so that he will not be embarrassed into stopping. She rules out saying that she is frightened of the harness, as selfishness. She starts to promise recovery by tomorrow as repayment for the drive. A bonus strip has her guilty that his injury and the journey were burdens she caused. Set beside Volume 3's "making someone help a person like me" and the secret of Volume 4, this is enough to state as a standing feature of how she is with him: she manages what he is exposed to on her account, at her own cost. It is also where the volume shows her change. The fear she would not say at the start of the lesson she says in full before its end, and nothing bad follows.

## It is all right if you cannot

`MD-CL-0007@6` distinguished his absolutes of commitment from absolutes of outcome and left the ethics of "gold or nothing" divided. The lakeside is the other side of that ledger, and it comes with the wager against Yodaka nine days off and the required jump gone.

She offers him an outcome as payment, with his own word in it. He will not take it. He changes the subject to a frozen lake, to skating as something one would simply want to do on a surface that size, and then says the sentence: it is all right if she cannot jump it tomorrow. The future he describes has no deadline in it. Rinks all over the country, every method, together, and the verb for piling up that he used before her test in Volume 4.

This is the strongest evidence yet that the declaration at the block was an instrument for one occasion and not what he has become. A reading of Volumes 5 and 6 in which he had turned into a coach bound to results has to account for this night. It also has to be weighed against what he does a day later, when he decides that an untested quadruple goes into her program and that making it succeed is his job. The two fit if his commitments attach to his own work and his demands on her do not attach to results. They are still in tension, because she will be the one in the air. `MD-CL-0007@7` keeps both.

The same distinction governs the choice in Niigata. He tells her that he means to make her win whichever she chooses. The certainty is in his effort and is indifferent to the option.

## Born to skate

`MD-CL-0001@6` noted that what the adolescent Tsukasa wanted was a verdict on his talent as leave to go on, that no one gave it, and that what he gave Inori unasked in Volume 1 was that verdict. Volume 7 closes the circuit. A child half asleep, working out why her coach's back is warm, tells him he was born for the ice. She does not know what she is saying. The page shows what it meets in him: clubs that would not have him, the question why it had to be skating, his own description of a career as clinging on by changing the dream's shape.

Three things should be kept distinct. Her sentence is not an appraisal; it is a child's affectionate reasoning from body heat, and its force is entirely on his side. His weeping is the third time the work has shown him cry at being given something, after a boy's bow and a patron's thanks, and the first time the giver is his pupil. And his vow answers in kind. He does not promise her a medal. He promises that the world will say of her what she has just said of him. It is recognition returned as a purpose, spoken to someone asleep.

`MD-CL-0010@6` asked whether the purpose he claimed as his own on the night ice could be held apart from her. This scene does not answer that. It shows the purpose deepening in the opposite direction, toward her, and for the first time shows something passing from her to him that is not a result. The pencil page that follows marks where his history is still closed. Asked about his own teacher, he flinches. A gag strip has him freeze at a question about his childhood. And Juna, who became what Tsukasa once auditioned to be, reduces him to monosyllables in a way Yodaka did not.

Her remark in the van belongs here too. In a year and a half they had never talked about anything but skating. The relationship this ledger has followed since Volume 1 has been conducted entirely inside the work. The drive is the first time it is anything else, and the author's note takes care to say that the adults around them arranged it deliberately.

## Not magic

The coaching act at the centre of the volume is four sentences long and reverses the title of the chapter before it. That chapter is called hot blood. Here, with fifteen minutes left and his pupil pleading with the air, he tells her that she is wishing too hard, that jumping is not a magic powered by feeling, and that she does not need to be frantic.

What he puts in its place is specific to the two of them. He cannot jump a quadruple, so he asks her to teach him what one is like. That turns her concern for him into a task. It gives the specialist's instruction to find a landmark a form she can carry out, since at that speed she could find nothing. And it changes what an attempt is for: one more word. His general statement, that how finely one feels the world is the key to control, is the best single account of his method the work has offered, and it is continuous with everything earlier: skating before jumps, logs kept by condition, a body that learns by watching.

The image that follows is the lake he described the night before. The reading takes the plain inference that the place she concentrates from was made by that conversation, the one in which nothing was demanded of her. The harness comes off, the inventory she runs is the task he set, and she lands.

`MD-CL-0015@5` called measuring herself against a gap and moving toward it a standing way of conducting herself and left its ground open. This scene bears on the ground. The same child is shown twice in ten pages in pursuit of the same jump. In the first state she is driven by the closing window and cannot perceive. In the second she attends, and can. The pattern is one of conduct; what fuels it at a given moment differs, and her coach treats the difference as the thing to be coached. The claim is refined to say that the pattern has at least two modes and that the work now distinguishes them on the page.

One landing in practice is step one. He says so himself in the next chapter, when he asks how he can judge an unknown jump in nine days.

## Who decides

`MD-CL-0024@2` gathered the range of adult responses to a child's risk. Volume 7 adds a case of each kind it lacked and puts two of them side by side on one morning.

Inori is offered a choice by a specialist, with her coach present. She does not take either adult's lead. She invokes a rule her coach taught her at her first competition, that some things are hers to choose and that she should know what follows from each, and makes him apply it. Her first leaning was toward the safer option. She chooses the other after hearing both. Later she gives her reason, and it is not arithmetic. She chose the quad because of the attention it would bring, on the ground that someone who is not beaten by attention is what a world-best medalist is. A rival coach in Volume 6 called that attention a noose. She has decided it is a qualification.

Miku is offered a quad by the same man an hour later. She declines to choose, on a stated principle: the competition belongs to a team of three and strategy has an owner. Her coach decides against. Their choreographer, absent at the time, calls his caution an adult's ego. The coach's defence is that the child asked him to decide.

These are not ranked. Inori's choosing is the series' long-standing model of agency, and it produced a decision that even her coach's friend thinks reckless. Miku's delegation is also a decision, reasoned and hers, made by a child with a month of skating left who will not regret a plan she trusts. The volume lets a performer who lived by risk and a coach who lives by completeness argue it out and gives neither the last word.

Tsukasa's own decision comes out of that argument and is the second reversal of Volume 6. Kōhei states the completeness creed as an ice dancer's creed, which is to say he states what Tsukasa did at the block. He adds that neither of them knows a quad's risk. Tsukasa hears Yodaka in it and resolves to learn the thing he is said not to know. So the coach whose method was vindicated a month ago by cleanness now chooses difficulty, against the advice of the man who shares his training, in answer to the champion who told him his method could not win. `MD-CL-0025@1` held that the two coaches shared a premise about proof. `MD-CL-0025@2` adds that Tsukasa has moved onto Yodaka's ground to contest it, and that Yodaka predicted he would.

The flashback under that decision gives it a precedent. As a competitor he staked everything he had on one championship, to become a medalist and so earn a reason to continue. He did not win it. He has done gold or nothing before, for himself, and it is the origin of the word in the series' title as he uses it. Kōhei's comment that he has not changed can be read as praise or as warning.

## The vanishing rink

`MD-CL-0022@3` listed the work's positions on time that cannot be had back. Miku adds one that none of the others resembles. Her limit is not her age or a late start or a rival. The place is being closed. She cannot continue without a rink nearby, so she will stop, and what she wants from the national championship is a result that buys one more month.

Her coach's account of her ability is the bleakest irony in the run. She is the second-ranked skater in the country by score because the rink failed. No customers meant unlimited ice, which is the resource Nagoya's champions draw lots for at midnight. The decline that made her is ending her. He calls it a common story, and the phrase rhymes with the volume's first title. Leaps are ordinary in this sport, and so are losses of this kind.

Ema's elimination belongs beside it. A score within 1.2 of Inori's winning one is seventh in a stronger region and goes home. `MD-CL-0020@1` read her in Volume 3 as the demonstration that a detour can become an asset, on her coach's account. Here she says it herself and extends it: the medal she won by not giving up is a charm, and starting over is what she is good at. The claim is strengthened by her own words and bounded by her own admission that she is still sometimes sad, and by what she sees at the ceremony. The clubmate who brought her into the sport has won by ten points, and proximity has only made the distance clearer. That is what Inori concluded about Hikaru a volume ago. `MD-CL-0023@2` described a prodigy as a condition other children live under. There are now at least two such children in two regions, and a table that puts six skaters between ninety and a hundred and one.

That table also measures what Volume 6's passport was worth. The Chūbu champion is thirteenth. The west's leading coach, listing the skaters he will watch, does not name her. Within a fortnight she is the most talked-about entrant in the hall, and the reason is one landed jump.

## Hikaru

She speaks twice. In the studio she says that she studies her coach's competitive programs in secret because she hates being ignorant of the world he saw and wants all of it for herself, beyond what he chooses to give. In the ballroom she says three words to Inori.

`MD-CL-0003@5` ended with an uncaptioned moment in which she turned away from Inori after a fall, and listed disappointment among its possible meanings. Nothing in Volume 7 explains that moment. What the volume shows is where she stands a month later: she walks the length of a room past every other skater to say that she has been waiting. Her direction is again plain.

The studio scene bears on `MD-CL-0016@5`. Yodaka has declared a design for her whole career. Whether she knows it is still not shown. But she is not a passive object of whatever she has been told. Her stance toward him is acquisitive. She means to take more than he hands her, and she does it without asking. With the earlier statement that she wants to be the one who works the magic, this makes three volumes in which every sentence she has about her own skating concerns mastery and possession, and none concerns a rival. Her words about Inori remain limited to greetings.

Yodaka, for his part, models Inori correctly. He names the jump she will attempt before he is told, by asking what could beat his pupil. In Volume 2 he said a lifetime would not be enough. In Volume 6 he called her a child who could not tell right from left. Here he treats her as a problem with one solution and wonders what to do about it.

## Written Japanese

The coaching sentences are plain and negative. 成功を願いすぎては駄目だ. 気持ちの強さで跳べる魔法じゃない. And 必要なのは ガムシャラになることじゃない, with the word in katakana that she used of herself on the train in Volume 3. His replacement is a request in the form 教えてくれないか, would you teach me. 成功のためじゃなくて 一つでも多く言葉を見つけるために跳ぶんだ.

At the lake her unfinished sentence begins 絶対. His answer is 明日跳べなくたっていい, with the colloquial concessive, and 一緒に積み重ねていこう. His joke about carrying her uses 証明, the word that carried Volume 6. Her line is built on a guess, きっと, and a purpose clause: so that he could skate a great deal on the ice. His vow copies its key phrase and adds 俺も, I too, and 世界中の人に, by people all over the world.

His career in his own summary is 夢の形を変えて どうにかフィギュアスケートにしがみついて: clinging.

Her request before choosing quotes him: 「私が選ばなきゃいけないこと」. His commitment is どちらを選んでも. Hers is in the polite form to two adults, 跳ばせてください, let me jump. Her reason later is わざとです, on purpose.

Miku's principle is that the event is 「3人の試合」 and that strategy belongs to 作戦担当. Kōhei's refusal is 俺達の武器で勝負しよう. Juna's charge is 大人が安全圏を走ろうとするのは全部エゴだよ. Kōhei's phrase for the rink is よくある話, and the first chapter's title is よくある飛躍.

The specialist's verb for hoisting is 釣る, to fish, and his name contains the character for fish. Ema's phrase is 頑張り直し. Hikaru's last line is 待ってたよ.

The Kinki skaters and coaches speak Kansai forms and the northern child a marked dialect. The new names continue the animal convention: fish, duck, wild duck, swan, koala, sparrow, cicada, spider, fox. None of this establishes how any line sounds.
