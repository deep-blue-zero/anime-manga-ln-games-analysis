# Volume 12 — what a name asks

For two of its three chapters Volume 12 belongs to the rival. It follows Hikaru out of the house that took her in, onto a train to Tokyo, into a new school and a new club, and back to the event at which she and the heroine are to meet. Only then does it return to Inori, who in the interval has won an overseas competition that the volume does not show.

The two girls' given names mean light and prayer, and the volume makes each of them say what she thinks hers demands. Hikaru believes she frightens people and casts shadows on them. She has watched club-mates leave, a friend displaced, and the man who shelters her carried out to an ambulance, and she takes her leaving as the way to stop being that. Alone on the train she finds that the house she left was her home. Inori, arriving at the arena where her coach once trained, thinks that but for her he would still be there, and resolves to win today as his pupil. Each reads her standing as a harm done to someone. Each is told otherwise by a person who knows her, and neither quite takes it.

The third chapter is the first day of the national junior championship, and it breaks the series' strongest assumptions in an hour. Hikaru, skating first, lands a combination nobody else can use, earns the step level that was Inori's alone, and falls. It is the first fall of her competitive life. Then the country's leading junior goes down in the warm-up and is carried off. The volume ends with the heroine at centre ice, about to begin.

The reading's argument is that Volume 12 states the terms on which Hikaru skates more exactly than any earlier volume, places beside them several adults who offer her something unconditional, and then shows the terms under strain for the first time. It keeps apart what she says of herself, what others say of her, and what the page shows.

## Developments in disclosure order

It is Hikaru, at a public session, where two adult beginners are astonished to find the national novice champion among them. She thanks the people who applaud her. Rio, who is with her, disapproves of her encouraging admirers and says that Inori, fourth in her first event, is unlikely to reach the final. Hikaru answers that Inori is the kind who wins precisely then. Looking at the screen she says that Inori grows stronger where an ordinary child would give up, that she therefore wants to be stronger herself, and that if she stands still she will be caught. (`MD-EV-V12-0002`.)

From his hospital bed Sonidori telephones his assistant. He cannot at present coach Hikaru as she needs. Riley Fox has agreed to take her on. She will change schools; only she is moving; she wants her club-mates not to know yet. He does not think his son knows. In the rink's lobby parents are saying why he collapsed: overwork, with too few staff, since the number of pupils rose, and rose mostly because of her. She hears it. A memory shows her asking Takamine how a transfer is arranged, because her coach changed clubs from his second novice year and she wants to go the way he went, and to recover what she lost while resting her back. (`0003`.)

She gives everything in her room to Rio's small sister. It is full of things she loves, she says, so it is best that someone she loves has them, and she does not need them any more. Rio, at the door, points out that some of those things were his presents. (`0004`.)

He follows her out and asks whether she is leaving. She confirms it lightly, and says that the decision was hers and not her coach's. He says they are family. She says she is glad he thinks so and that in fact they are not. He blurts a proposal of marriage and is horrified at himself. She declines, gently, and asks whether he wants her to stay because he likes her. (`0005`.)

Then her manner changes. What if she has been acting in front of him for six years? When she was brought to that house she understood that she must not be turned away, and that the rule of the house was whether she could get on with him. So she copied his mother's sunny temper. She hid that Yodaka had already taught her, and that she could do a double Axel long before he could, because he was a boy who loved being a medalist's son. She no longer needs to be liked by him. The girl he likes does not exist. He should not hold her back. (`0006`.)

He sinks to his knees, and then looks up smiling. Did she expect to put him off with so thin a story? He suffered for years at being overtaken; no act could have staged that. He can tell her real laugh from the one she forces. Does she know herself which is which? He never thought she resembled his mother; she is a poor actor anywhere but on the ice. He lists things that were nobody's performance: making his sister laugh, keeping a club-mate's biscuits like treasure, quietly fetching an adult for a schoolmate who looked ill, loving frills, and remembering his tears as suffering. (`0007`.)

The club's leading skater calls them in. She knows, and tells Hikaru that joining a new pack takes courage and that regrets should be settled first. After the session Hikaru asks Rio to try a little ice dance, which Takamine has been teaching her. They wobble through it laughing. Then she tells him what she thinks her name means. It was given by her birth parents, she understands. She is like a wolf: she frightens people and makes only shadows, so she will spend her life chasing a dazzling light. A collage shows what she counts: pupils leaving the club when she came, a friend in tears, an ambulance at the rink, a skater falling after her. (`0008`–`0009`.)

She tells the club. There is applause and crying. Yūna, whom she displaced as its best young skater, runs after her to thank her for having been her club-mate. Going back to second place this way galls her, she says, but that was her own fault for being slow to trust her talent; chasing Hikaru's back was a pleasure; she will not stop trying to pass her wherever she skates. Hikaru calls her the admirable one. (`0011`.)

On the platform Rio's mother tells her that the dormitory's matron seemed kind, and to telephone at any time. Hikaru begins to demur. The moment she leaves that road he stops being her coach. She asks whether that alone is enough, and says she has many rivals, one of them unstable and possessed by the ice, who will make it hard. He knows whom she means. He is bored of them, he says: the man could not win his wager, and is a fool led about by a dull child. She is stunned at the word. The club is nothing like the one she left. It has morning ice once a week, and Riley tells her to sleep until the sun is up. Most of the work is off the ice. Riley won her title without a serious injury, the pupils explain, and believes that less ice means fewer injuries. One says her back stopped hurting when she changed how she bends. Hikaru doubts that her coach chose the place for such a reason. (`0021`.)

Riley tells her quietly what she thinks caused the back injury. She practised too much because she minded her club-mates' eyes. Winning on less practice than they did would have felt like guilt. Hikaru thanks her and then answers firmly. She is not ashamed of having been wrong. Even wasted effort had people who watched it. That she touched hearts easily hurt, worried, and stood still was time she values, and it is not to be made light of. Riley apologizes, and gives her doctrine. Every pupil here is a transfer; nearly all are her age. This is the forest at night. Mind the others too much and you are the one eaten. Let a wild heart run. (`0022`.)

On the ice she reproduces a demonstration at once. Riley's appraisal is given in captions. The girl's gift for reproducing movement is very great, and what she cannot copy she can work out a route to. It shows itself because her coach supplies a finished form to copy. She had first wanted Tsukasa, who has a similar gift. (`0023`.)

A pupil brings news. Inori has won the seventh series event and is in the final; her entry had been moved from the sixth. Hikaru's face lights. (`0024`.)

A girl this uncanny cannot be dull. He is proud as a champion and thinks himself without character, and that is why a girl who is his living copy bores him. She does not want proof that she is first. She wants the medal as something taken from this small version of him. She prays, too, she thinks: that Inori will be a creature of the night who makes her tremble. (`0026`.)

The arena is in Yokohama. Hitomi shows her an old photograph and tells her what her coach had kept quiet: this was their home rink when they competed. Inori inspects the building for traces of him. Then: he chose to coach in Nagoya, so she is here; today she will take first as his pupil, in this place. (`0030`.)

Hikaru passes. He remembers the bridge and the condition she set: a year on, in junior. Today is that day. He tells Hitomi, who does not follow, that he still cannot acknowledge himself, and cannot be proud as a coach until he has led his pupil to the gold he promised. (`0031`.)

In the stands her mother explains pairs and ice dance to the patron and his daughter. A young woman adds what the event is for. Juniors are at the age of deciding their futures, and this is where they ask whether to go on, in what, and how seriously; and the result governs designation and entry to the world juniors and the senior nationals. She stopped skating before junior herself. It is Mika, who says she is a former skater and for that reason admires everyone still on the ice. On a staircase Iruka, warming up, hears the voice and turns. (`0032`.)

Hikaru has drawn first. The field's dread is drawn as comedy: every skater who has followed her has come apart, and now all of them will. Inori is twentieth. It is the first time she will see Hikaru's junior short program. (`0033`.)

It opens with a triple Axel and triple toe loop. The stands object that the Axel in this segment must be a double, and the rule is explained: the solo Axel must be a double, but a skater who can do the triple in combination may use it there. Her coach's inset remark is that the fixed layout binds most skaters. She does a double Axel and a step sequence called at the top level. Tsukasa notes the openings closing. Then she falls on the triple Lutz. (`0034`–`0035`.)

Riley approves: a fall scores better than a single. 71.10. Her own thought is that it is the first time she has fallen in a competition, and that she needed a margin over Iruka today. (`0036`.)

For three minutes this rink will be the ice that makes someone shine. Whatever road one chooses there will be injury and hardship; then she wants it to be here, on this cold white ice, that she is hurt. She takes her pose. (`0041`.)

## The contract

`MD-CL-0016` has assembled Yodaka's terms from fragments: a "contract" in Volume 6, a rule about one fall in Volume 8, seven years in Volume 10. This volume has him state them.

The origin is a child's request. She asked to be taken to the Olympics. He answered with a question about whether she could live as he had. The clauses are three. She follows his road. She wins gold at every competition. And if she leaves that road he is no longer her coach. A fourth, which he only alludes to, is tied to the run-through and is evidently the rule of Volume 8, still in force at the new club.

Two things about it are new. Its sanction is his withdrawal. Nothing is taken from her but him. And she now reads his biography as part of the road. He changed clubs from his second novice year, so she asks whether she should have moved sooner. Her leaving is therefore several things at once: a response to Inori's progress, a way of ceasing to harm a household, a recovery of time lost to rest, and obedience to a model. The volume gives all four on the page and ranks none. She also insists to Rio that the decision was hers.

His manner toward her is consistent with the terms. He does not eat at the dinner given for her. He accepts a firefly from her hands with one instruction. On the night she falls for the first time he refuses his choreographer's plea to ease the free skate and says that a loss here ends it. He says this where she cannot hear, while she is telling herself that he is watching and she need only obey.

What stands against the contract in this volume is not argument. It is four adults. Sonidori, who collapsed keeping her club running, arranges her transfer and her school and then comes to tell her that one spin is better. His wife tells her she is their daughter whether or not she skates. Riley Fox, the new coach of record, prescribes sleep, analyses her injury as guilt, and asks about her back before her score. Tsukasa is present only as a remembered voice against sacrifice. None of them opposes Yodaka. Each offers what the contract leaves out.

## Acting, and what Rio knows

Her speech to Rio is the longest account anyone has given of her inner life, and the volume surrounds it with reasons to weigh it carefully.

It is plainly true in part. A memory shown directly has the small child working out that this house wants a childlike child and resolving not to get it wrong. Sonidori guessed in Volume 10 that her reliance on his family might be shown out of consideration. A club-mate said she always takes care not to overawe people. And she sizes up her new club's hierarchy in exactly the way she says she sized up his house.

It is also an instrument. She says it in order to be let go, and ends by telling him not to hold her back.

Rio's reply is not sentiment. He has six years of evidence that nobody was performing for, and the claim that he can tell her laughs apart. His question is the sharpest thing said to her in the series: does she know which self is the act? Three later passages support him. On the train, with no audience, she calls the house her home and says she learned feeling there. At her new school she is called cheerful and loves it; the manner she disowned is simply hers. And a creator aside, at lower weight, shows the boy's first birthday present to her and her delight.

So the bounded statement is double. She adapted on purpose, from the first day, to secure her place. And what she built by adapting was real to her. `MD-CL-0003@11` records that she cannot cleanly separate the two and that the one person who claims he can has just been left behind. His own aim, to be someone who is not a shadow beside her, turns the measure he has lived under since Volume 3 into something he chooses.

## Shadow

In Volume 9 she said she knew a shadow falls when she shines and would not ask forgiveness for it. Here the knowledge has become a reason to go. She counts her shadows and the latest is an ambulance.

The volume answers her three times within a chapter. Yūna, the plainest case of someone she overshadowed, thanks her and blames her own slowness to trust herself. Rio says the name means what it says. And her own conclusion on the train is that the belief was mistaken: had she stayed she would have gone on thinking she could only hurt.

Riley Fox gives the belief a body. The back injury came from practising under her club-mates' eyes, because winning on less work than theirs would have felt like guilt. Hikaru does not deny it. She refuses to be ashamed of it, and defends the worry itself as time well spent. That is of a piece with Volume 9. She owns the cost of what she is; she no longer accepts that it is all she is.

Set beside `MD-CL-0029`, this makes a pair the volume plainly intends. One girl is ashamed of being protected. The other is ashamed of being gifted. Each takes her advantage for an injury to those round her. Each has been told, by someone in a position to know, that it is not. And each reads the other's name as a strength: Hikaru says Inori is strong because everyone protects her, which is the thing Inori cannot forgive in herself.

Riley's remedy is not Ava's. Ava removes the condition. Riley removes the audience: a club of transfers of one age, where nobody need be minded. Her image of a forest at night gives the chapter its title, and Hikaru ends the chapter by assigning the title to Inori.

## Sacrifice, tested once

Under the bridge in Volume 9 Tsukasa told her that sacrifice is the self-harm of someone who feels inferior, and that one can pay and lose. She conceded his point about weights and kept her coach's doctrine. On the train she returns to it with a case. She gave up a home, and only by giving it up did she learn what it was. She does not say it made her stronger. She says she wants to know what her coach means.

This is the first time either doctrine has been tried on a person in the reader's sight, and the result does not settle the argument. It shows that loss can disclose value, which neither man denied. It is followed by a creator's page saying that a path the heroine refused should not be read as the wrong one, and that a decision of that size takes courage even when its reason is partly negative. The reading notes the statement and does not lean on it.

The two coaches' positions are restated on the same evening in November. One says there is no absolute on the ice. The other says that a loss here is the end. `MD-CL-0025@7` records that the dated test set in Volume 9 has arrived, that Tsukasa knows it and has told no one, and that the champion's side enters it having fallen.

## Reproduction

`MD-OQ-V10-01` asked whether the faculty Tsukasa calls a hawk's eye can be taught. Volume 12 does not answer, but it gives the clearest description yet of what the other team has in its place.

Riley Fox's appraisal is that Hikaru reproduces movement with great accuracy and can reason her way to what she cannot yet copy, and that this works because her coach demonstrates a finished form. Between them the problem of conveying is absent. Riley wanted Tsukasa for the same purpose, since he has a similar gift.

Three earlier observations fall into line. Tsukasa said in Volume 10 that the hardest thing in coaching is to convey, and found his words not arriving. He said that Inori is not someone who reproduces movement easily, and in Volume 11 she said she needs a week for what he takes in at a glance. And Kyōboku said that Yodaka needed no coach.

The asymmetry between the two teams is therefore structural and has now been stated by a third party. One pair transmits by showing and copying. The other has a coach who can show and a pupil who learns another way, by taking a thing apart and assembling it. The second route is slower, and it is the one that produced a jump recovered in an hour and a step sequence nobody else had. This volume removes that last distinction: Hikaru's step sequence is called at the top level. Her coach's old teacher has done what he was hired for.

## The promised day

The championship chapter is built from reversals of things the series had made dependable.

Hikaru had never fallen in competition. She falls. The cause, by her own account, was an over-aimed jump that she chose to complete and fall on instead of letting it open. Her choreographer blames a layout raised too fast after a move. She is seventy-one points into an event she has said she must win, under a contract that requires it.

Iruka was the proof of a doctrine. Her coach held that children should suffer no pain but that of competing, and built her life round it. She is carried off before her program. The cause is not shown. The reading does not infer one.

The air after Hikaru had been a hazard to one skater at a time. With her first in the order it falls on a whole field; and the next skater, by a creator aside, is clean.

The heroine had been the one whose skate is withheld at a volume's end while others decide the terms. It is withheld again, but she has chosen the terms herself and stated them. She is aiming at an Olympic gold medal. She will skate for someone who cannot. And since no road is without injury, she wants hers on the ice. In Volume 11 she wished she knew hardship and was ashamed of being spared it. Here she names the hardship that is already hers and claims it. That is not the vocabulary of debt. Whether it holds through her program is the next volume's matter.

Her other statement of the afternoon uses the old vocabulary unchanged. In the building where her coach once trained she thinks that but for her he would be there still. Two people told her in Volume 11 that care is not a debt, and it has not reached this belief. `MD-CL-0027@4` and `MD-CL-0029@2` record both.

He, for his part, tells Hitomi that he cannot be proud as a coach until she has the gold he promised. In Volume 9 he told his pupil that a person is not what others evaluate. He does not apply the sentence to himself.

## A win offstage

The heroine's first international title is a headline, a points table and a pupil's report in another club. The volume declines to show it, as the last volume declined to show her free skate.

What can be said is limited to what is printed. She won the seventh event with a quadruple Salchow. Her entry had been moved from the sixth. With a fourth and a first she has twenty-four points and is one of six finalists. Kōhei's objection in Volume 11, that the final was too high an aim for a first season, is answered by the result. The cautious colleague, the trainer and the quota are the only shown causes, and none is shown at work on that event.

The reading records the win as a result and makes no claim about how it was skated.
