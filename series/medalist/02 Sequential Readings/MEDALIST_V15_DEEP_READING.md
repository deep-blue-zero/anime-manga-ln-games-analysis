# Volume 15 — no trial of good and evil

The short program ends in the first chapter. The last skater, a team-mate, beats the heroine by a third of a point, and the heroine goes into the free skate second. Nothing else is skated in this volume. It closes a day later in the six-minute warm-up, with a jump in mid-air.

Between those two points the volume belongs to the coach. Within an hour of the result a trainer tells him something his pupil has never told him: that she believes she skates at the cost of his life, and that to her a failure to win is a punishment for having chosen skating. He protests that he has denied this many times. The trainer's answer is that he has, in words, and that the girl reads his face. He does suffer when she loses. He does pare himself down. Those are facts, and she knows them.

He spends the evening doing what two people ask of him, which is to talk about himself. To his patron, beside a lake, he tells the part of his history he left out in December: a father who had broken down and who told his son that a man who only costs money has no right to be alive; a brother who took that father's shame on himself; a first competition entered alone, a score of nine points, and a wish to disappear. His patron gives him a rule in return. A problem is not to be met with a trial of good and evil, and making oneself the villain is one way of holding such a trial.

That night, in a hotel stairwell, the pupil says it to him herself. She adds what no adult guessed. She does not want to be relieved of the weight. She was glad in December because she thought he had given her a part to play. He does not argue. He promises her something about his own life instead.

The reading's argument is that Volume 15 reopens the settlement of Volume 13 and replaces it with something harder and better founded. The coach finds that, while denying that sacrifice makes anyone strong, he had put his faith in his own. The pupil is shown to have heard his declaration as a commission. The volume gives the cause of both, in a childhood the series had only outlined, and it gives four adults and one girl the same lesson about blame. The reading records two of its own earlier judgements as revised by this evidence, and keeps apart what the trainer infers, what the pupil says, and what the coach concludes.

Adults stirred his fears with their own guesses and steered him by a force a child could not resist, in good will and without noticing. He was handed other people's failed predictions. To find a cause is not to pass sentence on anyone's character, and must not be. He has kept the whole blame so as to shield those adults from a verdict. That what he did for the sake of their smiles should become a lifelong punishment is wrong. Would he turn into an arrogant man, hated by the world, if he stopped taking all of it on himself? Of course not. He need not make that suffering small. (`0040`.)

The last memories come out of order. An arena under applause. His partner holds him and says that it is all right. It was the first time in her career that she had missed the podium at the national championships, and she had been thinking of continuing if the result allowed. The costume had been paid for by her father, who said it was for her. To go on skating he had needed a result. Outside, his teacher was telling him to continue with a new partner and to make his worth known, when a message arrived from the patron. His wife had died. The young man said that he had been allowed enough, and thanked him. (`0041`.)

Furthest back is the boy at the screen. A man in black skates, and the boy does not know what the sport is or who this is. The man proves the strength of a life with his body alone. The boy wants to do that: to make people feel that a person has worth even without the power to be useful to others, that a life burning can give this much heat. Pages of search queries follow, on cost, on height, on age, on jobs. He wants someone to take him into that world. Someone. (`0042`.)

The patron says he has long regretted that he never tried to know the boy who worked for him. After the funeral, his pressing him toward the ice show and saying he wanted him to compete again came from that. It hurt that he should give up his dream on their account. He wants to carry, even now, what adults laid on him as a child, as one of the adults who ought to have protected him. The coach says there is no child this size. A lanky brat, says the patron, then and now; one child to worry about. The coach weeps over the rail. (`0043`.)

**Score 61**. In his room, over too much food, he puts it to himself. He wanted to save a child who could not step into that world. The devoted coach he imagined made her carry a mission. All along, while denying sacrifice, he had put his faith in his own. He telephones the trainer. (`0045`.)

In the lounge the heroine lies with her head in her mother's lap. Her mother has arrived after the short program and is sorry to have missed it. The girl says she is not lonely and will win tomorrow. Then she says there is something in the film she does not understand. The ghost goes, the boy stops being a genius, and that is called a happy ending. Had he stayed a genius he could have made everyone happy. So many people help him; for the sake of his art he ought to be willing to let himself disappear. In his place she would not mind being taken over by the ghost and vanishing. Her mother holds her and says she would hate that beyond anything. It is only a film, the girl says, laughing. She is glad she can skate. She will win without fail. Her mother looks down at her and does not answer. The coach arrives and the girl sits bolt upright. (`0046`.)

They settle the layout. Is there anything else she wants to talk about? Nothing, she says brightly. He is about to begin when both telephones sound. Three of the hotel's lifts have failed. Her room is twenty floors above his. He carries her up the stairs on his back, over her objection, for reasons he lists: sleep, an early start, the quadruple Salchow tomorrow. One has to prepare to excess if one is aiming at gold, and had she not told the press she would give everything? She asks whether that interview was wrong. He asks how long she has thought that her skating makes those around her suffer. She asks to be put down. (`0047`.)

Since she entered middle school, she says, she has felt herself going back to the self she disliked, the one who could not skate. Her mother cannot come home because of work, on her account. He agonizes when she loses, and works so hard. When she is no good, many kind people have to work harder. How is that different from before? If she takes the gold medal everyone is repaid and happy. To be told that she need not strain is what she hates. To be told one need not do what one must is like having one's strength drained. When he told her his past in December she was very glad, because she thought he had at last trusted her with a part. Even if those around her suffer she cannot let go of skating. She wants to live on the ice. For that she needs the gold. He wants to make her a happy skater; she wants to become the strongest and make happy the teacher who helped her. She does not want to be treated as weak. She wants to hold all of it, to carry and carry, and still to be the strongest skater, who lands lightly. (`0048`.)

He had meant to tell her that drawing in other people is no bad thing. He does not. The heat with which she chases a dream with her retreat cut off is the essence of her strength, and he recognizes it: it is what he saw on a screen as a boy. It is one with great suffering. To tell her that she must not wound her own heart would make her wish a mistake. May he press that flame down with nothing but a rightness he has reasoned out? He remembers a girl in the rain telling him that adults who cannot protect children are only afraid of blame. He knew that feeling. When he was made to choose a road by the words that it was for his own good, it hurt. Guidance along the safe road only is a rightness for the guardian's sake. A coach must widen his sight and not fix another's happiness by his own experience. (`0049`.)

He thanks her for having tried to make him happy. If it lets her bring out her strength, he will not deny her wish to win a medal for someone's sake. However precarious, he will stand by her decision. In return he makes a promise. Some day he will audition for the ice show again. He will go on supporting her with all his strength. If she becomes a medallist he is happy, and he does not become unhappy if she does not. His caption is that he changes shape, to protect the freedom of both her present self and her future one. He and his brother tried to erase their father's suffering in his place. His own problem he means to solve himself. If she tries to win gold for his life, she will end by living his life. He asks her to check with her own heart what each thing she takes up was chosen for, and to choose only the suffering of which she can say that this is her life. He will do the same, and will ask for help when he cannot manage alone. (`0050`.)

She says yes, after a pause. He does not know whether it reached her. The trainer had warned him that because he has been wholly a coach, talk of his own future might make her fear that he would leave. He is ready to say as often as needed that his dream now is to be her coach. What she says is: does that mean that some day she will see him skate in an ice show? She is overjoyed. He checks again, with December in mind. He is not leaving coaching. She knows. He told her that his dream is to be the coach who makes her happy, and she knows it is true; and the ice show is a promise. He tells her what he found today. The wall for both of them is a sense of guilt. A failure, examined, has causes, and a cause is not a crime. Neither of them needs to be punished. Next day Daria pins up the heroine's hair behind the curtain. The six are announced. ## What the trainer says, and what the heroine says

Volume 13 ended with the coach telling his pupil that she had cost him nothing and that coaching her was his dream, and with her repeating it and rewriting her vow. The reading judged that the silence at the centre of the pair had been broken from his side and heard. This volume shows what was heard.

There are three statements, and they are not the same statement.

The first is hers, to the press. She has made many people suffer and been a burden. She must repay it with the best result. She will give everything so that the self who was allowed to skate need not be ashamed. It is the creed of Volume 10, that a gold medal proves she belongs on the ice, spoken into recorders a month after the bench.

The second is the trainer's. The girl feels she skates at the sacrifice of her coach's life; she reads faces; his suffering when she loses is real and she sees it; so winning is her only means of lessening what he pays, and losing is a punishment for having chosen to skate. The trainer has grounds: a shared room in Bangkok, and a year of watching both of them. It is still an account given by one adult to another. The phrase about his life is the trainer's.

The third is hers, to him. It is narrower than the trainer's and goes further. She dates it: since she entered middle school. She names two people, her mother who cannot come home and her coach who agonizes and labours, and then the many kind people who have to work harder when she fails. She says it feels like a return to the self who could not skate. She does not say that she ended his career. The belief the ledgers have carried since Volume 9, that she cost him his own skating, is not what she tells him. What she tells him is about the present.

And she adds two things that no adult in the volume anticipated. Reassurance is what she hates; to be told she need not strain is like being drained. And December did reach her, as something else. She was glad because she thought he had trusted her with a part.

That last sentence corrects the reading's judgement at Volume 13. `MD-CL-0029@3` treated the bench as the first durable answer to her guilt and noted, as a counterweight, that her new vow was still a vow to give him something. The counterweight was the truth of it. He offered her a release, and she took a commission. His declaration that coaching her is his dream gave her a way to pay him: by being the strongest and the happiest. `MD-CL-0029@5` and `MD-CL-0027@7` record the revision. The earlier reading is left as written in its own volume.

It also answers `MD-OQ-V14-03`, and not as the question expected. The reading had cautioned against applying her film's ending, a helper who withdraws, to her coach. The caution stands; nothing here has him withdraw. But her conversation with her mother shows what the ending is to her. She does not think it happy. She would have the gifted self stay and the ordinary one vanish, for the sake of everyone who helped. In Volume 14 she skated that ending cleanly and captioned its last jump as her own. Here she says she would rather be taken over. Her mother hears it as being about her daughter, and the daughter says it is about a film.

## Seen through

The trainer's second lesson is addressed to the coach's method, and it limits a lesson the series taught one volume ago.

In December a senior colleague told him that an adult's face is a reward or a punishment to a child and that a coach's regrets are not the pupil's business. He took it as an instruction to govern his face. He recalls it here and proposes to apply it harder. The trainer's reply is the most exact criticism he has received. Concealment worked for a younger child. This one sees for herself. What she sees is true: his standing does depend on her results, he does give all of himself, and he does punish himself when she loses. To hide a fact from someone who knows it is to lie to her, and the lie will fail. The page has just shown the fact. Two dozen pages earlier, in shadow, he vowed that it was unthinkable for him to fail her again, with the girl standing behind him and looking.

So the mechanism the reading has followed since Volume 10 is confirmed a third time, by the observer best placed to know. `MD-CL-0028@5` keeps its status as a hypothesis about her formation, on which this volume says nothing, and records the mechanism as established: she reads his face first and takes its cause on herself. What changes is the remedy. It is not that he should show less. It is that what he shows should stop being true, which means changing what he does when she loses.

The trainer is equally exact about the other side. The same readable face is why a child who probes adults could trust him at all. She calls it his greatest strength. The reading had treated his face as the instrument of her burden. It is also the ground of the bond, by the judgement of a third party who has no reason to flatter him. `MD-CL-0004@13` records that his habit of indirection and of managing how things land is named, to his face, as acting that has been seen through, and that he accepts it.

## The house

The coach's history has been told three times before: by a colleague in Volume 9, in fragments in Volume 12, and by himself on the bench in Volume 13. Each telling began at a rink. This one begins at home, and it supplies the cause the others lacked.

His father had been driven out of his work and could not accept what had happened to him. He told his second son two things in one breath. A man who only costs money has no worth in being alive and ought to disappear. And if there is something you want to do, you must tell me. The boy could not answer the second without contradicting the first. Wanting costs money. The series prints the first speech as a blur when the adult first remembers it, and in clear type only after he has been promised that no one will be judged.

Much that the ledgers have recorded as his character follows from that room without strain. He would skate only on what he earned. He could not ask his parents, and called asking unfilial. He refused a reduced fee. When he did ask, once, and was refused for good reason, his first thought was that he should not have asked. When he was then helped, his thought was that he must repay the favour with results at all costs. That sentence is his, about his patrons, at twenty-three. It is the sentence his pupil gave the press at thirteen.

The house also explains what he believes in. His father's pledge was to sacrifice himself for his sons, and it had no strength behind it. His brother took on the father's shame and called it protecting his brothers' freedom. Two people in that house tried to carry a suffering that was not theirs, and the boy was told to live freely on the proceeds. He names the pattern himself in the stairwell: he and his brother tried to erase their father's pain in his place.

And it explains why he skates. The boy who saw a champion on a screen wanted to show that a person has worth even without being useful to others. The reading can say plainly what that answers. It is his father's sentence, turned over.

The patron's diagnosis, to which the coach assents, is that his ideal of a coach is a figure made from what that boy wished for: an adult who gives everything and never breaks. The coach's own formulation, an hour later, is the volume's central sentence. While denying sacrifice, he had put his faith in his own. In Volume 9 he told a furious child in the rain that sacrifice is the self-harm of those who feel inferior and that one can win without it. He believed it of her, and of his pupil. He had not noticed that he excepted himself. `MD-CL-0010@15` and `MD-CL-0025@9` record this as the completion of his account and the revision of his creed by its holder.

One limit should be kept. The page distinguishes two things the boy wanted. In his own memory the wish is to be taken into that world by someone. In his patron's reading the wish was for an adult who would not break. The first is what he did for a child in Volume 1. The second is what he has been trying to be since. He agrees with the patron, with the word probably.

## The circle, in its own colour

In December he told his pupil that as a boy he skated with adults who shared their ice and knowledge, forbade him to jump as too tall and too late, and that he hid his practice for years and left believing he had no talent. A creator's page softened it at the time: they never saw him jump, and he bears no grudge. The reading recorded the account as his.

This volume tells it again with nothing judged in advance, which is the method the patron has just asked for, and the facts move. The prohibition was advice with no rule behind it. He practised at another rink so as not to trouble them and entered a competition without telling them. They came anyway. What they said afterwards was a mixture: that they felt betrayed, that they would have helped had he asked, that the costume was wonderful. The next day they apologized for having scolded him unheard. He left because he had concluded, by himself, that he was in the way.

Nothing in the December account was false. It had been sorted. In it, kind adults gave a wrong verdict and a boy obeyed. Here a boy who could not ask for anything hid what he was doing from people who would have helped, was hurt, and then took their relief at his going as proof. The margin carries a note in the author's voice that there is no truth in the idea that tall people cannot jump. The adults were wrong on the point. The patron's account of how that worked is the careful one: they steered a child with a force he could not resist, in good will, and their mistaken forecast was then counted as his own choice.

The reading's use of this is twofold. It corrects the chronology and institutions ledgers, which now hold both tellings. And it is the volume's demonstration of its own rule. `MD-CL-0031@1` registers that rule as a claim.

## Four adults and a girl

Five people in this volume say the same thing in different words, and three of them did not know the others would.

His teacher said it first, years ago. Skating is a team contest. Guardians and coaches are not sacrifices to a skater's life; they are participants in the sport. No one continues alone. Find people who will bear it with you, and talk to them. Be willing to burden someone, or stop.

His patron's wife said it next. There is a limit to what one person can do, and to revere a strength that does not exist is the shameful thing. Share the problem. There may be a way of helping that weighs on neither side.

The trainer says it now. Do not hide what is true. Do not weigh one suffering against another, since any pain can be made to look small by comparison. Borrow someone else's eyes.

The patron says it by the lake. Hold no trial. Blame taken whole is a way to stop thinking. So is the verdict that everyone was kind.

And Daria reaches it alone. Her program is a verdict of guilt on the adults around her friend and on herself. Having skated it, she remembers that the film it comes from warns against exactly that. To make someone the villain is to stop seeing her. She had done it to a younger girl in the space of a morning, and she undoes it with a bow.

The film says one more thing, which the others leave implicit. Its heroine asks how the size of her pain could have been made known except by spending a life, and resolves to make a dress of it and dance. The alternative to injury as proof is art. Iruka's refusal in Volume 13 to be seen covered in wounds, and the heroine's attempt that month to ruin her legs, belong to the same question. `MD-CL-0026@8` takes the five statements together as the series' fullest answer to it: what is given in care is not paid for by suffering, because those who give it are not creditors but members of the same undertaking.

The coach was told this at twenty and at twenty-three, by people he loved, and acted on it once, and went back. The volume does not present the lesson as easy to keep.

## The stairwell

The conversation the series has deferred for six volumes takes place on a landing between the twentieth and twenty-fourth floors, and it does not go as either party planned.

He arrives with a prepared message, tested on the trainer by telephone: that relying on others is no fault. Before he can give it he carries her up twenty flights against her protest, with good reasons, and tells her that one must over-prepare for gold. An hour earlier he recognized that he had put his faith in his own sacrifice. The page does not comment. She asks to get down.

She then says what she thinks, and it is a claim as much as a confession. She will not give up skating whatever it costs those around her. She needs the gold in order to stay. She wants to return what he gives. She will not be handled as weak. She wants the weight and the lightness both. It is the first time she has owned the cost of her skating to others without apologizing for it. `MD-CL-0002@15` records it as the strongest assertion of her agency in the series, and records what it asserts: the right to carry.

His answer is a decision about himself. He sets the prepared message aside. His reason is drawn from the evening: to tell her what is good for her, along the safe road, is what was done to him. He will not call her wish a mistake. He will watch over it. `MD-CL-0007@12` notes that the coach who in December drew a line at self-harm here declines to draw one at a self-imposed burden, and says why. The reading's evaluation is favourable with a reservation. He has distinguished between forbidding a harm and overruling a purpose, and he pairs his restraint with a change in himself. The reservation is his own: he sees in her the heat that he saw in a champion on a screen, and says in the same caption that it is one with great suffering. A witness in Volume 13 called that heat a last flame.

What he gives her is neither reassurance nor correction. He promises to audition for the ice show again some day. The promise is about his life. It removes the premise that his fulfilment rests on her results, which no statement could remove while it remained true. He adds the sentence he could not make stick in December, now as a promise: happy if she medals, not unhappy if she does not. And he tells her the thing his own history taught him an hour before. If she wins for his life, she will be living his life.

He also gives her a test, in place of a prohibition. Choose only the suffering of which you can say, this is my life. He binds himself to it and to asking for help.

Her reception should be stated exactly. She agrees, after a silence. She is delighted that she will one day see him skate. She says, correctly this time, what he told her in December, and that she knows it is true. She calls the ice show a promise. She does not say that the weight is lifted, and she does not withdraw anything she said on the landing. He does not know whether it reached her, and the page prints his doubt twice. `MD-CL-0027@7` holds that the asymmetry which organized the pair since Volume 9 is now resolved in form: each has said to the other what had been withheld. Whether it is resolved in effect is the free skate's to show, and the volume stops before it.

## Daria

The skater introduced in Volume 13 as a finalist by another's misfortune is given a program, a motive, a fault and its repair in two chapters.

Her coaching relation is a fourth instance, in two volumes, of an adult's position on a young skater's risk. Her coach thought the costume change a mistake and said so. She gave a reason that was about who she wants to be, and he backed her and was proud to. It worked on the day. The heroine's coach, watching without that knowledge, judged that he would have changed the music or the layout. `MD-CL-0024@9` sets the two side by side. One coach supported a risk he had argued against. The other, the same evening, decides not to argue against a burden.

Her hostility in Volume 14 is explained and withdrawn. She had spent years beside a friend whose home was harming her and had not seen it. She was told it could not be helped. She has never forgiven the phrase. A newcomer who said lightly that she would skate for that friend's share looked to her like someone else who did not know what she was talking about. Her account of what Iruka has lost is the most detailed statement of institutional cost in the series: 850 points, a senior debut without a record behind it, and an Olympics three years away that depends on both.

The exchange that follows is between two girls, with their coaches standing by. One apologizes for an injury she intended and did not inflict. The other thanks her for the anger and states a principle of her own, that she is afraid of hurting people without knowing it. That principle sits oddly beside her own conduct toward her coach, whom she has kept ignorant for a year of what she feels. The page does not draw the connection, and the reading only notes that within the day she tells him.

The hair is a small matter and belongs here. By a strip, the heroine asked that her hair for the free skate be done like Iruka's, and Daria did it and sent Iruka the picture. The vow that Daria called presumptuous is being kept with her hands.

## Rulers

Volume 14 gave the series a word for how programs are judged: a shared ruler of beauty. This volume uses the same object for something else, once, in the coach's last captions. As a boy he looked at a ruler in order to stay good and ordinary, and he no longer remembers who gave it to him.

The patron's black and white, the crows on the jacket and on the wires of a childhood street, the single white bird among them at the lake, the costume that turns from black to red, the doves and the rainbow of the final captions all belong to one figure. People are measured and sorted, and the volume is against it. `MD-CL-0030@2` records that the doctrine of the shared ruler is given its boundary by the work itself: a ruler is for programs.

It also records the counter-case to that doctrine which this volume supplies. Daria's coach prizes originality, was himself a skater of that kind, and backed a device the criteria do not ask for. She won the segment.

## Written Japanese

The first two chapters are 鴉の濡れ羽, the wet feather of a crow: a set phrase for glossy black hair, here also a bird that recurs in the art. The third is 記憶の距離, the distance of memory. The fourth is 牧師と子羊, a pastor and a lamb; the patron's daughter's name is written with the character for sheep, and the chapter is a confession heard without judgement. The fifth is 螺旋の途中, midway on a spiral, over a stairwell.
