# Volume 13 — not a substitute

The promised day goes as badly for the heroine as a day can. Her short program at the national junior championship opens to a single where a combination should be, and the rules give her no second chance at it. She falls on the Lutz. The step sequence that was hers alone is called two levels down. She falls on the Axel. Forty-seven points; twenty-four skaters advance and she is not one of them. A finalist of the world junior series is out of her own country's championship before the free skate.

The next afternoon her rival, who had fallen for the first time in her life the day before, skates three quadruples in a program she rebuilt overnight and wins by thirty-six points. That night her coach tells her he is finished coaching her. He is not angry. On the road he walked, he says, a skater ends by letting her coaches go.

The volume's last third is the consequence in Nagoya. The heroine cuts school to train, breaks a blade, runs a fever, and tells her coach on a railway platform that she would sooner ruin her legs than lose. She stays off the train he boards. He finds her beside a broken merry-go-round and, in place of the reassurances that failed an hour earlier, tells her how he once punished himself in the same way, who stopped him, and what he decided then. Coaching her is not what he settled for, he says. It is his dream. She hears it.

The reading's argument is that Volume 13 resolves the question the series has carried since Volume 9, what each of the pair believes the other gave up, and that it does so by having three people refuse the idea that being cared for must be paid for in suffering: a coach to his pupil, a rival to herself on the ice, and an injured skater in a hospital bed. It also ends one coaching relationship in the way its own terms required, and the reading keeps apart what that coach says, what his pupil infers, and what remains unknown.

## The short program

`MD-OQ-V12-01` asked whether her steadiness would hold. What the page shows is a skater who was steady until her first jump and then lost every element that depends on precision.

The preparation is not presented as hollow. She ate, laughed, warmed up well. Her performance side had moved furthest since Bangkok, and the hall answered it. Then one jump opened. The page offers a mechanism for that jump and no more: a new, harder entry with the head dropped. It offers none for the rest. The Lutz that she landed in Bangkok and fell on in its free skate fell again. The step sequence, level four at two championships, came back at level two with a fault in each cluster. The double Axel, the jump commentators singled out abroad, fell.

The reading does not supply a cause. Two things can be said. The junior short program is built so that an early error cannot be repaired; the same rule that gave the field one layout takes away the second attempt. And the faults were not confined to jumps, which weighs against a purely technical account and is all that can be said for a psychological one. The volume's own later explanation concerns what she did afterwards and not why she fell.

`MD-CL-0008@12` records the consequence for the series' oldest technical claim. The foundation her coach built is not a guarantee. On this day it did not hold even in its own element.

The institutional result is harsher than any before it. Fourth place twice left her standing. A cut removes her from the event. A caption has already said what the event governs: entry to the senior nationals and the world juniors. She remains a finalist of the series by a result abroad that the series did not show.

## Being thought not in earnest

What she says in the thicket is the most exact statement of her fear the series has given, and it is not the fear the adults guessed.

She is not afraid of failing or of being laughed at. She says she fails at too many things for that. She is afraid of being taken for someone who is not serious. That is why Hikaru is the one person she cannot face, and why the first thing she does after the result is perform contentment for her family. It is also why a child's idle question in the stands, whether she practised, lands where it does, and why her remedy in Nagoya is visible excess.

Read back, this organizes a good deal. In Volume 11 a team-mate asked whether she was serious, since the serious relocate. In Volume 10 her creed was that gold proves she ought to be on the ice. In this volume the proof is gone and the fear is exposed: without a result she has, in her own words, no means of showing she is in earnest. `MD-CL-0001@13` records that recognition, the first good of the series, has turned inside out. She now has standing, and experiences it as a charge she can default on.

Her coach's first answer is evidence. He shows her strangers with banners and says that support does not depend on the result. It is the playground sentence of Volume 9 with something to point at. It does not prevent what follows.

## What Hikaru takes back

In Volume 10 Hikaru decided that Inori, and not Inori's coach, was the true likeness of her own coach. In Volume 12 she wanted the medal as something taken from a small copy of him. In this volume she withdraws the reading twice, in her own voice.

In the thicket the withdrawal is quick and rests on one observation. A girl who laughs for the adults' sake is like Hikaru herself, who told Rio a volume ago how she made herself agreeable to a household. To Riley Fox that night it is fuller. She had looked only at the coach she thought she saw in the girl. The skating that moved her was made of the girl's own effort and courage.

What she puts in its place is plainer and, the reading judges, better supported by everything since Volume 6. She likes this girl for her tenacity, and because that tenacity keeps her from being alone. Her attention to Inori has always outrun any use Inori could be to her. Here she says what it was for.

It leads her to the larger admission. Her own achievements have never felt hers. They were his jumps. She has been unafraid in competition because she was hiding behind him, which explains her ease better than the rule about falls ever did. `MD-CL-0003@12` records this, and that the crossed likeness registered at Volume 10 as one girl's construction has been retracted by its author.

## Leaving the garden, and being left

Her free skate is the longest sequence the series has drawn, and it is an argument in three steps.

She dances him, in the manner he gave her, and then stops and dances herself. The technical reading is supplied by the other team's coach: line to curve, a man's part to a woman's. She re-made it overnight with her coach of record. She did it knowing the clause that he ceases to coach her if she leaves his road, and the page gives her fear in so many words.

She then does what he did. Three quadruples, one of them the jump his old coach remembered. No fall. The widest margin the series has printed. And the testimony of the man who stood beside him is that this, of all her performances, was the most like his, in the one respect she did not rehearse: the heat of someone burning what she has.

And he ends it. `MD-CL-0016@11` read his contract as a threat, and Volume 12 had him say that a loss would finish things. Neither is what happens. She wins, in her own way, and he stops. He says in terms that it is not anger and that he forbids her nothing. His reason is that his road includes the point at which a skater discards her coaches, and that she cannot pass it while he guides her.

Three things follow, and the reading keeps their status distinct. First, what he says: this is the road. Second, what she infers at once: that he concealed his coaching from the start so that the role could be made not to have existed, and so that he could leave. It is her inference; it fits four volumes of secrecy better than any reason given before. Third, what the first-lesson memory shows: that he told her at the beginning to find an impulse of her own, and that his teaching would not make her happy. He knew the cost and named it.

What is not known is whether this is the whole of it. His pupil thought in Volume 12 that he regards himself as without character. His old coach said the talent needed no adult though the child did. He leaves his boots. An under-jacket aside has her keeping them for him, and has Riley Fox looking for a way to hold him. `MD-CL-0016@12` records the act and leaves his motive beyond his stated reason open.

Her situation is exact. She is thirteen, in a dormitory, the national junior champion, and without the coach she left a home to follow. She has Riley Fox, who has just promised her former guardian to protect her; a choreographer who disapproves of what she did; and a teacher of steps who was himself once discarded. She has been told that this is what the road is.

## Permit, then limit

Riley Fox's doctrine on risk is disclosed after the fact and is new to the series' range.

She let a thirteen-year-old with a recent back injury attempt three quadruples. She did it by agreement: once the girl's growth stops, quadruples will be limited for a time. Her reasoning is that a skater will not accept talk of peaking until the wish to challenge has been discharged, and that no guardian's argument can answer for the regret of jumps that later became impossible. So let her put the result on the board now.

Set beside the others, it is a fourth position. One coach refuses needless sacrifice and has just, for the first time, forbidden something. One held that children should bear no pain but competition, and his pupil is in hospital. One burned what he had and has now walked away. This one permits the fire once, with a promise attached, and stands guarantor. `MD-CL-0024@7` records it. The margin note beside her speech, in the trainer's voice, says that nothing here is always so.

Her profile page, at a creator's weight, gives the source: a gifted child forced to excel, who found in sport the one field her parents could not enter, and who counts her life from the day a medal freed her.

## The platform and the bench

The quarrel and its repair are the volume's centre for the two principals, and they are one experiment run twice.

On the platform he says everything. He tells her that her results cannot disappoint him, that his troubled face was not about her, that she owes no one a medal, and that he did not give up skating to coach a prodigy. That last sentence is the answer to the belief she has carried since Volume 9. None of it arrives. By his own analysis she heard his first clause as an order not to skate, and nothing after.

What she says in return is the fullest statement of her side. She is expected now. The event was one she had to win. She would ruin her legs sooner than lose, and having lost she does not want to protect her body. And why does he only look troubled? He names it: a wish to punish herself, long held down. The series has a word for it from Volume 4, and he uses it.

He holds one line for the first time since that volume. He cannot approve of missing school, and her training volume has to be regulated while she grows. `MD-CL-0007@11` records that the coach who chose in Volume 11 never to be the brake has applied it, in words, to her face.

By the bench he does not repeat himself. He tells her a story in which he is the one who hid his practice from the adults he depended on, believed their verdict, and then tried to atone by self-harm. He tells her who stopped him and with what question. He dates his decision to coach to that time, years before he met her. Only then does he say that coaching her is his dream and no substitute. She repeats it accurately and draws the consequence herself.

The difference between the two attempts is the volume's lesson about conveying, and it is consistent with every earlier instance. Instruction that arrives as correction is not taken in. What is taken in comes sideways: a game in Volume 10, a quota in Volume 11, a story here. He learned the last step, to listen first and to stop defending himself, from another coach that week.

`MD-CL-0027@5` records that the silence at the centre of the pair is broken. He has said that she ended nothing. She has heard it. She has not, on the page, told him what she believed. Her vow changes shape on the spot. Since Volume 8 it has been to make him the coach of a gold medalist, or of the world's best. Now it is the strongest and the happiest. Her own well-being has entered what she owes him. `MD-CL-0029@3` treats this as the first durable answer to her guilt, on the ground that it came from the person the guilt was about and with a history attached.

## A coach's face

Gori's lesson completes a thought he began at the camp, and it corrects the reading in one respect.

In Volume 10 he guessed that the girl watches her coach's face for disappointment. Volume 11 led the reading to downgrade the hypothesis built on that guess, because her own account pointed elsewhere: to guilt at being cared for. This volume shows that both were describing one mechanism from two ends. She does read his face. What she reads there is not disapproval. It is his distress on her behalf, which she takes as a cost she has imposed. Her complaint on the platform is that he looks troubled.

So `MD-CL-0028@3` is revised and not restored. Its premise, that her capacities were formed under deprivation, stays where Volume 11 left it. Its observation about the face is confirmed, by her words and by his recognition. And the remedy Gori prescribes is aimed at the adult: a coach's regret is not the pupil's business, and apology offered to show sincerity is one more weight.

Tsukasa has been doing exactly that since Volume 9. He called a fourth place his complete defeat, wept before an Olympic champion that fourth was not good enough, and told a colleague he could not be proud until she won. `MD-CL-0010@13` records that he has now seen it, named it a blind spot, and told his pupil that his results-face was never about her. The man remembered for smiling after a death, for his daughter's sake, is the patron who first backed him.

## Not covered in wounds

The hospital chapter gives Iruka's wish and its refusal, and in doing so states the volume's thesis a third time.

Her wish is old and specific. The girl who rescued her once should see her hurt and rescue her again. The injury is, among other things, a chance of that. The series has drawn the temptation before in other forms: a creed that sacrifice proves earnestness, a child who wants to ruin her legs to show she cared.

Against it stands a promise made to her as a child, that she would not need to be covered in wounds to be noticed. And then her own reasoning, which is the strongest thing anyone says in the volume. Her talent was raised by people who knew the hurt her. Strength is therefore not a denial of the wound. To choose pity would be to disown the years they spent protecting her.

That is the same structure as the coach's sentence on the bench and the rival's on the ice. What was given in care is not a debt to be discharged by suffering; it is what the strength is made of. `MD-CL-0026@6` registers the convergence, and that in two cases of three the person says it of herself.

Mika's visit is the quietest act in the volume. She does not ask to be seen and assumes she is forgotten. She leaves what helped her when she was the one in bed. She knows nothing, so far as shown, of a vow or a grievance. The girl behind the curtain cannot call to her, and her voice begins to come back afterwards.

## Written Japanese

The second chapter is 本気の証明, proof of being in earnest. 本気 is the word a team-mate used in Volume 11 for those who relocate, the word Tsukasa uses twice on the bench for what he once wanted and for what he wants now, and the word in her thought when she cuts school. Its partner 証明 has belonged to the champion-coach since Volume 6.

Hikaru's decision uses 箱庭, a miniature garden, from her program's title. Her coach's reason uses 道, the road, and a verb for letting go; his word for what his teachers' ideas amounted to is ornament. Her word for what he told her to find is 衝動, an impulse.

The last chapter is 人魚と刃, a mermaid and a blade, the second word glossed with an old reading. The injured skater's name means dolphin; she has lost her voice and the use of a leg; the heroine's blade breaks.

Tsukasa's declaration turns on 代わり, a substitute, negated, and on 夢, which he had used of what he lost and now uses of what he has. Her vow adds 幸せ, happy, to 最強, strongest. His proposed second aim is a loan-word for resilience that she mishears. His word for her new state is the 冷たい気持ち of Volume 4.

Gori's lesson uses 報酬 and 罰, reward and punishment, of an adult's expression. Iruka's two options set 可哀想, pitiable, against 強い, and her resolve negates being born again.

The heroine's phrase for her rival and the rival's for herself are the same: 希望の光. None of this establishes how any line sounds.
