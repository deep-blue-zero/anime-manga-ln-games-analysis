# Volume 10 — another pair of eyes

Volume 9 ended on a reunion and a list with her name on it. Volume 10 has no competition for its heroine. It goes back to fill the months the last volume skipped, takes her to a four-day camp with every rival she has, and spends its longest stretch in a hotel room where coaches drink and talk.

What the volume supplies is diagnosis. Three experienced adults say what they think they are looking at. A head coach who was a schoolteacher for ten years thinks the girl listens so well because she is watching faces for disappointment. A head coach who had Yodaka for one season thinks the man never needed a coach at all, and could see himself from above. And her own coach, having heard both, decides that her speed of growth was never ease; it was a concentration that pares her down. He also decides something about himself: that he has the sense the second man described, that he denied it for years out of inferiority, and that he will now try to hand it to her.

The girl gives her own account, to the rival she cannot look at without crying. She has never aimed at gold because she had grounds. She aims at it because she cannot forgive a self that fails to take it, and because gold is how she proves she ought to be on the ice. On the night of her defeat her coach told her that a person is not made of what others evaluate. Nine months later she states the opposite as her creed. He does not hear it. The rival does, and concludes that this girl, and not her coach, is the one who resembles Yodaka.

The reading's argument is that Volume 10 moves the series' question from whether she will be recognized to what she and the adults round her take her to be made of, and that it deliberately leaves three accounts standing: an instrument formed by lonely years, a will that punishes itself, and a child who loves steps. It is careful about which of these is observation and which is a tired man's inference after midnight.

## Developments in disclosure order

**Score 37** opens in an aquarium. An older man reads a letter aloud. A girl wants a level-four step sequence and asks to be taught by an ice-dance teacher with Olympic experience. It is signed Kamisaki Hikaru. He asks the man standing apart whether he is deliberately having her do what he once did, and whether she knows that he taught him. He writes out the classes on the letter: if the age limits do not change again she is a junior until seventeen, and the Games she can enter are seven years away, when she is twenty. The same as you, Jun. A country like theirs sends three, chosen by a committee, by other people. Seven years is too long. Do not make her keep winning. Over that time the body changes and so does the heart. Growth is loss. Yodaka's answer is two words: I could. One place goes to whoever wins the national title before the Games. She will take gold at every competition. What decides it is necessity and not other people. He asks for help and addresses the man as Takamine. (`MD-EV-V10-0002`.)

A caption turns the clock back five months, to February. Tsukasa introduces a new coach to the club's parents: Kōhei, who agreed at once when he telephoned. The parents are old fans of Hitomi and are delighted. Hitomi and Kōhei tease Tsukasa about his early days until he asks them to stop, and tells Inori that when someone makes a toy of you, you may be angry even if they are enjoying themselves. (`0003`.)

Then he lays cards on a table, one for each jump she has landed in competition: every single and double, four triples, and the quadruple Salchow. Two cards are still in his hand, the triple Lutz and the triple Axel. He names two tasks. Get the Lutz. Raise the quality of every jump. He explains the criteria by which a jump earns a positive mark and the rule that the top marks need the first three, of which height is the hardest. Hers have never passed plus three. In junior the aim is plus five. The rest will be decided from Kōhei's reports "while I am away". (`0004`.)

So she knew. She tells him she will not telephone. When he broke his ribs she understood that he might one day be absent at an important competition, and she wants to be able to skate well then. By the time he returns she will have grown enough to surprise him. He wails that watching her grow is the best part of his work and that he hates this. Her thought, with a dark face: he decided it himself. And: I hate it too. She asks him to leave his drawstrings. Kōhei, encouraging her, calls her by his old pupil's name. (`0005`.)

Her mother arrives with news of her own. From spring she goes back to full-time work. She has always travelled to away events; her daughter will be a middle-schooler; it seemed the time. That night Inori's knees hurt. The hospital finds nothing: growing pains, worst in the evening, which is when private ice and tests are held. Hitomi advises making friends with the pain. A message arrives from the federation. (`0006`.)

It is an invitation to the selection meeting for the Junior Grand Prix. Kōhei explains over dinner. These are overseas events. The skater chosen is the one who can win abroad, because anyone sent for the country is expected on the podium. A diagram sets the road she had imagined, best at home and then abroad, against the real one, in which a skater is sent abroad first. Her mother, whose elder daughter stopped as a novice, says this is a world she does not know. The selection is in the evening. (`0007`.)

Tsukasa telephones, shouting congratulations from a dark street. She says it has been one day and she is lonely already. His advice is to use the pain: singles for a while, and the basic turns, with Kōhei as the model. She wonders whether she is so bad that she must begin again. He says what is hard in sport is to go on being strong. Her body is changing and cannot go back. A skater at the top has to be chosen again every year. From this stage she becomes not the one who chooses but the one who is chosen. Kōhei adds that the Olympics, too, are decided by being chosen. She answers him with her absent coach's name. (`0008`.)

A wordless montage covers spring. At the selection she skates alone before rows of federation staff with his cords wound round her wrist. She is named, with two others, for two events. Her thought is a question: why? (`0009`.)

The story reaches the present. A month after the selection the Lux party arrives at a national training rink in Osaka. Tsukasa is still marvelling that she did all this while he was away. Under her cap she is sober. At the selection she fell on the triple Lutz. Her legs hurt and she cannot practise the quadruple at all. She cannot now skate the layout of last October. I could not grow. (`0010`.)

The camp gathers the juniors assigned abroad and the best novices. Ako, who beat her to the podium, says her aim here is to beat Inori, and gives a reason: Inori is the first skater Hikaru ever came up into the stands to watch. Ryoka, Ema, Suzu and Rio are there. (`0011`.)

Hikaru watches her go, flushed and smiling. Inori, at a tap: there is no going back to laughing together. (`0012`.)

She resolves to leave the camp stronger than she lost, and asks the first person she meets to show her what a junior who can fight abroad looks like. It is Iruka, last season's winner of the series final and now a special designated skater. (`0013`.)

The camp divides into groups; Inori is with Iruka, Ako and Hikaru. She resolves to grow at double speed. (`0017`.)

Then the rules that give the chapter its name. Junior competition has two segments, a short program and a free skate. The short program has three jumping passes, one of which is a required solo jump that rotates each year; this year it is the Lutz. Women may not attempt quadruples in it. She learned the quadruple in place of the Lutz. Every junior woman present has a triple Lutz except her. A federation adviser warns that her take-off risks an edge call and that repetition will mark her as a skater who cannot do the jump. Tsukasa's answer is that she is under no curse; when a jump fails there are causes, and they will find them. For now, find habits, not successes. She hears correction, and hurries. (`0018`.)

Iruka's Lutz goes over her head. Iruka insults her sister again. Inori asks another junior, Daria, what lies between the two. Daria knows only that Mika won her block as a younger novice and that Iruka was once at the same club. A memory surfaces: her mother at a rink, and a small helmeted child proud of a snowman made of ice shavings. Iruka overhears, kicks a chair, and Inori, to keep her out of trouble, throws herself on the floor and tells the staff she fell practising a handstand. (`0019`–`0020`.)

Iruka brings her dinner and feeds her in silence. They made promises when they were small, she says; Mika forgot them and quit; she is just angry. She will not speak to her until she has an Olympic gold medal. Tsukasa, outside the door, is relieved and guilty at listening. His reckoning of the day: she is treating him as someone to show results to, and trying to solve things alone, when he had meant her to know that not being able is allowed. (`0021`.)

He asks Iruka's coach for a word. That night the coaches drink. Gori says that children do not always count adults as the same species, and that Inori is unusually good at putting things into words and telling things apart by ear. Then the room fills. (`0022`.)

**Score 39** is the room. Two head coaches arrive, and several assistants, and the head of the Fukuoka club, who lets slip that until recently Tsukasa was with them. He had been going round the country. (`0023`.)

His account goes back to December. He wanted a trainer for the club. One pupil had lost her double Axel when she began to grow. Hitomi agreed and found the money. He wanted more than that: a medical trainer, chosen from the whole country, someone he could be absolutely sure of, a companion who would go to the Olympics with them. His list of conditions filled a page. The search took until June. He found her in Fukuoka, attached to that club, and got her as a shared trainer in exchange for teaching skating there. (`0024`.)

Gori takes up growth. Ten years ago body change was blamed when skaters stopped being able to jump. Now the common cause is injury. That growth takes jumps away is half fact and half curse. Told that what they have built since infancy will vanish, children restrict food and train without rest to check each day that the jump is still there. He sets out what prolonged energy deficiency does to bone, and where it ends. So he wants more precedents of skaters who keep their jumps and are not hurt. To children who chose this sport he wants to give no pain but that of competing. Tsukasa bursts into tears and agrees. He admits that when he heard of her growing pains he could hardly get up, and that he sounded cheerful only because it was a telephone call. (`0025`–`0026`.)

A sentence follows over the image of a small girl among falling flakes: however adults try, something unreasonable can take everything at once, so one wants at least to protect children from what can be foreseen. The page fixes neither the speaker nor the child. (`0026`.)

Tsukasa raises his hand and asks about the Lutz. His diagnosis is exact. She lays her take-off edge over so deeply that the free leg will not draw through. It is a fault made by a virtue. He could flatten the edge and will not, because it would have to be unlearned. He is telling her this and she cannot do it. When he looks, she is doing a different practice, the basic skating he stressed at the very beginning. Jakuzure calls it a preconception that new information cannot get past. (`0027`.)

Gori offers a reading, saying it may not connect. He thinks she is a child who moves by adults' faces. He had taken her steady gaze for politeness. He now thinks she is searching for what Tsukasa really feels, for whether he is disappointed. Perhaps at school she was with teachers more than with friends; such children learn that adults do not say what they feel. Often they become unable to listen. That she listens so well may be because she trusts him as someone whose face cannot lie. Tsukasa's thought is a question. If the hours she had to spend with adults made up her speed of growth, is that strength built on the days of the cold feeling? He remembers that since his return her eyes slide away from his. Gori's last word is a rule: when teaching does not get through, never put it on the one who receives. (`0028`.)

Next morning he proposes a contest. He will try to land a double Lutz within the hour; she, with Hitomi, will try to get a triple to a two-footed landing. His purpose is stated: to loosen what she thinks she ought to be doing, so that she finds the right practice herself. She voices the preconception while doing the drill. She had thought she could not hold the outside edge because her skating was worse than everyone's. Gori's doctrine is given over the scene: a skater sprints through a maze and sees nothing; a coach looks down from above and becomes another pair of eyes for someone moving inside a dream. She lands the triple Lutz cleanly. Her pleasure is slight; she feels she ought to be able to. Across the rink he lands his double. Her delight is for that. He stands still. So easily, now. Yodaka's sentence returns to him, that he knows he has a rare talent. (`0029`–`0031`.)

The chapter cuts back to the night before, and to the bespectacled head coach of the Okayama club. He agrees with the maze. There are exceptions. He coached Yodaka Jun for one season. (`0032`.)

**Score 40** is that season remembered: an arena that forgets to breathe, a quadruple Lutz to which every judge gives the top mark, a triple Axel taken straight into one spin and then another, a landing that does not sink, a rival in a club jacket saying that it is not a human feat. (`0033`.)

Kyōboku's account is brief. Coaches use video because the body lies to its owner. Yodaka did not need it. He corrected himself alone, as if another self looked down on him. In football they would call it a hawk's eye. He had it for his life as well as for his body: what he lacked, for how long, whom he needed. He did not need a coach. I was an ornament and ashamed to sit beside him. He asked to leave before the season ended. He changed coach every year from his first season as a novice. What Kyōboku wanted to say and did not: your talent may not need an adult, but you, a child, need one who will stay. Sonidori says he feels the same beside Hikaru. If she says she wants to leave, he cannot hold her. Tsukasa protests that no child can master this alone; someone in the family, perhaps? Three older men say no at once. (`0034`–`0035`.)

Tsukasa looks at his hands. He, too, knows his own movement. He sensed something in his body as a boy; it was why he believed he could become a skater alone. But he had no one to guide him, and a map made by self-study, and by the time he met his teacher he expected nothing of himself. When his quickness was praised he denied it, out of inferiority. Did I waste my own weapon? (`0036`.)

A voice at the boards asks why he is practising jumps among the juniors. It is the teacher. He is also Hitomi's father, and the man in the aquarium. The camp goes on. Tsukasa notes that Hikaru has grown and that her landings are lighter, and that under his own teacher her steps will improve; the one edge they had over her is being closed. (`0038`.)

On the third day there are press interviews. Inori cannot do them; the others learned at a novice camp she was too old to attend. Hikaru offers to practise with her. The room, which has been quietly keeping them apart, holds its breath. A clubmate observes that Hikaru always takes care not to overawe people and now seems to have no margin. Hikaru asks her goal. To reach the final and win it. Then, close: you always aim only at gold; tell me on what grounds you believe in victory. (`0039`–`0040`.)

The answer is the volume's centre. She has never once aimed at winning because she had grounds. She aims at gold because she cannot forgive a self that cannot take it. She is on the ice thanks to the person who believes in her victory. She wants to prove that the self on the ice is the strongest, and to go on proving with gold medals that she is someone who ought to be here. She wanted to prove it that day. (`0040`.)

Hikaru hears another voice in it. A page fuses two speakers, a young Yodaka and Inori, saying that the ice is the only place where one can be strongest and that failing there leaves life without meaning; she remembers being told to become a person who can live only on the ice. Rio once said that Tsukasa is sometimes very like Yodaka, and she denied it. Her thought now: he is the same kind as me. The one who truly resembles Yodaka is Inori. She takes Inori's hands and asks for a promise to win the final. An unattached line asks what Inori still lacks, and says: I can still wait. (`0041`.)

Iruka breaks it up. Ako, crushed, had thought a triple Axel would make her special; Suzu tells her that if she needs someone's recognition for that, she can have Suzu's. In the locker room Iruka hands Hikaru a back support and tells her it is time she noticed she is taking skating lightly. Hikaru explains to a clubmate that her back hurt a little, and that even her coach has not noticed. The clubmate, who keeps to the prescribed training, cannot see why it would. (`0042`–`0043`.)

After the camp Tsukasa sits over her practice record. A Junior Grand Prix place after two years looks like plain sailing. She is not someone who reproduces movement easily. What raised her was a concentration that drives her as if paring her body away, and it narrows her sight. When she is out of form her notes fill with words that corner her. His verdict on himself is an idiom for a treasure left unused. (`0044`.)

## What the absence was

`MD-OQ-V09-03` asked where he went and what it did to the pair. Volume 9 had one line of her narration and a thought on a park bench. Volume 10 replaces inference with a record, and the record changes the picture in three ways.

He did not go to study in any abstract sense. He went to find a person. The pattern `MD-CL-0017` has followed since Volume 2, in which he meets a limit by acquiring someone else's knowledge, is here applied to her body before it fails and on behalf of the whole club. The search took six months because he would not lower his conditions, and it ended in a characteristic bargain: a trainer shared with another club in exchange for his own teaching. What he is afraid of is stated without ornament. When he heard of her growing pains he could barely stand, and hid it.

The separation was announced, formal and mutual in its unhappiness. He ran a handover. She set her own rule, not to telephone, and gave a reason that looks forward to competing without him. Both said they hated it, he aloud and she to herself. Her park-bench thought in Volume 9, that if she had not lost they might have stayed together, is not cancelled by this. It is placed. She knew the plan and its purpose, and she still read the months as the price of October.

And the months produced the one thing the plan could not supply. She had vowed to surprise him with growth. She fell on the Lutz at the selection, could not train the quadruple, and judged that she could not grow. When he came back she began to show him results and to hide the rest. He noticed within a day.

## Three readings of one child

The volume gives three adult accounts of Inori and one of her own. They do not agree, and the reading keeps them apart.

Gori's is offered as a guess by a man who says it may not connect. She is governed by adults' faces. Her attention is a search for disappointment. It was learned somewhere, perhaps beside teachers when other children were with each other. It makes her an unusually good listener to someone she trusts. The evidence he has is her gaze at one practice a year ago and what her coach has just told him. A creator aside adds that he taught middle school for ten years. That is a reason to weigh his guess, and it is still a guess.

Tsukasa's first response is a question he does not answer: is her strength built on the cold days? His second, weeks later, is firmer. She does not acquire movement easily. Her speed came from a self-consuming concentration, which also narrows her sight and leaves her open to anxious words. For this he has a document, her practice log, in which the sentences turn on herself when she is out of form.

Her own account, to Hikaru, is about will and desert. She cannot forgive a self that fails. Gold is proof that she belongs on the ice, and the proof has to be renewed.

Earlier volumes support each of these in part. The first volume showed a child alone in a classroom reading her mother's face. Volume 4 gave the cold feeling a name and had her say goodbye to it. Volume 9 showed composure offered to her coach and grief kept for her sister. They also support a fourth account that none of the adults gives here: the child who loves steps, who was happiest when a jump came as a puzzle solved, and who on the day after her defeat whirled a clubmate over her head for joy at his medal.

`MD-CL-0028@1` registers the first three as a hypothesis with its counterevidence, and at low confidence. Two things can be said more firmly. The Lutz did not come back by surveillance or by self-punishment. It came back inside a game, from a different teacher, when the instruction arrived without the weight of being corrected by him. And the thing that blocked it, in her own words, was a belief that her basic skating was worse than everyone's. The fact, by her coach's analysis, is that her edge is too deep: too good. Whatever formed her, at this boundary she misjudges her own foundation in the direction of inferiority, which is the word he uses about himself the following night.

## The creed and the sentence

On the night of her defeat he told her that she is not only what is evaluated and may aim at first without anyone's expectation. `MD-OQ-V09-02` asked what she kept. The answer is on the page twice.

To Iruka she gives his sentence back almost word for word, as the reason she will not quit, and adds that the dream is not for herself alone. To Hikaru she gives the creed. Its first clause sounds like his: she has never needed grounds. Its second clause is the reverse of his. He said worth does not wait on proof. She says she cannot forgive herself without it, that she is on the ice by someone's belief in her victory, and that she must go on proving she ought to be there.

So she has kept the permission and refused the release. The debt that `MD-CL-0027@1` described is intact and has acquired a general form. It is no longer only that she ended his dream. It is that her place on the ice is conditional, and gold is the condition. The first volume's child wanted a place where she was allowed to be. The thirteen-year-old holds that the place has to be earned again each time.

He has not heard it. What he has seen is its surface: a pupil who presents results, avoids his eyes, and tries to solve alone. His hypothesis is shame over a jump. Nothing shows that he connects it with himself. And his own position has moved toward hers. When the jacket arrives he calls it a decoration and does not want her to lose it. The chapter's last caption, in a voice that belongs to both of them, calls the medal the proof of strength. The man who denied proof the status of a ground in October now uses its vocabulary in July. The reading does not treat this as hypocrisy. He holds, as he did in Volume 9, that this is how the system works. But the sentence from the playground has no second speaker in this volume.

## The hawk's eye

Kyōboku's metaphor is his own, and he says so. What he reports is narrower and more reliable than the image: a skater who corrected himself without video, who planned his own career, who left every coach within a year, and beside whom a coach felt like an ornament. His regret is the part that carries weight for the series. The talent did not need an adult. The child did.

That one testimony reorders what is known of Yodaka's coaching. `MD-CL-0016` has described a man who governs by telephone, sets a rule and is absent. Volume 10 makes this the practice of someone who was never coached in the ordinary sense and is passing on the only apprenticeship he knew. The aquarium says as much. He has had his pupil write to an old teacher for one specific skill, as he once did. Asked whether seven years of compulsory victory is too much, he answers from his own case: he could.

Tsukasa hears the metaphor and recognizes himself. The recognition is an inference of his, made in a hotel room and confirmed for him the next morning by a jump. It should be held as that. What the page establishes is that he believes he shares the faculty, that he believes he smothered it out of inferiority, and that he means to transmit it. Whether a sense of this kind can be taught is the question the two men who spoke before him leave open. One said its owner needed no teacher. The other, who taught them both, says he has no capacity to guide.

The project also restates his purpose. In Volume 6 he called coaching his way of using what he has. In Volume 9 he said he gave up nothing. Here, for the first time, he says he wasted something, and proposes to spend it on her. `MD-CL-0010@10` records the change and its risk: the faculty he wants to give her is the one that, in its only known owner, made adults unnecessary.

## Who resembles whom

Hikaru's thought in the interview room is the volume's boldest move, and it is one character's construction.

She has been told to become a person who can live only on the ice. She hears Inori say that the self on the ice must be the strongest and that she cannot forgive failing there, and the page prints the sentence in two voices at once, a young man's and the girl's. Her conclusion crosses the pairs. Her own coach's likeness is Inori. Inori's coach is the same kind as herself.

Each half has some support outside her head. Inori's creed does sound like the doctrine of proof that Yodaka gave Tsukasa in Volume 6, and Volume 9 showed her adopting it alone. And Tsukasa and Hikaru now share a teacher, a faculty by his own account, and a lightness she is being trained toward; in Volume 9 she came out in the rain to argue with him and conceded his point.

But the second half sits oddly with what Volume 9 had her say. There she called Inori abnormal and seemed to mean a likeness to herself. Here she assigns Inori to Yodaka and herself to the man who refuses sacrifice. One reading is that she has discovered she is not what her coach asked her to become, and that the discovery pleases her. Her blush, her promise and her "I can still wait" fit that. Another is that she is simply delighted to find the rival she wanted. The page does not choose, and `MD-CL-0003@9` keeps both.

What is observable is conduct. She broke a quarantine her peers had set up to protect the room. She asked the one question that separates the two coaches' creeds. She has asked for the other team's best weapon and is being given it by the other team's teacher. And her back hurts.

## Growth: loss, curse, weapon

Three sentences about growth are set against one another, and a body is put between them.

Takamine says growth is loss, and means it as a reason for mercy: do not bind a child to seven years of victory, because she will not be the same child. Gori says the belief that growth takes jumps away is half fact and half curse, and that the curse now injures more skaters than the fact. Tsukasa, to Inori, says that staying strong through change is the difficulty a top skater takes on; the closing captions turn a heavier body into a weapon.

Inori is the case. She is taller, her knees hurt at night, her quadruple is out of reach, and the junior rules bar it from one segment anyway. Her novice strategy has inverted. The jump she skipped is compulsory and the jump she chose instead is forbidden where she most needs points. Iruka adds the social half: the ones who quit are those who grew heavy and stopped being praised, and nobody stops them.

Hikaru is the counter-case and the warning. She has grown and her landings are lighter. She also has a back that hurts, a nightly regime the coaches in that room do not know about, and a guardian who says he could not hold her if she chose to go. The volume puts Gori's speech about what cannot be seen in a training load forty pages before a senior junior notices what the girl's own coach has not.

`MD-CL-0022@6` carries this. It also notes a pattern the volume makes hard to miss without stating it. Careers here keep being oriented by someone who stopped: Inori's by her sister and then by her coach, Iruka's by the same sister, Tsukasa's by a champion who retired at twenty, Kōhei's creed by a friend who failed, Takamine's return by something left undone. The exceptions are the two who are said to need no one.

## Rio's gold

The side story is a counterweight, and its placement after the main chapters is pointed. The boy who has measured himself against Hikaru since Volume 3 wins the national title on the day after she set a score he cannot approach, without the jump the favourites fell on, and cannot be glad.

What mends it is the fourth-placed girl. She recognizes the move he is making, calling oneself worthless before anyone else can, as one she knows from inside. And she defends the value of the thing she herself failed to get. The night before, she told her sister that anything but first was unbearable. Her generosity is not a contradiction of that; it is the same valuation of gold applied to someone else. It does bound the darker readings of her. On the worst morning of her career she is exuberant, jealous of her coach's attention, and right about a friend.

His father's remembered line, that gold looks the most beautiful to him, is the only thing the volume says about how a man who competed beside Yodaka regards his own medals.
