# Volume 14 — a program everyone made

A month after she was cut from her own national championship, the heroine skates the short program of the Junior Grand Prix Final in Beijing and leads it. Seven elements, all credited, the step sequence back at the top level, 71.26. Two hundredths of a point separate her from the series' top qualifier. One skater is still to come when the volume stops.

That is the event. Most of the volume's pages are spent on something else: teaching a thirteen-year-old in the stands what the second half of a skating score measures. Four rivals skate before the heroine, and each program is used to explain one part of the component mark, through a former ice dancer who has come as a spectator and a patron's daughter who thinks she has no eye for art. By the time the heroine takes the ice the reader has been given a vocabulary, and her program is read with it.

The program itself turns out to have a story, which three earlier volumes did not tell. It is set to music from a film about a timid boy who is trained by a ghost with his own face. The heroine says in a flashback which part of the film she likes and why, and during the step sequence she casts her coach as the ghost. The film ends with the ghost gone and the boy able to look at his own reflection.

The reading's argument is that Volume 14 gives the settlement of Volume 13 a public and measurable form. On the bench the coach told his pupil that she had cost him nothing. Here the series argues, through its spectators, that a skater's artistry is jointly made and that the bond of coach and skater shows in the mark, and it lets the heroine say on the ice both that her strength is not hers alone and that she will finish by her own. The reading keeps separate what the film says about a helper who withdraws and anything the volume says about the pair, because the page asserts only the first.

## A ruler of beauty

The volume's long lesson has a thesis, and it is put in a spectator's mouth. Beauty is judged by rulers that differ from person to person. Where many people must reach one verdict a shared ruler is required, and the component mark is that ruler.

Three things follow in the lesson as given. First, a good part of the ruler can be read with knowledge. Whether a five-beat piece is skated in five is a fact, and the patron sees that feeling alone would have rewarded a mistake. Second, a good part of it is technique that happens to earn no base value: looseness on an unstable surface, both feet and both directions used, the quality of an edge. Third, one part of it does need the live experience, and the lesson is candid that this is the part where a hall and a panel can disagree.

The choice of pupil matters. Yō has avoided art because she takes her failure to be moved in a museum as proof that she lacks sense. Kōhei's answer is that her ruler was grown in her own life and that not sharing one is no defect. He then gives her the shared ruler as something learnable. By the end she is taking notes and makes the volume's best observation unprompted. The series has taught rules through spectators before. It has not before made the spectator's fear of art the thing to be answered.

The lesson has a visible interest behind it, and the page says so. Kōhei is a former ice dancer who lights up at choreography; the patron remarks that his ruler and the component mark are the same ruler. What he praises most is design that satisfies the criteria. The other view is given a voice as well, and is not refuted. Mia's uncle, an Olympic champion, holds that a skater should charm the world and change the rules, and cites Yodaka as someone who did. The hall is on his side. The mark is not. `MD-CL-0030@1` registers the doctrine as the volume's and records that counter-voice.

Belinda Bergamasco has a limit. A leg injury lowered her jump content, and her team rebuilt her strategy on the component mark. She is fifteen, and the spectator's verdict is that hardly a senior dances like this. Her component total stands as the highest of the first four.

Mia Ocelot has two adults. One coaches for the rules, the other for the crowd, and they argue over her head in front of the cameras. The observation attached to her is exact: her policy would settle if she decided what she wants. The page shows her blowing kisses and gives her no thought.

## Feeling, calculated in reverse

Since Volume 11 the page has flagged the heroine's performance side. A choreographic sequence scored poorly in Bangkok and both coaches blamed rehearsal space. Volume 13 showed the hall clapping to a program whose expressive side had plainly moved, and gave no account of how. This volume supplies it.

Hitomi makes the diagnosis: the room is in Presentation. Tsukasa answers with a method that is characteristic of him in three respects. It decomposes. The way a body moves when it is angry is broken into stance, shoulder, twist and breath, and each piece is added to the choreography, so that the feeling is read from a distance whether or not it is felt. It demonstrates. He throws the punch with her, and later has two colleagues dance the piece while he studies them. And it refuses a premise. Expression is usually left to a skater's own past or to slow cultivation, both of which depend on her sensibility. He wants to find out how far teaching reaches into what people call sense.

That last sentence should be read beside the bench in Volume 13. He was told as a boy that he had no talent, believed it for years, and found it false. ## The steady road

Until now the Olympics have been a dream in the series' own word: the thing a child said in a rink office and a coach agreed to chase. This volume lays an administrative road under it.

The road has numbers. One place goes to the national champion before the Games. Two more, when the country has them, are weighted by a ranking that sums three years of results by the grade of event. She is 141st with 432 points, and the rivals beside her have two thousand. The age rule has not moved, so the Games in question are seven years away and she will be twenty.

## Tsukasa and his senior

The side chapter is early work, collected late, and comic. It is also the only depiction of a rule the coach has followed since the first volume.

His first lesson at the ice-dance club came from a senior who taught by ridicule, calling it banter.
