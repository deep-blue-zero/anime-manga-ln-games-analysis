# Volume 8 — what a skater carries

Volume 7 ended with a greeting at an opening ceremony. Volume 8 is the first third of the championship that followed, and like Volume 5 it stops before its heroine skates. Twelve of thirty-six entrants have taken the ice when it closes. The leader's score is forty-eight points above the one that made Inori a regional champion a month earlier.

Three things happen on the way there, and the reading takes them in turn. Inori lands her quadruple at official practice with the whole building watching, and the effect is not the one her coach intended: two rivals copy what they saw, and the girl she most wants to beat asks for a quadruple of her own. A skater from Kyoto wins the first group by twenty points on what her coach calls the shallowest motive in the country, under a rule he gave her never to carry anyone's hopes. And Hikaru skates. The program is her coach's, the music is the piece that made his name, and its last jump is Inori's own winning combination with a harder jump at its head.

Around the skating the volume discloses the terms Hikaru lives under. If she falls once in the nightly run-through she must stop skating. Competition, she says, is the easy part. Her coach has taught her that victory requires sacrifice. She asked Tsukasa, alone on a road at night, whether the job he had taken to pay for a harness was that kind of sacrifice, and he told her it was not: he had never thought of his work as a price, and he did it for his own happiness whether his pupil won or lost.

The reading's argument is that Volume 8 turns the work's long question about recognition into a question about load. Each coach in it has a stated view of what a child on the ice should be made to bear, and the views are opposed: nothing; everything; whatever she chooses. The volume gives the highest marks to the heaviest regime and shows the children under the other two in tears. It ends with Inori picking up a burden no one assigned her. She tells her coach that this time she will make him the coach of a gold medalist.

## The punch and what it bought

`MD-OQ-V07-02` asked how Tsukasa would decide whether the quadruple went in. Volume 8 does not show the three-jump test's result. It shows a different act: he uses official practice to prove the jump in public before the event begins, and says he means it to alter the event's mood.

It worked as intended in one respect. Coaches rewrote plans. The event's own list of contenders includes her, on the strength of that jump alone. And she did it under exactly the condition an experienced coach says breaks people, with a method she learned nine days earlier and now runs at will. `MD-CL-0015@6` named the test as which of her two modes she would be in at the championship. In practice, at least, she was in the attentive one, under the heaviest outside pressure the work has staged.

It also did three things he did not intend, and the volume lays them out without comment. A rival took the closing combination he had designed for the block and beat Inori's block score with it. A rival saw the arm position once and attempted the quadruple herself that evening. And Hikaru, who had never shown a quadruple, watched Inori's and asked for one. The opening jump of the program that scored 128.98 exists because of the punch. `MD-CL-0004@7` records the tactic and its recoil. Volume 6 showed him testing a layout against months of logs; Volume 7 showed a specialist arming every club alike; here his own demonstration arms the field in a morning.

His scouting was also wrong where it mattered. He watched Suzu fall on her triple Axel at practice and expected her to drop it. Her coach kept it and she landed it. An old coach had warned that success in practice proves nothing; the same day shows that failure in practice proves nothing either. `MD-CL-0005@7` adds both, with the under-rotation rules that explain how a landed jump can still lose marks.

The burden of the Axel is the volume's sharpest piece of arithmetic and bears on `MD-CL-0008@6`. Inori's quadruple adds a high card. It leaves her spending a pass on a double Axel like nearly everyone else. The two skaters above 110 are the two who do not. Her coach knows this as he watches.

## Carry nothing

Kameganya's promise to Suzu is a coaching doctrine as complete as any in the work, and it is aimed, without knowing it, at the work's protagonists.

Its terms are exact. A skater should not need a reason to be on the ice that comes from other people. Gratitude owed, a wish to make someone happy, the feelings of those left behind: these are weights, and a jumper should be light. Where such weights arrive, the coach takes them. Suzu applies it to the letter. She tells the friend she brought into the sport that she will not skate for her. The friend replies that she did not come for Suzu either.

Set that beside what earlier volumes established. Tsukasa as a competitor staked everything on one event to become a medalist and so earn a reason to continue. He spent years believing he had defaulted on a debt to a dead woman and her husband. Inori told a friend in Volume 4 that she hurried so that her coach would not be blamed, and in Volume 7 offered him a recovered jump as repayment for a night's driving. By Kameganya's standard both are the kind of person he told a nine-year-old not to become. The inset of a weeping man and a skater on her knees suggests he has seen what that costs. The page does not say whom he saw, and the reading does not guess.

Three things keep this from being the volume's verdict. The motive it protects is called shallow by the man who protects it, with admiration; depth of motive and quality of result are simply uncoupled. Suzu's profile shows the vanity to be a discipline, kept by a child who also manages examinations and looks after younger clubmates. And when Hikaru's score goes up, the girl who carries nothing weeps as hard as the girl who carried her coach's club for eight years. Travelling light did not spare her.

`MD-CL-0026@1` registers the doctrine as one pole of a designed opposition, and `MD-CL-0024@4` adds Kameganya to the range of adults: the one who held to a layout after a failed practice, on his own reading of his skater.

## Offer everything

The other pole is disclosed in pieces across the last chapter, and the pieces come from three people.

From Hikaru, in a remembered talk with Rio: the rule. One fall in the nightly run-through and she stops skating. From Rio in the same scene: his father thinks it wrong and cannot stand against the man who set it. From Hikaru on the road, reporting her coach: victory needs sacrifice above all, and fate cannot ignore an offering. From the telephone call on the day: asked whether an untested quadruple may open the program, the same man says yes and raises the stakes of the last jump besides.

`MD-CL-0016@6` carried Yodaka's purpose as stated by him in Volume 6, that Hikaru is to win everything for life, and left open the terms on her side. This is the term. It also resolves several smaller things the ledgers had noted without explaining: her ease at ceremonies and before crowds, her absence from school, her practising alone on a road at night, and why a coach could authorize by telephone a jump no one had seen her try on that ice. By the standard she trains under, a championship is lenient. She says so in nearly those words.

It revises `MD-CL-0024`. Through Volume 7 that claim could say that in no case shown was a failed risk punished. That is no longer true of the work. The arrangement between Yodaka and Hikaru makes a single failure in practice terminal, and it is the arrangement under which the highest score in the series was produced. The claim now has to hold both facts.

And it complicates `MD-CL-0023`. The prodigy has been read since Volume 5 as a condition other children live under. She is under one herself, harsher than anything she imposes. The girls who cry at 128.98 do not know that.

What she makes of it is the part to be careful with. Three of her statements bear on it. She has no use for a self that would fail: the rule has become her own standard. She will challenge her coach: handed the piece he reserved to himself, she means to take it. And, having heard a rival's coach say that his work is no sacrifice and that he will ask none of his pupil, she says only that in the end everyone is like that. The last can be read as wistful, as dismissive, or as the remark of someone who has just confirmed that she is alone in her terms. The page gives no more, and the reading holds all three.

Tsukasa's interior image, of a child devouring what her teacher gives until his glory is her flesh, is his interpretation and is staged as such. Her intent to surpass is hers by her own words. Whether surpassing him is escape, obedience or both cannot be said. Her choreographer, who was his, has told her that she is the one who led a confined dancer out.

One object should be recorded without interpretation. Yodaka still uses the towel Inori gave him in Volume 2.

## What Tsukasa said on the road

The scene with Hikaru is placed late and happened early: days before Niigata, before the lake, before the choice of the quadruple. It matters for three claims.

`MD-CL-0010@7` asked whether the purpose he had claimed as his own could be held apart from his pupil's result. Here, to a child who is not his pupil and has no reason to be flattered, he says that it can. He does the work for his own happiness whether Inori wins or loses. It is the plainest answer the question has received, and it predates the night he told Inori that it was all right not to jump. The limit is that it is a statement. He made it while hiding cracked ribs and a second job he was ashamed of, taken to pay for equipment he bought for other people's children.

`MD-CL-0007@7` held his commitments and his release of outcomes in tension. The sentence about sacrifice adds a commitment of a different kind: he will not make Inori pay one. That is a constraint he has placed on himself in advance of the choice he faces at the end of this volume.

And `MD-CL-0025@2`. The two coaches' creeds had been about certainty and proof. This adds what each thinks effort is. For one it is an offering that compels a result. For the other it is a good in itself. Tsukasa nonetheless begins the day by adopting his opponent's sentence about proof as his own measure, and ends it by doing what his opponent invites.

## Believe in me once more

The last five pages repeat the end of Volume 5 with the parts exchanged, and `MD-CL-0007` and `MD-CL-0002` both turn on how.

At the block the declaration was his. He chose the words, feared them, and at her asking said that he believed. Here the request is hers. She has just watched the whole of her rival's program, by her own insistence and against a plan she had agreed to, on the stated ground that she must be able to take it in and stand against it. Having done so she does not ask what is possible. She says what she wants and asks him to believe again.

His reasoning immediately before is the most careful he has done on the page. The event has a second prize that matters, entry to the junior championship; the present layout secures it; a change made to chase an eighteen-point lead over second place could lose it. This is the truth-telling coach of Volume 4, and it is the position his senior argued in Niigata. He is about to say it. He says the opposite.

The reading does not resolve whether that is right, and thinks the volume means it to be unresolved. In favour: she asked, with reasons, having seen everything; he has never overruled her stated wish; he knows a new layout exists that he judges she can do, though the page does not show it; and a man watching on a screen has just told him it is his turn, which is the wager he accepted. Against: he had reached a contrary judgment a moment earlier on grounds he did not withdraw; the stake is hers; he told another child days ago that he would not make this one pay a sacrifice; and what her request offers him, by its own wording, is a title for himself.

That wording is the other half. In Volume 5 he said he would make her a gold medalist. She now says she will make him a gold medalist's coach. The grammar is his and the direction is reversed. It is the public form of what she kept from him in Volume 4, when she wanted to be strong enough to be able to choose him and feared he would be blamed. `MD-CL-0002@8` records it as agency of the fullest kind the work has shown: she overrode an instruction with reasons, obtained his consent, and then set the terms of the attempt. It is also, by Kameganya's rule, exactly the load a skater should not pick up. `MD-CL-0026@1` notes that the volume ends with its heroine choosing the weight.

## Rivals who are not Hikaru

`MD-CL-0023@3` had the prodigy as a condition repeated in a second region. Volume 8 fills in the people.

Suzu is not under Hikaru's shadow in the way the Chūbu field was. She built a triple Axel for the purpose of beating her and demands that Hikaru jump hers so that the contest is fair. Her coach, by contrast, shouts at Hikaru not to, out of anger that the jump his pupil put first could be someone else's easy last. The pupil's stance is the more generous.

Yūna's line reaches a kind of rest. In Volume 5 she wanted the strength to make her coach turn round and resolved not to let a late starter win. In Volume 6 she called pursuit her skill. Here she skates clean with the jump that was Hikaru's signature, is praised for it by Hikaru in front of everyone, visibly struggles, and answers with good wishes. Minutes later she is asking why her coach makes only Hikaru do dangerous things. She has identified, correctly, that he is not himself where that girl is concerned, and does not have the fact that would explain it. The cost of the secret falls on his first pupil's trust in him.

Hikaru's own conduct toward rivals is consistent across three volumes and this one: open praise, easy warmth, and no visible awareness of what her existence costs them. What is new is that Inori moved her. She saw a quadruple and wanted one. It is the first evidence on the page that the regard runs both ways in the matter of skating itself. `MD-CL-0003@7` records it.

Ema travels to another island to watch the clubmate she follows, for her own instruction. Kago's daughter has taught herself the six jumps. An old coach hopes to see in a stranger's child what his own pupil never managed. A champion from abroad wants the young man in the hooded top on her staff. Recognition in this volume is plentiful and moves in every direction. It does not decide anything.

## Written Japanese

The volume's argument is carried by a few words used by different mouths. The word for proof, 証明, is Yodaka's in Volume 6, Tsukasa's own measure in this volume's first chapter, and the jacket's chosen line. The word for sacrifice, 犠牲, is given to Hikaru as her coach's teaching, with 生贄, an offering, as its gloss; Tsukasa's reply negates both and substitutes 幸せ, happiness. The word for absolute, 絶対, is used by an assistant sure of a stranger's jump, by Inori of her intention before the quadruple and of her wish at the rail, and by Tsukasa only in the negative.

Kameganya's doctrine is phrased as prohibitions in Kansai speech and turns on いい子, a good child, as the thing not to be. His summary of Suzu uses 浅い, shallow, as praise. Suzu's own speech is Kyoto dialect in a register of comic self-regard, with her name in the third person.

Hikaru calls Yodaka コーチ and Sonidori by his given name with 先生; to Rio she refers to Yodaka as that person. Yodaka's last line addresses Tsukasa with 君. ## Manga form

**Stopping short again.** As in Volume 5 the protagonist's skate is withheld, and the last page is a declaration at a rail. The repetition is exact enough to be an argument: the reader is asked to compare the two.

**The gaze drawn.** The hall's attention before the practice quadruple is a ring of silhouettes with lit eyes and a weight of lettering on two small figures. Before Hikaru's program the same attention is drawn as the removal of all sound but a blade.

**A program told three times.** Hikaru's skate is cut with the making of the decision that morning, the history of the music, and a conversation on a road, so that each jump arrives with a reason attached. It is the method of Inori's block skate in Volume 6, applied to her rival.

**Film within the program.** The story behind the music is drawn as its own sequence, a great room with a piano and a figure on the floor, and then laid over the two people it is being applied to.

**Animal.** For one panel after the last landing she has a wolf's grin, and a black shape crosses the foreground. An allegorical page follows of a child among feathers.

**A voice without a mouth.** The captions over the score have no tail and pass over three faces. The last clause is set beside the winner.

**Posted boards.** Group two's results appear as a board of combinations and values, the form the block's protocols took.

**Two rails.** The coach and pupil at the rail, the champion in a dark room before a screen, cut back and forth for the last five pages; the screen-watcher speaks to the man at the rail.

**Echoes.** The drawstrings, thrown by him. The frozen lake. The towel. A child at a window. The chairs, heaped into a tower with one girl on top. The jacket's return to the pair.
