# Integrated provisional synthesis through Volume 15

This is a checkpoint on an unfinished work. The manga stops, at the limit of what had been published when this project fixed its boundary, in the warm-up before the free skate of a junior final. Inori Yuitsuka is second by a third of a point. Nothing below says how that ends.

What can be said is what the first sixty-one chapters have built, what question they have come to turn on, and where the evidence leaves real disagreement. The argument is that the series began as a story about being let in and has become a story about what being let in costs. Its first volume asks who will allow a child to skate. Its fifteenth asks what Inori believes she owes the people who did, and shows that Tsukasa Akeuraji, the adult who let her in, has been living by the same belief for twenty years. The series has an answer and puts it in several mouths. It also shows the answer failing to arrive in time, and shows Inori declining it.

This is an organizing thread, not an exhaustive structure or a claim that debt first appears in V15. Inori’s private worry about wasting work already accompanies V01 choice, and V04 keeps her coach-protection motive private. Middle-volume achievement, sensory delight and rivalry, and V14 artistry retain their own ends. The independent audit tests achievement/recognition, autonomy, reciprocal recognition and their coexistence with burden against early, middle and late evidence; those alternatives remain live.

Companion studies carry the detail: monographs of Inori and of Tsukasa, a study of what passes between them, a study of Hikaru Kamisaki and of Yodaka Jun, who made her, and specialist accounts of the skating, the page and the adults. Each argues its own case. This synthesis says how they fit and where they do not.

## The shape of sixty-one chapters

The series has moved in four arcs, and each ended by changing what winning meant.

**Admission, Volumes 1 to 4.** Inori Yuitsuka, eleven, teaches herself to skate from a manual, in secret, at a rink whose clerk, Sekoma, lets her in for a tin of worms. Her mother, Nozomi, who watched her elder daughter Mika stop after an injury, means to refuse her. Tsukasa Akeuraji, twenty-six, who started too late, failed at the national championships and failed his auditions for an ice show, sees her and argues for her. The first good the series gives is recognition joined to instruction and to somebody's willingness to pay (`MD-CL-0001@1`, `MD-CL-0011@1`). The arc's competitions are small and its discoveries are about time: Inori accepts that the years she lost cannot be recovered, and at its end she thanks the lonely feeling that drove her and says goodbye to it (`MD-EV-V04-0038`; `MD-CL-0019@1`, `MD-CL-0022@1`).

**The national novice championship, Volumes 5 to 9.** The author calls these volumes one arc. Inori wins her region by a strategy Tsukasa built from his own discipline, learns a quadruple jump with a borrowed specialist, Uobuchi Kakeru, and meets Hikaru Kamisaki, the prodigy of her generation, on the day it counts. She alters her layout alone in mid-program, attempts a jump she has never practised, and finishes fourth. Her reason, given in thought, is the first statement of the belief that will organize the rest: by becoming Tsukasa's pupil she ended his dream (`MD-EV-V09-0031`). That night Tsukasa tells a furious Hikaru, who has come out in the rain to find him, that sacrifice is the self-harm of people who feel inferior and that one can win without it (`MD-EV-V09-0046`; `MD-CL-0025@10`). Winning here stops being a wish and becomes a proof.

**The junior class, Volumes 10 to 13.** Inori is thirteen, growing, and in pain. A designation by the federation gives her standing she did not have. She relearns a jump she had lost, places fourth abroad, wins a series event the page does not show, and qualifies for the final. Then, at her own national junior championship, her short program collapses and she is cut after one segment (`MD-EV-V13-0004`–`0006`). The work does not say why. Hikaru wins that title with a free skate rebuilt overnight to be her own, and is left that night by Yodaka Jun, the coach who made her, who says his road ends with a skater letting her coaches go (`MD-EV-V13-0028`–`0030`; `MD-CL-0016@14`). Inori cuts school to train and tells Tsukasa she would sooner ruin her legs than lose. He answers by telling her his history and that coaching her is his dream and no substitute (`MD-EV-V13-0044`). Winning is now a debt.

**The final, Volumes 14 and 15.** In Beijing Inori skates the same short program clean and is second by 0.33 (`MD-EV-V14-0033`, `MD-EV-V15-0009`). The fourteenth volume spends most of its pages teaching a spectator what the artistic mark measures. The fifteenth spends most of its pages off the ice. The trainer Kanayumi Miho tells Tsukasa that Inori believes she skates at the cost of his life and that his conduct, not his words, sustains the belief (`MD-EV-V15-0017`, `0021`). He tells his patron, Kago Kōichi, the childhood he had left out, and is told that a problem must not be met with a trial of good and evil (`MD-EV-V15-0034`, `0035`). In a stairwell Inori herself describes present labour and distress as burdens she wants to carry; she does not explicitly confess the older belief that she ended his skating career (`MD-EV-V15-0048`). He promises her something about his own life (`MD-EV-V15-0050`). Winning is, at the boundary, a burden she has chosen and he has agreed not to forbid.

Two structural habits run through all four arcs. The series ends its arcs on losses and near-losses: fourth, fourth, eliminated, second. And it tells Tsukasa's past backwards, a layer at a time, each telling beginning nearer the source than the last, until the fifteenth volume reaches a kitchen and a father (`MD-CL-0010@16`).

## The central problem

Stated plainly: a child is supported at real cost to others. What does she owe?

The work shows children answering this in four ways.

Some **stop**. Ahiru Miku, whose career ends at the national novice championship, says that if she continued others would have to strain for her (Volume 9). Two creator statements in the fanbooks, which are reported conceptions and not story pages, give the same motive to Inori's elder sister, Mika, and add a dead mother and a working father to Miku's reasons (`MD-EV-FB01-0007`, `MD-EV-FB02-0016`).

One **could not begin**. Tsukasa, as a boy, was told by a father who had broken down that a man who only costs money has no worth in being alive, and in the same breath to say what he wanted. He did not say that he wanted to skate (`MD-EV-V15-0020`, `0035`). He taught himself, hid his practice from the adults who helped him, entered one competition alone and scored nine points (`MD-EV-V15-0037`). As a young man he would skate only on what he earned, and when he was at last helped he resolved that he must repay the favour with results (`MD-EV-V15-0032`).

One **pays**. Inori told the press in Beijing that she has made many people suffer, that she must repay it with the best result, and that she will give everything so that the self who was allowed to skate need not be ashamed (`MD-EV-V15-0016`). Her payment has taken other forms: a jump relearned in secret because it was the one Tsukasa was hurt on, school missed, a wish to spend her legs (`MD-CL-0029@6`).

And one adult doctrine tells a child to **refuse** the burden outright. Kameganya Chōkaku, head coach of a strong club, forbids his best skater, Kamoto Suzu, to carry anyone's hopes (Volume 8); a fanbook reports that he once carried a temple's (`MD-EV-FB01-0019`).

Five different propositions in one volume address related problems of support, cost and judgment (`MD-CL-0026@9`). Takamine Shō, Tsukasa's teacher: those who support a skater are participants in the sport and not sacrifices to her; no one continues alone; be willing to burden someone, or stop (`MD-EV-V15-0026`). Meiko, Kago's wife: a problem shared may be solved at nobody's expense (`MD-EV-V15-0031`). Kanayumi: sufferings are not to be compared or made small (`MD-EV-V15-0023`). Kago: finding a cause is not sentencing a person, and taking all the blame is one way of not thinking (`MD-EV-V15-0034`; `MD-CL-0031@1`). A film, through Karasuba Daria's program: pain can be made known by making something of it, where otherwise a life would have to be spent (`MD-EV-V15-0008`).

Three things keep this from being a moral. The first is timing. Tsukasa was told all of it at twenty and at twenty-three by people he loved, and went back to paying. The second is that the answer's own spokesman turns out to have exempted himself: he had denied that sacrifice makes anyone strong while putting his faith in his own (`MD-EV-V15-0045`). The third is Inori. Told in effect that she owes nothing, she says that being told so drains her; that she was glad of his history because she took it for a part entrusted to her; that she will not be treated as weak; that she wants to carry everything and still land lightly (`MD-EV-V15-0048`). The work does not overrule her. Tsukasa, having prepared to, decides that to tell a child what is good for her along the safe road is what was done to him (`MD-EV-V15-0049`; `MD-CL-0007@12`).

So the position at the boundary is a tension the series has built on purpose. Several supportive adults resist turning support into debt, with different reasons and methods; this is not a consensus of all coaches. Inori holds that carrying it is her right. Both are on the page with reasons, and no result has yet tested either.

## Recognition, proof and standing

The athletic story supplies the terms in which the central problem is felt.

What Inori first lacks is permission, and the series never lets the word go. At thirteen, leading a world final's short program, she reads her first jump as the moment a boy in a film earns the right to be taught, and likens it to the day her mother said she might skate (`MD-EV-V14-0022`, `0023`; `MD-CL-0001@15`). A day later she tells Tsukasa that she needs the gold in order to stay on the ice. Standing, for her, is tenure that results must keep earning.

The series' account of results does not support that belief, and it has not supported it since the first volume. From the start it has kept apart what a skater can do, what she does on a given day, and what she is thought to be (`MD-CL-0005@1`). By the junior volumes the separation is institutional. Inori, a finalist of a world series, is cut from her own national championship after one segment; a month later the same program leads the final; the spectators who watch it look up her world ranking and find her 141st (`MD-CL-0005@14`). A ranking is assembled from three years of events and a selection from several channels (`MD-EV-V14-0009`). The system the work describes is one in which no single result decides standing. Inori behaves as though each one does.

Tsukasa's first decision about Inori's training, to build edges and steps before jumps, is the work's longest technical argument (`MD-CL-0008@14`). It bought a step sequence called at the top level at a national championship, lost it on the day of the collapse, and had it again in Beijing, where Baek Horang's coach reads Inori's margin over Baek from exactly that (`MD-EV-V15-0002`). The series also charges the method its costs: a jump she could not learn because her edge was too good, and a junior short program that excluded her best weapon.

The fourteenth volume adds an account of the artistic mark that bears on the central problem directly (`MD-CL-0030@2`). Taste differs from person to person; a panel needs a shared ruler; most of that ruler can be read by knowledge; and because no young skater can meet it alone, the solidarity of skater, coach and choreographer shows in the mark. Yō, Kago's daughter, who has just learned this as a spectator, says she can see many people standing behind a skater (`MD-EV-V14-0032`). That is the adults' doctrine of participation restated as a fact about scoring. The next volume draws the limit, in one image of a ruler put into the hand of Tsukasa as a boy so that he would stay good and ordinary (`MD-EV-V15-0052`). Rulers are for programs.

## Hikaru Kamisaki, and the other way of being made

Hikaru Kamisaki is the series' control case. She was given everything Inori lacked: early teaching, a champion, Yodaka Jun, for a coach, a field that concedes before it skates. The work then shows from inside what that was. An orphan placed by adults; a manner in the house of her guardian, Sonidori Shin'ichirō, that she later calls an act; achievements that never felt hers because she had only done Yodaka's jumps; an ease in competition that was hiding behind him (`MD-CL-0003@14`). In her first year of middle school she rebuilds a program overnight to skate something of her own, wins by thirty-six points, and is left by Yodaka that night.

Set beside Inori, Hikaru inverts the central problem. Inori is supported by people who say she owes nothing, and insists on owing. Hikaru was bound by a contract and released on the night she fulfilled it, and is told that being released is the road. Yodaka's stated reason is that he went furthest by letting his own coaches go. What else moved him the work has not said, and this project has kept that open through three volumes and two fanbooks (`MD-CL-0016@14`). Hikaru does not appear in continuity after the thirteenth volume. Inori does not know that Hikaru has been left.

Hikaru’s revised reading and Inori’s account of rivalry are set beside one another. Hikaru took Inori for the likeness of Yodaka and withdrew the reading in a thicket by a river (`MD-EV-V13-0008`). Inori thought herself unfit to be called Hikaru’s rival. Hikaru’s private thought during her skate is that only her strongest self is suitable as Inori’s rival (`MD-EV-V13-0025`): 私 is Hikaru and あなた is Inori. It is not spoken to Inori. The reaching hand and Inori’s separate light-of-hope thought sustain reciprocal orientation for the reader; communicated recognition rests on Hikaru’s earlier promise to skate for her, not on receipt of this exclusivity judgment. In the fifteenth volume Karasuba Daria does in the space of a day what Hikaru did over three volumes: she files Inori under a word, skates, and comes to apologize (`MD-EV-V15-0013`). Two rivals have now cast Inori in a part and then seen her. The work has a settled interest in that sequence.

## The adults

The series was first conceived, by a creator statement in the first fanbook, without Tsukasa. A rich child, the figure who became Kago's daughter Yō, was to back Inori. An editor asked what the adults were doing, and Tsukasa was the answer (`MD-EV-FB01-0012`). Whatever weight that report deserves, the finished work does keep asking the editor's question, and its answer has grown more exact with each arc.

Early on, adults are gates and helpers: Nozomi, who refuses and then sews; Sekoma, who subsidizes; Kago, who pays (`MD-CL-0011@1`). By the middle volumes they are doctrines. More than a dozen named coaches and guardians hold stated positions on how much a child should carry and how much risk she may take, and the series sets them side by side without crowning one (`MD-CL-0024@9`). The heaviest regime produces the highest scores and ends in a departure. The gentlest produces steady children who do not win. Riley Fox permits Hikaru three quadruples once against a promise of later limits. Kyōboku Yutaka backs a device of Daria's that he had advised against and sees it succeed.

In the fifteenth volume the adults become people with pasts who failed someone. Kago says that when Tsukasa was a child the adults did nothing for him, and asks, twenty years late, to carry his regret (`MD-EV-V15-0015`, `0040`). Sekoma, by a fanbook report, helps Inori out of regret over a boy he once ignored (`MD-EV-FB01-0020`). The work's rule about blame is stated in exactly this setting: causes may be named, including what adults did to a child, and nobody is to be sentenced (`MD-CL-0031@1`). It demonstrates the rule by retelling one episode of Tsukasa's youth without sorting anyone, and letting the facts move.

The evaluation this project has carried since the first volume is that Tsukasa does not overrule Inori's stated wish (`MD-CL-0007@12`). It distinguishes purpose from bodily welfare: Tsukasa orders rest in V03, caps training in V04 and prohibits harm in V13, and it was restated by him at the boundary as a principle with a reason. The author, in a conversation reprinted in the second fanbook, gives the same criterion in her own words (`MD-EV-FB02-0030`). Whether the criterion is right is not something the work has finished testing. His restraint in the stairwell is its hardest application, and its consequences are beyond the boundary.

## How the page carries it

The companion study of form argues the detail. Three points matter for the whole.

The series reports skating as a record. Element boxes, level calls, base values and standings boards are drawn into the panels, and the same row of boxes that fills without a gap in Beijing recorded a collapse a volume earlier (`MD-CL-0012@16`). The reader is given selected elements, values and calls also used in judging, which is why the divergence between a hall and a panel in the fourteenth volume can be shown and not only asserted.

It withholds. A fall is a sound heard over two spectators' backs. An elimination is a line sliding down a board, confirmed only when Inori says it. The speech of Tsukasa's father is printed as a blur and released fifty-two page positions later (0076→0128, with fifty-one intervening pages), after Tsukasa has been promised that no one will be judged. A sentence begun at the end of one volume is finished in the next. These delays withhold a cause, result or remembered sentence instead of completing the reader’s knowledge at once. Their formal force does not require the reader’s wait to equal elapsed story time.

And it thinks in a small number of figures that change sides. Hands that reach become hands that carry, and in the last volume Inori, on Tsukasa's back, asks to get down. Tsukasa's face, which was Inori's burden, is named as the ground of her trust. A double at the water's surface, which Hikaru once renounced, becomes the helper in Inori's program and then the thing Inori says she would not mind being replaced by. Black and white, the colours of a verdict, are answered in the last captions with a rainbow between them.

## Two corrections this project made

A synthesis of a work read in sequence should say where the sequence misled.

At the thirteenth volume this project judged that Tsukasa's declaration beside a merry-go-round was the first durable answer to Inori's guilt. It noted as a caveat that her new vow was still a vow to give him something. The fifteenth volume showed that the caveat was the truth of the matter: she had heard him accurately and had taken what he said as a commission, and he had taken having said it as a settlement. The judgement was withdrawn by recorded revision (`MD-RV-0259`, `MD-RV-0261`), and the earlier reading stands unedited in its own volume. The lesson is specific to this work. A scene of reconciliation here is evidence of what was said. What was heard has to be read from what the hearer does next.

At the tenth volume Gori Seiji, the head coach of Okazaki Iruka's club, guessed that Inori watches adults' faces because of years of deprivation. This project registered the guess as a hypothesis, downgraded it a volume later, and has since separated its two halves. The mechanism is established by three observers and by her own words: she reads Tsukasa's face first and takes its cause on herself. The premise about her formation has never been supported and is not adopted (`MD-CL-0028@5`). Her own dating of the feeling, to middle school, tells against extending it to her childhood.

## What is unresolved at the exact endpoint

The open register of the fifteenth volume stands (`MD-OQ-V15-01` to `-08`). In order of consequence for the syntheses:

1. The free skate and the result. Every study in this set ends on it.
2. Whether a change that both principals have stated survives a result.
3. Whether Inori's wish to carry is ever distinguished, by her, from her wish to vanish.
4. Why Inori's short program collapsed in the thirteenth volume. The work has not said, and this project has not supplied a cause.
5. Yodaka's motive and whereabouts; Hikaru without him.
6. What Okazaki Iruka's return from injury costs and whether she and Mika meet.
7. What Inori's elimination cost her institutionally.
8. Tsukasa's family as it is now.

The second fanbook reprints a statement by the author, made before the fifteenth volume's chapters were collected, that Tsukasa has not accepted his past self and that the series will be his journey toward coming to like himself. This project records it as intent and adopts it as no forecast. It corroborates a reading of what is on the page. It says nothing about any of the eight items above.

## Boundary, omissions and what this synthesis does not claim

The boundary is `MD-FR-FB02`: the Japanese manga through Volume 15 and Score 61, with two official fanbooks audited as supplements. The series is ongoing and this synthesis is provisional through that boundary. It says nothing of the television animation, of any English edition, or of anything published after Score 61. No external rulebook was consulted at any point in the project; every statement about the sport is the story's own. Points resting on fanbook pages are marked where they occur and are reported creator statements, dated and revisable; none carries a conclusion alone. This synthesis offers no model of how any character would act in a situation the work has not shown, and no assessment of any character's worth.
