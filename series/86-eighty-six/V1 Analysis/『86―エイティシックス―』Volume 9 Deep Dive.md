# 『86―エイティシックス―』Volume 9 Deep Dive  
## Pride as Curse, the Children Inside the Machines, and the Choice to End the War

**Japanese title:**  
**Ep.9 ―ヴァルキリィ・ハズ・ランデッド―**  
*Valkyrie Has Landed*

**Scope:** The complete original Japanese light novel Volume 9, including the epigraph, prologue 「暴食の獣」, Chapters 1–5, all four interludes, the conclusion 「ワニの腹でも時計は進む」, and the author’s afterword.

**Spoiler level:** Complete spoilers for Volume 9, with no assumptions drawn from Volume 10 or later material.

---

# I. Executive thesis

Volume 9 is the culmination of the thematic movement that began in Volume 4.

The author states in the afterword that the story of the Eighty-Six’s **pride and wounds** reaches one major stopping point here. What follows will be their battle directed toward the future.

That transition is earned through a severe moral confrontation.

For the first time, the Eighty-Six encounter a society whose child soldiers resemble them not merely in circumstance but in self-understanding.

The Holy Theocracy of Noiryanaruse has spent centuries teaching one class of people that they are not human citizens but **神戟 / shingeki**, divine weapons:

- living blades sent by the Earth Goddess;
- beings born to fight so ordinary believers need not shed blood;
- people granted education and ceremonial honor;
- yet denied the right to determine their occupation, body, or future.

After eleven years of war, almost every adult divine weapon has died.

The remaining pilots are children.

Some are younger than ten.

Their machines are so small because they were designed around children’s bodies.

The Holy Theocracy therefore presents the Eighty-Six with a horrifying mirror.

The Republic said:

> You are not human.  
> You are machine components.  
> Fight and die because your lives do not count.

The Theocracy says:

> You are not ordinary humans.  
> You are sacred weapons.  
> Fight and die because this is the divine role that gives your lives meaning.

The language differs.

The structure is nearly identical.

Both systems:

- assign identity at birth;
- remove civilian alternatives;
- aestheticize coerced sacrifice;
- absolve the protected population of bloodshed;
- and teach children to experience their destruction as fulfillment.

The novel’s central question is therefore:

> **What happens when the victim’s survival ethic becomes indistinguishable from the doctrine that victimized someone else?**

The Eighty-Six’s pride was once necessary.

It told them:

- the Republic could take their homes but not their dignity;
- they could be sent to die but could choose how they faced death;
- they would carry one another;
- and they would never become morally like the people who persecuted them.

By Volume 9, that pride has acquired a second function.

It preserves not only dignity but continuity.

If they cease fighting, they fear they may cease to be themselves.

That fear appears in several forms:

- Theo believes losing combat means losing his place beside the others.
- Kurena fears peace because war is the only structure in which she knows how to remain useful to Shin.
- Shiden wants to die with Shana because death beside the lost person appears more faithful than surviving her.
- The Myrmecoleon Regiment seeks heroic combat because its members have been taught that martial achievement is their sole route to human value.
- Svenja clings to her ceremonial role because without it she believes she and her “siblings” possess nothing.
- Hymelnaade Reze would rather destroy her country and perhaps humanity than allow the one role forced upon her to be declared meaningless.

The epigraph names the chain:

> **Pride. Wishes. Bonds. Prayers. Or perhaps—curses.**

Every good thing in the list can become coercive.

- Pride becomes a prohibition against changing.
- A wish becomes an obligation imposed upon the future.
- A bond becomes a demand that another person remain wounded beside you.
- Prayer becomes a sanctification of exploitation.
- Memory becomes a command to repeat the dead.
- Love becomes fear that the beloved will grow beyond the relationship’s original form.

Volume 9 does not tell the Eighty-Six to discard pride, bonds, memory, or love.

It asks them to stop using those things as chains.

## Kurena as the volume’s central character

Theo’s injury ends Volume 8.

Kurena’s response to it becomes Volume 9’s main psychological movement.

She is terrified by several connected possibilities:

- Theo can no longer fight.
- Shin wants the war to end.
- Shin has chosen Lena.
- Other comrades are beginning to imagine education, travel, love, and civilian life.
- The original Five may no longer move toward one shared destination.
- Kurena herself possesses no future image beyond fighting beside Shin.

She finally admits the thought she has been avoiding:

> Perhaps she does not want the war to end.

This is not because she enjoys death.

It is because the war guarantees:

- usefulness;
- proximity;
- identity;
- and a place in the group.

Hymelnaade articulates the same desire in its terminal form.

The Theocracy used Hymelnaade’s murdered family to manufacture a “saint of war.” Now that the country intends to abandon the doctrine that justified generations of divine weapons dying in its place, she feels that the last meaning left to those deaths is being stolen.

Her answer is:

> Let the war continue.  
> Let the country die.  
> Let every nation lose faith in one another.  
> Let humanity perish rather than permit our suffering to become meaningless.

Kurena recognizes herself in this.

She realizes that asking Shin and the others never to change may become a subtler version of the same destruction.

Her breakthrough is therefore not simply accepting that Shin loves Lena.

It is recognizing:

> **A bond does not become less real because the people inside it move forward.**

She tells Svenja not to bind Gilwiese with their shared wound.

She understands that she has attempted to bind Shin in the same way.

Then she turns outward.

The Holy Theocracy’s child soldiers remind the Eighty-Six of the children they once were:

- terrified;
- trapped in machines;
- commanded by adults;
- and unable to protect themselves.

The Eighty-Six initially cannot shoot them.

Kurena finally says:

> **We will not let them kill us anymore.  
> We will stop this absurd battle.  
> We will stop the war that binds us.**

This is the decisive transformation.

The Eighty-Six no longer fight merely because fighting is their pride.

They fight to make fighting unnecessary.

## Theo’s answer to disability

Theo supplies the quieter counterpart.

He says:

> He does not want to stop being a Processor.  
> He wanted to fight beside the others until the end.  
> He hates this unfinished separation.

The novel does not demand immediate inspirational acceptance.

Theo cries.

He is angry.

He becomes apathetic.

He cannot immediately imagine another self.

Ishmael and Annette offer two complementary answers.

Ishmael tells him:

- an inherited identity may end;
- grief need not be rushed;
- and another place or purpose can eventually be found.

Annette tells him:

- the next purpose need not be grand;
- eating something good;
- watching a film;
- buying clothes;
- drawing;
- or throwing a pie at friends

is sufficient.

Theo eventually asks for his sketchbook.

This is the small but decisive return of desire.

At the end, he leaves the base carrying a pirate’s hook given by Ishmael.

He tells Kurena:

> He will look at the world outside the battlefield first.  
> Perhaps he can later help others who are forced to leave it.

Theo does not lose membership in Spearhead.

He becomes the first member to prove that belonging can survive a different path.

## Shin and Lena

Volume 9 also completes the emotional movement begun in Volume 7.

Lena finally says:

> **わたしも──あなたが好きです。**  
> **I love you too.**

She does not merely reciprocate romance.

She offers Shin a form of dependence that does not require him to remain invulnerable.

He admits that Theo’s injury has left him:

- helpless;
- unsure whether he will be all right;
- and unable to determine what he should say or do.

Lena answers:

> Rely on me.  
> Give me part of that grief.  
> I will support and protect you.  
> Let us go together beyond this war.

She deliberately refuses the familiar marriage formula “until death parts us.”

Death is too ordinary in their world.

Instead she says:

> She will wait.  
> He must not leave her.  
> He must return.

Love becomes neither rescue nor cure.

It becomes a negotiated promise of mutual reliance.

## The volume’s central achievement

Volume 9 transforms the meaning of ending the war.

Before this volume, “ending the war” often meant:

- removing the Legion;
- surviving;
- reaching the sea;
- or creating the conditions for peace.

Now it also means:

> **Ending the identity system in which wounded children can exist only as weapons.**

That is why Kurena’s destruction of the Halcyon matters.

She fires humanity’s first deployed railgun and says:

> **終わらせよう。──この戦争を、あたしたちの手で。**  
> **Let’s end it—this war, with our own hands.**

The girl most afraid of peace becomes the person who chooses it.

# II. Volume 9’s structural position

Volumes 4–9 form a distinct second movement in the series.

| Volume | Core problem |
|---|---|
| **4** | The Republic remains inside its survivors as memory, guilt, and despair |
| **5** | The Sirins reveal the danger of the living imitating the dead |
| **6** | Shin discovers a wish, a home, and a reason to return |
| **7** | Shin and Lena choose one another under uncertainty |
| **8** | Theo’s injury proves that survival may separate comrades without killing them |
| **9** | The Eighty-Six confront pride becoming destiny and choose to fight toward a future beyond war |

Volume 9 is therefore not simply another national campaign.

It is the final stage of the Eighty-Six’s confrontation with the identity forged in the Sector.

The key transition is:

> **We fight because this is what Eighty-Six do**

becoming:

> **We fight because we have chosen something beyond fighting.**

That change does not occur uniformly.

Shin has already moved substantially toward it.

Kurena reaches it during this volume.

Shiden reaches it through burying Shana.

Theo begins constructing life outside combat.

Many other Eighty-Six remain uncertain.

The afterword is therefore precise when it says the pride-and-wound story reaches **one stage of completion**, not total resolution.

# III. The epigraph: pride, wish, bond, prayer, curse

The epigraph is attributed to Frederica’s later memoir, 『戦野追想』.

> **誇り。願い。絆。祈り。あるいは──呪い。**  
>  
> **Pride. Wishes. Bonds. Prayers. Or perhaps—curses.**

Each noun corresponds to a major relationship in the volume.

## Pride

Eighty-Six pride preserved dignity.

It also threatens to make combat mandatory.

## Wish

Shin’s wish for the war to end creates a future.

Hymelnaade’s wish that the war never end seeks universal destruction.

A wish becomes curse when it denies other people the right to possess different wishes.

## Bond

The original Spearhead bond kept its members alive.

Kurena and Svenja reveal how a bond may become:

> Do not change.  
> Do not leave.  
> Remain wounded in the same way as me.

## Prayer

The Holy Theocracy calls its social order divine.

The supposed sacredness does not prevent:

- breeding human weapons;
- sending children to war;
- or changing doctrine when the protected population is finally threatened.

Prayer becomes curse when religion transforms political convenience into cosmic necessity.

## Curse

The word does not mean merely something evil imposed from outside.

The most powerful curses in Volume 9 are things the characters love.

- Theo’s promise to become happy is still a curse because it is an obligation inherited from the dead.
- Kurena’s love for Shin can become a curse if it prevents him from moving.
- Svenja’s devotion to her “siblings” binds them to an abusive role.
- Hymelnaade’s memory of her family binds her to the identity manufactured from their deaths.
- The Eighty-Six’s pride binds them to battle.

The volume’s ethical task is not exorcism.

It is transformation.

# IV. Chapter and fairy-tale architecture

Volume 9 is organized through myths, fairy tales, cards, and children’s stories.

| Section | Title | Principal function |
|---|---|---|
| **Prologue** | **The Gluttonous Beast** | The Legion consumes machines, minds, and identities into the Halcyon |
| **Chapter 1** | **The Mermaid’s Bargain** | Theo survives the sea but loses a bodily function and the life he expected |
| **Interlude** | **The Very Long and Very Pointless Quarrel Between the King of Spades and the Queen of Hearts** | Vika and Zelene debate mind, identity, and the Shepherds’ fear of death |
| **Chapter 2** | **The Ash-Covered Battlefield** | Cinderella imagery, Kurena’s “invitation,” airborne descent, and a nation buried beneath toxic ash |
| **Interlude** | **Where Was the Blue Bird?** | Theo discovers that home and possible happiness were not limited to blood or birthplace |
| **Chapter 3** | **Behead Her!** | Shiden must kill the reproduced Shana and separate love from mutual destruction |
| **Interlude** | **Incidentally, to Learn How to Kill Siegfried…** | Theo learns that life may continue through ordinary wants rather than heroic invulnerability |
| **Chapter 4** | **Mirror, Mirror, What Does an Ordinary Mirror Reflect?** | The Theocracy, Myrmecoleon, Kurena, and the Eighty-Six reflect one another’s wounds |
| **Chapter 5** | **The Piper Leads Away the Rats and Children** | Child soldiers follow a voice, while military institutions lead children toward death |
| **Conclusion** | **Even in the Crocodile’s Belly, the Clock Keeps Ticking** | Theo’s hook, time, adulthood, and movement beyond a frozen childhood identity |

The fairy-tale framework is especially appropriate because the volume concerns children raised inside stories adults created for them.

The stories say:

- You are a saint.
- You are a divine weapon.
- You are a failed hybrid.
- You are a heroic knight.
- You are an Eighty-Six.
- You are a death god.
- You are a victory goddess.

The children’s maturation begins when they recognize that these stories are:

- constructed;
- politically useful;
- emotionally powerful;
- and revisable.

# V. Prologue: the gluttonous Legion

The damaged Noctiluca crawls ashore after the naval battle.

It calls for rescue.

A city-sized land battleship answers.

The two experimental models identify one another as:

- **Plan Schwertwal**, the assault-landing battleship;
- and **Plan Ferdinand**, the land battleship.

The Ferdinand asks:

> **統合は可能か。**  
> **Is integration possible?**

The result is the **攻性工廠型 / offensive factory type**, called the **Halcyon**.

## Gluttony as material process

The new machine consumes:

- the Noctiluca’s surviving structure;
- its railguns;
- its command intelligence;
- its combat data;
- and the stolen human minds integrated within it.

The Legion does not merely repair one wounded individual.

It absorbs it into another body.

## Gluttony as identity destruction

The Noctiluca already contained multiple captured human command minds.

Its integration into the Halcyon adds another layer of forced combination.

The new machine becomes a composite of:

- machinery;
- dead officers;
- captured soldiers;
- copied personalities;
- and prior battlefield experience.

No identity is permitted to remain separate.

## Contrast with human inheritance

The volume repeatedly asks how people carry the past.

Humane inheritance allows:

- Theo to remain connected to Spearhead without remaining a Processor;
- Shiden to remember Shana without becoming her;
- Kurena to love Shin without owning his future;
- and the sea clans to preserve memory without preserving the fleet’s exact form.

The Legion’s inheritance is consumption.

Everything becomes one war machine.

## The beast and the state

The Halcyon resembles the political systems examined in the volume.

The Theocracy consumes children into divine weapons.

The Brandtrotte faction consumes unwanted mixed-blood descendants into a heroic regiment.

The Republic consumed Colorata into “unmanned” processors.

The Halcyon is the mechanical endpoint of institutions that cannot permit persons to remain more than their assigned function.

# VI. Chapter 1: the mermaid’s bargain

The chapter opens aboard the wounded *Stella Maris* and in the Fleet Countries’ military hospital.

Theo has reached the sea.

The price is his left arm.

## The allusive logic

In the familiar mermaid pattern, crossing from one world into another requires surrendering part of the self.

Theo’s movement is similarly costly.

He crosses:

- from sea battle to land;
- from Processor to injured survivor;
- from shared combat fate to individual future;
- and from a world in which death would resolve his identity to one in which he must live without that resolution.

## He is saved because he left the Sector

Theo recognizes an essential fact.

Had he suffered the same injury in the Eighty-Sixth Sector:

- medical care would have been unavailable;
- he would have been abandoned;
- or Shin would have needed to mercy-kill him.

Outside the Sector, surgeons treat him.

He can say:

> **戦えなくても、死ななくていい。**  
> **Even if I cannot fight, I do not have to die.**

The sentence is one of the clearest proofs that the group has truly escaped.

Legal freedom and physical rescue become tangible in medicine.

## The limits of technology

The novel refuses an easy technological reversal.

Potential prosthetic systems all have limitations.

- Roa Gracian prosthetics cannot match the Reginleif’s speed.
- Alliance neural interfaces are more precise but require invasive ports and vehicle redesign.
- The Republic’s former biotechnology might once have helped, but much of its expertise died in the large-scale offensive.

Theo’s arm cannot simply be replaced so the old status quo may resume.

## Disability is not a temporary plot obstacle

The injury changes:

- occupation;
- unit role;
- physical identity;
- and imagined future.

The moral problem is therefore not:

> How quickly can Theo return to normal?

It is:

> What new life can be constructed when the old normal is impossible?

# VII. The original Five and the shock of nonfatal separation

The others struggle more with Theo’s injury than they expected.

They understand death.

They do not understand a comrade leaving combat while remaining alive.

## Raiden

Raiden accidentally asks Theo to help with routine work, then remembers he is absent.

The mistake reveals how deeply the Five’s daily habits assumed permanent proximity.

## Anju

Anju admits that they had begun assuming none of them would ever leave.

After surviving so many impossible operations, the original Five’s continued unity had begun feeling natural.

## Kurena

Kurena reacts with:

- withdrawal;
- guilt;
- anger;
- and fear.

Theo’s injury demonstrates that the group may break apart even when nobody dies.

## Shin

Shin is unable to fix the situation.

He cannot:

- restore Theo’s arm;
- give him a new purpose;
- or say something that makes the loss acceptable.

The Reaper is useful when someone must be carried toward death.

He is less certain how to accompany someone into another life.

## The hidden developmental problem

Their group identity was formed under a shared sentence.

All would fight until death.

Survival removes the common ending.

Their bond must now accommodate:

- different bodies;
- different occupations;
- different loves;
- and different destinations.

That is a more mature form of intimacy than dying together.

It is also more difficult.

# VIII. Theo and Shin: “heavy because precious”

Theo and Shin’s hospital conversation gives the clearest account yet of what the Reaper role meant to both of them.

## Theo’s admission

Theo says:

- he does not want to stop being a Processor;
- he wanted to remain beside the others until the war’s end;
- and he hates that his role ended without his consent.

He does not perform cheerful resilience for Shin.

He initially tries.

Then he admits the grief.

## The apology

Theo apologizes for treating Shin as “our Reaper.”

He recognizes that the group placed upon Shin:

- names;
- mercy killings;
- final responsibilities;
- command;
- and the expectation that he would always remain last.

## Shin’s answer

Shin says the burden was always heavy.

He then adds that being relied upon also supported him.

The responsibility:

- gave him a role;
- prevented him from dying;
- and kept him connected to the others.

It was heavy because it was precious.

## Burden and bond

The scene complicates simplistic advice that people should eliminate burdens.

Human relationships create obligations.

The ethical distinction is between:

- a burden that may be named, shared, and renegotiated;
- and a burden that someone must carry alone because the group refuses to see it.

Theo says he wants to learn to ask for help.

He also hopes that one day Shin will rely upon him.

That wish proves Theo does not imagine disability as the end of reciprocity.

He may no longer support Shin through combat.

He may support him in another way.

## “Will you be all right without me?”

Theo asks.

Shin answers:

> Not fine. But all right.

This is the mature definition of separation.

The loss matters.

The relationship survives.

# IX. Theo, Ishmael, and the Blue Bird

The interlude 「青い鳥はどこにいたのか」 asks where happiness or home may be found.

Theo’s answer has changed since Volume 8.

## The confession Theo cannot give his comrades

He cries before Ishmael:

> He does not want to stop fighting.  
> He wanted to remain with the others.  
> He hates an incomplete ending.

He can speak because Ishmael has lost a comparable identity.

The former captain is no longer truly a sea-conquering warrior.

The *Stella Maris* has completed its last military voyage.

## Kinship reconstructed

Theo had previously rejected Ishmael’s appeal to distant Jade ancestry.

Now he sees that blood and place are neither meaningless nor exclusive.

A person is shaped by relationships.

Those relationships can be:

- inherited;
- chosen;
- found after displacement;
- and extended beyond the original group.

Ishmael becomes a ridiculous, intrusive, valuable “thousand-year-old relative.”

## Home was not nowhere

Theo had believed he possessed no home.

He now recognizes:

- Shin;
- Raiden;
- Anju;
- Kurena;
- the other Eighty-Six;
- and the people who have chosen to care for him

as places to which he may return.

The Blue Bird was not one fixed homeland.

It was the relational capacity to build home.

## The outside world as additional belonging

Theo’s recovery also requires people outside the original Five.

This matters.

A closed group composed only of people carrying the same wound may preserve identity but also preserve the wound’s authority.

Ishmael offers Theo another model.

He can understand the loss without demanding Theo remain unchanged.

# X. Annette and the legitimacy of trivial desire

After returning to the Federacy, Theo enters another phase.

He becomes listless.

He no longer even wants to distract himself.

Annette offers the volume’s most ordinary—and therefore most important—philosophy of life.

## No grand purpose required

Annette says she wants:

- good food;
- clothes;
- films;
- and opportunities to throw cream pies at Lena, Shin, Dustin, and perhaps Vika.

Theo finds this almost absurdly trivial.

That is the point.

## The reversal of causal order

Theo has assumed:

> I must discover a great purpose so that I may justify living.

Annette proposes:

> You are alive, so you may do something.  
> After that, another thing may appear.

Life does not need one final reason established in advance.

## Desire as renewable

Small desires differ from a single grand mission.

Once one is fulfilled:

- another meal;
- film;
- picture;
- conversation;
- or joke

may appear.

A life built from these is less heroic.

It is also less brittle.

## The sketchbook

Theo asks Annette to bring his sketchbook.

This is one of the volume’s quietest turning points.

Art existed before his injury as:

- personal mark;
- military identity;
- memorial;
- and unit culture.

It may now become something else.

The hand that painted is injured.

The desire to draw remains.

# XI. The crocodile, the hook, and time

The conclusion title is:

> **ワニの腹でも時計は進む**  
> **Even in the crocodile’s belly, the clock keeps ticking.**

The imagery evokes the crocodile that has swallowed a clock and pursues the pirate captain with a hook.

Theo now wears an oversized hook sent by Ishmael.

## Childhood frozen outside time

The Eighty-Six have been developmentally trapped.

War denied them:

- normal adolescence;
- gradual adulthood;
- and time to explore identities.

Combat made every day urgent while psychologically freezing them inside the conditions of childhood persecution.

## Theo as the hook-handed survivor

The hook is comic, tasteless, affectionate, and symbolically precise.

Theo resembles a pirate pursued by time.

He can no longer remain the unchanging young warrior.

The clock continues.

## Inside the beast

The crocodile may also be:

- war;
- trauma;
- the military;
- the Halcyon;
- or history itself.

Even while swallowed by those systems, time proceeds.

Children age.

Relationships change.

Bodies alter.

The war identity cannot remain permanent merely because it once preserved them.

## The opposite of Peter Pan’s refusal

The fantasy of never growing up is not freedom in *86*.

It resembles the Eighty-Six’s temptation to remain eternally:

- seventeen;
- armed;
- together;
- and moving toward death.

Theo’s injury forces him into time.

He becomes the first to leave the battlefield and begin looking at adulthood from outside it.

# XII. Shin’s helplessness and Lena’s reciprocal love

After speaking with Theo, Shin privately admits what he could not say before him.

He does not know whether he will be all right.

## The old Shin

Earlier Shin would have:

- suppressed uncertainty;
- accepted the role;
- and continued until collapse.

## The new Shin

He tells Lena:

- he is powerless;
- he cannot solve Theo’s loss;
- and he does not know what to do.

The admission is a major act of trust.

## Lena’s answer

Lena says:

- rely on me;
- share the grief;
- I will support you;
- I will protect you.

She does not claim she can fix Theo either.

Her offer is presence rather than solution.

## The verbal confession

Lena finally says:

> **わたしも──あなたが好きです。**

She then names a future containing:

- the sea;
- the Republic’s fireworks;
- the Federacy’s seasons;
- Roa Gracia’s aurora;
- Alliance landscapes;
- unknown cities;
- and flowers blooming again in the Eighty-Sixth Sector.

This is significant because the future is plural.

Shin’s life with Lena is not one symbolic destination.

It is a sequence of experiences.

## Kissing the scar

Lena kisses the scar around Shin’s neck.

The scar came from Rei’s violence and Shin’s childhood trauma.

Lena does not erase it.

She incorporates it into intimacy.

## Rejecting “until death”

Lena refuses the conventional phrase that love lasts until death separates the couple.

In their world, death is too easy.

Her chosen formulation is more demanding:

> I will wait.  
> Do not leave me.  
> Return.

It turns love toward repeated survival.

# XIII. Zelene and the pain of sounding human

Zelene normally speaks in machine language.

Human speech and laughter require painful computation.

She uses them with Shin because Shin treats her as a person.

## Speech as relational labor

Zelene’s human voice is not proof that she is naturally human.

It is an accommodation she chooses for a relationship.

She does not want Shin to feel that he is speaking only to an object.

## The ethical reversal

Human societies repeatedly force people to suppress personhood so they may function as weapons.

Zelene, a machine intelligence, expends effort to preserve the interpersonal form of a human conversation.

## Shepherds fear death

Vika concludes that Phönix development may be research into the immortality of Shepherds.

The Legion does not replace existing Shepherds with superior ones because the Shepherds themselves cling to:

- personality;
- identity;
- and survival.

The Legion’s most human minds fear being treated as replaceable components.

## The Legion’s contradiction

The Legion denies individual identity to countless captured minds while its highest command personalities seek to preserve their own.

This resembles the human institutions in the volume:

- elites preserve themselves;
- children and unwanted populations bear the cost.

# XIV. The Federacy’s aristocratic afterlife

The Federacy is democratic.

The Empire’s social machinery remains active underneath it.

Volume 9 makes that continuity unusually explicit.

## The unused private armies

Old aristocratic houses retained private military forces.

They withheld those forces while:

- ordinary citizens died;
- the Federacy’s army was depleted;
- and the war worsened.

They anticipated using intact forces after the war to regain political control.

## War as factional opportunity

When potential glory and valuable Legion information appear, nobles offer “volunteer” regiments.

The motive is not simply patriotism.

It includes:

- political reputation;
- military influence;
- and positioning for postwar succession struggles.

## The Night Onyx and Pyrope rivalry

Old ethnic-aristocratic divisions continue:

- the black-haired Night Onyx nobility;
- the red-haired Pyrope houses;
- and competing claims to Imperial legitimacy.

Shin’s surname makes him politically meaningful even though he wants no such role.

## The Federacy’s moral distinction

The Federacy is still far better than the Empire or Republic.

It possesses:

- legal citizenship;
- political debate;
- accountable command;
- and people attempting to protect the Eighty-Six.

Yet democratization did not automatically erase:

- blood hierarchy;
- eugenic projects;
- private armies;
- or inherited power.

Political liberation begins a struggle.

It does not conclude one.

# XV. The Myrmecoleon Regiment

The Myrmecoleon Regiment consists largely of mixed-blood descendants produced or gathered by the Brandtrotte faction.

Its name describes a chimeric creature:

- lion;
- ant;
- predatory;
- unable to consume what it catches;
- and eventually doomed to starve.

The name is both insult and prophecy.

## Children as failed breeding projects

The members are treated as:

- impure;
- unsuccessful;
- illegitimate;
- and unworthy of the houses whose blood they carry.

Many were deliberately produced through attempts to combine aristocratic traits.

Their failure to meet expectation becomes their identity before they are old enough to choose one.

## Heroism as conditional humanity

They are offered one route to recognition:

> Become exemplary warriors.  
> Win glory.  
> Prove the noble blood within you.

They therefore seek hero status with an intensity the Eighty-Six do not understand.

## The difference from the Eighty-Six

The Eighty-Six reject being made heroes because they know heroic images can become another form of use.

The Myrmecoleon soldiers need the image because no one has offered them value without it.

## Gilwiese

Gilwiese is intelligent enough to recognize the trap.

He understands that:

- their role was imposed;
- the Brandtrotte house views them as tools;
- and heroism may be another slave collar.

He still cannot see a practical way out.

His soldiers, Svenja, and his own sense of responsibility bind him.

## The tragedy of insight without exit

Gilwiese’s self-awareness does not free him.

This distinguishes knowing a curse from breaking it.

A person requires:

- alternative institutions;
- relationships;
- material security;
- and permission to imagine another self.

The Myrmecoleon Regiment possesses almost none of these.

# XVI. Svenja: the victory goddess as frightened child

Svenja Brandtrotte is presented as:

- a princess;
- a victory goddess;
- a noble mascot;
- and the symbolic center of the regiment.

She is approximately ten years old.

## The ceremonial surface

She wears:

- a red dress;
- a tiara;
- and the language of aristocratic superiority.

Her theatrical confidence initially makes her comic beside Frederica.

## The fear underneath

She is terrified of adult anger.

When someone raises his voice, she fears:

- punishment;
- rejection;
- and the authority of the Brandtrotte house.

## Contrast with Frederica

Frederica argues with:

- Shin;
- Ernst;
- officers;
- and adults

without fearing physical retribution.

She has political burdens and survivor guilt.

She has also been raised as:

- family;
- child;
- and person.

Svenja has been raised as a role.

## “Do not take our duty”

When Gilwiese calls their heroic function a slave collar, Svenja panics.

She says:

- it is all they possess;
- everyone must perform it;
- they must remain together;
- and Gilwiese must not leave.

Her love becomes coercion because she cannot imagine connection surviving his change.

## Kurena’s recognition

Kurena hears herself in Svenja.

She tells her:

> Do not bind your brother with your wound.

The advice is directed equally toward herself.

# XVII. The Holy Theocracy’s social theology

Noiryanaruse’s political order is built around predetermined role.

## The Earth Goddess

The society believes souls are born into the family suited to their purpose.

Family determines:

- occupation;
- marriage;
- status;
- and life course.

Freedom of occupational choice is not merely absent.

The concept itself is culturally weak.

## Bloodshed as absolute pollution

The religion prohibits believers from shedding human blood.

This creates a political contradiction:

- the state requires armed defense;
- the faithful cannot fight.

## The solution: divine weapons

The state declares its soldiers not ordinary believers but living weapons sent by the goddess.

Because they are not ordinary citizens:

- they need not follow the prohibition;
- and their violence does not stain the protected population.

## Honor without personhood

Divine weapons receive:

- education;
- ceremonial respect;
- military rank;
- and sometimes diplomatic responsibility.

They are not treated like degraded livestock in everyday life.

This makes the institution more psychologically stable than the Republic’s openly hateful model.

The essential deprivation remains:

> They cannot decide whether they are weapons.

## Education as manufactured consent

The divine weapons are taught from birth that military service is:

- their nature;
- destiny;
- honor;
- and sacred purpose.

They generally do not rebel.

The absence of rebellion does not prove freedom.

It may demonstrate how completely alternatives have been removed from imagination.

# XVIII. The doctrine collapses

For centuries, ordinary believers avoided military service because divine weapons fought for them.

Eleven years of war destroy almost the entire adult military caste.

The remaining pilots become younger and younger.

Eventually the state faces its own destruction.

The religious council then decides to conscript ordinary believers.

## The theological betrayal

The change reveals that the doctrine was never immutable divine truth.

When the cost finally reaches the protected population, the rule changes.

## Hymelnaade’s rage

She and the divine weapons were told:

- their sacrifices were necessary;
- their role was sacred;
- and their destiny could not be questioned.

Now the state implies that destiny was administratively convenient.

## Why reform feels like theft

From an outside perspective, conscripting ordinary citizens may appear like the beginning of equal responsibility.

To Hymelnaade, it means:

- generations of exclusive sacrifice were unnecessary;
- the divine weapons’ identity was contingent;
- and the one meaning given to their suffering has been devalued.

## The ethical paradox

An unjust system may become emotionally indispensable to the people it shaped.

Ending it is necessary.

Ending it can still feel like destroying the victim’s remaining self.

Reform therefore requires more than removing the role.

It must help people construct another life.

# XIX. Hymelnaade Reze

Hymelnaade is Kurena’s darkest mirror and the volume’s central antagonist.

She is not simply a fanatical religious commander.

Her faith has already been destroyed.

## Her family’s death

The Reze family supplied senior commanders.

At the beginning of the war, nearly all were gathered in one command center.

The Holy Theocracy deliberately or negligently stripped its protection and delayed rescue.

Young Hymelnaade was made to witness their deaths through a communication link.

## Manufacturing the saint of war

The state gained:

- a lone surviving child;
- an image of tragic resistance;
- and a symbolic saint whose suffering could unify the population.

Her family was converted into political theater.

## Her wound as identity

Hymelnaade knows the Theocracy betrayed her.

She cannot love it.

She also cannot relinquish the role built from that betrayal.

If “saint of war” is false, what remains?

## The plan

She stages what appears to be a national betrayal of the Federacy.

Her real purpose is larger.

She wants:

- the Eighty-Six killed;
- the Theocracy blamed;
- the Federacy morally traumatized;
- international trust damaged;
- foreign rescue reduced;
- vulnerable states abandoned;
- and humanity’s war effort weakened toward collapse.

This is a planned collective suicide expanded to geopolitical scale.

## Why the Eighty-Six?

Their deaths would create the perfect tragedy.

They are publicly understood as:

- persecuted children;
- heroic survivors;
- and symbols of moral responsibility.

If the Theocracy kills them, the outrage will be enormous.

Hymelnaade weaponizes their victimhood.

## The emotional motive

She wants the Eighty-Six to die unrewarded because their survival threatens her.

If they can:

- escape;
- form bonds;
- choose peace;
- and move toward futures,

then Hymelnaade must confront that her own inability to do so is not cosmically inevitable.

She wants them to fail so she can believe no one could have escaped.

# XX. “Mirror, mirror”

The chapter title asks what an ordinary mirror reflects.

It does not provide:

- flattering destiny;
- magical judgment;
- or an idealized self.

It reflects what stands before it.

Hymelnaade reflects Kurena.

The divine weapons reflect the Eighty-Six.

Svenja reflects Frederica.

Gilwiese reflects Shin.

The Myrmecoleon Regiment reflects Spearhead.

## Kurena and Hymelnaade

Both:

- lost family through state violence;
- were turned into child soldiers;
- possess combat as their principal identity;
- fear the end of war;
- and resent those who move toward futures they cannot imagine.

The difference emerges through choice.

Kurena does not want everyone else destroyed in order to preserve her place.

Once she recognizes the possibility, she rejects it.

## Shin and Gilwiese

Both are mixed-blood descendants of great martial houses.

Shin:

- does not seek noble recognition;
- has family and institutional alternatives;
- and wants war ended.

Gilwiese:

- was denied family;
- possesses only martial recognition;
- and cannot easily imagine life without heroic status.

Shin’s apparent personal strength depends partly upon relationships Gilwiese was never given.

## Frederica and Svenja

Both are politically symbolic children.

Frederica is allowed to remain argumentative and personally loved.

Svenja has been trained to perform the approval of soldiers and the Brandtrotte house.

Their difference is not natural personality.

It is relational environment.

# XXI. “Valkyrie Has Landed”

The title literalizes the new **Arme Furieuse** airborne deployment.

Reginleifs are launched across the Legion front by electromagnetic catapult and glide beneath **Frigg’s Robe**, using technology derived from Eintagsfliege wings.

They descend into the ash-covered city like white battle maidens.

## Landing rather than dying

A Valkyrie traditionally belongs to the relation between battlefield and heroic dead.

Volume 9 changes the emphasis.

The Valkyries do not arrive merely to select the fallen.

They land:

- to disable the Halcyon;
- to create a route for the living;
- and to return.

## One-way appearance, return-oriented purpose

The deployment system has the visual grammar of a one-way heroic insertion.

The characters’ emotional grammar has changed.

Shin tells Lena:

> **行ってくる。**

The phrase assumes return.

The battlefield descent is no longer an entrance into a proper grave.

It is one stage of a round trip.

## The white figures in ash

The Reginleifs appear almost mythic.

The novel repeatedly strips away that heroization.

Inside them are:

- frightened adolescents;
- grieving friends;
- jealous lovers;
- and people unsure whether they can kill another human.

The Valkyrie is a machine image.

The person inside must remain more than it.

# XXII. The ash-covered battlefield

The Theocracy’s northern war zone is called a Blank Region.

It is covered by volcanic ash containing:

- toxic metals;
- sharp glasslike particles;
- and material hostile to lungs, eyes, engines, and ordinary life.

## Purity and pollution

The Theocracy imagines itself as a pure society protected from the pollution of bloodshed.

Its territory is physically covered in contamination.

## Pearl-white architecture

Military buildings are:

- sealed;
- filtered;
- luminous;
- and religious in appearance.

The clean interior exists because the outside has become uninhabitable.

This mirrors the political order.

The believers’ moral cleanliness exists because the divine weapons absorb violence outside.

## Ash as failed purification

Ash often signifies what remains after burning.

The Theocracy’s entire social order is residue:

- centuries of doctrine;
- eleven years of sacrifice;
- and a military caste nearly consumed.

## Cinderella imagery

Kurena is positioned inside a strange battlefield Cinderella structure.

The experimental railgun manual resembles an invitation to a ball.

The **Trauerschwan**, the Black Swan, becomes the vehicle through which she enters a decisive role.

But no prince grants her worth.

Shin entrusts her with responsibility.

Kurena must decide whether she believes she can carry it.

# XXIII. The Halcyon

The Halcyon is an offensive factory type formed from the land battleship and Noctiluca.

It is approximately:

- forty meters high;
- seven hundred meters long;
- armed with five 800-millimeter railguns;
- capable of production and repair;
- and controlled through a distributed micromachine network.

## Factory and battleship combined

It can:

- manufacture war;
- transport war;
- repair war;
- and fire war across strategic distance.

It is not simply a weapon.

It is an institution of self-reproducing violence.

## The five railguns

The guns are individually named:

- Frida;
- Gisela;
- Helga;
- Isidora;
- Johanna.

Johanna contains the Shepherd derived from Shana.

The intimate names contrast with the monstrous machinery.

Human identity survives only as labels attached to artillery.

## Adaptive ammunition

The Halcyon uses new munitions developed against Reginleif mobility:

- shells splitting into linked nodes;
- monomolecular wire forming a wide lethal net;
- self-propelled mines acting as acoustic spotters.

The Legion learns from prior defeats.

It converts human survival into data for the next killing method.

## Distributed cognition

Destroying one core does not end it.

Control and cooling are distributed.

This materially embodies the political problem of Volume 9:

> Systems cannot always be defeated by beheading one villain.

The doctrines creating child soldiers exist beyond Hymelnaade.

The aristocratic order exploiting Myrmecoleon exists beyond Brandtrotte.

The Republic’s racism survived individual dissenters.

The Halcyon is war without one clean head to cut off.

# XXIV. Human innovation: the Trauerschwan

The Federacy deploys its first experimental railgun, **Trauerschwan**, the Mourning or Black Swan.

It is:

- unfinished;
- slow;
- not designed for field use;
- nearly immobile;
- and capable of only one practical shot in the operation.

Kurena’s Reginleif is attached as an improvised fire-control system.

## The human counterpart to Legion escalation

The Legion’s railguns represented strategic terror.

Humanity now creates an equivalent weapon.

The event is triumphant.

It should also be unsettling.

The technological arms race continues.

## Black Swan

The weapon resembles a long-necked bird lying against the earth.

Its name carries:

- mourning;
- rarity;
- and disruptive exception.

Kurena herself has feared becoming useless.

She is placed inside a machine regarded as:

- awkward;
- experimental;
- and useful for one singular purpose.

## Trust rather than demotion

Kurena interprets the rear assignment as evidence Shin does not trust her.

Gilwiese reveals the opposite.

He opposed assigning her because of her Volume 8 failure.

Shin insisted:

- one failure is insufficient reason to discard a soldier;
- he trusts her ability;
- and if she fails again, recovering from that failure is the commander’s responsibility.

## Command as absorbing failure

This is one of Shin’s strongest leadership developments.

Trust does not mean believing the subordinate cannot fail.

It means:

> The subordinate may act without knowing that one mistake will erase her value.

The Republic’s system made every person disposable.

Shin creates repeated chances.

# XXV. Kurena’s original love

Chapter 3 opens with a memory from Kurena’s early days under Shin.

She had seen him mercy-kill a wounded comrade.

She asks whether it hurts.

She offers to take over because she is a skilled shot.

## The original structure

Kurena’s desire to help Shin did not initially mean:

> I must remain useful so he will not leave me.

It meant:

> I do not want him to carry pain alone.

That is a healthier form of love.

## Shin’s thanks

He refuses because he promised to perform the role.

He thanks her.

Kurena remembers his first smile toward her.

## Love distorted by fear

Over time, repeated abandonment transforms:

- sharing burden

into:

- proving indispensability.

Volume 9 allows Kurena to recover the earlier form.

She can help Shin without requiring him to choose her romantically or remain unchanged.

# XXVI. Shiden and Shana

Shana died during the sea operation.

Her body was recovered by the Noctiluca.

Her mind becomes one of the Halcyon’s Shepherds.

## Shiden’s first impulse

Shiden wants to join the operation specifically to destroy Shana.

Beneath that stated purpose is a desire for mutual destruction.

She may die with the person she lost.

## Shin’s refusal

Shin uses Shiden’s own earlier standard against her.

A soldier seeking a double death is unreliable.

She may participate only if she intends to return.

## The multiplied Shana

When the Halcyon copies one control structure into several **Mélusine** units, Shana’s voice appears in multiple places.

The duplication demonstrates:

- a copied voice is not singular personhood;
- memory can be mechanically reproduced without restoring the dead;
- and Shiden may need to “bury” the same image repeatedly.

## Olivia’s answer

Shiden asks what Olivia would do if he faced the Shepherd made from his fiancée, Anna Maria.

He says he would fight her.

He would not die with her.

He must survive to:

- report to her family;
- help recover the landscapes she loved;
- visit her grave;
- bring flowers;
- and cry for her every year.

He may someday love another person.

He may not.

Neither outcome requires forgetting.

## Memorial ritual requires time

Olivia gives Shiden a reason to survive that depends upon remembrance.

To mourn annually, one must remain alive through years.

This is a major revision of the Eighty-Six memory ethic.

## “Goodbye, Shana”

Shiden destroys the copied Shana and says farewell.

She decides:

> If the Legion reproduces Shana again, she can bury her again.

The dead no longer possess one final battlefield capable of trapping the survivor forever.

# XXVII. The human betrayal

After the Halcyon is initially immobilized, the Theocracy’s Third Corps suddenly attacks the Federacy expeditionary force.

The timing is deliberately irrational.

That irrationality reveals Hymelnaade’s real goal.

## Not conquest

If the Theocracy merely wanted:

- the Eighty-Six;
- the railgun;
- or Legion intelligence,

it would attack after the Halcyon was destroyed and the Federacy troops were exhausted.

## Maximizing casualties

Hymelnaade attacks while:

- the Halcyon remains active;
- the airborne battalion is isolated;
- and both sides are still under Legion pressure.

The objective is not acquisition.

It is tragedy.

## The false asylum offer

Hymelnaade tells the Eighty-Six to bring:

- themselves;
- achievements;
- and glory

into the Theocracy.

The language resembles rescue.

The actual structure is coercion.

They may join or die.

## Victimhood as propaganda material

Hymelnaade wants the Eighty-Six killed by the very nation they came to assist.

Their deaths would become:

- international outrage;
- emotional spectacle;
- and evidence against trust between states.

The child victim is turned into narrative ammunition.

# XXVIII. Lena’s precaution

The Theocracy blocks ordinary radio communication.

Hymelnaade assumes the battlefield is informationally isolated.

Lena has withheld knowledge of Para-RAID.

## Trust and secrecy

Lena had genuinely liked Hymelnaade.

She still obeyed restrictions against disclosing:

- Para-RAID;
- Shin’s ability;
- Zelene;
- and Sirin technology.

The decision is not personal betrayal.

It is command responsibility.

## The message already escaped

Para-RAID transmits events to the Federacy despite jamming.

The Federacy communicates diplomatically through Roa Gracia.

The Theocracy’s Second Corps receives orders and moves to stop Hymelnaade.

## Lena as “wise Queen”

The Second Corps commander thanks the Strike Package’s wise Queen.

The title is earned less by tactical brilliance than by refusing to let personal affection erase prudent boundaries.

## Secrecy as protection

Volume 7 explored how secrecy can become avoidance.

Volume 9 shows its legitimate use.

Not every intimate relationship entitles the other person to strategic knowledge.

# XXIX. The Eighty-Six encounter human enemies

The betrayal creates a combat problem the Eighty-Six have never truly faced.

They have killed people through mercy.

They have not fought human beings as enemy soldiers.

## Why Legion combat was morally simpler

The Legion are:

- machines;
- dead fragments;
- and weapons incapable of surrender in ordinary battle.

Fighting them does not require deciding that another living person may be killed.

## Human targets

The Eighty-Six hesitate to fire upon the Theocracy’s manned Feldreß.

They target unmanned-looking support machines instead.

## The child’s body

When one is destroyed, a small hand falls from the cockpit.

The pilot is a girl younger than ten.

The Eighty-Six freeze.

## Recognition through trauma

They see:

- themselves;
- the children they once were;
- and the bodies the Republic would have placed inside smaller machines if technologically convenient.

They cannot kill the child without symbolically killing their own past self.

## “Do not shoot”

The plea transmitted across Para-RAID is not:

> Do not make us shoot.

It is:

> **Do not shoot.**

It sounds like the child’s own terror.

The Eighty-Six hear the plea they once could not articulate before Republic guns.

# XXX. The divine weapons as children inside “unmanned” machines

The support unit **Ryano-Shu** is approximately 120 centimeters high.

Everyone assumed no person could fit inside it.

The machine was designed precisely for very small pilots.

## The Republic parallel

Lena immediately recognizes the horror.

The Republic called its Juggernauts unmanned while placing people inside them.

The Theocracy never explicitly calls the Ryano-Shu unmanned.

Its size allows outsiders to make the same false assumption.

## Different ideology, same result

The Republic’s lie:

> The pilot is not human.

The Theocracy’s doctrine:

> The pilot is not an ordinary human but a sacred weapon.

Both permit the protected society to avoid responsibility.

## Childhood optimized for weapon design

The small pilot is not an unfortunate accommodation.

The machine benefits from:

- reduced frontal area;
- reduced armor material;
- and cheaper construction.

The child’s body becomes an engineering parameter.

## Religious honor does not repair coercion

The divine weapons may be treated respectfully in daily life.

A beautiful doctrine does not change what the cockpit contains.

# XXXI. The Pied Piper

Chapter 5’s title evokes a figure whose music leads away both rats and children.

The Theocracy’s command system gives this motif literal force.

## The saint’s voice

The divine weapons are conditioned to obey a particular acoustic quality in the voices of saints.

A hidden true name or command can force them to cease action.

Their bodies respond before personal choice.

## Children following sound

The child pilots:

- depend emotionally upon older commanders;
- panic when the command machine dies;
- and move according to sacred voices.

They are led through war by sound.

## Hymelnaade as piper

Hymelnaade leads them toward:

- rebellion;
- national destruction;
- and collective death.

She is herself another child led by the Theocracy’s narrative.

## The state as piper

The larger piper is the institution.

It teaches children which voice means:

- duty;
- holiness;
- family;
- and obedience.

The resulting choice feels internal.

Its melody was composed by adults.

# XXXII. The Myrmecoleon massacre

The Eighty-Six cannot shoot.

The Myrmecoleon Regiment can.

Its heavy Vánagandrs are overwhelmingly superior to the Theocracy’s machines.

The battle becomes slaughter.

## The knights’ joy

The mixed-blood soldiers:

- count kills;
- announce names;
- compete for glory;
- and laugh through the massacre.

They imitate ancient heroic warfare.

## Why they need victory

They were told from childhood that they were:

- failed mixtures;
- wastes of resources;
- kennel animals;
- and unworthy of family recognition.

Victory is their one chance to prove that they are true descendants of warriors.

## Laughter as scream

Their joy is not uncomplicated cruelty.

The narration frames it as something like a scream.

They must win.

If they are not heroes, the identity imposed upon them collapses into worthlessness.

## Invisible bodies

High-caliber shells leave little recognizable human remains.

The killers do not have to see the children they destroy.

Technology protects heroic fantasy from bodily consequence.

## Svenja sees

Svenja is not in a cockpit.

She sees:

- crushed machines;
- blood;
- and children’s deaths.

She must praise each soldier.

Her ceremonial function requires her to transform horror into recognition.

The victory goddess becomes the person who validates slaughter.

# XXXIII. Lena and the dirty work of command

Lena ultimately orders the Myrmecoleon Regiment to open the path.

She does so because her Eighty-Six cannot kill the children.

## Partiality

Lena chooses:

- her own soldiers’ psychological survival;
- over the Myrmecoleon soldiers’ moral burden;
- and over the lives of hostile Theocracy children.

This is not morally pure.

## Owning the choice

She does not pretend the order is neutral.

She says she is their Queen.

She will accept:

- hatred;
- blame;
- and the coldness required of command.

## Gilwiese’s correction

Gilwiese refuses to let Lena claim all responsibility.

He says:

- he offered to do it;
- he leads his regiment;
- and she must not absorb his people’s agency into her own guilt.

This is important.

Lena’s habit of taking moral burden can become another form of control.

## Shared responsibility

The mature command relationship is neither:

- commander alone guilty;
- nor soldiers merely obedient.

Different participants own different parts of the action.

The tragedy cannot be purified by assigning it to one conscience.

# XXXIV. Kurena’s crisis: “Do I want the war never to end?”

Hymelnaade’s words force Kurena to confront her hidden desire.

## What peace threatens

If the war ends:

- Shin may leave combat.
- Theo already has.
- Lena occupies Shin’s romantic future.
- The group may scatter.
- Kurena’s shooting skill may lose social meaning.
- The identity “Eighty-Six” may become historical rather than immediate.

## The unspoken wish

Kurena asks:

> Would it be better if the war never ended?

The thought horrifies her.

It also feels honest.

## The distinction from Hymelnaade

Kurena does not want:

- others dead;
- civilization destroyed;
- or suffering universalized.

She wants the security of the only world she knows.

The emotional seed is similar.

The moral action differs.

## Fear of the future

Kurena’s deepest trauma is helplessness.

As a child, she watched her family be taken and killed.

The future means:

> another unknown place from which violence may emerge.

Remaining on the battlefield paradoxically feels safer because she knows its rules.

# XXXV. Kurena, Svenja, and the bond that binds

Svenja pleads with Gilwiese not to abandon the role.

She says:

- they possess only their duty;
- they possess only one another;
- and shared wounds require shared permanence.

Kurena hears herself.

## Love becoming curse

Svenja is not malicious.

She is afraid.

Her fear makes her ask another person to remain imprisoned.

Kurena recognizes the same structure in her desire that Shin never change.

## Kurena’s apology

She tells Gilwiese she is sorry.

Not because she personally harmed him.

Because she realizes what someone in his position would need to hear.

She then says she must apologize to someone else.

## Advice to Svenja

Kurena asks Svenja not to bind her brother with her wound.

She adds, implicitly:

> You may fear that he will leave.  
> That does not mean you must prevent him from moving.

This is Kurena speaking to herself.

# XXXVI. Kurena chooses movement

Kurena remains afraid.

Her breakthrough is not the disappearance of fear.

It is action despite fear.

## “Let’s go”

She tells the Eighty-Six:

> **進もう。**  
> **Let’s move forward.**

## The new reason to fight

They will:

- help Shin’s airborne battalion;
- destroy the Shepherd Shana;
- reach the Second Corps;
- and stop the child soldiers.

## Rescuing the past self

The Eighty-Six could not save themselves as children.

Now they can attempt to save children placed in the same position.

The act does not rewrite history.

It gives the past another consequence.

## Self-rescue through another

Helping the divine weapons is also a way of telling the Eighty-Six’s younger selves:

> Someone should have come.  
> What happened to you was wrong.  
> You deserved rescue.

## From pride to purpose

Their pride no longer means only enduring whatever is imposed.

It becomes the strength to interrupt repetition.

# XXXVII. The difference between fate and pride

Hymelnaade claims:

> The battlefield is the Eighty-Six’s destiny.

Lena rejects the statement.

## Fate

Fate means:

- role assigned externally;
- no legitimate alternative;
- and suffering interpreted as natural order.

## Pride

Pride means:

- a form of conduct chosen under constraint;
- a refusal to be defined entirely by the oppressor;
- and the capacity to revise one’s path when circumstances change.

## Lena’s command

She tells the Eighty-Six:

- they need not save the Theocracy;
- they are not obligated to be heroes;
- they may fight for their own reasons;
- and fighting is not their destiny.

The operation continues because abandoning the Halcyon would threaten:

- the Federacy;
- other fronts;
- and the possibility of ending the war.

This is strategic choice, not sacred fate.

## The boundary is imperfect

The Eighty-Six remain under military command.

Their alternatives are constrained.

The distinction lies not in absolute freedom but in:

- reasons acknowledged;
- dissent heard;
- withdrawal considered;
- and personhood preserved.

# XXXVIII. Shin’s command decision

Some Eighty-Six propose abandoning the operation and leaving the Theocracy to the Halcyon.

Their anger is legitimate.

Shin refuses.

## Not heroism

He does not say:

> We must save these people because we are noble heroes.

He says:

- the Halcyon may later attack the Federacy;
- Legion intelligence is strategically necessary;
- the operation’s consequences exceed present anger;
- and abandoning it impulsively may reduce everyone’s chance of survival.

## Dissent recognized

Shin says the objection is not wrong.

He does not shame the speaker.

## No demand for martyrdom

He says they need not stake their lives without limit.

If the danger becomes unacceptable, withdrawal can be reconsidered.

## Leadership after the Sector

The Republic’s command said:

> Fight because you are disposable.

Shin’s command says:

> Continue because there is a reason, but your lives remain part of the calculation.

This does not remove coercion from military command.

It changes its ethical structure.

# XXXIX. Trust distributed

The Halcyon battle also requires Shin to relinquish singular control.

## The Eighty-Six’s response

Other Processors tell Shin:

- they survived before his ability;
- they can react to mines and artillery;
- and he does not need to warn every person about every threat.

## The Reaper’s habit

Shin’s supernatural hearing encouraged a belief that he must:

- perceive everything;
- warn everyone;
- and personally prevent every death.

## Institutional maturity

A functioning unit cannot rely upon one omniscient protector.

It requires:

- distributed awareness;
- delegated command;
- and acceptance that others may make judgments.

## Relationship and trust

Trust does not mean Shin ceases caring.

It means he allows others to possess competence and risk.

This is another form of not binding people through fear.

# XL. Shiden’s burial of Shana

Shiden finally destroys Johanna and the copied Shana.

She fires while saying:

> **お前のことを葬ってやるのは──このあたしだ。**  
> **I’m the one who will bury you.**

## Rivalry as intimacy

Shiden and Shana disliked one another.

They:

- fought;
- argued;
- competed;
- and promised to dig the other’s grave.

The hostility was also attachment.

## Burial rather than union

Shiden initially approached the battle as possible reunion through death.

She ends by assuming the survivor’s task.

## “I never want to do this again”

After surviving, she tells Shin:

- she is tired of war;
- she does not want more friends to become Shepherds;
- and she wants to live easily and happily after the war.

This is one of the clearest statements of future desire from an Eighty-Six outside the original Five.

## The warrior chooses peace

Shiden is among the most combative characters.

Her desire for peace therefore cannot be dismissed as weakness or naïveté.

She knows exactly what war demands.

That is why she is finished with it.

# XLI. Kurena fires the Trauerschwan

The operation’s final shot belongs to Kurena.

## One shot

The experimental railgun requires approximately two hundred seconds to reload.

The Halcyon may recover sooner.

Kurena effectively has one chance.

## The old fear

After Volume 8, she fears another failure will prove:

- Shin does not need her;
- she is weak;
- and she will be left behind.

## The new trust

She learns that:

- Shin selected her despite objections;
- he accepts responsibility for possible failure;

She no longer interprets trust as a test she must pass to retain love.

It is something already given.

## Remembering prior usefulness

Kurena recalls that she has long helped Shin:

- shooting Shepherds;
- eliminating command units;
- and sharing battlefield burden.

She did not need to become his romantic choice to have mattered.

## The prayer

She says:

> **終わらせよう。──この戦争を、あたしたちの手で。**

The line transforms shooting from:

- proof of usefulness;
- into a chosen contribution toward ending the world that made shooting necessary.

## Human railgun against Legion railgun

The technological symmetry matters.

Humanity defeats the monstrous weapon using a weapon built from the same principle.

The victory is not moral purity.

It is appropriation directed toward another end.

# XLII. The end of the Third Corps rebellion

Once the Halcyon falls and communications are restored, the Second Corps broadcasts the sacred command compelling the divine weapons to cease battle.

## The safety mechanism

The divine weapons possess a conditioned shutdown command.

The Theocracy designed them with an anti-rebellion failsafe.

Even their supposed sacred duty contains proof that the state never fully trusted them as persons.

## Immediate mercy

The command stops further child deaths.

Its benevolent use does not erase its coercive structure.

## Hymelnaade’s defeat

Hymelnaade asks why the goddess abandoned her.

Her rebellion fails because:

- Lena preserved communication;
- other Theocracy commanders resisted;
- the Eighty-Six rejected her mirror;

## Her soldiers’ loyalty

Some of Hymelnaade’s units continue fighting the Legion even after her scheme is exposed.

They do so partly to:

- reduce Eighty-Six casualties;

The people she would consume into her revenge still attempt to protect her.

## Lena’s final argument

Lena tells Hymelnaade:

> Do not degrade what your soldiers tried to protect.  
> Do not take yourself from them through death.

This is not absolution.

It is a demand that Hymelnaade accept the burden of survival.

# XLIII. Hymelnaade after defeat

The Holy Theocracy does not execute her.

Its religion prohibits killing even convicted criminals.

She will likely face:

- loss of house;
- confinement;
- and political disgrace.

The Second Corps commander hopes for eventual clemency.

## Living without the role

Hymelnaade’s punishment is also her thematic challenge.

She must live after:

- saintliness;
- command;
- rebellion;
- and the identity of sacred tragedy.

## The Theocracy’s possible reform

The Second Corps commander says the divine-weapon institution must be reconsidered.

The war has demonstrated that it cannot continue.

## Reform remains ambiguous

Lena understands that removal from combat may not feel like liberation to the divine weapons.

Like the Eighty-Six, they were shaped to experience battle as selfhood.

A humane transition must permit them to participate in determining what follows.

## No complete solution

Volume 9 does not show:

- successful reintegration;
- abolition of caste;

It establishes the necessity and difficulty of change.

# XLIV. Kurena’s confession to Shin

On the return train, Kurena tells Shin:

Kurena names Shin as:

- rescuer;

- fellow Eighty-Six;

- beloved;

## Shin’s answer

He says:

> **悪い。**

- offer false hope;

## Kurena’s final line

She says:

> **あなたを好きで悪かったことなんて、なかったもの。**

Unreturned love does not become:

- wasted;

She can retain it without using it to bind him.

# XLV. Kurena and Lena

Kurena later asks Lena whether she properly answered Shin.

## From rival to supporter

# XLIX. The Sirins as disposable intelligence assets

The Sirins must not be captured by:

## Ethical discomfort

They are again treated as ideal expendable agents.

The Sirins possess:

# LI. The meaning of “hero”

## The Eighty-Six

Hero status can become an expectation that they:

- forgive betrayal;
- save everyone;
- accept sacrifice;
- and remain inspirational.

## Myrmecoleon

Its members desperately want to be heroes.

## Hymelnaade

She wants the Eighty-Six’s deaths to produce the perfect heroic tragedy.

## Lena

She explicitly tells them:

Reports quickly turn Gilwiese and Svenja into:

- noble prince;
- beautiful victory goddess;

Reality is rewritten into a marketable heroic story.

## Heroism as unstable public property

The person does not control the heroic meaning attached to action.

That meaning belongs partly to:

- government;
- media;
- audience;
- and political faction.

The series increasingly separates:

> heroic action

from:

> the social role of Hero.

# LVI. Character deep dive: Hymelnaade Reze

She concludes that because meaning was constructed, all future meaning must be impossible.

## Narcissism of universal despair

## Child beneath the saint

## The necessary defeat

Kurena’s movement disproves Hymelnaade.

A person may retain:

- wound;
- pride;
- and memory

without turning them into universal law.

# LXI. Tensions and limitations

## 1. The Holy Theocracy’s extremity is highly concentrated

Its:

- religious caste system;
- child pilots;
- conditioned command voices;
- saintly bloodlines;
- and centuries of institutionalized military outsourcing

create a powerful thematic mirror.

The society risks feeling designed primarily to reflect the Eighty-Six rather than emerging with the fuller social complexity of the Federacy.

## 2. Hymelnaade’s geopolitical plan is intentionally childish

Gilwiese calls it immature.

The narrative agrees.

Its broad consequences depend upon many states reacting in precisely the most destructive fashion.

The emotional logic is stronger than the strategic logic.

## 3. Child-soldier horror is escalated almost maximally

The revelation that very young children are inside tiny machines is effective.

It can also feel like the series intensifying atrocity to force thematic recognition.

## 4. Myrmecoleon’s massacre is partially displaced

The Eighty-Six avoid killing children by allowing another exploited group to do it.

The text recognizes the moral cost but does not dwell on individual Myrmecoleon perpetrators after the battle with the same intensity given to Eighty-Six trauma.

## 5. Lena’s “Queen” framing remains risky

Her willingness to choose her people and accept hatred is serious command development.

The monarchic-romantic language can aestheticize unequal moral authority.

## 6. The Halcyon battle is mechanically dense

The:

- distributed control;
- multiple railguns;
- Mélusine copies;
- blade-pile improvisation;
- airborne insertion;
- and experimental human railgun

create exciting military spectacle.

The density occasionally competes with the psychological drama.

## 7. Theo’s recovery begins quickly at the level of theme

His grief remains real.

Within one volume he already:

- identifies new relationships;
- requests the sketchbook;
- jokes about the hook;
- and considers helping future injured soldiers.

The movement is credible but narratively compressed.

## 8. The Sirin expendability remains largely unchallenged

The novel condemns children being turned into weapons while continuing to deploy artificial persons as disposable infiltration units.

The contradiction may be intentional.

It remains unresolved.

# LXVI. The volume’s central philosophical argument

Volume 9 distinguishes three relationships with identity.

## 1. Identity as destiny

> I was made for this role.  
> Therefore no other life is legitimate.

This is the Holy Theocracy’s doctrine.

## 2. Identity as curse

> This role wounds me, but losing it would leave me with nothing.  
> Therefore everyone connected to me must remain inside it.

This appears in:

- Hymelnaade;
- Svenja;
- Kurena’s fear;
- and the Myrmecoleon Regiment.

## 3. Identity as authored inheritance

> This role preserved me and remains part of me.  
> I may carry its meaning into another form.

This appears in:

- Theo;
- Shiden;
- Kurena’s final decision;
- and Shin’s evolving pride.

The third position does not require condemning the past self.

The Eighty-Six were right to develop pride.

They are now permitted to use that pride to leave the battlefield.

# LXVIII. Final synthesis

Volume 9 begins with one machine asking another:

> **Can we be integrated?**

It ends with human beings beginning a counteroffensive toward the possibility of ending the war.

The difference between those movements is the difference between consumption and relationship.

The Legion integrates by erasing boundaries.

The damaged Noctiluca, stolen command minds, railguns, combat data, and land battleship are fused into the Halcyon.

Everything survives only as part of a larger weapon.

Human beings must discover another way to remain connected.

Theo cannot remain a Processor.

Kurena cannot become Shin’s chosen lover.

Shiden cannot bring Shana back.

The sea fleet cannot remain a fleet.

The divine weapons cannot continue existing only as sacred soldiers.

The Myrmecoleon soldiers cannot become legitimate children merely by piling up kills.

Hymelnaade cannot restore her family by making the world share her ruin.

The mature response to loss is not integration through erasure.

It is relationship across difference.

Theo remains part of Spearhead while leaving its battlefield formation.

Kurena remains part of Shin’s life while surrendering romantic possession.

Shiden remains faithful to Shana by surviving and burying her.

Lena and Shin become lovers without either becoming the other’s sole function.

The Eighty-Six remain proud while refusing to treat battle as destiny.

This is why the Holy Theocracy is such an effective culmination of the series’ second movement.

The Republic persecuted the Eighty-Six through contempt.

The Theocracy destroys its divine weapons through reverence.

Contempt says:

> You are beneath humanity.

Sacred militarization says:

> You are above ordinary humanity.

Both statements deny the person the right to be ordinary.

The ordinary is what Volume 9 increasingly defends.

Theo does not require a magnificent replacement vocation.

He may:

- draw;
- eat fried bread;
- watch a movie;
- wear a ridiculous hook;
- and help someone later because he understands what leaving combat feels like.

Shiden does not need to die beautifully beside Shana.

She may:

- bring flowers;
- drink with old comrades;
- curse Shana’s memory;
- laugh;
- and live an easy life.

Kurena does not need to win Shin.

She may love him forever and still permit:

- Lena;
- herself;
- and Shin

to become different people.

The Eighty-Six do not need to embody the perfect tragedy the public wants.

They may be frightened children concerned primarily with saving themselves.

This defense of ordinariness is not anti-heroic cowardice.

It is the moral purpose of the war.

If the future contains only new stories requiring children to become:

- saints;
- death gods;
- victory goddesses;
- heroic hybrids;
- or sacred weapons,

then defeating the Legion would not end the deeper violence.

Hymelnaade’s tragedy is that she can imagine no self beneath the story.

Her family’s deaths were stolen by the Theocracy and rewritten as her sainthood.

When the doctrine changes, she experiences it as another murder.

Rather than admit that the meaning was imposed, she chooses to make the imposed meaning eternally true.

She tries to destroy:

- her country;
- the Eighty-Six;
- international trust;
- and perhaps humanity

so that no future can prove her role unnecessary.

Kurena could have followed the same logic.

She has also been told by history that:

- battle is her only home;
- usefulness is the condition of belonging;
- and losing the war identity means losing everything.

She sees Shin moving.

She sees Theo forced outside.

She sees Lena beside Shin.

She sees others choosing school, love, travel, and peace.

Her first response is terror.

Her great achievement is not that terror vanishes.

It is that she refuses to make her terror everyone else’s prison.

She tells Svenja not to bind Gilwiese.

She tells the Eighty-Six to move.

She tells herself that she will no longer let the helpless child she was be killed again.

Then she fires the Trauerschwan.

The shot destroys more than the Halcyon.

It destroys Kurena’s belief that shooting has value only because it keeps her beside Shin.

She fires because she has chosen:

- the war’s end;
- the children’s rescue;
- her comrades’ future;
- and her own unknown future.

Her final confession completes the transformation.

She does not say:

> Choose me.

She says:

> I loved you.  
> I still love you.  
> Become happy.

The love remains.

The chain breaks.

Theo’s conclusion provides the same movement in another form.

He wears the hook of a pirate pursued by a ticking crocodile.

Time has caught him.

War allowed the Five to exist inside a suspended adolescence where nothing mattered beyond the next battle.

Theo can no longer remain there.

The clock continues even inside the beast.

He goes ahead.

But unlike the dead who “went ahead” in Volume 1, Theo is alive.

Kurena can say:

> Go, and come back.

That may be the most complete proof yet that the Eighty-Six’s relationship with separation has changed.

Departure no longer means:

- death;
- betrayal;
- or erasure.

One person may leave the battlefield and remain part of the group.

One person may change without condemning those who are not ready.

One person may go first and later show the others what lies beyond.

The volume’s closing strategic revelation enlarges this personal movement.

Humanity has found the likely location from which the Legion may be shut down.

The war may possess an endpoint.

This prospect would once have terrified the Eighty-Six more than they could admit.

It still frightens them.

They now begin moving toward it deliberately.

The author’s afterword says that from here begins their struggle aimed at the future.

That future has not yet been secured.

Frederica’s blood remains politically dangerous.

The Legion hides another immense structure.

The old nobility continues maneuvering.

The divine weapons remain shaped by centuries of coercion.

The Myrmecoleon Regiment has received public glory that may strengthen its cage.

The Sirins continue dying as usable artificial people.

Kurena still does not know what she will become.

Theo has only begun rehabilitation.

None of this diminishes the turning point.

The first volumes asked whether the Eighty-Six were human.

The middle volumes asked whether people so thoroughly formed for death could want to live.

Volume 9 asks whether they can permit life to change them.

Its answer is finally yes.

Not easily.

Not all at once.

Not without grief.

But yes.

The strongest final formulation of Volume 9 is:

> **Pride becomes a curse when it forbids the future from changing the person who survived. It becomes freedom when it gives that person the courage to choose what the survival was for.**

For Theo, survival is permission to search.

For Shiden, it is the time required to remember.

For Lena and Shin, it is a life they may build together.

For Kurena, it is the terrifying step beyond usefulness and unreturned love.

For the Eighty-Six collectively, it is the decision that their final great act as soldiers should be to end the world that permits children to exist only as soldiers.

The Valkyries have landed.

Their true task is no longer to gather the glorious dead.

It is to return alive—and to make a world in which the next children will not need Valkyries at all.
