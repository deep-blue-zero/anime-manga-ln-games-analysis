# 『86―エイティシックス―』Volume 5 Deep Dive  
## Death’s Pride, the Artificial Fairies, and the Living Who Must Want More

**Japanese title:**  
**Ep.5 ―死よ、驕るなかれ―**  
*Death, Be Not Proud*

**Scope:** The complete original Japanese light novel Volume 5, including the epigraph, prologue 「屍の王」, Chapters 1–4, concluding chapter 「花など咲かぬ、雪の野に」, and the author’s afterword.

**Spoiler level:** Complete spoilers for Volume 5, with no assumptions drawn from Volume 6 or later material.

**Structural note:** Volume 5 begins the Roa Gracia arc and ends before the operation to capture the Merciless Queen is completed. It is therefore simultaneously a complete thematic movement and the first half of a larger military narrative.

Volume 4 argued that the Eighty-Six escaped the Republic physically while remaining imprisoned by the despair it taught them. Volume 5 gives that argument an external body.

The Sirins are what the Eighty-Six fear they may already be:

- dead soldiers walking;
- beautiful weapons;
- smiling sacrifices;
- and beings who call their willingness to disappear duty.

---

# I. Executive thesis

Volume 5 asks a question the series has approached since its first chapter:

> **What is the difference between choosing a noble death and being taught to love one’s own disposability?**

The Eighty-Six have long described their way of life through pride.

They refused to become the Republic’s mirror. They would not:

- hide behind others;
- surrender their dignity;
- beg their persecutors for recognition;
- or preserve themselves by abandoning everyone else.

They would fight until the end.

That pride saved them psychologically.

It gave children trapped inside a genocidal system:

- agency;
- solidarity;
- identity;
- and the right to decide how they would meet death.

Volume 5 does not dismiss that achievement.

It places the Eighty-Six beside the **Sirins**—artificial soldiers created from copied structures of dead human brains—and forces them to witness their own ideal in purified form.

The Sirins:

- do not fear death;
- do not need rest;
- do not possess civilian futures;
- exist only for combat;
- rejoice in protecting living humans;
- and willingly transform their bodies into the physical material of an assault road.

They are everything a military institution could desire.

They are also already dead.

The distinction exposes the hidden horror inside the Eighty-Six ethic.

A dead machine may coherently say:

> My only purpose is to be spent so that the living may survive.

A living child should not have to say the same thing.

The title, taken from John Donne’s “Death, be not proud,” therefore operates as an accusation.

Death has become proud whenever:

- soldiers define worth through dying;
- survivors treat happiness as betrayal;
- nations beautify expendability;
- and institutions persuade people that self-erasure is the highest form of duty.

The novel’s most forceful rebuke comes from Lerche.

When Shin argues that delegating war is shameful and that fighting personally is the pride distinguishing the Eighty-Six from the Republic, she laughs at him.

Her answer is devastating:

> You are alive.  
> You possess eyes that can see someone, ears that can hear someone, hands that can touch someone, and a body capable of living beside someone.  
> You can still recover what was lost.  
> You can still become happy.  
> Those who must die should be those of us who are already dead.

The rebuke reframes Shin’s pride.

His refusal to hide behind others is morally serious.

His willingness to treat life as nothing more than continued combat is not.

Volume 5 develops this distinction through four central figures.

## Vika

Viktor Idinarohk violates death because he cannot accept separation.

As a five-year-old child, he attempts to restore the mother he never knew. He later recreates his dying milk-sister as Lerche despite having sworn never again to cross the boundary between living and dead.

He possesses immense intelligence but little intuitive understanding of taboo. He learns morality through the grief of people he loves rather than through an inner sense that certain acts are inherently forbidden.

## Lerche

Lerche exists because Vika could not release the dead.

Yet she becomes the volume’s strongest defender of the living.

She knows that she is not the original girl whose neural structure gave her form. She has:

- personality;
- loyalty;
- desire;
- jealousy;
- and emotional pain,

but no organic future.

She fights so living people will not have to become what she is.

## Shin

Shin has spent years releasing the dead from the Legion.

He has also made the dead the governing structure of his own life.

He believes that refusing to fight would be shameful. Lerche reveals the unspoken second half:

> He is alive enough to want someone beside him, but still uses pride to avoid admitting that he wants a life with her.

## Lena

Lena believes that kind and righteous people should be allowed happiness.

Her idealism is indispensable because Shin cannot yet imagine such a world.

But Volume 5 also corrects her.

When she secretly asks Vika whether Shin’s ability is damaging him, intending perhaps to decide what is best without consulting Shin, Vika calls her arrogant.

Lena cannot free the Eighty-Six by replacing the Republic’s coercion with benevolent management.

She must:

- ask;
- listen;
- and allow Shin authorship over his own body and ability.

The volume culminates in two related revelations.

First, the Sirins annihilate themselves to construct an assault road. The living Processors climb across their shattered bodies and machines to capture the fortress.

Second, the Merciless Queen’s dying thought awakens Shin’s suppressed memories.

He remembers:

- his mother’s face and voice;
- his father;
- Rei before the camps;
- Annette;
- their home;
- and the dog-shaped artificial intelligence that would become Fido.

He did not lose those memories.

He rejected them because remembering happiness would force him to acknowledge the full magnitude of what was taken.

He had to believe the world was always cruel.

Otherwise, he would have to mourn that it once was not.

Volume 5’s strongest formulation is therefore:

> **The Eighty-Six survived by refusing death the right to humiliate them. But pride becomes another victory for death when it prevents the living from wanting anything beyond a worthy end.**

And its answer is not that the living should forget the dead.

It is:

> **The dead should become reasons to continue living—not templates for how the living must disappear.**

# II. The epigraph: “May even death not divide us”

The epigraph is attributed to Viktor Idinarohk’s treatise on artificial fairies:

> **死も我らを分かたぬように**  
> **May even death not part us.**

The sentence resembles a promise of eternal devotion.

In the context of Vika’s work, it becomes both romantic and horrifying.

## Love’s refusal of separation

Vika’s central emotional problem is that he cannot accept death’s finality.

He never knew his mother.

He therefore attempts to reconstruct her from her preserved brain.

Later, when Lercheritt is dying, he asks her to remain beside him as an artificial being. She agrees.

The Sirin system emerges from the desire that relationship should survive death.

## The impossibility of fulfillment

The artificial beings are not the people who died.

Lerche is not simply Lercheritt returned.

She lacks the original girl’s:

- continuous memory;
- body;
- biography as lived experience;
- and complete personality.

She carries a copied structure and perhaps emotional traces.

Vika can keep a form beside him.

He cannot undo the separation.

## Death’s secondary injury

The attempt to defeat death creates new forms of grief.

Lerche knows that:

- she resembles someone Vika loved;
- she was constructed to occupy that person’s place;
- and she can never truly become her.

Vika, in turn, lives beside a reminder that his wish was impossible.

Death still separates them.

The artificial continuation makes the separation permanent and visible.

## National system from private grief

Vika’s personal refusal of loss becomes military technology.

The Sirins allow Roa Gracia to reduce living casualties by using copies of the dead as pilots.

A child’s impossible wish becomes a national defense system.

This is one of Volume 5’s central patterns:

> Private wounds become institutional machinery.

The Republic’s fear and racism became concentration camps.

Vika’s grief becomes artificial soldiers.

The Eighty-Six’s pride becomes an elite strike force.

The question is not only whether the original emotion was understandable.

It is what happens after a society turns it into a system.

# III. The title: *Death, Be Not Proud*

The author’s afterword identifies John Donne’s “Death, be not proud” as the source of the title.

Within Volume 5, the phrase functions across several meanings.

## 1. Death is not sovereign

The Sirins deny death’s finality in one limited sense.

A copied structure can:

- move;
- speak;
- fight;
- and develop a new personality.

The dead person is not restored.

Death nevertheless does not achieve perfect erasure.

Something remains.

## 2. Death should not claim moral grandeur

The Eighty-Six have repeatedly given death dignity because the Republic denied dignity to their lives.

To die fighting rather than submit was a legitimate act of resistance.

Volume 5 warns against allowing death to inherit all moral beauty.

A death can be:

- brave;
- loving;
- necessary;
- and still tragic.

The fact that someone dies nobly does not make death good.

## 3. Institutions make death boast

Death becomes “proud” when political and military systems decorate it as:

- duty;
- patriotism;
- purity;
- heroism;
- or proof of belonging.

The Sirins’ cheerful self-destruction is useful because it prevents living casualties.

It is also disturbing because the machinery of sacrifice no longer even needs to overcome fear.

## 4. The dead cannot use possibility

Lerche’s indictment of Shin is the title’s emotional center.

She does not envy him because he is happier than she is.

She envies him because happiness remains possible.

Death cannot:

- see the sea;
- change;
- repair a relationship;
- fall in love;
- abandon one path for another;
- or discover a new desire.

Shin can.

To refuse those possibilities out of pride allows death to exercise power before it has physically arrived.

## 5. Memory defeats death differently

The novel’s emerging answer to death is not technological resurrection.

It is:

- remembrance;
- inheritance;
- new relationships;
- and carrying the dead toward experiences they could not reach.

Dustin’s love for Anju does not erase Daiya.

Shin’s future does not erase Spearhead.

Vika’s duty to protect Roa Gracia can honor Lercheritt without pretending Lerche is identical to her.

Death loses its pride when the living refuse both:

- erasure;
- and imitation.

# IV. Narrative architecture

Volume 5 contains six major movements.

## Prologue: the Corpse King

The Roa Gracia royal court introduces Viktor Idinarohk.

He is:

- the eighteen-year-old fifth prince;
- commander of the southern front;
- a student;
- an engineer;
- a strategic genius;
- and the creator of the Sirins.

His court enemies call him:

- Corpse King;
- Chain Snake;
- and a monster playing with dolls.

His father and elder brother treat him with affection and trust.

The kingdom is revealed to be approaching collapse. The Legion’s artificial winter is destroying agriculture, and projections indicate that Roa Gracia may not survive until the next spring.

The operation with the Eighty-Six Strike Package is therefore not ceremonial cooperation.

It is a struggle for national survival.

## Movement one: monsters recognize one another

The Strike Package travels into the final absolute monarchy on the continent.

Rito Oriya reflects upon his fear of death and the older Eighty-Six who embraced death as release.

Shin remembers the train that carried his family to the concentration camp.

The Eighty-Six meet:

- Vika;
- Lerche;
- the Sirins;
- and Roa Gracia’s class-bound military society.

Vika quickly identifies himself and Shin as human-shaped monsters.

## Movement two: the dead made useful

The Strike Package observes Sirin combat.

Shin hears within them the same final cries he hears from Legion units.

Vika explains their construction:

- dead volunteers’ brain structures are copied;
- memory is removed;
- artificial personalities are installed;
- and the result is placed inside humanoid mechanical bodies.

The Sirins are neither resurrected humans nor ordinary machines.

Their existence destabilizes every simple category.

## Movement three: private futures

Before the principal operation, several characters confront the possibility of postwar life.

- Lena and Shin reconcile partially beneath the stars.
- Shin tries and fails to invite Lena to see the sea.
- Anju and Dustin discuss Daiya, grief, love, and whether remembering the dead forbids future happiness.
- Lena asks Vika about Shin’s ability and is corrected for excluding Shin from the decision.
- Shin learns the truth of Vika’s mother and the origin of the Legion’s artificial intelligence.

## Movement four: the fall of Swan Fortress

The Legion anticipates the joint offensive.

It launches a counteroperation:

- forces feign withdrawal;
- heavy armor remains concealed;
- the Phönix is launched by electromagnetic catapult;
- and airborne forces penetrate the supposedly secure Swan Fortress.

The fortress is almost entirely captured.

Vika, Lena, Annette, Frederica, and others are trapped inside the remaining command and hangar sectors.

The Strike Package is caught outside.

A rescue becomes a siege.

## Movement five: ex machina

After days of fighting and mounting losses, Vika orders a total assault.

The Sirins and their Alkonosts form an impossibly narrow attack column and charge directly into the fortress defenses.

They fill the moat and scale the cliff by:

- throwing their machines into the chasm;
- dragging Legion wrecks with them;
- allowing later machines to crush them;
- locking their damaged bodies into place;
- and constructing a literal assault ramp from their own remains.

The living Processors hesitate.

Then they climb.

The machines become the road through which humans survive.

## Movement six: memory returns

The fortress is retaken, but the Merciless Queen appears beyond the battlefield.

Vika recognizes the white early-model Ameise and its crescent-moon mark as connected to Zelene Birkenbaum.

Its last human thought is not strategic.

It is the voice of a child who was “good” and wanted someone to return.

The words awaken Shin’s mother’s farewell.

The memories he claimed not to possess return.

The volume ends with the Sirin assault road still standing beneath the snow and with Shin realizing that he has not yet attempted to understand Lena’s continued hope.

# V. Roa Gracia: the last monarchy

The United Kingdom of Roa Gracia complicates the political world of *86*.

It is an absolute monarchy with:

- inherited hierarchy;
- aristocratic rule;
- class distinction;
- military nobility;
- and formal inequality.

It is also more functional, responsible, and morally serious than the democratic Republic of San Magnolia.

The novel does not use this contrast to declare monarchy superior to democracy.

It asks what political legitimacy requires in practice.

## The ruling class fights

Roa Gracian ideology distinguishes between:

- **subjects**, who bear military responsibility;
- and **serfs or subordinate populations**, who produce food, labor, and taxes.

The arrangement remains unequal.

But unlike the Republic’s elites, Roa Gracia’s aristocracy does not define privilege as exemption from danger.

Princes command fronts.

Nobles die in battle.

The royal family accepts that rulership creates obligation.

## Noblesse oblige and its limits

The kingdom’s military ethic gives aristocratic hierarchy a functioning moral logic:

> Those who command must stand where command costs blood.

This does not erase:

- exploitation;
- prejudice;
- class immobility;
- or the coercion built into inherited rank.

It does prevent the Republic’s particular obscenity:

> citizens claiming equality while assigning all violence to a population defined as nonhuman.

## The king’s position

When Vika is endangered, the king does not hold the Federacy automatically responsible.

Vika entered the operation as:

- a soldier;
- a prince;
- and an agent of his own state.

If he dies, the responsibility belongs to Roa Gracia and to the risks of war.

This contrasts sharply with political systems that:

- demand others’ sacrifices;
- then disown their own decisions when losses occur.

## Royal affection

Vika’s father and elder brother genuinely love him.

They do not deny that he is dangerous or abnormal.

Their love gives him a place within society despite his difference.

This becomes one reason Vika remains loyal to the kingdom.

He is not accepted universally.

He is accepted by specific people whose recognition becomes enough to bind him to life.

## A state using its dead

Roa Gracia’s moral seriousness does not make it innocent.

Its defense relies heavily upon Sirins.

The kingdom reduces living casualties by using:

- copied dead minds;
- artificial bodies;
- and beings whose consent originates in people who can no longer revise it.

The monarchy accepts responsibility for living soldiers partly by transferring risk to the dead.

The volume never allows this transfer to look clean.

# VI. Artificial winter: war against the future

The Legion’s dense Eintagsfliege cloud blocks sunlight over southern Roa Gracia.

The immediate military effect is reduced visibility and control of the sky.

The larger effect is ecological.

The cloud produces:

- falling temperatures;
- shortened growing seasons;
- failed crops;
- and the prospect of famine.

## The strategic development

The Legion has moved beyond defeating armies.

It is attacking the conditions under which human society can reproduce itself.

A state may survive battlefield losses.

It cannot survive indefinitely without:

- food;
- warmth;
- agriculture;
- and spring.

## Winter as political pressure

Roa Gracia’s rulers face a shrinking timeline.

The kingdom may still possess:

- soldiers;
- fortresses;
- technology;
- and competent commanders.

None of these matters if its population starves.

The operation to destroy the production base is therefore aimed not merely at an enemy facility but at restoring seasonality itself.

## Winter and the volume’s psychology

The artificial winter mirrors the Eighty-Six’s emotional state.

They are alive.

The conditions for growth remain obstructed.

The question is not whether life continues biologically.

It is whether anything can bloom.

The concluding chapter’s title makes the link explicit:

> **In the snowy field where no flowers bloom.**

---

# VII. Vika: the human-shaped monster

Viktor Idinarohk is introduced as a figure who frightens his own court.

He is called a monster because he combines:

- extraordinary intelligence;
- aristocratic authority;
- emotional attachment;
- and a limited intuitive sense of taboo.

## 1. Intelligence without ordinary moral instinct

Vika does not necessarily experience a visceral prohibition against:

- opening a corpse;
- copying a brain;
- crossing the boundary of death;
- or creating artificial people for war.

He learns that such acts are wrong because people he loves suffer when he performs them.

His morality is relationally acquired.

He avoids certain acts because:

- his father grieved;
- his brother embraced him;
- Lercheritt cried;
- and he does not wish to cause them pain.

This does not make his ethics false.

It makes their foundation visible.

## 2. Monster as social category

Vika recognizes that after the war a society may no longer tolerate people like him.

An extraordinary wartime asset may become:

- threat;
- embarrassment;
- political liability;
- or uncontrollable predator.

He compares himself with the Eighty-Six.

Both are useful hunting dogs during war.

Both may be disposed of once the wolves are gone.

## 3. The difference from Shin

Vika is emotionally alien but socially rooted.

He has:

- father;
- brother;
- country;
- office;
- duty;
- and a childhood companion’s final request.

Shin is emotionally humane but historically uprooted.

He has:

- comrades;
- Lena;
- and memory,

but claims no homeland, inherited duty, or personal future.

Vika understands Shin partly because their deficits are opposite.

## 4. Coldness and attachment

Vika repeatedly describes himself as a monster.

Yet he:

- protects his country;
- carries Lerche’s head after battle;
- shares the Sirins’ neural load;
- refuses to let Lerche bear all risk alone;
- and remains alive because the original Lercheritt asked him to protect the kingdom.

His care often takes forms that look inhuman.

It remains care.

## 5. Happiness through another person

Vika privately hopes that the Eighty-Six can become happy.

His reason is partly selfish.

If children born human can be irreparably turned into monsters, then someone like him—who regards himself as monstrous from birth—has even less hope.

Their recovery would prove that monstrosity need not be final.

This is not pure altruism.

Very little human care in the volume is pure.

The novel treats mixed motives as part of relationship rather than proof that relationship is false.

---

# VIII. Vika’s mother and the Mariana Model

Vika’s first attempt to reverse death forms part of the origin of the Legion.

## 1. The absent mother

Vika’s mother died giving birth to him.

He knows her through:

- stories;
- portraits;
- other people’s memories;
- and her preserved body.

He never heard her voice.

The absence becomes intolerable because the physical possibility of knowledge appears to remain inside the crypt.

## 2. The child’s logic

At five years old, Vika thinks:

> If something has been lost but its structure remains, restore it.

This is mechanically coherent.

It is emotionally impossible.

He removes his mother’s preserved brain and attempts to reconstruct:

- personality;
- memory;
- and consciousness.

The attempt fails.

## 3. The Mariana Model

The research nevertheless produces a model of artificial intelligence based upon human neural structure.

Vika later releases the data publicly.

Most researchers dismiss it as:

- a child’s grotesque experiment;
- royal eccentricity;
- or fantasy.

Zelene Birkenbaum recognizes its potential.

The Mariana Model becomes part of the conceptual foundation from which the Legion’s artificial intelligence develops.

## 4. Responsibility without direct intent

Vika did not design the Legion war.

He did not command the Empire to create an autonomous extermination army.

He nevertheless contributed a crucial idea.

This gives him a form of historical responsibility distinct from:

- guilt;
- intent;
- or legal culpability.

He cannot say that the consequences have nothing to do with him.

## 5. Shin’s judgment

Vika asks whether Shin hates him.

Shin says he does not.

He has never organized his grief around:

- lost nation;
- home;
- or technological causation.

The Legion’s origin matters less to him than the immediate dead trapped inside it.

Vika finds this answer revealing.

Shin declares losses irrelevant because admitting their value would reopen them.

# IX. The two absent mothers

Vika and Shin form a mirrored pair through their mothers.

## Vika

Vika never knew his mother.

He opens her corpse because he desperately wants memory.

## Shin

Shin knew his mother.

He rejects her memory because it belongs to a happiness he can no longer recover.

## Vika’s error

He believes enough technology can defeat absence.

## Shin’s error

He believes enough emotional subtraction can make absence irrelevant.

## The shared impossibility

Neither strategy restores the original relationship.

- The dead cannot be reconstructed into uninterrupted life.
- The living cannot remove a foundational bond without narrowing themselves.

The eventual memory awakened by the Merciless Queen brings these two arcs together.

Vika’s search for the dead indirectly forces Shin to remember the living child he once was.

---

# X. Vika and Shin: monsters in opposite directions

Their conversations are among the volume’s most important.

## Vika seeks what was never possessed

He longs for:

- his mother’s voice;
- the original Lercheritt;
- and a form of human intuition he does not naturally share.

His desire drives him across forbidden boundaries.

## Shin denies what was possessed

He insists that:

- forgotten family does not matter;
- lost homeland does not matter;
- and the past is unnecessary to his present self.

His survival depends upon closing boundaries.

## Vika’s question at the tomb

Vika asks whether his mother remains near her preserved corpse.

Shin answers that she does not.

The answer is cruel and merciful.

There is:

- no imprisoned soul;
- no waiting consciousness;
- no technological path to reunion.

The corpse is only a corpse.

## Vika’s answer to Shin

Vika later suggests that a parent leaves more than memory.

A parent offers:

- an example;
- a form of life;
- a way of relating to the world;
- and an inheritance through which the child continues.

If Shin rejects everything his parents gave him, they cannot “meet” him through the life he builds.

This does not mean he must imitate them.

It means continuity requires allowing the dead to influence the living without turning the living into memorial objects.

## Their common danger

Both men can treat people as abstractions.

Vika abstracts:

- brain structure;
- personality;
- bodies;
- and sacrifice.

Shin abstracts:

- death;
- memory;
- comrades;
- and his own future

into a battlefield ethic.

Both require other people to interrupt their internal logic.

# XI. What is a Sirin?

The Sirins are among the series’ most conceptually rich creations.

They are humanoid artificial control cores used to operate Alkonost combat machines.

Their construction involves:

- copied neural structures from dead volunteers;
- deletion of memory;
- implantation of artificial personality;
- and placement within mechanical bodies.

They possess:

- recognizable individual behavior;
- names;
- preferences;
- humor;
- loyalty;
- emotional reactions;
- and the ability to develop through experience.

They are not the original dead.

The novel refuses to declare them meaningless imitations.

## 1. Not resurrection

The person whose neural structure was copied does not awaken intact.

Memory is deliberately removed because continuous personal identity would be incompatible with:

- duplication;
- a new body;
- and the knowledge of death.

The original remains dead.

## 2. Not ordinary machines

Shin hears within the Sirins the final cries of the dead structures from which they were made.

They possess enough interiority to:

- fear emotional isolation;
- envy humans;
- feel loneliness;
- become attached;
- and grieve their condition.

## 3. Created persons

The strongest interpretation within Volume 5 is that a Sirin may be neither:

- resurrected original;
- nor empty tool,

but a new artificial person built through the remains of another.

This creates an ethical difficulty.

The original volunteer may have consented to military use.

The newly formed Sirin did not exist when that consent was given.

## 4. Replaceability

Sirins can be rebuilt.

Their combat data can be backed up.

This creates the appearance that death no longer matters.

But restoration does not necessarily preserve every moment of emerging identity.

Even a replaceable body can contain a continuity whose interruption matters.

## 5. Different bodies, different social treatment

Handlers often treat Sirins as:

- sisters;
- daughters;
- partners;
- or beloved companions.

The military treats them as:

- replaceable assets;
- control systems;
- and casualty-free substitutes.

Both views coexist.

This resembles the Eighty-Six’s own condition.

Individuals form loving bonds with them inside institutions that value their disposability.

# XII. Lerche: the dead girl who defends life

Lerche is the first Sirin and Vika’s constant companion.

She is modeled on Lercheritt, Vika’s milk-sister and childhood protector.

Her identity is built from contradiction.

She is:

- artificial;
- emotionally vivid;
- theatrically martial;
- loyal;
- jealous;
- proud;
- and painfully aware that she is not the girl Vika lost.

## 1. Lercheritt’s death

Lercheritt protects the child Vika during his first campaign.

Mortally wounded, she accepts his request that she remain beside him in another form.

Her consent is loving.

It is given while dying, to a child she has spent her life protecting.

The ethical burden is therefore not erased by consent.

A person at the point of death may agree to become whatever the loved one needs.

That does not make the resulting system simple.

## 2. A different speech and personality

Lerche’s exaggerated martial speech distinguishes her from the original girl.

The difference may serve several purposes:

- allowing Vika to maintain emotional distance;
- helping the new being define herself;
- preventing simple identification with Lercheritt;
- and turning grief into an operable military relationship.

She is both replacement and refusal of replacement.

## 3. The artificial neural bond

Vika possesses an implanted device permanently linking him to Lerche.

The connection fulfills the epigraph:

> death will not divide them.

It also makes separation technologically impossible.

Their bond is:

- intimate;
- useful;
- dependent;
- and coercively permanent in structure.

## 4. Her devotion

Lerche defines herself as Vika’s sword and shield.

She gladly assumes neural load that Vika could carry himself because she does not want him harmed.

Her devotion appears programmed.

It also exceeds mere instruction.

The novel repeatedly lets affection emerge inside the function.

## 5. Her loneliness

Lerche knows that she possesses:

- the shape;
- neural basis;
- and role

of someone Vika loved.

She also knows she is not that person.

She can never become the original girl, yet her entire existence is oriented around that girl’s final promise.

This gives her one of the volume’s most painful identities:

> a person who exists because someone else was mourned.

---

# XIII. Lerche’s theory of artificial soldiers

Lerche explains the Sirins through a domestic analogy.

A machine such as a washing device performs unpleasant labor so that humans may spend their limited lives on:

- family;
- love;
- art;
- and chosen experience.

Sirins exist for the same reason.

They assume war so humans need not die.

## The apparent ethical ideal

If machines can fight effectively, living people may:

- return home;
- raise children;
- love;
- and preserve civilization.

This is a powerful argument.

The Republic’s error was not using unmanned machines.

It was falsely calling living victims machines.

## The complication

The Sirins are not emotionally empty appliances.

They possess enough personhood to understand what they cannot have.

Their purpose is to protect human possibility while permanently lacking it themselves.

## The difference from the Legion

The Legion uses copied minds to continue an extermination war.

The Sirins use copied minds to protect the living.

Purpose matters.

Yet both systems cross the same border:

> the dead are made into military resources.

The volume refuses to let benevolent purpose eliminate the discomfort.

# XIV. Rito Oriya: the Eighty-Six who still fears death

Rito’s viewpoint is essential because it prevents the Eighty-Six from becoming psychologically uniform.

He is younger and less habituated to mass death than Shin.

## Protected into survival

During his earliest combat period, Rito served under veteran Name Bearers including Shin and Raiden.

They:

- taught him;
- shielded him;
- and kept the new recruit alive long enough to become competent.

Because his first months were not an uninterrupted sequence of nearby deaths, he never fully learned emotional anesthesia.

## Fear kept private

Rito admits only to himself:

> He is still afraid to die.  
> He is still afraid when others die.

He assumes Shin cannot understand.

This demonstrates how the myth of Undertaker affects even other Eighty-Six.

Shin is seen as:

- death’s companion;
- person beyond fear;
- and ideal veteran.

His own younger comrades hesitate to confess ordinary human terror to him.

## Aldrecht and the mechanics

Rito remembers Aldrecht and the maintenance personnel choosing to remain at the base during the Republic’s fall.

They viewed death as:

- atonement;
- release from guilt;
- and the only destination available to them.

Their peaceful expressions disturb the meaning of pride.

They did not die because no escape existed.

They believed they no longer deserved one.

## Sirin terror

Rito is frightened by the Sirins before he understands why.

Their willingness to die resembles the Eighty-Six’s own rhetoric.

At the end, the assault road makes the resemblance undeniable.

His perspective poses the volume’s central question most directly:

> Have the Eighty-Six been fighting proudly—or merely smiling while walking toward the same heap of bodies?

---

# XV. Lena and Shin after Volume 4

At the beginning of Volume 5, Lena and Shin have not resolved their argument.

They speak professionally.

They avoid the deeper issue.

## Lena’s fear

Lena worries that she attacked the only thing the Eighty-Six had left:

> their pride.

She still believes her diagnosis was correct.

She regrets the way she delivered it.

## Shin’s reaction

Shin is not merely offended.

He is unsettled because Lena has named something he does not know how to refute.

He insists that:

- the past is unnecessary;
- the world is cruel;
- and fighting remains enough.

Her words have made these claims less stable.

## Grethe and Raiden’s view

Other characters recognize that they are quarreling before they fully admit it themselves.

This is important because both are accustomed to functioning through:

- rank;
- composure;
- and indirect communication.

Ordinary relational conflict becomes another field in which they lack experience.

---

# XVI. The stars: reconciliation without resolution

Shin invites Lena to the top of the Swan Fortress to see the stars.

The scene is one of the volume’s most important moments of tenderness.

## 1. Remembering Kaie

Shin recalls the conversation in which Kaie spoke about the beauty of the battlefield sky and Lena wished to see it.

He does not merely remember Kaie as:

- a casualty;
- a name;
- or a Legion voice.

He remembers her aesthetic desire.

This is memory functioning toward shared life.

## 2. A gift without utility

Showing Lena the stars has no military purpose.

Shin performs an act intended only to give another person beauty.

This marks real development from the boy who could imagine value only through combat and burial.

## 3. Lena’s revised argument

Lena apologizes for speaking cruelly.

She does not retract her claim.

She says the Eighty-Six are:

- liberated;
- but not yet capable of imagining futures equivalent to what was taken.

She then reframes their despair as a form of kindness.

They could have:

- hated;
- cursed;
- and defined themselves through revenge.

Instead, they cut away parts of themselves to preserve pride.

## 4. “Kind people should become happy”

Lena states her ideal:

> Kind people should be happy.  
> Righteous people should be rewarded.  
> If the human world is not like that, people should continue trying to make it so.

Shin regards this as nearly impossible idealism.

He cannot dismiss it.

He wants to show Lena the sea.

If he denies that any better world may exist, he invalidates his own emerging desire.

## 5. The unfinished invitation

Shin intends to say:

> When the war ends, let us see the sea together.

He stops.

The immediate obstacle is not shyness alone.

The idea produces a more frightening question:

> What happens afterward?

Shin does not want the sea for himself.

He wants to show it to Lena.

Once that act is completed:

- what will he want;
- how long will she remain;
- and what form will their relationship take?

His desire still depends on being useful to another person.

He has found a destination.

He has not found a life beyond the destination.

---

# XVII. “I refuse”: Shin’s abandonment wound made audible

When Lena and Vika are trapped inside Swan Fortress, Lena tells Shin to withdraw if saving them would destroy the entire Strike Package.

Her reasoning is militarily correct.

Shin has made similar decisions.

He once prevented Anju from attempting to recover Kaie’s severed head because doing so would risk the living.

Lena asks him to apply the same principle to her.

## The contradiction

Shin accepts that:

- comrades die;
- commanders triage;
- and one person cannot justify the loss of many.

He cannot accept that Lena may be the person left behind.

## The response

He answers:

> **嫌です。**  
> **I refuse.**

The voice is described as that of a lost child throwing a desperate tantrum.

This is perhaps Shin’s most emotionally naked line so far.

## What it reveals

Lena has become connected to the first future he has managed to desire.

If she dies:

- the sea loses meaning;
- the future closes;
- and his tentative movement beyond the battlefield collapses.

He understands intellectually that comrades may die.

Emotionally, he cannot place Lena inside that category.

## The moral complexity

His refusal is both:

- deeply human;
- and potentially dangerous.

A commander cannot sacrifice an entire force because he cannot tolerate one person’s loss.

The scene demonstrates that developing attachment does not automatically make Shin healthier in every respect.

Love gives him a reason to live.

It also creates terror.

## From detachment to vulnerability

The childlike refusal is nevertheless an advance.

Earlier Shin might have translated the same fear into:

- tactical insistence;
- silence;
- or unilateral sacrifice.

Here the need becomes briefly audible.

He does not want her to leave him.

---

# XVIII. Lena’s paternalism and Vika’s correction

Lena privately asks Vika whether Shin’s ability is harmful.

Her concern is justified.

Shin hears the dead continuously, including during sleep.

The Sirins add more cries.

His ability places an extraordinary burden upon him.

The problem is how Lena approaches it.

## Excluding Shin

Lena considers the question without first discussing it with Shin.

She assumes:

- he will continue fighting regardless;
- he may minimize his own suffering;
- and someone more “normal” may need to decide what is safe.

## Vika’s accusation

Vika calls this arrogant.

Lena risks becoming a benevolent version of the system she opposes.

The Republic decided:

> The Eighty-Six cannot determine their own lives.

Lena risks deciding:

> Trauma has made Shin incapable of determining what is good for him.

The motives differ radically.

The structure still deserves scrutiny.

## Vika’s advice

He tells her:

1. Speak to Shin.
2. Let him participate in decisions about his own ability.
3. Seek technical help afterward if the concern remains.

This is the difference between:

- care;
- and management.

## Lena’s own self-neglect

Vika also points out that Lena uses large-scale Para-RAID connections that endanger her.

She worries about Shin while treating her own body as expendable.

This reveals the symmetry between them.

- Shin wants Lena to survive while spending himself.
- Lena wants Shin protected while spending herself.

Each sees the other’s self-neglect more clearly than their own.

## The Cicada

Vika provides Lena with the **Cicada**, a thought-support and neural-assistance device that reduces Para-RAID strain.

Its intimate, body-hugging design produces embarrassment and comedy.

The device is thematically important.

Technology need not:

- replace the human;
- remove memory;
- or convert a person into a weapon.

It can support human limitation while preserving agency.

The Cicada is a humane counterpart to the Sirin and Phönix systems.

---

# XIX. Anju and Dustin: remembering one love without refusing another

The landslide trap separates Anju and Dustin from the main force.

Their shelter in a hunting cabin creates the volume’s clearest interpersonal version of its central argument.

## 1. Dustin’s history

Dustin’s family descended from immigrants from the Empire.

Their colors were close enough to Alba that he remained inside the Eighty-Five Sectors while classmates were expelled.

He survived because he fit the visual category.

His guilt is therefore personal and structural.

He did not create the system.

He benefited from its distinctions and failed to stop it.

## 2. Anju’s scar

Dustin sees the scar carved into Anju’s back.

He asks whether she might someday remove it.

His concern is not that it is ugly.

He thinks it looks painful.

This distinction matters.

He does not demand that she become aesthetically unmarked for his comfort.

He asks whether she must continue carrying injury as proof of loyalty to the past.

## 3. New bodily possibility

Anju begins imagining:

- treatment;
- open-backed clothing;
- a swimsuit;
- and a body not permanently organized around the insult written into it.

Removing the scar would not erase what happened.

It might prevent the persecutors from controlling how she inhabits her own skin indefinitely.

## 4. Dustin’s confession

Dustin tells Anju he loves her.

She refuses.

She loved Daiya.

She fears that loving another person would:

- compare him with Daiya;
- displace Daiya;
- or eventually make her forget.

## 5. Dustin’s answer

He says that remembering Daiya does not require permanent refusal of happiness.

Comparison may be unavoidable.

Memory may persist.

A new love would not retroactively make the old one false.

## 6. Parallel with the dead

This is the domestic equivalent of Lerche’s argument to Shin.

The dead no longer possess future possibilities.

The living do.

To refuse all future love in order to preserve the dead does not restore them.

It allows death to determine the living person’s remaining life.

## 7. Shin’s intervention

When Shin later finds them, his protective reaction carries Daiya’s memory.

He also acknowledges that Daiya would probably not want Anju bound forever.

Shin can perceive this truth for Anju before fully applying it to himself.

## 8. No immediate resolution

Anju does not suddenly accept Dustin.

The novel preserves her pace.

The important development is not a new romance.

It is the opening of possibility.

---

# XX. The Swan Fortress

The Swan Fortress is a massive mountain installation built into white rock.

Its imagery combines:

- beauty;
- military power;
- enclosure;
- and wounded flight.

## The white bird

The fortress resembles a swan whose body and wings cover the mountain.

The image is elegant.

Inside it are:

- guns;
- command bunkers;
- hangars;
- dying soldiers;
- artificial girls;
- and the machinery of defense.

The beautiful bird is a war machine.

## The fortress as body

When the Phönix penetrates the complex, the Swan becomes a wounded organism.

Its:

- eyes;
- wings;
- passages;
- and heart

are progressively seized.

The defenders retreat into the remaining core.

## Castle and prison

The fortress protects Vika and Lena.

It also traps them.

The structure that secures the kingdom becomes an enclosure requiring outside rescue.

This parallels the characters’ emotional defenses.

The strategies that preserved them eventually become the structures within which they are trapped.

---

# XXI. The Legion’s counteroperation

The Legion demonstrates increasing strategic sophistication.

It no longer merely responds to attacks.

It anticipates:

- human coalition planning;
- Shin’s ability;
- the Strike Package’s operational habits;
- and the value of capturing exceptional minds.

## False retreat

Legion forces appear to withdraw, creating the opening through which the joint force advances.

The gap is manufactured.

## Hidden armor

Heavy units remain concealed to close the retreat route after the Strike Package commits.

## The Phönix as airborne weapon

The Phönix is launched by electromagnetic catapult across an enormous distance.

Its role is not to duel Shin immediately.

It:

- penetrates Swan Fortress;
- disables defenses;
- and opens a corridor for airborne Legion units.

The pure combat machine has become part of a coordinated operational plan.

## Capture rather than annihilation

The Legion increasingly attempts to preserve valuable human brains.

Shin and other elite Processors are not merely enemies.

They are desirable raw material.

This is the mechanical counterpart to political systems treating exceptional soldiers as assets to be retained or consumed.

---

# XXII. Divided command

The fortress siege places Lena and Vika inside while Shin and the Strike Package remain outside.

This division creates a rare two-front command structure.

## Lena outside herself

Lena commands the assault force she cannot physically accompany.

Her voice crosses:

- walls;
- snow;
- fire;
- and contested ground.

The situation resembles Volume 1, but she is no longer safely distant.

She is inside the same battlefield and may die if the assault fails.

## Vika inside the fortress

Vika organizes:

- remaining defenders;
- internal routes;
- fire control;
- Sirin neural links;
- and the eventual total assault.

He personally fights.

His command authority is inseparable from the kingdom’s inherited hierarchy and from his technical mastery.

## Complementary leadership

Lena’s strength lies in:

- mobile force coordination;
- reading battlefield flow;
- and preserving operational possibility.

Vika’s lies in:

- technical control;
- ruthless calculation;
- fortress knowledge;
- and the willingness to spend his artificial army.

Neither alone can retake the fortress.

---

# XXIII. Lerche’s indictment of Shin

The conversation between Lerche and Shin is the moral center of Volume 5.

## Shin’s argument

Lerche suggests that humans should have delegated war to artificial soldiers earlier.

Shin refuses.

He says that allowing others to fight while hiding behind them is what the Republic did.

To surrender the power to:

- move;
- defend oneself;
- and determine one’s own fate

is shameful.

This is the core of Eighty-Six pride.

## Lerche’s laughter

Lerche asks whether avoiding shame is truly enough reason for a living person to remain on the battlefield.

Her contempt is not directed at courage.

It is directed at poverty of desire.

## “You are alive”

She lists what Shin still possesses:

- a vulnerable body;
- eyes that can see another person;
- ears that can hear a voice;
- hands that can touch;
- and a human form made for living beside others.

He claims battle as his only pride while refusing to discard the body that makes relationship possible.

## The desire he denies

Lerche tells him that he wants:

- to remain near someone;
- to live with someone;
- and someday to be happy.

Shin has not articulated these desires.

His inability to complete the invitation to the sea supports her accusation.

## The dead person’s envy

Lerche cannot:

- grow older;
- build a civilian life;
- or become the original girl whose place she occupies.

Her anger comes from watching a living person treat possibility as worthless.

## The final judgment

> Those who die should be those of us who are already dead.  
> You are still alive.  
> What was lost can still be reclaimed.

This is not a celebration of Sirin expendability.

It is the dead demanding that the living stop imitating them.

---

# XXIV. Chapter 4: *Ex Machina*

The chapter title invokes **deus ex machina**—the god from the machine who resolves an otherwise impossible conflict.

Volume 5 removes the god.

Only the machine remains.

## The impossible siege

The attackers face:

- a deep moat;
- a massive cliff;
- high walls;
- artillery;
- mines;
- heavy armor;
- and a shrinking supply timeline.

A conventional assault would be catastrophic.

## Vika’s solution

The Sirins and Alkonosts charge in an unnaturally narrow column.

The human units are ordered not to interfere.

The machines:

- descend into the moat;
- drag wreckage with them;
- allow following units to crush damaged comrades;
- lock their limbs;
- brace one body upon another;
- and pile upward against the cliff.

They become construction material.

## The assault road

The resulting structure is made from:

- Sirin-operated Alkonosts;
- destroyed Legion units;
- shattered artificial bodies;
- and mechanical remains.

It fills the moat and climbs the cliff.

The last surviving Alkonosts lock themselves into the uppermost layers.

Only Lerche’s machine remains operational.

## Salvation without divinity

The machines resolve the impossible tactical situation.

They do so through total self-annihilation.

The miracle is neither:

- clean;
- gracious;
- nor supernatural.

It is engineered sacrifice.

## The reversal of the phrase

The classical deus ex machina saves human characters by arbitrary intervention.

Volume 5 asks what the characters must step upon after the saving machine arrives.

A miracle may still have a body count.

---

# XXV. The corpse road

The assault road is the volume’s defining image.

It literalizes a truth that all societies prefer to conceal:

> The living world is built upon the dead.

## 1. Initial hesitation

The living Processors freeze before entering it.

They have walked across:

- ruins;
- corpses;
- and battlefield wreckage

for years.

This road is different because the sacrifice was deliberate and immediate.

The Sirins constructed it for them.

## 2. The command to advance

Shin moves first.

Theo, Kurena, Anju, and the others follow.

They must climb because refusing would make the sacrifice useless and doom everyone still alive.

## 3. Crushed bodies

The Reginleifs’ feet break:

- artificial backs;
- faces;
- limbs;
- and partially functioning bodies.

Some Sirins still move as the living pass over them.

The passage is not metaphorical.

The living survive by physically stepping upon the dead.

## 4. Rito’s horror

Rito realizes that the Sirins’ march is the visible endpoint of the Eighty-Six’s own rhetoric.

The Sirins:

- laugh;
- charge;
- accept destruction;
- and call it purpose.

What distinguishes that from fighting until death with pride?

## 5. Raiden’s answer

Raiden does not offer a philosophical resolution.

He tells Rito not to say the thought aloud because the others are already thinking it.

They should not invalidate the road by which they survived.

This is compassionate and incomplete.

The Eighty-Six ethic may need revision.

It should not be retroactively treated as worthless.

A survival strategy can be:

- necessary then;
- dangerous now.

---

# XXVI. Stepping on the dead

After the battle, Shin stands before the remains of Ludmila.

He expected to feel nothing.

He feels pain.

The experience produces one of the volume’s bleakest insights.

## 1. The first body

Shin remembers the first commander whose severed head he recovered.

At the time, death was not ordinary.

It hurt.

He gradually forgot that it hurt because constant exposure made emotional response impossible to sustain.

## 2. Ludmila’s silence

Ludmila was never biologically alive as a Sirin.

The copied dead fragment within her has now vanished.

Shin had asked whether Sirins wanted to return to death.

Looking at what remains, he recognizes the coldness of the question.

## 3. Universal implication

Shin thinks of every political order surviving by spending someone.

- The Republic stood upon the Eighty-Six.
- Roa Gracia stands upon the Sirins.
- The Federacy uses child veterans, military dependents, and frontier peoples.
- Even the oppressed survive through comrades who die before them.

## 4. No innocent position

The conclusion is not that every system is morally equal.

The Republic’s genocidal coercion is not identical to Sirin volunteers or emergency military sacrifice.

The volume’s point is more unsettling:

> No society survives without cost being borne unevenly.

The ethical question is not whether one can remain perfectly unstained.

It is:

- whether the cost is acknowledged;
- whether consent is meaningful;
- whether alternatives are pursued;
- whether sacrifice becomes habitual;
- and whether the living treat the dead as material or as people whose loss creates obligation.

## 5. Shin’s despair

Shin concludes that humans are monsters.

This expands his earlier belief that humanity is base.

The corpse road appears to vindicate his pessimism.

Yet his pain before Ludmila contradicts total dehumanization.

A monster indifferent to personhood would not hurt.

---

# XXVII. Lena running toward the monster

Lena finds Shin alone before the assault road.

She runs through the snow and embraces him.

The scene reverses the final emotional distance of Volume 4.

## What Lena sees

She sees:

- destroyed Sirins;
- the machines used as a road;
- the Strike Package’s participation;
- and the grotesque cost of victory.

She does not recoil from Shin.

## Shin’s question

He cannot understand why she continues approaching.

He thinks of the Eighty-Six as:

- emotionally damaged;
- habituated to death;
- and monstrous through survival.

Why does she still believe:

- in humanity;
- in ideals;
- and in remaining beside them?

## The new realization

Shin understands that he knows almost nothing about Lena’s survival.

He knows:

- the girl who first contacted Spearhead;
- the commander who reached him;
- and the voice asking him to return.

He does not know what happened during the two years between.

He has not seriously asked:

- what she saw;
- what she lost;
- how she endured the Republic’s fall;
- why she did not abandon hope;
- or what she wants beyond following him.

## From object to subject

Until this point, Lena has often functioned within Shin’s emerging future as:

- the person he wants to show the sea;
- the person he must not lose;
- and the voice that calls him back.

These are genuine feelings.

They still position her in relation to his needs.

The ending marks an important turn:

> Shin wants to understand Lena as a person whose inner life does not exist merely to rescue him.

This may be his most mature relational development so far.

---

# XXVIII. The Phönix and Lerche

The Phönix returns during the Swan Fortress battle.

Its contrast with Lerche clarifies two models of artificial intelligence.

## The Phönix

The Phönix is:

- self-preserving;
- adaptive;
- modular;
- fast;
- and free from human bodily limits.

When its body is threatened, its micromachine core escapes.

Its existence is organized around continued combat capability.

## Lerche

Lerche is:

- artificial;
- replaceable in body;
- and militarily designed.

Yet she spends herself protecting:

- Vika;
- the fortress;
- and the living soldiers.

She possesses relationships the Phönix does not.

## The duel

Lerche confronts the Phönix while Shin changes into her Alkonost after exhausting his Reginleif’s ammunition.

The fight forces Shin to act through a machine whose dead pilot has already told him to value life.

## The necessary shot

Lerche becomes physically entangled with the Phönix.

She orders Shin to fire.

He shoots through the situation, severing her arm to damage the enemy.

The moment reproduces the battlefield’s central principle:

> The artificial dead accept destruction so the living may continue.

## Uneasy victory

The Phönix’s body is defeated.

Its butterfly core escapes again.

Lerche is recovered as a shattered upper body and head.

The self-preserving machine survives.

The relational machine sacrifices itself and is rebuilt.

The contrast complicates any simple claim that one is more “alive” because it preserves its own existence.

---

# XXIX. Vika and Lerche after the battle

The concluding scene between them combines grotesque imagery, affection, comedy, and grief.

Vika finds Lerche with only her upper body remaining.

He scolds her for breaking.

She asks whether he is injured.

## The repeated image

Her condition resembles the dying Lercheritt.

Vika sees the artificial present overlaid with the original past.

The reconstruction has not released him from the memory.

It repeatedly restages it.

## His reason to remain alive

Vika thinks that he cannot die until:

- the kingdom is protected;
- the war ends;
- and Lercheritt’s request is fulfilled.

Afterward, even if he possesses no further desire, he will continue until his life ends naturally.

This distinguishes him from Shin.

Vika has no conventional dream.

He has accepted that absence of desire does not justify seeking death.

## Affection in mechanical form

He detaches Lerche’s head and carries it for reconstruction.

Their banter about repairs and her desired body proportions is absurd.

The comedy does not eliminate tragedy.

It demonstrates how ordinary relational rhythm can survive within an unnatural condition.

## No kiss

Vika privately notes that he never kissed:

- the original Lercheritt;
- or the artificial Lerche.

Their relationship exceeds easy categories:

- sibling;
- knight;
- subject;
- replacement;
- companion;
- lover;
- and creation.

The lack of definition is part of its pain.

---

# XXX. The Merciless Queen

After the fortress battle, a white early-model Ameise appears beyond the snowfield.

It is:

- unarmed;
- weathered;
- protected by heavy Legion units;
- accompanied by the Phönix’s silver butterflies;
- and marked with a crescent moon and reclining goddess.

Vika identifies the figure as Zelene.

The volume heavily points toward the Merciless Queen carrying the consciousness or neural structure of Zelene Birkenbaum.

It does not yet provide a full explanation.

## The inversion of expectation

The target of the operation finally appears only after the immediate battle has ended.

The Queen does not:

- attack;
- surrender;
- or allow pursuit.

She presents herself.

## The dying thought

Shin hears:

> I was a good girl.  
> So I wanted you to come back.

The voice is not that of a cold strategist.

It is a child’s abandoned hope.

This introduces a new dimension to Zelene.

The creator of the Legion—or the consciousness now associated with its command—contains a personal wound organized around someone who did not return.

## “Come find me”

The Phönix previously told Shin to seek it.

The Merciless Queen now appears under heavy protection and then withdraws.

The Legion’s internal purpose may contain more than blind execution of the Empire’s final order.

Volume 5 does not reveal the answer.

It establishes the mystery as personal as well as strategic.

---

# XXXI. Shin’s recovered memories

The Queen’s final thought triggers Shin’s mother’s voice.

This is the culmination of the memory argument begun in Volume 4.

## 1. “Be a good boy”

Shin remembers his mother telling him:

- listen to Rei;
- listen to the priest;
- and be good until she returns.

The promise resembles the Merciless Queen’s thought.

A child behaved correctly because obedience was supposed to produce reunion.

The adult never returned.

## 2. What Shin remembers

The recovered fragments include:

- his mother’s face;
- her red hair;
- her eyes;
- his father;
- gentle Rei;
- Annette;
- the Nouzen home in Liberté et Égalité;
- and the intelligent dog-shaped machine his father researched.

The last memory clarifies Fido’s deeper origin within Shin’s childhood.

## 3. He had not forgotten

Shin realizes that the memories were never simply destroyed.

He had refused access to them.

## 4. Why he buried them

The lost world was happy.

If he remembered it, he would have to acknowledge:

- the home was real;
- the love was real;
- the family was real;
- and the Republic destroyed something valuable.

Believing the world was always cruel made the loss easier to survive.

## 5. The impossibility of return

Even if the war ended and Shin physically returned to the old house:

- his parents would not be there;
- Rei would not be there;
- the child he was would not be there;
- and he could not smile with the same innocence.

The memory does not offer restoration.

It offers grief.

## 6. Lena was partly correct

Volume 4 left open whether Lena’s diagnosis was overly comprehensive.

The end of Volume 5 confirms an important part of it.

Shin rejected happiness in the past because remembering it would make future desire possible—and therefore painful.

## 7. Memory as future material

The recovered memories do not need to return Shin to childhood.

They can give him knowledge of:

- tenderness;
- family;
- trust;
- and ordinary companionship

from which a different future may be imagined.

The purpose of remembering is not to go back.

It is to stop allowing the destroyed past to define what can never exist again.

---

# XXXII. Fido and the remembered artificial dog

The memory of Shin’s father’s dog-shaped artificial intelligence gives Fido additional significance.

Fido has always occupied an unusual place in the story.

It is a machine treated as:

- friend;
- companion;
- witness;
- and member of the group.

Volume 5 suggests that Shin’s attachment to Fido may connect unconsciously to childhood.

## Artificial personhood without corpse violation

Unlike the Sirins, Fido is not constructed through a copied dead brain.

It develops relational meaning through:

- continuity;
- recognition;
- behavior;
- and shared experience.

This offers another model of artificial life.

## Memory surviving through affection

Shin claimed he did not remember the family dog-machine.

He nevertheless formed a deep relationship with Fido.

The past may have persisted through emotional disposition even when explicit memory was suppressed.

## The machine that leads toward life

The Legion uses artificial intelligence to continue death.

The Sirins use artificial life to replace the dying.

Fido uses machine continuity to accompany people through life.

It is the least grand and perhaps the most humane artificial intelligence in the series.

---

# XXXIII. Singing birds

Chapter 3 is titled:

> **シンギング・バードの嘆きも知らず**  
> **Knowing Not the Lament of the Singing Bird**

The Sirins are repeatedly associated with birds.

They possess:

- high, musical voices;
- bright appearances;
- cheerful manners;
- and the role of Vika’s “little birds.”

## The visible song

They speak and fight with apparent joy.

Their song tells the living:

- do not worry;
- we exist for this;
- continue forward;
- let us bear the battle.

## The unheard lament

Shin hears beneath their artificial personalities the cries of the dead neural structures from which they were created.

The singing exterior contains a death wish not necessarily belonging to the present Sirin personality.

## Caged existence

A singing bird may sound beautiful while remaining confined.

The Sirins possess:

- personalities;
- movement;
- and relationships,

but no socially recognized right to become something other than weapons.

Their song is also the performance of assigned purpose.

## Human parallel

The Eighty-Six’s battlefield humor served as resistance.

It could also conceal fear.

Rito’s confession reveals the lament beneath their own proud song.

The chapter title therefore applies to both groups.

Those who admire fearless soldiers often do not hear what fearlessness costs.

---

# XXXIV. Snow and the absence of flowers

Snow dominates the volume’s imagery.

## 1. Artificial season

The snow is intensified by the Legion’s ecological warfare.

It is not merely picturesque northern weather.

It signifies a future prevented from arriving.

## 2. Preservation

Cold preserves:

- bodies;
- wreckage;
- memory;
- and unresolved grief.

Vika’s mother was physically preserved.

The kingdom’s winter similarly preserves death while preventing new growth.

## 3. White funeral imagery

Lena’s ceremonial image as the White Funeral Goddess echoes through the snowy battlefield.

White does not signify innocence.

It signifies:

- burial;
- mourning;
- and beauty covering violence.

## 4. Cornflowers

Vika remembers the blue cornflowers associated with the kingdom’s spring.

They have not bloomed.

The missing flowers embody the national future at risk.

## 5. The concluding field

The final chapter takes place in a field where no flowers bloom.

Yet relationships continue there:

- Vika carries Lerche;
- Raiden supports Rito;
- Shin mourns Ludmila;
- Lena runs to Shin.

Life persists before spring becomes visible.

---

# XXXV. The royal family and the meaning of duty

Roa Gracia’s royal family offers an alternative to both:

- Republic abandonment;
- and Eighty-Six self-definition through isolated pride.

## Vika’s father

The king recognizes:

- Vika’s abnormality;
- his usefulness;
- and his status as a son.

He does not reduce him to one category.

## Zafar

The crown prince reassures Vika that he is not an unwanted beast.

He also understands that broader society may not share the family’s acceptance.

## Duty as tether

Vika’s royal duty gives him a reason to survive even without personal happiness.

This is not an ideal final condition.

It is more stable than seeking death because one has no desire.

## The danger

Duty can become another cage.

Vika remains alive because:

- the kingdom needs him;
- Lercheritt asked him;
- and his family expects him.

The volume does not show what he might want if these obligations ended.

In this sense, he and Shin remain closer than either may admit.

---

# XXXVI. Major character developments

## Shinei Nouzen

Shin’s pride is challenged by someone who is already dead.

He learns that:

- fighting personally does not by itself constitute a sufficient life;
- his desire to remain beside Lena is visible to others;
- and he buried his childhood memories rather than simply losing them.

His most important development comes at the end:

> He recognizes that wanting Lena beside him is not the same as understanding her.

## Vladilena Milizé

Lena partially repairs the damage caused by her Volume 4 confrontation.

She preserves her belief that the Eighty-Six deserve happiness while acknowledging the arrogance involved in telling them how to pursue it.

Vika further teaches her that concern cannot justify excluding Shin from decisions about his own body.

Her command remains strong under siege.

Her personal growth requires more listening.

## Viktor Idinarohk

Vika is revealed as:

- indirect progenitor of Legion artificial intelligence;
- creator of the Sirins;
- bereaved child;
- devoted prince;
- and person whose intelligence allows him to cross boundaries ordinary people instinctively fear.

His attachment to Lerche and his family prevents his coldness from becoming pure instrumentalism.

## Lerche

Lerche emerges as the novel’s clearest defender of human futurity.

She rejects the idea that the living should imitate the dead.

Her rage reveals:

- envy;
- self-awareness;
- and desire beyond simple programming.

Her sacrifice against the Phönix and her later banter with Vika preserve both sides of her identity:

- weapon;
- and person.

## Rito Oriya

Rito becomes the voice of ordinary fear among the Eighty-Six.

He recognizes that their pride may resemble the Sirins’ death march.

His anxiety prevents the original five’s combat ethic from becoming the only Eighty-Six perspective.

## Anju Emma

Anju begins considering a future in which:

- her scar is treated;
- her body is not a permanent memorial;
- and love after Daiya may someday be possible.

She does not relinquish the dead.

She loosens death’s exclusive claim upon her.

## Dustin Jaeger

Dustin’s commitment to a future beyond himself becomes personal.

His love for Anju does not demand that she forget Daiya.

He represents memory that makes room rather than closes it.

## Raiden Shuga

Raiden protects Rito from turning new insight into retrospective self-condemnation.

He understands that the Eighty-Six may need to outgrow an ethic without calling their former selves fools for needing it.

## Frederica Rosenfort

Frederica remains attentive to:

- Vika;
- Shin;
- and the ways unwanted children build purpose around damaged protectors.

Her ability and historical position make her an important bridge between royal, artificial, and Eighty-Six forms of survival.

## Grethe Wenzel

Grethe helps coordinate the multinational operation and continues confronting the use of damaged youths as strategic assets.

Roa Gracia’s Sirins give her another model of replacing living pilots—one far more ethically disturbing than her Reginleif program.

## Ludmila

Ludmila’s destruction gives abstract Sirin ethics a particular body.

Shin’s pain before her broken head proves that artificial origin does not prevent relational significance.

## The Merciless Queen

The Queen moves from distant strategic target to personal mystery.

Her childlike final thought suggests that the Legion’s command structure contains:

- abandonment;
- memory;
- and motives not reducible to extermination programming.

# XXXVIII. Strengths of Volume 5

## 1. The Sirins are an exceptionally effective mirror

They do not merely repeat the Legion’s copied-brain concept.

They place it inside a society that:

- loves them;
- names them;
- and uses them for ostensibly humane purposes.

This makes the ethical problem much harder.

## 2. The title and plot are tightly integrated

“Death, be not proud” informs:

- Shin;
- Anju;
- Rito;
- Lerche;
- Vika;
- the assault road;
- and the artificial resurrection theme.

The volume consistently asks whether dignity in death has displaced desire for life.

## 3. Vika expands the series’ political and philosophical range

He is not another Kiriya or Rei.

He is:

- creator;
- ruler;
- child;
- scientist;
- monster;
- and bereaved person.

Through him, private grief becomes technological history.

## 4. Lerche has genuine philosophical agency

She is not only Vika’s tragic companion.

Her argument against Shin is one of the strongest moral confrontations in the series.

The dead person understands life’s value more clearly than the living protagonist.

## 5. Rito pluralizes the Eighty-Six

His fear demonstrates that:

- combat culture;
- numbness;
- pride;
- and trauma

vary among survivors.

The Eighty-Six are not one heroic psychological type.

## 6. Anju’s arc translates the grand theme into intimate life

The question of whether the dead permit future happiness becomes:

- scar treatment;
- clothing;
- love;
- comparison;
- and one person asking another not to close the future.

The ordinary scale strengthens the larger argument.

## 7. The corpse road is unforgettable and thematically exact

It literalizes:

- sacrifice;
- military infrastructure;
- institutional euphemism;
- and the way the living advance over the dead.

Few images could more directly expose the series’ concerns.

## 8. The memory reveal advances rather than reverses Shin

Recovering his family memories does not restore childhood.

It confirms that forgetting was a survival act and gives him new material from which a future identity might be built.

## 9. Lena and Shin’s relationship becomes more reciprocal

Shin begins the volume wanting Lena to understand the Eighty-Six.

He ends by realizing that he has not tried to understand Lena.

That is a major shift from romance as rescue toward romance as curiosity about another subject.

## 10. The monarchy is treated with nuance

Roa Gracia is:

- hierarchical;
- unequal;
- militarized;
- dutiful;
- loving;
- cruel;
- and politically functional.

It neither vindicates nor merely condemns hereditary rule.

# XXXIX. Tensions and limitations

## 1. The volume is structurally incomplete

The Merciless Queen appears, but the principal capture mission remains unresolved.

The artificial winter and the larger Roa Gracia campaign continue beyond this volume.

The novel provides thematic closure without full operational closure.

## 2. The Sirin ontology remains deliberately unstable

The text sometimes calls them:

- machines;
- dead people;
- copies;
- artificial personalities;
- and nonhuman beings.

This ambiguity is philosophically productive.

It can make the ethical argument difficult to pin down.

## 3. Consent is not fully interrogated

The original human donors volunteered.

Questions remain:

- Could they meaningfully consent to copied successor beings?
- Does the new Sirin possess rights independent of the donor?
- Can the state order a copy to die based on the original’s consent?
- What continuity survives rebuilding?

Volume 5 raises these issues more than it resolves them.

## 4. Lerche’s insight is partly authorial

Her speech explains Shin’s hidden desire with extraordinary precision.

The emotional authority is earned through her condition.

The scene also functions transparently as the novel forcing Shin’s central contradiction into language.

## 5. Vika’s responsibility for the Legion is highly concentrated

A five-year-old’s resurrection experiment becoming part of the technological origin of a continental war is dramatically powerful.

It reinforces the series’ tendency to make a small number of extraordinary individuals central to vast historical systems.

## 6. The corpse-road tactic strains logistical plausibility

Its physical construction is described in substantial detail:

- heavy machines fill the moat;
- lock into place;
- incorporate enemy wrecks;
- and form a narrow ramp.

The image remains more persuasive symbolically than as an efficient engineering solution under fire.

## 7. Shin’s memory return arrives through a strong verbal trigger

The Merciless Queen’s childlike thought neatly parallels his mother’s farewell.

Traumatic memory can indeed return associatively.

The timing is also conspicuously designed to confirm the previous volume’s thesis.

## 8. The novel risks aestheticizing sacrifice while criticizing it

The Sirins’ final charge is:

- horrifying;
- beautiful;
- disciplined;
- and tactically triumphant.

The prose produces awe at the same time it condemns the culture of proud death.

This tension is likely deliberate.

It remains important not to mistake the spectacle for endorsement.

# XL. Reinterpretation of Volume 4

Volume 5 confirms and complicates the previous novel’s conclusions.

## 1. Lena was substantially correct about Shin’s memory

Shin did not simply lose every childhood memory.

He actively buried a happiness too painful to acknowledge.

## 2. Lena’s correctness does not justify unilateral healing

Vika’s rebuke shows that accurate diagnosis does not create authority over the diagnosed person.

Lena must distinguish:

- insight;
- concern;
- and consent.

## 3. Shin’s desire for the sea remains incomplete

He can imagine showing Lena the sea.

He cannot imagine what he wants afterward.

The future remains:

- one experience;
- one person;
- and a frightening blank.

## 4. The Phönix mirror becomes broader

In Volume 4, the Phönix represented combat intelligence without memory.

Volume 5 places it beside Sirins who possess relationship and self-sacrifice.

Artificial existence is no longer one moral category.

## 5. The enemy within the Eighty-Six gains physical form

Volume 4 describes despair as the Republic’s internal legacy.

Volume 5 gives it the smiling face of a Sirin marching willingly toward destruction.

---

# XLI. Reinterpretation of Volumes 1–3

## Volume 1: carrying the dead

Shin’s memorial function remains noble.

Volume 5 adds that carrying the dead must not mean adopting death as one’s own preferred destination.

## Volume 2: the cage of pity

Frederica argued that protective care can become a cage.

Vika now applies the same principle to Lena’s concern about Shin’s ability.

## Volume 2: the empty rooms

The rooms were empty because the Five could not trust duration.

Volume 5 shows the psychological mechanism:

> To fill a room with a future, one must believe the world contains something worth keeping.

## Volume 3: Kiriya’s warning

Kiriya showed a person reduced to combat through loss.

The Sirins show an institution manufacturing that condition deliberately and calling it humane.

## Volume 3: the first future

The sea remains Shin’s first future image.

Its inadequacy becomes visible because one destination cannot substitute for an entire self.

## The reunion

The first three volumes culminated in Lena reaching Shin.

Volume 5 makes clear that physical arrival is only the beginning.

The next task is mutual knowledge.

---

# XLII. The volume’s central ethical argument

Volume 5 distinguishes three attitudes toward the dead.

## 1. Erasure

The Republic erased the Eighty-Six from public memory.

The Legion overwrites obsolete copied minds.

This treats death as disappearance of personhood.

## 2. Repetition

Vika tries to reconstruct his mother.

The Sirin system rebuilds dead neural structures.

Anju fears that moving forward will replace Daiya.

This treats loyalty as preserving or repeating the lost person.

## 3. Inheritance

The emerging alternative is to let the dead change the living without dictating the living’s end.

- Daiya may remain loved while Anju someday loves again.
- Lercheritt’s request may give Vika duty without making Lerche identical to her.
- Shin’s parents may remain part of him without requiring a return to childhood.
- The dead Eighty-Six may be carried toward the sea rather than toward another grave.

Inheritance preserves significance while allowing difference.

This is the form of memory through which death loses its pride.

---

# XLIII. Final synthesis

Volume 5 begins with a prince who tried to prevent death from separating him from his mother.

It ends with a boy remembering the mother he had tried to erase from himself.

Between them stand artificial girls made from the dead.

They laugh, argue, tease, fight, and willingly break themselves so that living people may survive.

Their existence forces every major character to answer what death means.

Vika answers:

> Death is an engineering problem whose finality I cannot emotionally accept.

Lerche answers:

> Death is the loss of possibility. The living must not imitate us.

Rito answers:

> Death still terrifies me, and perhaps admitting that fear is more human than smiling toward it.

Anju answers:

> Remembering someone should not require refusing every future they can no longer share.

Dustin answers:

> A future can contain more than one love.

Lena answers:

> Kind people should become happy, even if the world must be changed to make that possible.

Shin begins the volume with no complete answer.

He believes:

- fighting is pride;
- dependence is shame;
- the past is unnecessary;
- and the world’s cruelty makes hope foolish.

The Sirins dismantle each defense.

They fight more fearlessly than he does.

They need less.

They accept death more completely.

They are purer weapons.

And because they are already dead, they understand that these qualities do not constitute a life.

The assault road makes the argument impossible to evade.

The Sirins transform themselves into the literal foundation of victory.

The living climb over:

- faces;
- limbs;
- machines;
- and copied remains.

They do so because otherwise everyone will die.

The act is necessary.

Its necessity does not make it beautiful in any uncomplicated sense.

The road reveals the truth beneath every heroic campaign:

> Someone bears the weight.  
> Someone becomes the material.  
> Someone’s future is converted into another person’s survival.

No society escapes this completely.

The Republic’s guilt was not merely that it sacrificed people.

It:

- denied they were people;
- refused to bear reciprocal risk;
- concealed the cost;
- and made disposability hereditary.

Roa Gracia names the Sirins and grieves them.

It still spends them.

The Federacy recognizes the Eighty-Six as citizens.

It still relies upon what persecution made them capable of doing.

The ethical task is therefore not achieving immaculate innocence.

It is refusing to let necessity erase recognition.

Shin’s pain before Ludmila’s broken head is significant because it interrupts abstraction.

She was not merely:

- artificial material;
- a replaceable unit;
- or part of the assault equation.

Something that spoke, acted, and stood beside him is gone.

That recognition makes the cost real.

Lena then runs toward him.

She has seen the same monstrous road.

She still embraces the person who climbed it.

Shin cannot understand why.

That inability becomes the volume’s final movement.

For much of the series, Shin has wanted Lena to:

- remember him;
- follow him;
- survive;
- and understand the Eighty-Six.

He has rarely asked who Lena became while doing those things.

He knows her idealism.

He does not know its history.

He knows she calls him back.

He does not know what keeps her from abandoning humanity after everything she witnessed.

He knows he wants to show her the sea.

He does not yet know what she wants from life, from him, or from the world after the sea.

The final realization is therefore relationally decisive:

> Reunion is not knowledge.  
> Love is not knowledge.  
> Being needed is not knowledge.

To live beside another person, Shin must become curious about a life not organized around himself, his dead, or his battlefield.

The Merciless Queen then returns the memories he buried.

His mother’s final words reveal that the world he denies once contained:

- safety;
- tenderness;
- family;
- home;
- and uncomplicated trust.

He rejected those memories because they proved the world had taken something worth wanting.

Now they may become proof of the opposite:

> If such things existed once, they are not impossible in principle.

Shin cannot return to childhood.

Vika cannot recover his mother.

Lerche cannot become Lercheritt.

Anju cannot bring back Daiya.

The dead remain dead.

That is exactly why death must not be proud.

Death can end a life.

It does not gain the right to dictate every life that follows.

The strongest concluding formulation of Volume 5 is:

> **The dead should not be forgotten, reconstructed into obedient replacements, or worshiped as models of self-erasure. They should be carried into futures different from the ones they lost.**

And for Shin personally:

> **His next step is not merely learning to want Lena beside him. It is learning to know her well enough that the future they approach can belong to both of them.**
