## 1.3 Visual paratext

The visual layer supports—but does not settle—the volume's political argument.

- The **cover / color spread** centers the young woman around whom the Hail Mary revolt forms while placing Shin, Frederica, Annette and other figures around her. The publication image therefore refuses to make the rebels anonymous background antagonists; their faces and inherited roles occupy the same visual field as the regular protagonists.
- The large promotional composition prints: `その弾丸は誰に向けて──生き抜くためには、愚かなままではいられない。` The editorial proposition is unusually direct: survival requires more than possession of a weapon or conviction. It requires ceasing to remain `愚かなまま`—a visual-paratext echo of the volume's education/judgment argument.
- The image paired with Chitori foregrounds bodily delicacy and ordinary dress rather than military equipment. This matters prospectively because she belongs to an Eighty-Six population whose injury did not primarily take the form of battlefield soldiering.
- The giant native sea creature and the heavy-engineer Legion illustrations materially enlarge the battlefield beyond human political symbolism. Their bodies have practical tactical consequences before they acquire thematic meaning.
- The split Shin/Lena illustration late in the volume presents intimacy through mediated distance: each is in a different space, but their expressions and framing align. This suits the volume's treatment of support as something that need not collapse two people into one location, role or mental state.
- The Frederica/Vika confrontation is framed as a face-to-face ideological dispute rather than action spectacle. Her claim to become `わらわ自身の王` is given visual seriousness.

These are `VI` / `PT` evidence. They establish publication emphasis, body/role presentation and formal rhyme; they do not independently establish internal motive.

## 1.4 Adversarial reading protocol for V12

V12 was assigned one of the most dangerous inherited V1 hypotheses:

> **Formal freedom requires education, habits, social support and institutional capacity before it becomes substantively usable.**

That claim resembles a capabilities-oriented political theory closely enough that the V2 reread must actively search for disconfirmation.

The volume must therefore be tested for at least five alternative possibilities:

1. Does the text simply say some people are naturally incapable of freedom?
2. Does it nostalgically vindicate aristocratic paternalism?
3. Does it make education a sufficient cure for bad judgment?
4. Does institutional support expand agency, or sometimes replace it with a new guardianship?
5. Does refusal to exercise agency remain morally attributable after structural deprivation is acknowledged?

The primary-source result is more complex than the legacy shorthand.

V12 supports **neither** radical atomistic autonomy **nor** aristocratic rule-by-guardian.

Its strongest positive model is:

> **freedom becomes more usable when institutions cultivate capacities for learning, judgment, responsibility, rest, accessible information, and recoverable error—while still preserving the person's standing to decide.**

But the same volume also insists that people can refuse those capacities, misuse them, or prefer obedience because responsibility is frightening.

# 2. Primary thesis

Volume 12 is about the difference between **being given freedom** and **becoming able and willing to govern oneself**.

Its political problem begins in the former Empire.

For generations, peasant/serf communities were organized so that aristocrats and local notables:

- decided occupations;
- controlled education;
- monopolized technical knowledge;
- assumed political judgment;
- and presented protection as the reciprocal justification for obedience.

After revolution, those subjects receive legal equality, compulsory education and responsibility for choices that had previously belonged to their rulers.

Some experience the change as liberation.

Others experience it as abandonment.

Kiahi's complaint is explicit:

> `郷士様や街長に任せておけばよかった面倒な決断を、それが『自由』だと押しつけられた。`

and:

> `使うあても覚える必要もない、無駄なばかりの読み書きなんかを『平等』に学ばされた。`

This is real subjective evidence that freedom can feel burdensome when previous institutions trained people **not** to decide.

But V12 does not allow that complaint to become an aristocratic proof of natural hierarchy.

The same social background produces soldiers who:

- learn when teaching is made usable;
- follow temporary guidance without surrendering all judgment;
- protect unrelated refugee children;
- endure isolation for a month;
- and discover that `元農奴` can perform acts they themselves call good and admirable.

Shiden's contrasting response to ignorance is equally important:

> `全然わかんねえから知りてえ`

Miarona responds by giving her accessible introductory material, then progressively harder material, while saying:

> `君たちのペースでぜひ、楽しんでくれたまえ`

The central V12 distinction is therefore not:

> rulers can think; sheep cannot.

It is:

> **the capacity for self-government can be cultivated or suppressed, and the moral use of freedom requires both opportunity and participation.**

That participation cannot be coerced into existence. Education opens doors; it cannot make someone walk through them.

This is why Marcel's confession matters as much as the aristocratic speeches:

> `考えないって、楽だから。`

Obedience can become attractive because it transfers blame.

V12 therefore distributes responsibility across both structure and subject:

- old institutions bear responsibility for deliberately withholding practice in judgment;
- new institutions bear responsibility for making transitions and education materially usable;
- leaders bear responsibility for not turning dependence into self-worship;
- individuals still bear responsibility for what they do with capacities they actually possess.

The volume's political ideal is not independence from every other person.

It is closer to **supported self-government**.

# 3. Competing theses and interpretive risks

## 3.1 Risk: V12 proves democracy is cruel to weak people

Several aristocratic characters say almost exactly this.

Miarona's harshest formulation is:

> `自由や平等など、……残酷なだけだ。群の羊でありたかった者どもには`

But this is focalized aristocratic judgment, not neutral narration.

The text immediately surrounds it with counterevidence:

- the Empire deliberately restricted education and decision-making;
- people from the same class learn successfully when instruction is accessible;
- a former-serf infantry unit protects civilians with courage;
- Noelle's followers have repeatedly refused opportunities to learn;
- Noelle herself occupies command authority without doing the intellectual labor command requires.

V2 should therefore classify the aristocratic critique as **a serious in-world argument that the narrative tests**, not the narrative's final political doctrine.

## 3.2 Risk: the volume says uneducated people need permanent guardians

The stranded infantry-company episode is the strongest counterexample.

Their dying commander gives extremely specific instructions because his soldiers are frightened, undereducated and soon to be leaderless.

But the instructions are an **emergency scaffold with an exit condition**:

- fortify here;
- protect the children;
- trust that help will arrive;
- survive until it does.

He does not teach:

> never decide for yourselves again.

Likewise an NCO rewrites technical doctrine into simpler language so soldiers can use it independently later.

The support is therefore best understood as **capacity transfer**, not permanent guardianship.

## 3.3 Risk: education is sufficient for freedom

No.

Noelle had access to officer education and still fails to learn enough nuclear physics, logistics, strategic planning and political judgment to support the role she claims.

Others possess educational opportunities and reject them.

V12 repeatedly separates:

- opportunity to learn;
- willingness to learn;
- actual competence;
- moral judgment;
- and responsibility for decisions.

Education is enabling, not magical.

## 3.4 Risk: failure to use freedom is entirely the individual's fault

Also no.

Former imperial institutions deliberately made many subjects dependent. The text repeatedly notes that decisions, literacy, technical knowledge and social advancement were historically routed through local elites.

The transition to formal equality therefore imposes tasks for which many people were never prepared.

Miarona herself admits:

> `背負えぬ荷を押しつけた奴輩にも、罪の一端はある`

Even if her language remains patronizing, the structural point survives adversarial testing.

## 3.5 Risk: structural deprivation erases personal responsibility

Marcel supplies the opposite correction.

He recognizes from his own past that refusing thought is sometimes attractive precisely because it makes blame easier:

> `自分は命令されただけだって、全部誰かのせいに……できるんだから`

V12 does not make self-government effortless.

It does insist that once some capacity exists, refusing to use it can become an ethical choice rather than merely a wound.

## 3.6 Risk: Noelle is merely a cynical demagogue

Noelle sincerely wants to protect her people.

She refuses the easiest opportunity to save herself by sacrificing followers.

Her failure is more interesting:

- she needs to remain the reliable princess;
- followers need her to remain infallible;
- she mistakes their trust for evidence that she must always have an answer;
- she cannot admit ignorance or incapacity without experiencing it as betrayal of the role.

Her saviorism is **co-produced** by leader and followers.

## 3.7 Risk: Noelle's followers are simply stupid

Their nuclear project is disastrously ignorant, but V12 supplies multiple causes:

- inherited educational deprivation;
- contempt for formal learning;
- resentment of examinations;
- overconfidence in local status;
- desire for a single magical solution;
- willingness to identify an enemy rather than revise a hypothesis;
- and the psychological appeal of returning to a world where someone else tells them what is right.

The volume's target is not low intelligence. It is **epistemic abdication**.

## 3.8 Risk: the Eighty-Six are naturally superior self-rulers

Miarona sometimes speaks as if their survival proves ruler-like quality.

But the text makes the mechanism brutal:

> those who failed to learn, decide, adapt or assume responsibility often died.

That is lethal selection under oppression, not evidence of hereditary nobility.

The Eighty-Six's competence is real.

Its historical production is horrific.

## 3.9 Risk: V12's `弱い死神` means Shin now thinks himself weak or worthless

The exact line is:

> `一緒に戦うお前たちでもう手一杯だ。……一人きりじゃ戦えない、弱い死神だから`

The context is a rejection of outsiders' demand that Shin perform universal heroic salvation.

`弱い` here functions as anti-grandiosity and acknowledgement of dependence:

> I cannot carry everyone; I fight with the people beside me.

It is not self-loathing.

## 3.10 Risk: accepting rest means Lena has completed her recovery

Lena is medically ordered away from duty for roughly a month after poor sleep and food intake.

She initially feels ashamed and compares herself unfavorably with Annette and Dustin.

Acceptance matters, but the causal structure is plural:

- Shin notices;
- peers report concerns;
- medical staff diagnose;
- command enforces leave;
- the療養所 supplies activities and time;
- Lena gradually experiences the difference.

This is not autonomous insight alone.

It is precisely an example of an institution temporarily constraining immediate preference in order to preserve later agency.

## 3.11 Risk: institutional support is always emancipatory

The military police officer who tells wounded/retired Eighty-Six:

> `君たちは操縦士だ。〈レギオン〉を狩るのが君たちの仕事だ。ないがしろにしてもらっては困る`

proves otherwise.

Even Giad can reduce people to an assigned function.

Support becomes domination when the institution decides that the person's role exhausts their legitimate choices.

## 3.12 Risk: Ernst's idealism is uncomplicated moral virtue

Ernst opposes using refugee volunteers as expendable screens:

> `施設は直せるけど、失われた命は取り返しがつかない`

That is morally serious.

But the official confronting him notices that Ernst treats his own office, support and perhaps life as though none possesses value.

The charge is:

> `我々への裏切りです`

An ideal can become inhuman when its bearer believes **everyone except himself** deserves preservation.

## 3.13 Risk: emergency limits on liberal rights invalidate Giad's liberal order

V12 records temporary suppression of free reporting while counterintelligence runs a deception operation against Legion surveillance.

This is a genuine liberal-institution tension.

But one temporary emergency restriction does not by itself prove Giad's constitutional commitments are false.

The important V2 question is whether exceptional measures remain:

- bounded;
- accountable;
- temporary;
- and reversible.

V12 provides only part of that evidence.

# 4. Causal and chapter map

## 4.1 Epigraph — removing human will from military uncertainty

Vika's `人造妖精概説` states that Sirins offer a second military advantage:

> `彼女たちは命じられた以外を行わない。`

They do not mix:

> `己の意思という夾雑物`

into operations.

The word `夾雑物` deliberately recalls V4's Legion discussion, where personality and identity were treated as production impurities.

V12 begins by presenting **will itself as military fog**.

The rest of the volume then argues almost the opposite for human political life: judgment, uncertainty, reflection and the possibility of saying “no” are precisely what self-government requires.

A perfectly obedient soldier may reduce tactical uncertainty.

A perfectly obedient citizen is a political danger.

## 4.2 Prologue — Mary Blue before the revolution

Nine-year-old Mere and his friends live in the prosperous special city created around nuclear power and the patronage of local aristocracy.

Noelle is already `姫さま` in their childhood social world.

Her promise is affectionate and absolute:

> `私についてくればできないことなんかないわ！`

Before revolution, this feels like safety.

V12 later reveals the cost hidden inside that sentence: if Noelle is the person who can always make things right, then both she and her followers become trapped by a role in which uncertainty cannot be admitted.

## 4.3 Chapter 1 — recovery, surveillance, and the return of hidden Eighty-Six children

### Frederica and the object of rescue

Vika asks Frederica whether she wants to save soldiers—or wants to avoid the pain she would feel if she failed to save them:

> `卿が守りたいのは将兵どもか、それとも兵どもを見捨てれば傷つく、卿自身の良心か？`

The question does not invalidate compassion.

It forces a distinction between:

- another person's welfare;
- one's identity as rescuer;
- and one's need not to feel guilty.

This is V11's bounded-responsibility problem transferred directly to Frederica.

### No Face is directly identified

V11 ended with a strong but prospectively incomplete `ヴァーツラフ` identification.

V12 now states directly:

> `かつてヴァーツラフ・ミリーゼであった〈羊飼い〉`

The legacy/source status moves from **strong prospective identification** to **CONFIRMED primary fact**.

### Lena is ordered to rest

Lena's physical and psychological deterioration is observed through ordinary indicators:

- inadequate eating;
- poor sleep;
- shock after the Republic's destruction.

Shin tells her:

> `休むのも仕事だと思って`

Several Eighty-Six likewise receive temporary leave after counseling.

The narration states plainly:

> `本当は、辛いのだ。`

This is an important correction to any romanticization of habituation to atrocity. Familiarity with corpses does not mean immunity to witnessing suffering.

### hidden children and coercive cognition

V12 reveals that some very young Eighty-Six survived because Republic citizens purchased them.

The survival is not rescue.

Children were treated as property, tortured, sexually abused, used through Para-RAID sensory sharing and sometimes killed.

One boy has been conditioned to answer:

> `ぼくは、ごしゅじんさまにかわれてしあわせな、いやしいおすのこぶたです`

After Giad rescues and legally protects him, he still cannot conceptualize that the master no longer has power:

> `自分は今は主人の支配下にないのだと...思い至ることすら彼にはできない`

This is the strongest V12 evidence for the difference between **formal liberty** and **usable liberty**.

The law has changed.

The coercive world-model has not yet changed.

But this extreme abuse case must not be generalized into the claim that ordinary citizens are similarly incapable of freedom.

### Para-RAID as abuse technology

Annette explains that the devices were used to make perpetrators share victims' touch and emotion during torture and sexual violence.

A technology that enabled Shin and Lena's most intimate recognition is therefore once again shown as morally neutral in itself and radically dependent on consent and institutional use.

### Giad counterintelligence and emergency liberal tension

The Federacy suppresses reporting temporarily while it captures the surveillance network and feeds false movement information to the Legion.

The narration explicitly notes:

> `連邦では保証されているはずの、自由報道さえしばらくは抑えてある`

This prevents a simplistic “free institutions never coerce” account.

### role reduction inside Giad

An MP tells Eighty-Six who want to become involved with the abused children that their proper role is killing Legion.

The formulation:

> `君たちは操縦士だ`

repeats a structure V2 has tracked since San Magnolia: person reduced to function.

The scale and consequence are radically different from genocide, but the structure can recur even in a better society.

### Ernst's ideal and self-erasure

Ernst opposes using refugee volunteers as expendable buffers and insists that facilities can be rebuilt while human lives cannot.

But his willingness to disregard his own political survival alarms an old ally.

The charge of `裏切り` reframes self-neglect as a relational wrong:

> a leader's life, credibility and office are resources other people also rely upon.

This parallels Shin's development.

## 4.4 Chapter 2 — Hail Mary and the seduction of easy explanation

The revolt develops from people whose social world was transformed by revolution.

Kiahi interprets academic screening as illegitimate because it denied him the martial role he believed his local reputation entitled him to.

The story repeatedly shows the cognitive chain:

1. I expected recognition.
2. Institutions did not give me the role I wanted.
3. Therefore the institution is corrupt.
4. Someone identifies the corrupt elite.
5. Therefore defeating that elite will restore a correct world.

Noelle gives the group what it wants most:

- an enemy;
- a total explanation;
- a single solution;
- a leader who claims certainty.

The followers' final comfort is:

> `姫様の導きにただ、従っていれば。`

That sentence is the political opposite of V12's positive self-government model.

### `弱い死神`

In the same chapter, Shin rejects exactly the heroic demand Noelle accepts.

He says he has no obligation to answer strangers' arbitrary expectations and admits:

> `一人きりじゃ戦えない、弱い死神だから`

This is an explicit refusal of savior singularity.

A healthy leader can say:

> I cannot do everything.

Noelle cannot.

### Shiden's educational counterexample

Shiden asks what nuclear power actually is because she does not understand it.

Miarona's response is pedagogically excellent even if her later politics are aristocratic:

- start with accessible animation;
- offer follow-up material;
- invite questions;
- let students proceed at their own pace;
- encourage exploration across fields.

The key proposition is:

> `わからないから知りたい、学びたいと考えられるのは本当に大事な資質`

V12 therefore treats the admission **I do not know** as a strength.

Noelle's tragedy is partly that she experiences the same admission as failure of identity.

## 4.5 Chapter 3 — responsibility versus outsourced conscience

Vika and Miarona debate the post-revolutionary burden of freedom through openly aristocratic concepts of rulers and sheep.

The useful insight is not their class ontology.

It is the question:

> what happens when formal responsibility is transferred to people who have not been permitted to practice judgment?

Marcel then secularizes and democratizes the issue through self-critique.

He understands why “just following orders” is emotionally easier: failure can be assigned upward.

His own earlier scapegoating of Shin gives the reflection evidentiary weight.

The ethical target becomes **responsibility evasion**, not class position.

### Noelle lacks the craft of the role she claims

Frederica observes that Noelle behaves like a ruler without acquiring the knowledge and discipline required to bear that role:

> `王として振舞いながら、その技量を持っておらぬ。持たねばならぬとも──思うておらぬような`

This is crucial.

V12 does not romanticize strong leadership. It demands that authority be answerable to competence.

### Chitori

Chitori appears as an Eighty-Six of the same broad generation but outside the familiar child-soldier pathway.

Her hands are explicitly described as hands unaccustomed to gun grip or control stick.

She says `じかんがない` and asks Yuto for information.

Prospectively, V12 does not yet reveal enough to close her ontology/status. The `仔鹿` thread must remain an open V13 question.

## 4.6 Chapter 4 — the battlefield tests the political argument

The dam operation supplies the most important counterevidence to aristocratic essentialism.

### democracy without universal heroism

Watching ordinary Federacy soldiers fight competently, Shin thinks:

> `民主制を選んだ連邦では、市民全員が己の王だ。`

He immediately narrows it:

> `少なくともこの軍人たちはそう在れるし、そう在ろうとしている。`

The qualifier matters.

This is not a claim that every Federacy citizen already possesses perfect autonomy.

It is an account of people **trying to inhabit self-government**.

Then:

> `自分以外まで支配し守護する、英雄など連邦軍には必要ない。`

V7's anti-hero doctrine is now linked explicitly to democratic political anthropology.

A free polity should not require one person to decide and protect for everyone.

### accessible doctrine as capability-building

A low-literacy soldier remembers an anti-Legion manual because an NCO rewrote it in simple language.

The institutional solution is not:

> fail the soldier because he cannot read the standard document.

Nor:

> let the officer make every decision forever.

It is:

> make the knowledge usable enough that the soldier can act.

This is perhaps V12's purest example of supportive capacity expansion.

### the stranded company

A group of former-serf soldiers survive for roughly a month while protecting refugee children.

Their dying commander gives detailed instructions and tells them:

> `絶対助けは来るから、諦めるな`

and:

> `あの子らのヒーローになってやれ`

When rescue arrives, one soldier cries:

> `俺らみたいな元農奴にだって何か、いいことやすごいことはできる`

This is the decisive adversarial finding.

People from the same social background as Hail Mary are **not naturally sheep**.

Given:

- comprehensible guidance;
- meaningful responsibility;
- justified trust;
- training;
- and the expectation that they can succeed,

they act with endurance and care.

### Shin delegates

When another combat configuration is better suited to a task, Shin simply asks a subordinate/peer to handle it.

This is the behavioral opposite of heroic singularity.

## 4.7 Chapter 5 — the revolt collapses under epistemic refusal

Noelle's failure is not merely technical.

It is epistemic and relational.

The group wants a `silver bullet`: an overwhelmingly simple answer to a frighteningly complex war.

They misunderstand nuclear material badly enough to irradiate themselves while imagining they have made a decisive weapon.

Noelle's internal defense becomes:

> `私は何も、間違っていない`

and:

> `私は正しいのだから、全部うまくいくはず`

The logic is circular.

Correctness is inferred from identity, not evidence.

### Miarona's aristocratic indictment

After killing the rebels, Miarona condemns a republic that gives rights to people who do not want to learn or decide.

Her critique has real targets:

- refusal to study;
- refusal to think;
- rejection of responsibility.

But she turns those failures into a class ontology in which some people are rulers and others sheep.

The narrative has already supplied enough counterevidence to reject that leap.

### Frederica: conscience and self-authored kingship

Frederica refuses Vika's advice to look away from people she cannot save.

Her answer is not that she already possesses power to save everyone.

It is:

> witnessing failure can be part of preparing to act better later.

Then she declares:

> `わらわ自身の王となろうぞ`

This is perhaps the clearest articulation of V12's ideal political metaphor.

She does not need to become sovereign over others.

She needs to become capable of bearing responsibility for herself.

### Ernst exposed as another failed rescuer structure

The end of the revolt reveals that Ernst's idealism is partly sustained by emptiness:

> `大切なものが　ない`

He has made himself expendable to the ideal just as Shin once made himself expendable to Undertaker duty.

The problem is not caring too much about lives.

It is acting as though one's own continued existence carries no relational or political value.

### support as reciprocal rather than upward

Shin later reflects on how followers can push leaders upward until the role crushes them.

He asks Lena whether the Eighty-Six burdened their `女王陛下` in that way.

Lena's answer distinguishes their relation from Noelle's:

> `従うのではなく支えてくれた。求めるのではなく信じてくれた`

That is V12's best relational test for legitimate leadership.

## 4.8 Epilogue — the `仔鹿` and the limit of institutional trust

Yuto learns enough about Chitori and the `仔鹿` that he believes reporting them would lead to permanent confinement.

He chooses to violate his duty to the Federacy and help them travel.

The choice is not framed as pure anti-state individualism.

He still arranges delayed reporting through Amari so that the Federacy receives some warning.

The `仔鹿` ask for two things:

- a route back to the Republic, their birthplace;
- information about Dustin Jaeger.

V12 therefore ends by opening a new problem:

> **What happens when a generally better institution cannot be trusted to respect the autonomy of a particularly vulnerable group?**

That question belongs prospectively to V13.

# 5. Formal freedom versus usable freedom

V12 supplies unusually direct evidence for a distinction that V2 has previously inferred.

## 5.1 Formal liberty

The Federacy abolishes hereditary political hierarchy and establishes:

- legal equality;
- public education;
- occupational mobility;
- civic rights;
- military advancement through formal qualification.

These changes are substantive improvements.

Nothing in V12 justifies saying the old imperial order was morally preferable simply because it felt easier to some former subjects.

## 5.2 Usable liberty

A right becomes difficult to use when a person lacks:

- literacy;
- practical information;
- confidence that choice is permitted;
- experience making decisions;
- capacity to tolerate uncertainty;
- social support after error;
- or a model of responsibility other than obedience.

The abused Eighty-Six child provides the extreme case: the master is gone, yet the learned cognitive world still contains only obedience.

The former-serf communities provide the political-scale case: revolution changes law faster than inherited habits and competencies change.

## 5.3 Willingness matters too

V12 refuses a purely structural account.

Kiahi resents the academic test instead of treating it as information about a skill deficit.

Noelle refuses evidence that contradicts her self-conception.

Marcel openly identifies the comfort of shifting blame.

So the final V12 formula is not:

> freedom requires capacity.

It is:

> **usable freedom requires opportunities and capacities for judgment, plus a willingness to exercise judgment and accept revisable responsibility.**

# 6. Support, scaffolding, paternalism, and domination

V12 offers enough examples to distinguish four relational structures.

## 6.1 Supportive scaffolding

Examples:

- simplified manuals;
- introductory educational media;
- progressive follow-up resources;
- command instructions during acute crisis;
- mental-health leave;
- officer-development courses;
- peers flagging overload;
- leaders delegating decisions.

The test is whether support tends toward greater future agency.

## 6.2 Protective paternalism with an exit condition

Lena's enforced rest is not fully voluntary at first.

But its aim is to restore capacities needed for later self-direction.

The restriction is time-bounded and medically justified.

## 6.3 Role paternalism

The MP's `君たちは操縦士だ` moves the other direction.

It protects military readiness by denying that the Eighty-Six may have legitimate noncombat obligations or relationships.

The institution defines what they are for.

## 6.4 Domination

Noelle's follower structure—and the old imperial model behind it—makes another person's judgment permanently authoritative.

The follower's ideal state becomes:

> `ただ、従っていれば`

That is precisely the condition V12 rejects as a civic ideal.

---

# 7. Noelle: saviorism as reciprocal trap

Noelle is one of V12's strongest comparative characters because she is neither a cartoon tyrant nor simply an incompetent victim.

## 7.1 Why followers believe in her

She is:

- inherited local nobility;
- childhood protector-symbol;
- military officer;
- the commander whose unit has avoided losses;
- someone who genuinely grieves each death from her home region.

Trust has a factual basis.

## 7.2 Why she needs the belief

Noelle has internalized the role of the benevolent princess who must always protect.

Followers' expectations therefore become evidence that she must possess an answer.

When she does not know, she cannot say:

> I do not know.

That is where Shiden's humble curiosity becomes the decisive counter-image.

## 7.3 Hail Mary

The “Hail Mary” is both:

- an American-football all-or-nothing long pass, as the afterword notes;
- and a prayer-name embedded in the volume's Mary vocabulary.

Noelle turns politics into the expectation that one desperate move will erase structural complexity.

The real problem is not boldness.

It is belief in a solution insulated from correction.

## 7.4 Mutual production of the savior

Followers say:

> tell us what is wrong;
> tell us who caused it;
> tell us what to do;
> save us.

Noelle hears:

> if I cannot answer, I have failed to be who I am.

The result is the same structure V12 warns Shin and Lena against: a leader pushed upward until no admission of weakness remains possible.

# 8. Shin: the `弱い死神` and democratic anti-heroism

V12 gives the old legacy phrase its exact evidentiary home.

Shin rejects demands from outsiders and says:

> `そこまで自惚れてもいない`

then:

> `一人きりじゃ戦えない、弱い死神だから`

Three things follow.

## 8.1 weakness is relational realism

Shin no longer imagines his own capacity as sufficient.

He needs:

- Raiden;
- Kurena;
- the Strike Package;
- Lena;
- scouts;
- infantry;
- engineers;
- logistics;
- institutions.

## 8.2 anti-heroism is not anti-excellence

Shin remains extraordinarily capable.

The point is not to deny excellence.

It is to prevent excellence from becoming the political theory that everyone else may stop developing capacity because the hero exists.

## 8.3 the line links directly to democracy

Later he observes:

> `自分以外まで支配し守護する、英雄など連邦軍には必要ない。`

The military and political arguments converge:

> **healthy institutions distribute capacity so that no singular hero must own everyone else's survival.**

---

# 9. Lena: rest, identity, and the right not to function

V12 gives Lena the first sustained institutional removal from command for health reasons.

## 9.1 why the rest matters

Her prior identities have repeatedly been organized around usefulness:

- Handler;
- Bloody Regina;
- Queen;
- commander;
- survivor responsible for those who did not survive.

Being ordered to rest therefore threatens more than schedule.

It threatens role-continuity.

## 9.2 she does not choose rest entirely by herself

This matters methodologically.

Shin notices eating problems.

Others notice sleep problems.

Grethe and mental-health staff act.

The institution tells Lena she is not currently the best judge of whether she should continue working.

This is paternalistic in a narrow descriptive sense.

But it is temporary, medical and capacity-restoring.

## 9.3 rest produces positive space

Late in the volume, Lena recognizes that rest has emptied the mental space previously filled by guilt and circular anguish.

The text then names what could occupy that space instead:

- future dreams;
- enjoyable plans for tomorrow;
- the person she loves.

Recovery is therefore not mere symptom reduction.

It creates **cognitive room for futurity**.

## 9.4 relational support without worship

When Shin worries that the Eighty-Six have turned Lena into another Noelle, she answers:

> `従うのではなく支えてくれた。求めるのではなく信じてくれた`

The difference between Queenhood and savior cult is not that Lena bears no burden.

It is that the people around her also carry responsibility.

---

# 10. Frederica: from inherited crown to self-government

Frederica's V12 material is one of the volume's most philosophically explicit arcs.

Vika asks whether her rescue impulse serves others or her own conscience.

Later, when she cannot save doomed rebels, he recommends detachment.

She refuses.

Her conclusion is:

> `わらわ自身の王となろうぞ`

This is not a declaration of political sovereignty.

It is a declaration that:

- conscience can be retained without pretending it guarantees success;
- powerlessness need not justify looking away;
- witnessing failure may become preparation for later responsibility;
- inherited imperial identity need not determine the form of her adulthood.

Frederica's “crown” becomes behavioral rather than genealogical.

That substantially strengthens the legacy hypothesis that her imperial inheritance is becoming a revisable language rather than destiny.

---

# 11. Ernst: ideals require a bearer who also counts

Ernst's V12 crisis is structurally parallel to Shin's earlier self-disposal.

He says lives cannot be replaced and refuses policies that make refugees disposable.

That is one of the Federacy's clearest moral strengths.

Yet he treats:

- his popularity;
- office;
- political future;
- and perhaps life

as though they do not matter.

The official's accusation of `裏切り` reframes the problem:

> **self-sacrifice can betray people who need you to remain present.**

This is the political version of V6–V9's relational correction to Shin.

A leader is not morally purified by treating himself as the one expendable exception to universal human worth.

---

# 12. Kurena and post-combat trauma without totalization

V12 gives unusually direct evidence that combat functionality can coexist with delayed distress.

During the V11 operation, Kurena does not revisit her parents' deaths because attention is consumed by fighting and vigilance.

Only after returning to safety do nightmares emerge.

The narration says:

> `本当は、辛いのだ。`

This should revise two bad models at once.

It rejects:

- “the Eighty-Six are fine because they can fight”;
- “the Eighty-Six are nothing but damaged victims.”

Kurena is both highly capable **and** vulnerable to memories whose expression depends on context.

The text also gives recovery an ordinary social form: a peer hears her cry, brings cocoa, and stays.

---

# 13. The abused children: freedom after coercive identity formation

This is V12's harshest personhood material.

## 13.1 administrative rescue does not immediately rewrite self-model

The child is legally outside the master's control.

He cannot use that fact.

The conceptual category “I may refuse” is absent from the world he has been trained to inhabit.

This gives unusually direct literary form to the idea that **external noninterference is insufficient when coercion has shaped the subject's repertoire of imaginable action**.

## 13.2 the ethical response cannot be “make choices for him forever”

The volume does not give a complete recovery program.

But its broader structure implies that the answer cannot simply be replacement domination by benevolent authorities.

The child needs conditions under which refusal, trust, preference and self-description can become possible.

## 13.3 technology and consent

Para-RAID demonstrates again that a technology of connection becomes monstrous without consent.

The same sensory link can support:

- command;
- friendship;
- romantic intimacy;
- torture;
- sexual violation;
- surveillance.

Connection itself is not the moral good.

**Mutual standing within connection** is.

---

# 14. The Hail Mary followers and epistemic abdication

The volume's most biting political critique is epistemological.

The followers repeatedly prefer:

- certainty over inquiry;
- identity over evidence;
- leader validation over correction;
- blame over reflection;
- miraculous technology over technical understanding.

One core cognitive rule becomes:

> if something is frightening, it must be wrong;
> if I am right, things must work;
> if things fail, someone else must have betrayed us.

This is not merely lack of education.

It is a **closed error-correction system**.

The positive democratic countermodel is not “everyone knows enough.”

It is:

> **everyone must remain capable of discovering that they were wrong.**

---

# 15. Education as opening doors, not normalizing lives

Shiden's nuclear-learning sequence is one of the clearest confirmations of the V7 ordinary-life/capability thesis.

Miarona does not tell her which future she should choose.

She says, in effect:

> look into many doors;
> learn at your own pace;
> perhaps something will become deeply interesting to you.

This is very different from forcing a predetermined civilian norm.

The goal is not:

> become a nuclear physicist.

It is:

> **possess enough exposure to discover preferences that could not exist when only one world was available.**

The later scene where some Eighty-Six continue reading the materials while Shiden decides the subject is not for her is exactly the right outcome.

Capability expansion includes the freedom **not** to choose the offered option.

# 16. Military doctrine: human judgment as both fog and strength

## 16.1 Sirins and removed will

The epigraph defines Sirins as operationally valuable partly because they do not inject independent will into orders.

That can increase predictability.

It also marks precisely what human commanders and soldiers possess that machines do not: judgment capable of departing from initial instruction.

## 16.2 distributed combined arms

The dam operation is explicitly distributed.

Shin's ability helps locate the Legion.

But:

- infantry counters optical camouflage;
- snipers create timing windows;
- engineers understand demolition;
- other squadrons immobilize the heavy engineer;
- Kurena controls fire from high ground;
- command integrates native sea-beast behavior into movement.

The “weak death god” is operationally accurate.

## 16.3 retreat and preservation

Post-V11 Giad increasingly treats preserving trained personnel as strategically necessary rather than cowardly.

The contrast with the Eighty-Sixth Sector remains profound.

## 16.4 accessible doctrine

The simplified anti-Legion manual demonstrates that military competence is institutional as well as personal.

Good doctrine is not merely correct. It must be **usable by the people expected to act on it**.

## 16.5 the Hail Mary military failure

The rebels commit nearly every organizational error the positive model avoids:

- no reliable technical expertise;
- no disciplined dissent;
- no adequate staff process;
- command fused to charisma;
- confirmation bias;
- magical-weapon thinking;
- hostility toward correction.

Their collapse is political and military at once.

# 17. Political theory: V12's actual argument after adversarial testing

The legacy capabilities reading survives, but only in narrowed form.

## 17.1 what V12 does support

V12 strongly supports:

- legal freedom and practical ability are distinct;
- education can enlarge usable choice;
- decision-making requires practice;
- responsibility can be learned;
- recovery can require material/institutional scaffolding;
- people can be legally free while psychologically unable to imagine refusal;
- leadership should cultivate rather than monopolize agency;
- democratic orders are healthier when no single hero must rule/protect everyone.

## 17.2 what V12 does not support

V12 does **not** establish:

- that some classes are naturally incapable of freedom;
- that hereditary rulers are normatively superior;
- that everyone must desire maximum choice;
- that education guarantees wisdom;
- that freedom should be withheld until authorities deem someone ready;
- that Giad never reproduces role coercion;
- that responsibility is purely individual.

## 17.3 revised formulation

The strongest V2 statement after V12 is:

> **Formal freedom becomes substantively usable when people have realistic access to knowledge, practice in judgment, room to make recoverable errors, material support, and relationships that help without permanently deciding for them. Yet these conditions enable rather than guarantee self-government; the person still has to participate in the work of choosing, revising, and bearing responsibility.**

That is much narrower and more textually defensible than importing a complete external political-philosophy system.

# 18. Race and dehumanization

V12 adds several important layers.

## 18.1 hidden atrocity inside the Republic

The sale of Eighty-Six infants demonstrates that dehumanization was not confined to the battlefield bureaucracy.

Private citizens could consume racial domination directly as:

- property ownership;
- torture;
- sexual entertainment;
- experimentation.

This expands the Republic critique from state administration into civil society without implying every citizen participated equally.

## 18.2 backlash inside Giad

When the Republic surveillance scandal becomes public, some Giad citizens generalize blame to Alba/white-haired people broadly.

Annette is targeted with racialized language despite helping uncover the crime.

The episode is important because it shows how quickly moral outrage at one group's atrocity can reproduce **category-level contempt** toward people sharing visible traits.

The scale is not equivalent to San Magnolia's genocide.

The cognitive mechanism of collective substitution is recognizable.

## 18.3 revenge attribution

Giad civilians even imagine the abused Eighty-Six children as revenge-seeking traitors.

The interpretation converts victims into a morally satisfying story for outsiders without knowing their actual condition.

V4's `嗜好品の同情` problem therefore mutates into **consumable righteous anger**.

# 19. Personhood, AI, will, and moral agency

## 19.1 `己の意思という夾雑物`

V12 intentionally calls individual will an operational impurity in the Sirin context.

This is not a series-level rejection of will.

It is the military advantage of predictability.

## 19.2 Shepherd Dogs

The heavy-engineer unit contains human-derived neural substrate described as:

> `人の人格と意思は失った嘆き`

Again cognition/processing substrate survives without intact personality and will.

The V4 personhood distinction remains stable.

## 19.3 Zelene chooses continued existence

Zelene rejects an offered death:

> `わたくしは死なない。まだ死は選ばない。`

Her reason is relational and purposive: Shin and Vika have not given up, and her information may still matter.

This is especially important after V11's purpose-captured Shepherd model.

Zelene demonstrates:

- self-reference across biological/machine continuity;
- future-oriented conditional reasoning;
- preference about death;
- relational concern;
- ability to revise an earlier plan for self-destruction.

That substantially strengthens her moral-subject evidence.

# 20. Body, injury, disability, and rest

V12 treats bodies as constraints on political/moral aspiration.

- Lena cannot continue functioning indefinitely because she wants to.
- Shin's sensory ability forces sleep when load accumulates.
- Grethe tells him reporting strain is part of command responsibility.
- radiation kills people regardless of whether their cause is righteous.
- abused children carry implanted devices and conditioned bodily fear.
- Theo's absence from Processor status continues to change the social geometry of the Eighty-Six.

The strongest recurring principle is:

> **the body can veto narratives of pure will.**

This works against Noelle's belief that being right makes success inevitable and against heroic self-conceptions that treat recovery as optional.

# 21. Childhood, learning, and developmental asymmetry

V12 strongly reinforces V10's childhood thesis.

The Eighty-Six often possess exceptional military judgment because childhood development was violently redirected toward survival.

Meanwhile some former imperial adults were historically denied forms of civic practice that a democratic order suddenly expects.

Both cases show development as **environmentally specialized**, not a universal maturity scale.

The abused children present the darkest version: their developmental environment did not simply fail to teach autonomy; it actively trained obedience under pain.

The positive institutional answer repeatedly involves age-appropriate or capacity-appropriate teaching:

- accessible animation;
- simplified manuals;
- schools;
- officer courses;
- guided rest;
- permission to try unfamiliar activities.

# 22. Death, grief, and survival obligation

## 22.1 Ishmael

Ishmael calls continued life after the destruction of the Fleet Countries a kind of `生き恥`.

He says punishment would almost be easier.

But he cannot die because he must:

- preserve the Fleet Countries' memory;
- represent his people;
- prevent others from issuing reckless orders to them.

This is survival as political burden rather than private pleasure.

## 22.2 the stranded commander's inheritance

The dead company commander protects his soldiers after death through usable instructions and trust.

This is another counterexample to inheritance-as-curse.

His final authority is designed to **expire once rescue arrives**.

The dead support future agency rather than claim permanent control.

## 22.3 Noelle's dead

Noelle interprets deaths as proof that she must double down on being savior.

The difference is not whether one remembers the dead.

It is what the memory is allowed to authorize.

# 23. Ordinary life and future capacity

V12 contains extensive ordinary practice despite strategic crisis:

- school;
- cooking;
- film screenings;
- reading;
- coffee and cake;
- moon-viewing;
- Halloween;
- apple-rabbit carving;
- calling family;
- riding and疗養 activities;
- informal scientific curiosity.

These scenes are not mere tonal relief.

They demonstrate the material environments in which alternative selves become testable.

The most important detail may be that people respond differently.

Shiden tries nuclear material and decides it is not especially for her.

Other Eighty-Six keep reading and ask for more.

Freedom is therefore not measured by whether everyone ends up with the same enriched hobby.

It is measured partly by whether they can **encounter, accept or reject possibilities without existential consequence**.

# 26. Japanese voice, address, and terminology

V12's political argument depends heavily on lexical contrasts.

## 26.1 `自由` / `平等`

For the Hail Mary followers these words can mean unwanted burden.

For the Federacy ideal they mean self-government and rights.

For aristocrats they sometimes mean abandonment of the weak.

The volume refuses one stable emotional valence.

## 26.2 `王` / `羊`

Aristocratic speakers use `王` and `羊` as quasi-natural political types.

Shin/Frederica repurpose `王` inwardly:

> rule yourself rather than necessarily rule others.

This semantic appropriation is essential to the volume's political movement.

## 26.3 `弱い死神`

The adjective `弱い` is relationally productive rather than humiliating. It places a limit on Shin's claim to universal responsibility.

## 26.4 `支える` versus `従う`

Lena's contrast is exact:

> `従うのではなく支えてくれた。求めるのではなく信じてくれた`

This may be the volume's most useful vocabulary for legitimate authority.

## 26.5 `夾雑物`

Will/personality repeatedly appears as “impurity” from the perspective of systems seeking predictable replication or command.

The moral series-level irony becomes increasingly strong: what systems call impurity is often what allows a subject to refuse, reinterpret and become otherwise.

## 27.1 Mary / sheep / lamb / shepherd

The naming system makes obedience visible as a recurring social form:

- Mary leads;
- lambs follow;
- shepherds command;

The same vocabulary appears in Legion nomenclature, creating a deliberate tension between obedient machines and obedient politics.

The “bullet” is both weapon and fantasy of decisive simplicity.

The solution cannot be total certainty.

It is competence in acting while uncertainty remains.

## 27.5 moon-viewing / shared ordinary time

The moon-viewing scene links people across different locations and relationships. Ordinary ritual becomes repeatable future time, not merely respite from plot.

# 29. Retrospective / full-series rereading

With full-series hindsight available only as a separate V2 layer, V12 should be remembered as the volume where the master thesis becomes politically explicit **and simultaneously harder to state cleanly**.

Earlier volumes showed individuals learning to imagine futures.

V12 asks what social order makes that imagination possible.

The answer is not simply “Giad.”

Giad contains:

- schools;
- therapy;
- accessible military education;
- political rights;
- legal equality;
- voluntary military structures;

but also:

- racial backlash;
- emergency censorship;
- role-reducing officials;
- aristocratic contempt surviving after revolution;
- political leaders who erase themselves into ideals.

That complexity is methodologically useful.

The V2 thesis should not become:

> good state gives capability; bad state removes it.

It is better:

> **institutions continuously shape the range of selves people can realistically become, and even comparatively humane institutions require correction because support can slide into function, protection into control, and righteous ideals into self-erasure.**

# 31. Governing hypothesis tests

## H1 — Recognition must expand into conditions for a revisable life

**V12 status: STRONGLY STRENGTHENED / OPERATIONALIZED.**

The abused children demonstrate recognition/legal rescue without immediately usable refusal. Schools,療養, accessible doctrine and relationship scaffolds demonstrate conditions through which agency can expand.

## H2 — Pride is dignity and possible enclosure

**V12 status: STRENGTHENED BY COMPARATIVE CASE.**

> **Freedom is not made substantive by benevolent control; it becomes more usable when rights are joined by realistic opportunities to learn, decide, recover from mistakes and receive non-totalizing support—while individuals retain responsibility to participate in the work of judgment.**

# Closing V12 synthesis

Volume 12 is the point where *86*'s long argument about freedom becomes explicitly political—and immediately resists being reduced to a slogan.

The easy proposition would be:

> freedom is good.

The equally easy reactionary proposition would be:

> some people cannot handle freedom and need rulers.

V12 makes both insufficient.

The old Empire trained many people not to decide, then the revolution abruptly declared them responsible decision-makers. Some learned. Some resented the burden. Some discovered that obedience was psychologically easier because failure could always belong to someone above them.

Noelle offers the seductive solution: return responsibility upward. Follow the princess. Believe in the miraculous weapon. Let someone else identify the enemy. Let certainty replace inquiry.

And the result is disaster.

But the novel also refuses the aristocrat's satisfaction that the disaster proves sheep require masters.

A platoon of people from essentially the same historically subordinated background protects children for a month. A soldier who struggles with reading can use doctrine when his sergeant writes it accessibly. Shiden can begin from `全然わかんねえ` and move into knowledge because someone treats ignorance as a starting point rather than a shameful identity. Eighty-Six who learned under lethal coercion can use schools not because school makes them “normal,” but because new knowledge creates options that survival never gave them time to discover.

So V12's real political proposition is not paternalism.

It is **developmental freedom**.

A free person is not imagined as an isolated sovereign who never needs help.

Shin calls himself a `弱い死神` precisely because he cannot fight alone.

Lena has to be ordered to rest because sometimes a person's current preference is distorted by exhaustion.

Frederica can become `わらわ自身の王` while still needing others.

The stranded infantry survives because a dying commander temporarily makes the world simple enough to endure.

The decisive test is what the relationship does next.

Does support return judgment to the person?

Or does it teach that someone else must always decide?

That is also why the abused Eighty-Six child is the volume's most devastating political image. The state has rescued him. The law says the master can no longer touch him. Yet the sentence `I can refuse` does not exist inside the world he has been permitted to imagine.

Formal emancipation is real.

It is not yet enough.

And this is why V12 ultimately fits the series' deeper personhood argument so well.

To recognize someone as a person is not only to refrain from killing them or to grant them a legal title.

It is to build a world in which their preferences can become meaningful, their mistakes need not be fatal, their ignorance can become learning, their dependence can become support rather than ownership, and their future can contain more than the role somebody else once assigned.

But *86* adds the harder final clause:

> **no institution can perform the whole act of self-government for the person without destroying the very freedom it is trying to secure.**

The doors can be opened.

Someone still has to decide whether—and how—to walk through them.
