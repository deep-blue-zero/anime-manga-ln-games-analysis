# 06 — Giad and Comparative Institutions: Citizenship, Freedom, and Paternalism

## Scope and authority note

This document is the primary Phase-6 home for the Federal Republic of Giad and the comparative institutional problem opened by its rescue of the surviving Spearhead members. It analyzes Giad citizenship, Ernst Zimmerman and civilian authority, welfare and education, military professionalism, child protection, public information, social belonging, exceptional-unit dependence, Frederica's imperial inheritance, emergency coercion, and the V13-V14 crisis. It also compares Roa Gracia, the Fleet Countries, the Holy Theocracy, Milmelkoleo, Hail Mary, and the surviving political remnants of San Magnolia where those comparisons clarify what Giad does and does not achieve.

The governing question is:

> **What materially changes when the Eighty-Six become recognized citizens, and why do citizenship, welfare, education, and benevolent protection remain insufficient unless people can exercise judgment, refuse assigned futures, survive mistakes, and retain routes of return?**

The document does not treat Giad as a liberal-democratic ideal type imported from external political theory. It follows the institutions and vocabulary established by the supplied novels. Nor does it treat every hierarchy, military order, emergency restriction, or unequal burden as a new Eighty-Sixth Sector. The Phase-5 comparison rule remains mandatory: a later structure becomes Sector-like only as expendability, constrained refusal, blocked return, and political distancing converge. Historical equivalence with San Magnolia requires much more [V13-L121-V13-L127].

Document 05 remains the primary home for the Republic's genocide. Document 07 will provide the full military and strategic synthesis. Document 08 will decide the body/personhood implications of Sirins, Fawns, disability, and medical custody. This document uses those subjects only to test institutions.

---

# I. Executive thesis

Giad's moral importance begins with an uncomplicated fact that later criticism must not erase:

> **The Federacy recognizes the Five as persons and citizens when San Magnolia had organized itself around making them disappear.**

The grant—`今日から連邦市民`, citizens of the Federacy from today—is not empty symbolism [V02-L015]. It changes the state's obligations. The Five receive legal standing, housing, money, medical care, education, civilian time, public records, military review, and a polity that counts its dead. Suspicion is addressed through security procedures that can be satisfied by evidence rather than through a hereditary category from which innocence is impossible. When they later fight, the institution at least claims that fighting through the war means surviving it and returning, not dying conveniently outside the citizen body [V03-L018-V03-L021].

Those differences are decisive. They are why the Federacy's later regression is tragic rather than the exposure of a fraud that never mattered.

But legal recognition is a floor, not a completed life. The Five can be citizens while remaining socially exoticized as monsters, pitied as damaged children, converted into heroic symbols, or narrowed to exceptional military uses [V02-L003, V02-L039, V04-L035]. They can possess civilian options without yet possessing a confident desire for one. They can receive care that protects them while also discovering that the caregiver has decided what a healed child should want. They can enlist voluntarily while carrying preferences formed under genocide, terminal time, and childhood militarization. They can be medically valuable as people and strategically valuable as capabilities, with the second value threatening to consume the first.

Giad therefore becomes the series' sustained experiment in **substantive freedom**.

The strongest V2 formulation is:

> **A person is politically freer when legal standing, material capability, social infrastructure, and practiced judgment make several futures genuinely usable; when support can be questioned; when mistakes do not cancel membership; when roles can be revised; and when exposed people retain a credible route back.**

This model rejects two symmetrical failures.

The first is abandonment disguised as liberty:

> You have formal rights, so the institution owes you nothing further, even if health, education, information, transport, time, and security make those rights unusable.

The second is domination disguised as care:

> Because judgment is difficult and people sometimes choose badly, a wiser protector should determine their life indefinitely.

V02 begins the distinction through Ernst. His household, citizenship grant, and refusal to consume the Five as tools are real moral goods. His wish to return them to `ordinary children` becomes a `哀れみの檻`, a cage of pity, when his preferred image of recovery replaces their judgment [V02-L025, V02-L028]. The officer-school compromise is better because it does not make civilianization compulsory; it enlarges the paths available after military service [V02-L031].

V12 scales that problem into political theory. Formal freedom can arrive faster than the capacities and habits required to use it. The novels accept the diagnosis but reject the hereditary conclusion. Difficulty with self-government is an argument for education, information, rest, material support, recoverable error, and accountable institutions—not proof that ordinary people should remain subjects forever [V12-L023-V12-L025, V12-L031-V12-L036, V12-L052].

V13 then shows Giad losing many of the conditions that made its freedom usable. Catastrophic casualties, displacement, unresolved imperial cleavages, refugee politics, information warfare, exhaustion, coercive reserve policy, and scapegoating turn utility into desert: politically weak people are said to owe greater exposure because they are less useful [V13-L043]. Formal citizenship remains, but return routes narrow, command trust collapses, Lena becomes hostage leverage, and an exposed military population is trapped between the Legion and human malice [V13-L119-V13-L127].

V14 supplies two rival institutional answers.

One answer treats democratic citizenship as difficult labor. Citizens must look at bad facts, preserve records, study for a postwar future, communicate across fear, punish wrongdoing through law, and keep justice outside the community as a standard to which the community remains answerable [V14-L019, V14-L048-V14-L050, V14-L121-V14-L123].

The other converts failure into an anthropology of permanent incapacity. Ordinary people should never have possessed words, thought, freedom, or will; Giad should return to empire under a supposedly legitimate bloodline [V14-L206-V14-L214]. The transformation from `我ら、世界に誇る正義たらん` to `我らはすなわち正義である` is the grammatical moment at which aspiration becomes moral ownership.

The mature Giad thesis is consequently not that good institutions prevent moral collapse.

It is:

> **Giad is better because it builds real mechanisms of recognition, capability, accountability, role exit, and return. Its final-arc crisis asks whether those mechanisms are corrigible enough to survive the people and emergencies that can damage them—without relying on one exceptional ruler, one heroic unit, or one hereditary class to substitute permanently for political judgment.**

# II. Five layers of freedom the comparative analysis must keep separate

The novels repeatedly frustrate any single-axis account of freedom. For Document 06, five layers are load-bearing.

| Layer | Institutional question | Giad achievement | Persistent failure risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Legal recognition** | Does law treat the person as citizen, subject, property, caste, weapon, refugee, or nonperson? | The Five become citizens; Colorata identity is not a legal death sentence [V02-L015]. | Status can become unevenly protective under crisis; artificial and dangerous-bodied subjects remain unsettled. |
| **Material provision** | Are medicine, housing, money, food, transport, records, education, rest, and communications available? | Giad provides a substantial material floor and invests in survival. | Provision can be conditional on usefulness or administered paternalistically. |
| **Social belonging** | Is the person understood as a member rather than a tolerated burden, mascot, monster, or heroic exception? | Household and peer relations become possible; public citizenship exists. | Racism, pity, heroization, and refugee stigma persist [V02-L003, V04-L035-V04-L036]. |
| **Political agency** | Can the person speak, refuse, contest, know, organize, and hold institutions accountable? | Democratic and military offices create routes for answer and review. | Emergency secrecy, hostage leverage, coercive mobilization, and reactionary guardianship narrow those routes. |
| **Usable freedom** | Can the person realistically act on a choice and recover after error? | Education, leave, transport, care, training, archives, and return expectations expand the option set. | Formal options can exceed learned desire; infrastructure and safety can collapse; roles can become effectively no-exit. |

These layers interact but do not substitute for one another.

Citizenship without material provision can be nominal. Material provision without refusal can become guardianship. Social acceptance without legal standing remains fragile. Political voice without information is manipulable. A wide option list without health, transport, time, or practice may exist only on paper.

The positive model is therefore neither pure negative liberty nor comprehensive benevolent management. It is **supported, corrigible self-government**.

# III. The first Giad difference: citizenship restores reciprocal obligation

## 1. Legal personhood is not a sentimental achievement

The Five are not made human by Federacy recognition. Their names, humor, work, grief, preferences, mutual care, and ordinary life already refute San Magnolia's ontology. Giad changes something else: it changes what the state is required to do in response to their humanity.

Citizenship creates reciprocal claims. The state must house, protect, record, medically treat, educate, and legally situate them. It cannot officially treat them as enemy livestock or uncounted machine components. This is why T14-C32 is mandatory: citizenship is not superficial simply because it cannot create felt home.

The security review also matters. A state fighting an enemy that copies human cognition has rational reasons to investigate unknown survivors. Giad does so through a process that can be completed and cleared. Suspicion is tied to evidence and present risk rather than inherited racial guilt. That procedural difference is one of the first proofs that caution and humanity are not opposites.

Ernst's refusal to use the Five merely because they are useful is similarly foundational. The V02 argument that doing so would make Giad the same as the Republic identifies an anti-disposability limit internal to the new polity [V02-L014]. His furious conclusion that humanity would deserve extinction if it could survive only by consuming children is excessive, but it names a real floor [V02-L016].

## 2. Counting the dead changes public reality

Giad's public casualty reporting is painful because soldiers are admitted into national loss. Theo's reaction to the published lists shows how alien this is to people whose deaths were officially zero [V02-L021].

Public counting is not a complete ethics. A state can count citizens and still use them badly. But casualty acknowledgment makes political costs visible. It permits mourning, criticism, resource debate, and accountability in ways the Republic's zero-death fiction structurally prevented.

This is one reason the Federacy's later information crisis is so serious. The polity's difference partly depends on bad facts remaining communicable. When reporting is restricted, manipulated, or converted into enemy categories, a central correction mechanism weakens.

## 3. Majority rule remains bounded by membership

Giad's political ideal is not that whatever the majority wants becomes right. Ernst ties electoral legitimacy to the inclusion of people whom citizens may fear or dislike [V03-L015]. This is the opposite of San Magnolia's strategy of redefining the target outside the citizen body.

The distinction is fragile. V13 shows how quickly a frightened majority can seek smaller categories onto which cost may be transferred. But that regression does not make the earlier legal principle fake. It demonstrates why political membership must remain protected precisely when it becomes unpopular.

# IV. Citizenship does not automatically create home, trust, or representation

## 1. Racism survives legal equality

`共和国の化物め` appears inside the better polity [V02-L003]. The insult is not backed by San Magnolia's denaturalization regime, and that difference must remain visible. It nevertheless shows that legal equality does not erase inherited categories, wartime suspicion, or social disgust.

V12-V13 broaden the point. Anti-Republic anger can spread toward visibly Alba people generally. Refugees, minorities, returned captives, military families, wounded soldiers, and suspected Fawn associations can become new targets. A state may preserve citizenship in text while social life makes some citizens dangerous to shelter, employ, educate, or trust.

## 2. Pity can objectify without becoming genocide

Giad's public response moves between two vertical images: damaged children to be protected and frightening fighters to be feared. The Five are either pets, victims, monsters, or heroes before they are ordinary citizens with mixed motives and disagreeable choices [V02-L018, V02-L039, V02-L044].

The public heroic narrative in V04 adds another form. Accurate facts can be organized into consumable tragedy and `嗜好品の同情`, sympathy as a luxury product [V04-L035]. Recognition exists, but the recognized subjects do not control the meaning made from them.

This is not morally equivalent to nonpersonhood. It is a warning that representation is a separate institutional layer. A polity can legally include people while centering the majority's emotional experience of including them.

## 3. A citizen may possess more than one home

The Five's weak attachment to Giad in V02 does not show that citizenship failed. Nor does their continuing identification as Eighty-Six invalidate Federacy membership. Home, citizenship, origin, household, military unit, and chosen destination are different relations.

The mature model allows plural belonging. Giad need not replace the Republic, the Sector, Spearhead, lost family, or a future home. Its legitimate task is to make residence and membership possible without demanding emotional assimilation.

That is why recovery of addresses and personnel records in Alter.1 expands biographical knowledge without compelling homecoming. An old address can become available as information while remaining emotionally unfamiliar. Institutions restore options; they should not prescribe the meaning of recovered origin.

# V. Ernst Zimmerman: protector, president, father, and paternalism test

## 1. Ernst's care is real

Ernst gives the Five what the Republic denied: citizenship, a home, food, education, money, safety, and adults who regard their survival as an end. The analysis should not reduce this to manipulation merely because later paternalism appears.

He also gives Frederica an adoptive household rather than treating the last empress as a political relic. Across the corpus, his strongest ethical intuition is that children must not become the expendable remainder of adult institutions.

That intuition becomes explicit when Frederica's imperial genetics acquire species-level strategic value. `子供を犠牲にするような国は` marks a refusal to let numerical utility settle the status of one child [V08-L003]. This is not strategic naivete; it is a rights claim against sacrificial arithmetic.

## 2. The cage of pity

Ernst becomes paternalistic when he treats civilianization as the only future that would prove rescue succeeded. His wish that the Five return to being `ordinary children` is impossible in a literal sense and coercive as a normative one [V02-L025]. History cannot be reversed, and childhood cannot be restored by decree.

Raiden's `哀れみの檻` names the problem precisely [V02-L028]. The cage is benevolent. It protects. It also determines which desires count as evidence of health.

Pity and hatred are not equivalent. Their consequences differ radically. Yet they share a possible verticality: the powerful party defines the other through a simplified story and hears disagreement as evidence that the other does not understand their own good.

## 3. Officer school as better scaffolding

Ernst's capacity to revise distinguishes him from permanent guardianship. The officer-school condition does not prohibit military service. It demands education that adds qualifications, institutional literacy, and later options: `選べる道はなるべく多い方がいい` [V02-L031].

This is one of the strongest positive examples of developmental scaffolding in the series. The institution does not simply approve the choice already shaped by war. Nor does it replace that choice with an adult's preferred alternative. It changes the path so that entering one role does not close every future exit.

Successful scaffolding should eventually make the protector less necessary. That is the key test.

## 4. Love can become exclusionary rage

V13 exposes the limit of Ernst's ethical identity. Primvere defends sacrificing outsiders to protect family. Ernst condemns the logic and then nearly answers it through personal violence. Frederica's `もうやめて。──おとうさん` interrupts him [V13-L093].

The scene matters because the loved child does not ask to be protected through another person's destruction. Family becomes an internal check on paternal violence rather than its justification.

Ernst's office and fatherhood therefore contain reciprocal risks. The president can instrumentalize the child for the polity. The father can instrumentalize the polity for the child. A legitimate order must prevent either relation from becoming sovereignty over everyone else.

## 5. The vulnerability of self-erasing leadership

Ernst often treats moral office as a demand that he personally absorb every contradiction. That idealism can become another irreplaceable-person system. A polity that depends on one exceptionally compassionate leader is not yet institutionally humane.

V13's hostage crisis makes the weakness concrete. If capturing one man can destabilize political legitimacy or redirect policy, executive virtue has not been sufficiently distributed into offices, procedures, and citizens.

The question for Giad is thus not whether Ernst is good. It is whether the order can preserve his best constraints when he is angry, absent, captured, wrong, or dead.

# VI. Capability infrastructure: freedom becomes usable through ordinary systems

## 1. The state does not create desire

The Five already possess curiosity, practical skill, humor, care, artistic preference, and ordinary pleasure. Giad does not civilize passive weapons. It makes existing agency cheaper, safer, repeatable, and broader.

V02's civilian interval is the first demonstration. Raiden works, Anju studies cooking, Theo draws, Kurena shops, and Shin visits libraries and museums [V02-L020]. Their competence outside combat directly refutes the claim that they know nothing but war.

Alter.1 provides an especially clean infrastructure control. Shin walks for hours to reach a library because he has not learned to treat public transport as an ordinary tool. Curiosity is already present. Buses, trains, maps, money, and learned expectations change the cost of acting on it.

The general rule is:

> **Infrastructure expands the range over which agency can operate; it should not be credited with inventing the subject who uses it.**

## 2. Education should expand repertoire, not enforce normality

V07's ordinary-life interval gives the core capability principle: being able to do something creates more possible forms of enjoyment. The point of dance, school conventions, social knowledge, or civilian exposure is not compulsory assimilation. It is a wider repertoire.

V12 makes the pedagogy explicit. Knowledge is made accessible; a low-cost first encounter is offered; questions are permitted; deeper material remains available; and the learner may decide not to continue [V12-L031-V12-L033]. Shiden's choice that a field is not for her is not educational failure. The option was made real enough to test.

This is the opposite of obedience training. Education serves freedom when it equips judgment rather than supplying the one approved answer.

## 3. Rest and medicine are political capabilities

`休むのも仕事だと思って` turns rest into institutional duty rather than private weakness [V12-L006]. Lena can be removed from command for mental-health recovery. Shin can be ordered to report overload. Theo is treated after combat function changes and offered transfer or education rather than disposal [V09-L002, V09-L011].

These measures matter because the Sector withdrew time. A person who cannot heal, learn, grieve, or try again possesses fewer futures even if no statute explicitly forbids them.

Medical protection is not automatically emancipatory, however. V14 shows Shin fearing that disclosure will let the military use his body and ability without meaningful regard for his will [V14-L057-V14-L060]. Care must preserve standing and future judgment, not merely retain a valuable asset.

## 4. Records and transport are forms of political memory

Archives restore names, birth dates, families, and addresses. Transport connects desire to place. Leave creates time not monopolized by the current function. Public casualty lists connect private loss to national record. These are ordinary administrative systems, but they determine whether a person can act as someone with a past and future.

The War History Compilation Room in V14 extends the archive forward. Its task is `未来の戦史`: knowledge organized to prevent repeat loss and build coming operations, not memory as a sealed monument [V14-L002-V14-L003].

# VII. Military citizenship: necessary authority without ownership of the person

## 1. Voluntary service remains historically loaded

The Five reenlist after a genuine civilian interval. Their choice is textually real; it should not be dismissed as false consciousness. Shin can say he is now on the battlefield by choice and has a road he may choose [V03-L009].

But T14-C16 remains open/narrowed. Choice occurs inside a history that made soldierhood central to self-understanding before ordinary alternatives could become durable. The correct formulation is neither wholly free vocation nor mere conditioning. It is **chosen preference under historical gravity**.

This is why Giad's task cannot be to prohibit military service in the name of rescue. It must ensure that military service does not remain the only role in which the Eighty-Six can possess competence, esteem, income, community, or future.

## 2. High risk is not identical with disposal

The Morpho operation becomes the comparative template. Strategic necessity is real. Selection bias is questioned. Political and military leaders review whether the mission is delayed extermination. Refusal and volunteer status matter. Army-wide diversion, support, extraction, and an expectation of return matter. Ernst's command is not die honorably but return repeatedly [V03-L013-V03-L018].

At the political-constitutional level, a state that expects soldiers to return treats risk differently from a state whose legitimacy requires that they do not.

The Fleet Countries later show that severe material constraint can narrow choice without automatically creating a Sector. Leaders and sailors share risk; the operation has bounded purpose; Giad explicitly intends to bring the Strike Package back [V08-L017]. Constraint must be evaluated through information, membership, refusal, burden sharing, and return—not through danger alone.

## 3. Offices must survive singular people

Giad professionalizes delegated command, review, training, and recovery. Raiden can hold office without becoming a substitute Shin. Lena can return after forced absence. Other commanders assume responsibility when a leader is incapacitated.

The anti-hero doctrine states the organizational principle: a military should not require one person to remain a hero forever [V07-L002-V07-L003]. V14's operation proves the point without denying excellence. Shin is absent; Lena, Raiden, Kurena, Theo, engineers, artillery, infantry, Sirins, terrain, and improvised tools produce a functioning whole.

This is not proof that individuals are interchangeable. It is proof that functions must be recoverable even when persons are not. T14-C14 therefore remains mandatory: distributed command does not make Shin's specific capability unimportant.

## 4. Exceptional units create political temptation

The Strike Package is both a safeguard and a danger. Concentrating expertise permits oversight, interoperability, and rapid response. It also creates a formation whose public symbolic value and strategic capability make exit politically expensive.

Heroization becomes a contract: because this unit saved us, it must continue saving us. V07's warning against a formation that must remain heroic is therefore institutional, not merely psychological.

V13 shows the darkest version. When trust collapses, Lena is retained as hostage infrastructure so the Eighty-Six will continue fighting [V13-L119-V13-L120]. Relationship, citizenship, and military duty are converted into leverage. The formally recognized soldier begins to approach functional captivity without being declared nonhuman.

## 5. Military instrumentality requires bounded scope

Richard's V11 formulation—military personnel are instruments of politics—is deliberately uncomfortable [V11-L036]. The series does not imagine public service without institutional use. Offices exist to coordinate persons toward collective ends.

The ethical limit is that the office may claim role-bounded conduct, not ownership of the whole life. Richard can order soldiers to serve the polity and forbid self-chosen annihilation, while also insisting that adults protect the children and preserve their capacity to live well [V11-L037-V11-L040].

A legitimate military may issue binding orders. It may not treat membership as a waiver of personhood.

# VIII. Frederica: child protection, self-rule, and the return of imperial blood

Frederica concentrates almost every institutional problem in one body.

She is:

- an adopted child;
- the last empress of the old Giad Empire;
- a possessor of rare ability;
- a potential genetic key to Legion shutdown;
- a member of the Spearhead household;
- a symbol useful to republicans, monarchists, soldiers, and reactionaries;
- and a person trying to define herself outside all those uses.

## 1. Protection must not become strategic ownership

Ernst's refusal to sacrifice her provides Giad's strongest rights claim against utilitarian necessity [V08-L003]. Yet protection alone is not enough. A child can be kept safe while being denied a future of her own.

Her V12 language—`己一人であっても己の王たらん` and `わらわ自身の王となろうぞ`—does not demand isolation [V12-L035, V12-L063]. It rejects permanent ownership of her judgment. She may accept help, family, office, and inherited identity while retaining the right to answer what they mean.

## 2. Inherited office can be chosen and still constrain

Vika's retained royal identity offers a useful control: freedom does not require abandoning every inherited role. A person can want to remain royal or aristocratic. The relevant question is whether the office remains answerable, revisable, and nonexclusive.

High status can also reduce private freedom. A gift, friendship, marriage, or appearance by a royal person becomes political whether the actor wants it to or not. Frederica's imperial ancestry similarly makes her body public infrastructure.

## 3. V14 does not settle consent

The final pages place Frederica on a throne and Shin beside her as War Emperor [V14-L212-V14-L214]. These images establish political fact at the level of announced positions. They do not establish consent, constitutional power, symbolic strategy, coercion, hostage pressure, or long-term regime design.

T14-C39 and T14-OQ-02 therefore prohibit a triumphalist or condemnatory completion. The New Imperial Court is a final-arc gate, not an interpreted endpoint.

The central unresolved question is T14-OQ-25:

> **What does being her own king mean once other people activate monarchy around her body?**

# IX. Comparative polities: what each institutional control isolates

## 1. Roa Gracia — consent inside hierarchy

Roa Gracia is neither San Magnolia nor an egalitarian answer to it. Its social order distinguishes subjects with military obligations from subordinated productive classes. Royal family members bear real front-line risk. Hierarchy and reciprocal duty coexist.

The Sirin donor regime creates an important consent boundary: donor material comes from volunteers and is scanned only when the living person is medically unsalvageable [V05-L005]. That is morally different from coerced harvesting.

But initial donor consent does not settle the standing of the successor subject. Sirins are designed to absorb military death for living citizens. The institution may protect one class of persons by creating another class whose refusal and future remain restricted.

Roa Gracia therefore demonstrates:

> **Consent somewhere in a causal chain cannot replace continuing analysis of the subject who now exists.**

## 2. The Fleet Countries — necessity with shared membership

The Fleet Countries face severe scarcity. Their leaders and sailors commit inherited naval capacity and accept enormous risk. The choice is constrained by national survival, but risk is not simply exported to a politically erased caste.

The decisive differences from San Magnolia include membership, information, shared burden, bounded operational purpose, and the effort to return allied forces alive [V08-L017, V08-L026].

The case prevents two opposite errors: necessity does not make every sacrifice innocent, and severe sacrifice does not automatically make every institution a Sector.

## 3. The Holy Theocracy — honor without autonomy

The sacred warrior caste is revered, educated, and entrusted with diplomacy. It is not described as animal or machine. Yet `選択の自由はない`: occupation, marriage, and role are substantially inherited [V09-L022-V09-L023].

This is the decisive counterexample to the claim that esteem proves freedom. A role can be holy, materially supported, and publicly admired while remaining no-exit.

When doctrine later changes because elite survival requires it, allegedly eternal destiny is exposed as selectively administered authority.

## 4. Milmelkoleo — usefulness as conditional social worth

Mixed-blood or aristocratically embarrassing people can acquire status through exceptional military service. This gives opportunity, but the opportunity is conditional: worth is purchased through heroic function and patron prestige [V09-L050-V09-L052].

The structure differs from racial nonpersonhood. It nevertheless demonstrates how a better polity can preserve inherited blood hierarchies and convert service into proof that a marginal person deserves membership.

## 5. Hail Mary — formal equality without practiced judgment

The post-imperial Hail Mary material supplies the strongest V12 test. Former subjects receive freedom, education, and equality, yet some experience judgment as an imposed burden. Followers outsource thought to Noelle; Noelle internalizes the demand to remain right.

The diagnosis is real: legal transition can outpace civic capacity. The authoritarian conclusion is not. The same background also produces people capable of learning and responsibility when support and information are accessible.

Hail Mary demonstrates two forms of captivity:

- citizens who surrender judgment to a savior;
- the savior who becomes unable to admit uncertainty because followers require infallibility.

Supported self-government must free both sides from that relation.

# X. V11: mixed motives, political capital, and adult responsibility

The Republic evacuation debate refuses moral purity.

Giad's leaders consider strategy, resources, reputation, and humanitarian obligation together. Richard openly names the Federacy's `beautiful name of justice` as political capital [V11-L034-V11-L035]. This does not make rescue counterfeit. He also accepts operational cost and refuses to abandon civilians simply because helping them benefits the state.

The mature institutional test is not whether motives are unmixed. Political institutions rarely act from one motive. Ask instead:

- Are people actually protected?
- Who bears the cost?
- Are reasons publicly answerable?
- Can the action be criticized?
- Does the rescue create reciprocal membership or a new debt of obedience?
- Are later failures recorded rather than hidden behind the reputation gained?

V11 also relocates moral burden upward. Grethe and senior officers are told not to make child soldiers carry every cruel decision [V11-L040]. This is not denial of the younger soldiers' competence. It is recognition that adult office exists partly to absorb responsibility that children should not have to normalize.

The state is permitted to use military roles. It is not permitted to pretend that role competence makes every burden age-neutral.

# XI. V12: supported self-government and the burden of being one's own king

V12 is the conceptual center of Document 06.

## 1. Formal liberty can feel coercive without being wrong

People formed under hereditary rule may experience literacy, equality, and required choice as burdens forced upon them [V12-L023-V12-L025]. The text does not mock the difficulty. Self-government requires capacities and habits that domination prevented from developing.

But the complaint cannot be promoted into a permanent ontology of incapable classes. `学ばない。考えない。決められない` is a contemptuous character judgment inside an argument, not the narrator's caste science [V12-L036].

The relevant response is development: accessible education, opportunities to practice judgment, material stability, trustworthy institutions, and mistakes that do not become irreversible social death.

## 2. Rights and capability are mutually necessary

V12's critique that rights were merely thrown at people identifies a real failure [V12-L059]. Yet capability cannot replace rights. A guardian can provide excellent education, housing, food, and safety while still denying refusal.

The mature proposition is conjunctive:

> **Protected standing prevents domination; capability makes standing usable.**

Neither layer completes the other.

## 3. Democracy is a method for fallible people

`市民全員が己の王だ` distributes the burden previously concentrated in rulers [V12-L052]. It does not declare every citizen wise.

The series' strongest defense of self-government is therefore not confidence in popular virtue. It is distrust of every claim that one class possesses final judgment by nature. Citizens, presidents, nobles, commanders, and protagonists all fail.

A defensible institution makes failure visible, contestable, recorded, correctable, and survivable.

## 4. Emergency restrictions require a restoration path

V12 temporarily suppresses free reporting during counterintelligence deception [V12-L016]. The text does not provide enough evidence to classify every such restriction as legitimate or tyrannical in advance.

The more useful test is whether the action is necessity-bound, proportionate, temporary, reviewable, and directed toward restoring ordinary agency. Emergency becomes authoritarian when it is converted from an exception into proof that citizens should never again possess information or judgment.

V13-V14 make that danger concrete.

# XII. V13: how a better polity reproduces a Sector relation

V13 should not be summarized either as `Giad was always San Magnolia` or `Giad merely had a bad year`.

It presents a regression process.

## 1. Material catastrophe activates unresolved fractures

Casualties, displacement, logistics failure, exhaustion, and strategic desperation arrive in a society that still contains imperial class divisions, ethnic cleavages, Republic/Federacy resentment, refugee insecurity, aristocratic networks, and uneven civic integration.

The revolution removed aristocracy as the formal bond without automatically healing the divisions through which imperial rule had governed [V13-L057-V13-L060]. No Face's description of a democracy existing in name only is an antagonist's interpretation, not an omniscient verdict. The subsequent collapse nevertheless confirms that institutional integration was incomplete.

## 2. Information warfare does not fabricate every fact

The Legion exploits real events, real images, and real institutional failures. True footage can be arranged into a false causal story. That is why T14-C35 matters: visual truth does not guarantee interpretive truth.

Public judgment fails not simply because citizens are lied to, but because fear and preexisting division determine which true fragments become explanatory.

## 3. Utility becomes moral desert

Manpower scarcity produces proposals to turn refugees into replacement soldiers. The shift is from `they can be used` to `because they are less useful, they ought to bear more danger` [V13-L043].

This is an institutional warning of broad scope:

> **Once productivity becomes the hidden condition of citizenship, weakness can be redescribed as debt.**

The Fawn program supplies the body-level version. Its designers know the targets are human and study them as useful dangerous bodies. Recognition does not prevent disposal [V13-L019-V13-L027].

## 4. Coercion precedes rebellion

Reserve forces attempt to drive refugees toward the front before the later secession and hostage politics [V13-L066-V13-L067]. The revolt is not justified by that history, but the sequence prevents the state from narrating coercion as a response to inexplicable disloyalty.

Fear is causal. It is not exculpatory.

## 5. Accountability survives unevenly

Rito's killer is investigated and punished; the relevant command apologizes; Shin refuses to condemn every Federacy soldier as an identical demon [V13-L108-V13-L111]. These mechanisms matter because they prove the earlier institutional difference has not vanished everywhere.

A polity can regress while some offices continue to do the right thing. That unevenness is precisely why recovery remains possible but unguaranteed.

## 6. The Sector threshold is crossed

By the final arrangement, the exposed forces are separated from the interior, meaningful retreat is denied, Lena is held as leverage, the Legion closes the front, and human malice closes the rear [V13-L119-V13-L127].

At the political-constitutional level, the convergence is:

> **expendability + constrained refusal + blocked return + social/political distancing.**

The narrator explicitly authorizes the comparison. Historical genealogy remains different from San Magnolia's racial denaturalization and extermination project. The structural relation has nevertheless been reproduced.

# XIII. V14: civic repair and reactionary guardianship

V14 contains no settled post-crisis constitution. It contains competing practices.

## 1. Repair begins by restoring answerability

The War History Compilation Room preserves operational knowledge for future survival [V14-L002-V14-L003]. Military procurement records civilian property so later residents can be repaid, and avoids homes where possible [V14-L011-V14-L012]. Legal punishment and permanent record are demanded for Rito's killing [V14-L047-V14-L050].

These acts differ in scale, but they share a principle: institutions should leave a trace by which actions can be judged later.

## 2. Citizenship becomes public address

Unofficial radio repeatedly addresses `連邦市民の同胞`, fellow Federacy citizens [V14-L038, V14-L108]. The address attempts to reconstruct reciprocal membership after the official order has fractured.

Recipes, jokes, old songs, and ordinary preferences then make categories particular again [V14-L109-V14-L123]. These practices cannot replace law, welfare, or representation. They rebuild the social imagination without which those institutions may lack a public willing to sustain them.

T14-OQ-31 remains open: civic imagination has not yet been shown scaling into durable representation and law.

## 3. Study refuses terminal politics

`勉強をしましょう。こんな時だからこそ。` insists on education for the day the war ends [V14-L019]. The act is politically important because emergency regimes justify themselves through permanent present crisis. Study preserves a time beyond the emergency.

## 4. Justice remains safe only as an external standard

The motto `我ら、世界に誇る正義たらん` is aspirational. The community must become worthy of justice [V14-L001, V14-L123].

The reactionary version changes `let us be` into `we are`: `我らはすなわち正義である` [V14-L206]. Once community and standard collapse into identity, criticism becomes betrayal and exclusion becomes righteous by definition.

## 5. Reaction restores rule by converting failure into permanent incapacity

The reactionaries do not merely argue that emergency requires temporary command. They argue that ordinary people should never have possessed language, thought, freedom, or will and that Giad should return to empire under legitimate crimson blood [V14-L208-V14-L210].

This is the decisive authoritarian move:

> **A temporary failure of citizens becomes proof of their permanent unfitness; a temporary protector becomes a hereditary ruler.**

V14 does not vindicate the V12 aristocratic diagnosis. It shows how a partly correct diagnosis of difficult freedom can be converted into permanent guardianship.

# XIV. Paternalism and emergency authority: a graded institutional test

Not every override of present choice is domination. Military fiction would become incoherent if command, evacuation, quarantine, treatment during incapacity, or emergency secrecy were treated as identical to hereditary rule.

The following test preserves the distinctions established by T09 and T10.

| Criterion | More legitimate intervention | More dangerous intervention |
|---|---|---|
| **Threat** | immediate, severe, difficult to reverse | vague future risk or merely disfavored choice |
| **Capacity state** | temporary impairment, coercion, acute crisis | stable disagreement redescribed as incapacity |
| **Scope** | narrow act required to prevent imminent harm | authority over identity, vocation, relationships, or political status |
| **Duration** | time-limited | indefinite or hereditary |
| **Review** | reasons and facts can reopen the decision | authority treats itself as final |
| **Agency return** | judgment is restored after crisis | intervention becomes standing guardianship |
| **Refusal** | refusal is heard where practicable | refusal is itself proof that more control is needed |
| **Capability effect** | later choice becomes more usable | dependence becomes the condition of continued support |
| **Conflict of interest** | protector separates the other's good from personal fear or prestige | protection chiefly protects the authority's anxiety, reputation, or power |
| **Distribution** | emergency burdens are shared and justified | the same weak or marked population repeatedly absorbs risk |
| **Return** | exit, retreat, and re-entry remain institutional goals | exposure is organized around nonreturn |

Under this model:

- Raiden's interruption of Shin in V13 is narrow, crisis-specific, and followed by restored professional judgment [V13-L083-V13-L084].
- Ernst's V02 attempt to decide the Five's whole future is broad and identity-defining.
- Temporary medical removal can protect later command agency; permanent custody of a useful body can become functional captivity.
- Temporary wartime secrecy may be defensible; hereditary denial of information and thought is not.

The crucial question is:

> **What would successful help make possible?**

If success means the subject can eventually answer more fully, the structure resembles scaffolding.

If success requires the subject to remain incapable so that authority remains necessary, it has become domination.

# XV. The mature institutional model: supported, corrigible self-government

The supplied corpus does not offer a complete constitutional design. It does establish a demanding institutional standard.

A humane polity would need at least the following features.

## 1. Protected status

Citizenship and personhood cannot disappear when a population becomes inconvenient, frightened, weak, foreign, artificial, disabled, or strategically useful.

## 2. A material floor independent of productivity

Food, shelter, medicine, education, transport, records, and safety cannot be contingent on remaining useful. Weakness must not create a duty to disappear.

## 3. Capability without normalization

Institutions should expand skills and options without requiring everyone to choose the same civilian, military, familial, or cultural life.

## 4. Practiced judgment

Freedom requires opportunities to decide, make limited mistakes, receive criticism, and revise. Education is not complete when facts are transmitted; it must make answer possible.

## 5. Bounded office

Military, parental, medical, royal, and executive authority must remain role-specific and reviewable. No office should become ownership of the whole person.

## 6. Distributed competence

A state should survive the absence, incapacity, error, or death of exceptional people. Heroic action may be admired; hero-dependent systems should be treated as failures of design.

## 7. Information capable of re-entering judgment

Bad news, minority testimony, records, and institutional mistakes must remain communicable. True images require causal interpretation; official silence cannot become the only public order.

## 8. Emergency restoration

Restrictions should point back toward ordinary agency. Emergency cannot become a permanent anthropology of incapable citizens.

## 9. Role exit and return

A citizen should be able to leave a function, retreat from exposure, recover, re-enter public life, and remain a member after changing usefulness.

## 10. External justice

Justice must remain a standard capable of condemning the polity. The community must never own the word so completely that whatever it does becomes just by definition.

## 11. Differentiated accountability

Wrongdoing requires investigation, judgment, record, and repair without converting whole populations into identical guilt categories.

## 12. Political recoverability

Institutions must be able to say `we were wrong` without concluding either that the nation is metaphysically evil or that an infallible ruler should replace political life.

This is the political form of the series' wider anti-foreclosure ethic:

> **No failure, role, emergency, category, or inherited order should become the only future a person or polity is allowed to possess.**

# XVI. Adversarial counterreadings

## Counterreading 1 — "The Republic is evil; Giad is good"

This is too simple. Giad is genuinely superior in status, welfare, procedure, information, medicine, education, and return. It also contains racism, paternalism, aristocratic residue, hero dependence, emergency coercion, and later reaction. Moral difference is institutional and historical, not national essence.

## Counterreading 2 — "V13 proves Giad was always the same as San Magnolia"

Rejected by T14-C24 and T14-C27. V13 reproduces a Sector relation. It does not retroactively erase citizenship, medical care, officer education, public casualties, political review, or the expectation of return. Regression is meaningful because those differences existed.

## Counterreading 3 — "Citizenship is merely paperwork beside real belonging"

Rejected by T14-C32. Denaturalization in V01 proves that legal membership determines residence, protection, records, medicine, political voice, and countable death. Citizenship is insufficient for home; it is not superficial.

## Counterreading 4 — "Legal equality completes freedom"

Overturned. V02-V03 distinguish legal options from belonging and developed desire. V12 distinguishes status from practiced judgment. V13 shows how formal membership can become hollow when return and refusal are materially closed.

## Counterreading 5 — "Material welfare completes freedom"

Rejected. A benevolent guardian can provide excellent outcomes while determining the subject's future. Provision is freedom-enhancing only when answer, revision, and exit remain real.

## Counterreading 6 — "Education is sufficient for self-government"

Rejected by T14-C25. Education enables judgment but cannot guarantee willingness, wisdom, courage, or resistance to fear. It must be joined to practice, information, accountability, and recoverable error.

## Counterreading 7 — "Human fallibility justifies permanent guardianship"

Rejected by T14-C26. Leaders and elites are also fallible. V14 presents hereditary restoration as the authoritarian exploitation of citizen failure, not its necessary solution.

## Counterreading 8 — "Support is healthy whenever it protects"

Rejected by T14-C10. Protection can become pity, surveillance, hostage leverage, family partiality, medical custody, or permanent guardianship. Help must normally return judgment.

## Counterreading 9 — "Every military order limiting retreat is a Sector"

Rejected. The stronger comparison requires a marked population whose exposure combines constrained refusal, disposability, blocked return, and political distancing. Tactical withdrawal limits during a bounded operation do not automatically meet that threshold.

## Counterreading 10 — "Recognizing people as human prevents disposal"

Rejected by T14-C28. The Fawn project and other functionalized systems recognize human bodies while assigning them to absorb danger or solve political inconvenience.

## Counterreading 11 — "Giad's military is humane because service is voluntary"

Narrowed. Initial consent matters, but role conditions, ongoing refusal, medical standing, military hierarchy, conditioned preference, and realistic exit must still be examined.

## Counterreading 12 — "The series is anti-heroic"

Rejected by T14-C15. It admires courage and excellence. It condemns institutions that make everyone else's survival depend on exceptional people remaining permanently available.

## Counterreading 13 — "Mixed political motives make humanitarian action fraudulent"

Rejected. Reputation, strategy, and morality can motivate the same rescue. The relevant questions concern action, burden, truthfulness, accountability, and whether beneficiaries become owned by the debt.

## Counterreading 14 — "Aristocratic noblesse oblige rehabilitates monarchy"

Rejected. Privilege can generate present duty without generating a right to rule. V14 directly contrasts inherited resources spent as debt with bloodline converted into hereditary entitlement [V14-L004, V14-L210].

## Counterreading 15 — "Democracy in the series assumes the wisdom of ordinary people"

Rejected. Its stronger defense is fallibility management: distributed responsibility, records, contestation, bounded offices, and the possibility of correction.

## Counterreading 16 — "Returning to empire is one more form of recoverability"

Rejected by T14-C05. `戻る` is not inherently positive. Political recovery means restoration of judgment and standing; restorationism returns to an older hierarchy [V14-L209].

## Counterreading 17 — "Frederica's throne and Shin's title prove their consent"

Open under T14-C39. The text establishes public positions, not the full political reality behind them.

## Counterreading 18 — "Shin's belief that people can return guarantees political recovery"

Rejected as guarantee by T14-C40. Moral possibility requires institutions capable of realizing it. The final arc remains open.

# XVIII. Final synthesis — the polity that must make correction possible

Giad begins by doing something morally simple and institutionally enormous: it says that the people San Magnolia called livestock and machinery are citizens.

It then tries to make that status material. It gives the Five safety, care, education, money, public records, civilian time, military review, and repeated promises of return. It creates offices in which commanders can be challenged, leaders can be replaced, casualties can be counted, injured people can remain members, and children can be protected even when their bodies are strategically valuable.

The achievement is real.

The achievement is not complete.

Citizenship does not automatically become home. Welfare can become a cage. Education can be ignored. Military professionalism can become hero dependence. Protection can become hostage leverage. Public sympathy can become consumable tragedy. Emergency can turn weakness into debt. A republic founded against empire can preserve aristocratic networks, and a frightened public can ask hereditary rulers to take judgment away again.

The final-arc crisis therefore does not prove freedom was a mistake. It identifies what freedom costs.

People need rights that cannot be revoked for convenience. They need material conditions that make choice usable. They need practice judging and permission to fail without losing membership. They need offices that distribute competence and responsibility. They need information that can re-enter public argument. They need law capable of condemning citizens and state agents without converting accountability into collective erasure. They need support that aims to become less necessary. They need roles that can be left. They need roads home that remain open when return becomes politically inconvenient.

Most of all, they need justice to remain something the polity can fail.

Once a community says `we are justice`, correction becomes betrayal. Once it says ordinary people were never fit for language or will, support becomes permanent rule. Once it says one child, one hero, one unit, or one bloodline must save everyone, institutional weakness is converted into personal destiny.

The strongest political formulation available at the V14 boundary is therefore:

> **A humane state is not one governed by people who never fail. It is one that preserves standing through failure, builds capacity without owning the answer, distributes power without denying excellence, records wrongdoing without making grievance the only future, and keeps enough routes of refusal, revision, accountability, and return open that neither emergency nor protection becomes destiny.**

Giad has built some of those routes. V13 shows them closing. V14 shows citizens and reactionaries competing over whether they will be reopened.

The final arc must decide whether the Federacy can become more than the virtue of its best people: whether it can institutionalize the right to be wrong, corrected, protected, and still remain a citizen.
