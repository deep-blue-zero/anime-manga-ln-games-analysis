# 04 — Spearhead Relationships, Belonging, and the Right to Change

## Scope and authority note

This document is the primary Phase-6 home for **Spearhead as a social world**: Raiden Shuga, Theoto Rikka, Kurena Kukumila, Anju Emma, Frederica Rosenfort where she functions as household/family member, the wider Eighty-Six peer culture that forms around and beyond the original Five, and the ethical problem of whether belonging survives differentiation.

It does **not** treat Spearhead as one homogeneous trauma subject. It also does not use “found family” as a self-validating label. The question is narrower and harder:

> **What kind of belonging is created among people whose first durable community was built inside a system designed to kill them, and can that belonging survive when the conditions that created it no longer remain the same?**

The monograph follows the Phase-5 authority rule. Mainline claims route through locked V01–V14 locators. Alter.1 is used only in its audited supplemental role, primarily for low-pressure birthday, food, gift, teasing, and household evidence that tests whether relationships visible in crisis also exist in ordinary time. No V15+ development is imported.

The central T14 pressure test is `T14-OQ-27`: **how far can Spearhead lives diverge while the group remains a home rather than a role-enforcing identity?** The answer is not yet final at Volume 14. The current corpus, however, already establishes what the mature answer would have to protect.

---

# I. Executive thesis

The original Spearhead group is one of *86*'s strongest examples of **belonging created under atrocity that must later learn not to reproduce atrocity's logic inside itself**.

San Magnolia makes the Eighty-Six disposable by reducing them to one function: fight until death. Spearhead resists that reduction through names, jokes, rituals, art, food, irritation, memory, affection, quarrels, mutual correction, and chosen pride. In Volume 1, this resistance is already morally complete enough to refute the Republic's administrative fiction. The group does not become human because Giad rescues it. Its members are already human, already socially complex, and already capable of ordinary pleasure under the Sector. Theo's rebuke that Lena has not even asked their names is therefore not a request for humanitarian sentiment; it is a demand that recognition become particular rather than abstract [V01-L011].

But survival creates a second problem. An identity built in extremis can become another enclosure if continuing to belong requires everyone to remain the person the war made them.

That is the problem expressed in the progression from V03's `誇りしか` to V08's explicit refusal to say there is **only** pride [V08-L033], and from V09's critique of a group bound by `たった一つの御役目` and `同じ傷` [V09-L052] to the phrase `誇りという名の、傷という名の呪い` [V09-L053]. Shared history is not condemned. Shared pride is not condemned. The danger begins when common injury becomes the membership test and change is experienced as betrayal.

The mature Spearhead thesis is therefore:

> **Spearhead becomes an ethical home insofar as it can keep recognizing a member after the member changes function, changes relationship, becomes disabled, wants something different, fails, leaves, returns, or acquires another home.**

The group's strongest relationships are not the ones with the most dramatic declarations. They are the ones that preserve enough standing for someone to say:

- stop;
- no;
- not like that;
- I cannot do what I used to do;
- I still want this role;
- I do not want the role you think would be healthier for me;
- I love someone else;
- I still love the dead;
- I failed;
- I am afraid;
- I want to return;
- I want to stay elsewhere;
- I need help;
- I do not need you to become me.

This is why T09's model of **interruptible interdependence** is so important here. Spearhead does not mature by becoming independent. Nor does it mature by fusing more completely. It matures when dependence can be acknowledged, redistributed, argued with, and revised without expulsion from the relationship.

That model remains incomplete at V14. Raiden still lacks a clearly specified independent postwar vocation. Theo's adapted military return may remain chosen, but it can also become a new usefulness test. Kurena's competence is widening while her worth remains vulnerable to failure. Anju's future with Dustin must not rewrite Daiya into a defeated obstacle. Frederica's aspiration to become `己一人であっても己の王` faces the return of literal inherited monarchy. And the postwar status of “Eighty-Six” itself remains unresolved.

The group has therefore demonstrated a **right to change**, but has not yet demonstrated the full social world that will exist after the war no longer supplies common work, common danger, and repeated reasons to remain physically together.

# II. Spearhead before rescue: a social world, not a waiting room for civilization

A major interpretive error is to treat the Eighty-Sixth Sector as a pre-social wasteland from which the Five are later rescued into humanity. The text's architecture rejects this from the beginning.

The Republic has destroyed legal citizenship, family continuity, safe childhood, medical standing, political voice, and ordinary civilian expectation. But it has not successfully reduced the Eighty-Six to the “Processors” of its euphemism. Spearhead survives precisely because the group builds an unauthorized social order inside the one assigned to it.

This order has several layers.

## 1. Names as anti-erasure infrastructure

The dead-name practice matters because the Republic's violence is archival as well as lethal. The state does not merely kill; it tries to make the killed disappear from the category of human history. The shock of Lena's failure to ask names [V01-L011] exposes the gap between universal moral sympathy and actual relation.

Later V10 material reveals that carrying names was not a mystical property of Shin. It was a collective practice articulated by older Processors: those who survive carry the names and fragments of those who do not [V10-L003–V10-L004]. The practice can become heavy. It can contribute to Shin's inherited Undertaker office. But at origin it is also **counter-administration**: the group creates a record because the state refuses one.

This distinction matters for Spearhead as family. Their community is not merely emotional closeness. It performs institutional functions the Republic has withdrawn:

- remembrance;
- burial substitute;
- moral witness;
- practical care;
- informal education;
- protection of names;
- continuity between annihilated units;
- transfer of objects, stories, and obligations.

Found family here is partly a response to the destruction of ordinary family and civil institutions. That gives it enormous moral weight, but also means later synthesis must ask which functions should remain intimate and which should be transferred to actual public institutions once those become available.

## 2. Ordinary life exists inside war

The group also already possesses ordinary culture. The lightness can be easy to overlook because the surrounding conditions are genocidal, but that makes it more important rather than less.

They cook. They repair things. They drink bad coffee. They tease one another. Theo draws. Kurena sews and shops when given the opportunity. Anju cooks. Raiden mends and manages mundane problems. Shin reads. Fido participates in recurring routines. Alter.1's low-pressure evidence strengthens this baseline: birthday forgetting and birthday reconstruction, peer teasing, gifts, cooking failures, drinks, braiding, and the gradual adoption of rituals simply because they are enjoyable are all present outside climactic rescue scenes.

Volume 2's civilian exploration is especially valuable because it does not reveal new capacities created by rescue. It reveals capacities that finally have room to recur: Raiden works as a courier; Anju takes cooking lessons; Theo sketches; Kurena window-shops; Shin visits libraries and museums. The ordinary-life matrix locks this as the core low-pressure control for the Five. Rescue expands opportunity; it does not create personhood.

This distinction later becomes politically decisive. If the Five already possess ordinary preference under oppression, then post-rescue policy cannot legitimately define recovery as turning them into some adult's preferred image of “normal children.” Ernst's benevolence becomes a cage when it assumes that safety authorizes him to decide what a healed future must look like.

## 3. Pride is survival language before it becomes an enclosure risk

T14 explicitly rejects the claim that Eighty-Six pride is a latent pathology gradually outgrown (`T14-C07`). In the Sector, pride is one of the few remaining domains of self-definition. It says that the Republic may control food, territory, legal category, weapons, and life expectancy, but it does not get the final word on what kind of person the condemned soldier will be.

Later criticism therefore turns on exclusivity, not pride itself. V08's correction is exact: `誇りしかないとはもう言わない` [V08-L033]. The word `しか` is the pressure point. V14 can still let Theo identify himself proudly as one of the Eighty-Six [V14-L023] because mature plurality does not require repudiating the identity that once preserved dignity.

The ethical problem is instead whether the identity becomes a demand: if you stop fighting, fall in love, join another institution, become disabled, want a civilian career, recover differently, or reject a common grievance, do you cease to count as one of us?

That is the problem the later group must solve.

# III. From “same wound” to a home that permits difference

The series provides a negative model of trauma solidarity in Volume 9. Gilwiese's group is bound by one role and one wound: `たった一つの御役目` and `同じ傷` [V09-L052]. The bond is real. So is the mutual care. The failure lies in what the bond requires of change.

When one person's movement away from the shared role feels like desertion of the relationship itself, solidarity becomes a `奴隷の首輪` [V09-L051]. The narrative's own formulation, `誇りという名の、傷という名の呪い` [V09-L053], is not an argument that shared trauma invalidates community. It is an argument that community becomes curse-like when **the wound becomes jurisdiction**.

Spearhead increasingly differentiates itself from this model.

The Five do not recover at the same rate. They do not want the same things. Their military relationship to war is unequal. Their romantic lives diverge. Their bodily futures diverge. Their memory obligations diverge. Their relation to Republic people diverges. Their capacity to imagine postwar life diverges.

The key evidence is not that these differences disappear. It is that the group repeatedly survives them.

- Raiden can imagine a civilian life earlier than Shin [V02-L040].
- Theo's artistic identity remains active before disability and after it.
- Kurena's attachment to Shin becomes explicitly nonreciprocal without removing her from the group [V09-L061–V09-L064].
- Anju can continue loving Daiya while becoming attached to Dustin [V05-L019; V09-L025].
- Theo can cease being capable of his former Processor role without ceasing to belong [V08-L056; V09-L011].
- Shin can be absent and the unit can continue through distributed roles rather than treating his absence as organizational nonexistence [V08-L042–V08-L044].
- Raiden can later lead without being required to become a substitute Shin.
- Frederica can remain a child and family member even while her imperial ancestry has political consequences.

This leads to a precise definition of the “right to change” in this corpus:

> **The right to change is not a requirement to abandon durable identity. It is the right not to lose belonging merely because the meaning, function, intensity, or outward form of that identity changes.**

Stable bonds remain legitimate. Stable pride remains legitimate. Long military vocation may remain legitimate. Lifelong memory of the dead remains legitimate. What becomes dangerous is a relationship in which continued recognition is conditional on staying recognizable in only one old form.

# IV. Everyday culture: why jokes, food, art, and irritation matter

The strongest evidence that Spearhead is more than a combat compact often appears in scenes too small to carry a conventional plot summary.

This is methodologically important. Crisis evidence shows who will die for whom. Ordinary evidence shows whether they know how to **live around one another**.

## 1. Raiden as producer of mundane stability

Raiden is often summarized as the “normal one,” but Alter.1 and the cumulative ledgers make that shorthand inadequate. He does not merely possess more common sense than Shin. He actively produces repeatable ordinary structure: mending, cooking competence, practical correction, dry humor, emotional regulation, and small-scale continuity.

That labor is easy to naturalize because he performs it well. V14 reveals the cost of that naturalization. Once he must act as the group's de facto head, he recognizes that even his apparent stability depended on somebody else being there to stop him. He had been able to indulge anger because he trusted Shin to draw a boundary [V14-L081–V14-L083].

This reframes years of “Raiden stabilizes Shin.” Stability was always reciprocal infrastructure, even where it was asymmetrical in appearance.

## 2. Theo as artist and social critic

Theo's art is important precisely because it predates his amputation. Drawing is not a replacement hobby graciously supplied after combat loss. It is one of the threads proving that the person always exceeded the military role.

The same applies to his criticism. His Volume 1 anger can be cruel, but it also identifies failures of abstract sympathy. Later he becomes one of the group's best practitioners of boundary-respecting care: with Shin's sensory loss, he accepts a request to stop turning meals into tests and instead offers accommodation—eating together and describing taste in advance. Care changes method when the cared-for person says the method itself is intrusive.

That is one of the most mature everyday examples in the corpus because nobody is saving a country. The ethical achievement is simply knowing how to sit at a meal with a disabled friend without converting the meal into surveillance.

## 3. Kurena as more than “the girl who loves Shin”

Kurena's romantic attachment is important, but the ordinary record prevents it from exhausting her. She shops, sews, jokes, takes part in birthdays, develops command competence, and becomes capable of functioning inside increasingly complex operations.

The problem is therefore not that she needs to stop loving Shin. T14 rejects that purification model. The problem is whether she can stop treating usefulness and proximity as conditions of worth.

Her later confession is ethically significant because it lets love remain after entitlement is relinquished. `今でも好き。……きっとずっと、好きなまま` [V09-L061] is followed not by a demand but by a wish that he be happy [V09-L062]. Shin's apology [V09-L063] and Kurena's refusal to treat loving him as a moral mistake [V09-L064] create a relation in which asymmetry does not require annihilating the attachment.

That is a form of differentiation the group survives.

## 4. Anju as tenderness that does not erase anger

Anju's early presentation is socially gentle, but V2 already shows that domestic competence and grief coexist. Her later scar treatment, relationship with Dustin, battlefield fear, and V13 anger make it impossible to reduce her to the unit's soft caretaker.

Her tenderness is not passive essence. It is something she maintains under pressure and occasionally has to recover after rage threatens to reorganize her. That matters for the group's culture: “the kind one” must be allowed to be angry without everyone treating anger as identity failure.

## 5. Culture as anti-monster propaganda in V14

V14 makes ordinary culture explicitly political. Recipes, jokes, favorite foods, incompetence at ordinary tasks, old songs, and shared memories become evidence against the public construction of the Eighty-Six as monsters [V14-L113–V14-L118].

This is not a sentimental side point. It is a continuation of the original name archive by different means.

The Republic erased personhood through categories. The later reactionary public again tries to make “Eighty-Six” a totalizing category. The answer is not simply to insist that the category is false. Many characters still claim Eighty-Six identity proudly. The answer is to show the category's internal plurality:

> they laugh, cook, fail at things, like different foods, remember old media, want different people, and live ordinary particular lives.

The best defense of the group is therefore not homogenizing hero worship. It is **particularization**.

# V. Raiden Shuga — stabilizer, vice-captain, anger, and the problem of having a future of his own

Raiden is the clearest character through whom *86* interrogates the hidden labor of the dependable person.

He is competent, practical, socially legible, and often more capable than Shin of imagining ordinary continuation. In Volume 2, when Shin cannot project a postwar future, Raiden can imagine work, food, and living with much less existential disruption [V02-L040]. That difference is crucial. The Five are never a single trauma template.

## 1. Care as legitimate interruption

By Volume 4, Raiden can invoke not only friendship but office: as vice-captain, he has both the right and responsibility to stop an impaired commander [V04-L025]. Shin can then delegate command [V04-L026].

This is a foundational Spearhead principle. Their closeness does not make hierarchy disappear. Instead, relationship and organization overlap in a way that allows temporary transfer without metaphysical crisis. Shin does not cease to be Shin because Raiden commands. Raiden does not become Shin by accepting delegated authority.

The V13 version is even clearer. Raiden physically interrupts Shin's exclusionary moral spiral; Lena then asks Shin for his own judgment. Shin's answer is gratitude: `……ライデン、悪い。助かった` [V13-L085]. Being stopped counts as being helped because interruption returns the person to judgment rather than permanently replacing it.

## 2. The V14 reversal: the stabilizer needs a stabilizer

V14 destroys the easy binary in which Raiden is “healthy” and Shin is “dependent.” With Shin absent from his usual role, Raiden recognizes that his own ability to express rage safely had depended on trusting Shin to stop him [V14-L081]. As de facto head of the Processors, his anger now has institutional consequences [V14-L082–V14-L083].

This is a profound relational correction:

> **A stabilizer is not the person who has no need. A stabilizer is a person whose own needs may be hidden because the surrounding relationship has made them manageable.**

Theo's response completes the correction: Raiden does not need to become Shin in order to cover Shin's absence. Leadership can be distributed without identity substitution.

## 3. Raiden's anger is not a defect to be removed

Frederica's V14 intervention is important because she does not tell him simply to stop being angry. She treats anger as communication—evidence that something has been done to a person that deserves to be named. The problem is whether anger becomes group policy or total identity.

This distinction parallels the series' treatment of pride. Anger can be morally intelligible and socially necessary without being sovereign.

## 4. The open future

T14 therefore preserves `T14-OQ-21`: **what does Raiden positively want beyond stabilizing, commanding, and returning other people to balance?**

The corpus offers a small but important answer-direction in V14. The actual locked dandelion-coffee anchor is V14-L183, not the earlier pre-lock shorthand sometimes used in the cumulative ledger. Raiden imagines asking the Alba woman who once sheltered him about the past after the operation, over Spearhead's traditional dandelion-root coffee [V14-L183].

This matters because it is not a Shin-management task. It is Raiden generating his own return route to a person from his own history.

It is not enough to close the open question. But it proves the character possesses more future grammar than “vice-captain forever.”

# VI. Theoto Rikka — criticism, art, disability, and belonging after function

Theo is the decisive Spearhead case for separating **belonging from function**.

His Volume 1 significance begins in language. The demand for names exposes Lena's abstract relation to the unit [V01-L011]. Theo's criticism is not always fair in tone, but it refuses a form of benevolence that loves “the oppressed” while failing to know actual people.

That critical disposition remains useful throughout the series because he repeatedly notices when a role is swallowing a person.

## 1. Art existed before the injury

The ordinary evidence matters first. Theo sketches during the V2 civilian period, and Alter.1 preserves art and play from earlier windows. This prevents the common disability narrative in which a person “discovers creativity” only after losing a bodily function.

Art is not compensation. It is continuity.

## 2. V08: involuntary divergence

Theo's amputation is not liberation. His statement `僕はもう、──一緒には戦えないや` [V08-L056] is devastating precisely because the old form of togetherness really has been lost.

A weaker analysis would rush to say the injury “frees” him from the battlefield. The text does not. Theo initially does not want to stop being a Processor; later he explicitly says `プロセッサー、やめたくない` [V09-L026].

The right to change includes the right **not** to accept somebody else's preferred interpretation of one's change.

## 3. V09: the group survives a member who can no longer do the same thing

The institutional sequence around Theo is one of the series' strongest moral contrasts with the Republic:

1. save the person;
2. stabilize the body;
3. rehabilitate;
4. discuss prosthetic/adaptive possibilities;
5. allow future role choice.

`僕はもう、戦えない` and `でも、死ななくていい` [V09-L011] separate function from standing. The group does not need Theo to supply a replacement usefulness before allowing him to remain one of them.

His own later wish—`まだ死んでないから、何かをしたい` in the V09 sequence—begins from open survival rather than from a preselected heroic substitute.

## 4. Theo revises inherited memory rather than discarding it

V08's Laughing Fox material also shows how relationship to the dead can change without becoming betrayal. The former commander's injunction becomes a demand to return [V08-L050], find or make a place [V08-L051], and become happy [V08-L052]. Theo even names the risk that `幸福にならねばならない` could itself become a new curse [V08-L053].

That reflexivity is important. Even a life-affirming inheritance can become coercive if it tells the survivor exactly what happiness must look like.

## 5. V14: adaptation without restoration

Theo returns to useful military work through an adapted platform. The narrative does not pretend his old body or old Reginleif role has returned. This is accommodation, not cure.

His V14 care for Shin similarly reflects changed ethics. When Shin asks him not to keep testing taste, Theo stops. Concern does not disappear; the method changes. He offers practical information and companionship rather than constant checking.

Theo's interaction with Miel goes further. He understands the desire to keep a younger person away from danger, but refuses to reproduce the child-soldier logic used on his own generation [V14-L033–V14-L036]. Being able to understand why coercive protection feels tempting is not the same as endorsing it.

## 6. The open question: usefulness after usefulness

T14 preserves `T14-OQ-22`: **can Theo continue military/technical service without adaptation becoming a new duty to prove usefulness?**

This is necessary because his successful adaptation can itself create a bad retrospective reading: perhaps disability “did not really cost him much” because he found another platform. The source resists that. Real capabilities were lost. Real grief occurred. His standing survived before new function stabilized.

The final ethical test is therefore not whether Theo remains productive. It is whether he would remain fully one of the group if he chose—or were forced—to stop producing in militarily valued ways.

# VII. Kurena Kukumila — love without entitlement, competence without omnipotence

Kurena's arc is often flattened into unrequited love. The V2 corpus strongly rejects that simplification.

Her deeper problem is **conditional worth**: the fear that if she cannot protect, help, or remain useful, she becomes unnecessary.

## 1. Early attachment and the caretaker asymmetry

Kurena is one of the first people to identify Shin's structural problem. Her question about who carries Shin's heart when he carries everyone else already recognizes that the unit's caretaker arrangement is unstable.

Yet her own attachment becomes overconcentrated. By V8, Shin's disappearance threatens not only grief but Kurena's sense of place: `おいていかないで` [V08-L041]. The problem is not that love is morally wrong. It is that proximity to one person has become too close to a proof of belonging.

## 2. Usefulness is the real wound

V09 makes the structure explicit: `役に立てないのは嫌だ。無力なのは怖い` [V09-L029]. This is not merely romantic insecurity. It links childhood powerlessness, military competence, and fear of abandonment.

Shin's response to failure is therefore crucial. A commander who discards someone after one mistake would reproduce the logic that makes worth conditional on flawless performance. Instead the relation preserves return and retrial [V09-L031–V09-L033].

## 3. Love can persist after romantic nonselection

Kurena's confession in V09 is one of the corpus's strongest relationship controls because the story does not require her to “get over” Shin in order to become mature. She can continue to love him while acknowledging that the desired romantic place is not hers [V09-L061–V09-L064].

The key change is entitlement, not feeling.

This matters to the broader “right to change” thesis. A relationship can change category without becoming false. The earlier love is not invalidated by a later nonromantic form. Nor must the group erase Kurena's old desire to protect the main romance from discomfort.

## 4. Competence grows, but omnipotence does not

By V13 and V14, Kurena has meaningful independent military competence. Yet Rito's death triggers the old equation between strength and worth. She has worked to become stronger; somebody still dies. Theo's response is not that she failed to grow. He names the loneliness and pain instead [V14-L029–V14-L032].

That is a major ethical correction:

> **Growth is not the acquisition of enough power to guarantee that nobody important dies.**

Kurena's competence matters. It should not be transformed into omnipotence as the condition of self-respect.

## 5. The open question

T14 therefore preserves `T14-OQ-23`: **can Kurena build durable self-worth not organized around protecting or being useful to loved people?**

The evidence through V14 supports a directional answer only. She has more skill, more honest love, more peer connection, and more independent participation than the early stereotype suggests. But the corpus does not yet provide a sufficiently durable nonmilitary positive project to close the question.

The right to change must include the possibility that one day Kurena could be loved and belong even when nobody needs her rifle.

# VIII. Anju Emma — tenderness, scars, Daiya, Dustin, and additive love

Anju is the character most vulnerable to being written badly by retrospective synthesis because her gentleness and grief can be used to turn her into a static emblem of endurance.

The V2 record is much more complex.

## 1. Gentleness is not the absence of violence or anger

Anju's early social role includes emotional stabilization, cooking, and quiet competence. But the scars on her body preserve the history of intra-Colorata prejudice, and later volumes show that she can become furious enough for revenge to threaten her preferred ethical identity.

V13 is important because the crisis does not reveal that earlier kindness was fake. It reveals that kindness is maintained under pressure rather than guaranteed by temperament. She can feel retaliatory rage and still return to a different course.

V14 likewise refuses the “fearless gentle soldier” archetype. Grethe praises continuing while afraid, not the absence of fear. Anju's terrified smile under the Vaneseed field is therefore evidence of courage without emotional invulnerability.

## 2. Daiya is not a hurdle to be defeated

T14 explicitly preserves `T14-OQ-24`: **can future commitment coexist with Daiya's memory without writing him as a hurdle that Dustin helps Anju overcome?**

The corpus already gives the answer-direction in V05: Dustin's argument is that memory does not have to be deleted for new love to become possible [V05-L019]. This is one of the series' cleanest additive-memory formulations.

Anju's later development keeps this distinction. The new relationship is not morally validated by proving that Daiya matters less. The past love remains true; the new attachment becomes true as well.

This is a direct challenge to a zero-sum model of belonging. A person can acquire a new intimate future without betraying the dead.

## 3. Return rather than salvation

The mature Anju/Dustin relationship is built less around being saved than around **return**.

V09's promise is reciprocal: Anju will return to the place where Dustin is [V09-L025]. V13 sharpens the structure. Dustin returns because he hears the wish that he come back [V13-L101–V13-L102]. He finally realizes Anju did not ask him to save everyone or martyr himself to justice; she asked something far more bounded [V13-L103].

That is why Dustin's Chitri failure matters to this monograph even though he is not an original Spearhead member. His rescue impulse can become helper-centered. Anju's relationship with him becomes healthier when love narrows the moral fantasy from “save everyone” to “do not erase yourself in the attempt.”

## 4. Anju belongs outside romance too

The future synthesis must still resist reducing Anju to the Daiya/Dustin axis. She is a soldier, friend, body-bearing survivor, cook, peer, participant in unit humor, and somebody whose fear and anger have independent moral relevance.

The final-arc question remains open because war still supplies much of her visible action. But the evidence is sufficient to reject the notion that her development is simply grief recovery through replacement romance.

# IX. Frederica Rosenfort — child, adopted daughter, former empress, and family member under political inheritance

Frederica is not one of the original Spearhead Five, but she becomes part of the household structure that tests what Spearhead-like belonging means when a member arrives with radically different status and history.

She is simultaneously:

- a child;
- a former empress;
- an adopted daughter in Ernst's household;
- a strategic sensory asset;
- a bereaved person bound to Kiriya;
- a moral observer who can also be wrong;
- a younger companion to the Eighty-Six;
- and by V14, a body around which inherited monarchy again becomes politically active.

The group is therefore forced to recognize several versions of her at once.

## 1. Against sacrificial arithmetic

Frederica repeatedly becomes a test of whether protecting a group justifies offering the “smaller” person to danger. Shin's refusal of such arithmetic matters because the Eighty-Six themselves were once the minority whose sacrifice was rationalized as necessary.

Belonging to the household should therefore reduce rather than increase the ease with which her body can be converted into a strategic resource.

## 2. Self-rule is not radical independence

V12's `己一人であっても己の王` is frequently vulnerable to mistranslation at the level of interpretation. It does not mean nobody should matter to Frederica. Her whole character is relational. It means no other person should permanently own her judgment.

The V13 Ernst scene demonstrates this perfectly. Frederica's family relation gives her standing to say `もうやめて。──おとうさん` [V13-L093]. The bond constrains an adult's violence rather than licensing violence on the child's behalf. Ernst stops because being father is a reason not to become someone who destroys the children he claims to love [V13-L094–V13-L095].

Family is functioning here as **moral interruption**, not jurisdiction.

## 3. Child status remains load-bearing

T14-C21 rejects the claim that combat competence establishes adult status. Frederica's political intelligence, sensory ability, imperial history, and exposure to war do not erase her need for adult protection or justify treating her as a miniature sovereign whose every self-sacrificial impulse must be honored.

This is also why V14's affectionate Raiden/Frederica material must retain age and comic framing. Relationship intensity does not eliminate developmental asymmetry.

## 4. The final-arc contradiction

T14-OQ-25 remains open: **what does being her own king mean once inherited monarchy becomes politically active around her body?**

The series has spent many volumes transforming inherited title into self-government language. V14 now threatens to reverse the direction: the old title may again become an external institution that claims her.

This makes Frederica one of Document 04's most important final-arc tests. Can a family and polity love the same person without converting her ancestry into compulsory office?

# X. The wider Eighty-Six: Spearhead must not become the whole category

The architecture explicitly warns against treating Spearhead as synonymous with “the Eighty-Six.” Later characters and units continually widen the social field.

Shiden is a crucial example. She is an Eighty-Six commander with her own relationship to Lena, grief, tactical judgment, contempt for the Republic, curiosity, and postwar desire. By V09 she can say she is tired of war and wants an easier, enjoyable life [V09-L058]. V12 then gives her an ordinary learning sequence: ignorance produces curiosity rather than shame, and study remains valuable even when she later decides the topic may not suit her [V12-L030–V12-L033].

This matters because Shiden's life proves that Eighty-Six identity is not a single Spearhead personality distributed across more bodies.

Rito is equally important. He belongs to a younger cohort and preserves fear in a form the original Five often learned to hide. His V11 nonforgiveness is explicit: he may never forgive [V11-L013], but he can still let go of revenge [V11-L014]. He claims the Aldrecht confrontation as his own responsibility [V11-L024], and Shin answers `任せる` [V11-L025]. Undertaker does not monopolize every dead person's memory.

That transfer is a major development in group ethics. Memory becomes distributable rather than centered in one exceptional carrier.

V13 then makes Rito a catastrophic test. He refuses to let another Eighty-Six become a killer for revenge, performs a requested mercy killing, and is himself killed by a Federacy soldier who misreads the scene. The later group must decide whether grief will make them reproduce the same categorical generalization Rito resisted.

V14's answer is angry but non-totalizing. The murderer should face legal judgment; the state should record the truth; the public should be forced to confront its error [V14-L047–V14-L051]. The response refuses both forgiveness-as-obligation and collective revenge.

This is the mature form of belonging Rito helps make possible: **the group can demand justice for its own without turning every outsider into the category of enemy**.

Yuto and Chitri provide another widening. Their V13 relation proves that chosen obligation can be constraining without being illegitimate. Chitri asks to become a `curse`; Yuto knowingly accepts the ribbon and obligation [V13-L096–V13-L098]. The scene is a direct counterexample to the idea that healthy freedom means weak attachments. The question is authorship and revisability, not absence of binding.

The wider Eighty-Six therefore keep Spearhead honest. They prevent the original Five from becoming the only model of what survival is supposed to look like.

# XI. Living with the dead without making the dead govern the living

Spearhead begins as a community among the living organized partly around care for the dead. That makes memory one of its strongest bonds and one of its greatest enclosure risks.

## 1. Memory begins as anti-erasure

The names and fragments carried from dead Processors are a direct refusal of the Republic's attempt to make death administratively anonymous [V10-L003–V10-L007]. This memory is morally necessary.

## 2. Memory does not require imitation

Later volumes repeatedly detach remembrance from reenactment.

Theo can reinterpret Laughing Fox's final wish toward life rather than death [V08-L050–V08-L053]. Anju can remember Daiya while considering another love [V05-L019]. Shin can give Aldrecht's final confrontation to Rito [V11-L024–V11-L025]. Yuto can honor Chitri's request about how she is remembered [V13-L096–V13-L098].

T14-C33 therefore narrows the claim that preserving memory is inherently healthy. Counter-erasure can itself become enclosure if memory demands one future, one grievance, or one imitation.

## 3. The dead can generate future desire

V14's Lyudmila/Rito material is especially important because memory of the dead creates a new reason not to disappear. The dead relation generates an emergent future end rather than freezing the survivor in memorial stasis.

This is a deep continuation of Spearhead's name practice. To carry a dead person does not have to mean carrying only their final moment. A living archive can continue producing new relationships, questions, and obligations.

The moral criterion is whether memory enlarges the field of relation or closes it.

# XII. Belonging, citizenship, household, and home are not the same thing

Document 04 must inherit T04's lexical and conceptual caution. “Home” is not one stable category in *86*.

The Eighty-Six can have:

- a birthplace they no longer wish to live in;
- a lost `故郷` remembered differently by different people;
- legal citizenship in Giad;
- a military unit experienced as home;
- a household;
- a person who waits;
- a place they want to return to;
- a family of blood with whom relation is newly reopened;
- a grave or memorial destination;
- and future homes not yet chosen.

T14-C31 overturns the binary between “chosen home” and “inherited home.” Origin, blood, citizenship, household, memory, and chosen destination can coexist.

This is crucial for Spearhead because “found family” can otherwise become another purification story: the authentic chosen family replaces all compromised institutions and inherited relations. The text does not support that.

Shin can reconnect with grandparents without making them his exclusive home. Raiden can imagine returning to the Alba woman who once sheltered him. Theo can remain Spearhead while occupying a changed branch or function. Anju can preserve Daiya and choose Dustin. Frederica can be Ernst's daughter while remaining historically tied to imperial ancestry. Shiden can be Lena's comrade without becoming original Spearhead.

The mature model is **additive belonging**.

But additive does not mean frictionless. Multiple homes can make competing claims. V14's political restoration is precisely dangerous because inherited political belonging may try to become exclusive again.

The right to change therefore requires not only warm relationships but **exit, return, and multiple recognized standing**.

---

# XIII. Military vocation: chosen, conditioned, and still real

One of the hardest problems for this monograph is the Five's repeated return to military service.

Two symmetrical readings must be rejected.

The first says:

> They reenlist, therefore they never recovered and remain trapped in trauma.

T14-C17 rejects this. Civilianization is not the normative endpoint. A person can genuinely choose military vocation.

The opposite says:

> They reenlist, therefore the choice is wholly free and proves the war has no continuing formative claim.

T14-C16 narrows this. The Five's histories matter. Many learned competence, social belonging, status, and self-definition almost entirely inside military life. Wartime necessity also remains real through V14.

The better formulation is:

> **Their military choices are real choices made by historically conditioned people inside an ongoing war.**

That is not a contradiction. No important human choice emerges from nowhere.

What matters is whether the military role remains revisable without loss of personhood and belonging.

Theo is the strongest test because involuntary disability interrupts the old route. The group passes when his membership survives before a new role is found. Kurena is a different test because combat competence has become entangled with worth. Raiden is another because leadership may become the role everyone assumes he naturally wants. Anju's fear and anger test whether competence must look emotionally invulnerable. Shiden's desire for an easy life tests whether saying “I am tired of war” is compatible with Eighty-Six dignity [V09-L058].

The unresolved `T14-OQ-28` therefore remains essential: **how much of continued soldierhood after rescue is chosen vocation, gravitational habit, conditioned identity, or practical necessity?**

Document 04 cannot resolve that ahead of the source. It can state the criterion future material must satisfy: if somebody leaves the military, the group must still know how to call that person one of them.

# XIV. Conflict inside the family: why disagreement is evidence for belonging

The most sentimental reading of Spearhead imagines a group so bonded by suffering that deep conflict has been transcended. The actual text is healthier and rougher.

They criticize each other. They become jealous. They misunderstand. They project. They get angry. They tell each other to stop. They make unfair judgments. They sometimes want incompatible things.

This matters because the absence of conflict would make belonging indistinguishable from conformity.

T09's strongest relational principle is that **capacity to disagree and interrupt may be more diagnostic than emotional intensity**.

Raiden stopping Shin is care [V13-L083–V13-L085]. Theo telling Raiden not to become Shin is care. Kurena telling Lena not to decide other people's futures is care. Frederica stopping Ernst is family love acting as restraint [V13-L093–V13-L095]. Theo refusing Kurena's omnipotent self-blame is care [V14-L029–V14-L032]. Annette can fear Theo's return to battle without claiming authority to veto it [V14-L027–V14-L028].

These relationships become trustworthy not because everyone predicts what the other will do, but because disagreement does not automatically terminate recognition.

That is the opposite of the V09 “same wound” curse, where change threatens the bond itself.

The mature found family is therefore not the family in which everyone agrees. It is the family in which **correction can occur without exile**.

# XV. Japanese-language controls: the relationship argument is visible in the source lexicon

Several translation-sensitive distinctions should remain visible in later synthesis.

## 1. `誇り` and `呪い`

`誇り` is not coded as intrinsically healthy or unhealthy. V08's `誇りしか` distinction is decisive. Likewise `呪い` can describe imposed enclosure, but V13 deliberately lets Chitri ask to become Yuto's chosen `curse`. The text is testing authorship and future authority, not assigning fixed moral values to nouns.

## 2. `支える` versus `従う`

T09 and V12 make the difference explicit at the political/relational level: the Eighty-Six support rather than merely obey. In Spearhead, the same distinction appears behaviorally. Raiden can take command without becoming a passive executor of Shin's will. Theo can care without surveilling. Peers can stop a commander and then return judgment.

## 3. `任せる`

`任せる` matters because delegation is one of the ways the corpus separates trust from fusion. Shin can leave command to Raiden [V04-L026]. He can leave Aldrecht's confrontation to Rito [V11-L025]. A relationship grows when a person can say, in effect, “this is yours to carry,” rather than claiming every responsibility because one is strongest or most wounded.

## 4. `帰る` and `戻る`

T12 forbids collapsing all “return” language into one metaphor. `帰る` tends toward destination/recipient/homeward movement; `戻る` can describe re-entry, restoration, or regression. Spearhead's home language therefore cannot be summarized as “return to who you were.”

Theo cannot return to his old body. Anju cannot return to a pre-Daiya self. Shin cannot return to a childhood that genocide interrupted. Kurena's romantic relation cannot return to a hypothetical future in which Shin chooses her. Yet all can still have meaningful places and people to return **to**.

This is one reason T14's negative control of reactionary political `戻る` matters. Restoring an older state is not automatically homecoming.

## 5. `一緒に` and `共に`

Togetherness also has registers. Ordinary togetherness, battlefield coordination, vows, and future projection need not all mean fusion. The strongest Spearhead model is not “always together” but “able to remain in relation across different positions.”

# XVI. Counterreadings and adversarial controls

## Counterreading 1 — “Spearhead is healthy because it is chosen family”

**Too simple.**

The group is deeply chosen, but choice at one moment does not immunize a relationship against later enclosure. V09 shows chosen/shared solidarity becoming a demand for sameness. A found family can become coercive if membership depends on performing the founding wound forever.

## Counterreading 2 — “Their pride is trauma pathology they must outgrow”

**Rejected by T14-C07.**

Pride preserves dignity under administrative dehumanization. The mature correction is against `誇りしか`, not `誇り` itself [V08-L033; V14-L023].

## Counterreading 3 — “Healthy identity means maximizing the number of identities”

**Narrowed by T14-C08.**

Plurality protects against enclosure, but the series also values continuity, promises, long relationships, coherent vocation, and inherited names. The ethical test is not quantity of roles. It is whether one role claims exhaustive authority.

## Counterreading 4 — “Reenlistment proves the Five freely prefer soldierhood”

**Open/narrowed by T14-C16.**

Civilian capacity is real. So are war conditioning, social history, practical necessity, competence, and habit. The choice is meaningful without being ahistorical.

## Counterreading 5 — “Remaining soldiers proves they failed to recover”

**Rejected by T14-C17.**

The work does not make civilianization the universal therapeutic endpoint. Military vocation can remain chosen if it is not the only socially recognized self.

## Counterreading 6 — “Theo's disability liberates him from war”

**Rejected by T14-C18.**

The amputation removes real options and produces real grief. Later adaptation is neither restoration nor proof that injury was beneficial. Belonging survives first; function is rebuilt later.

## Counterreading 7 — “Combat maturity makes them adults”

**Rejected by T14-C21.**

The series repeatedly shows asymmetrical development. People can make life-and-death decisions while lacking ordinary developmental experience. Adult duties of protection remain.

## Counterreading 8 — “Chosen home replaces compromised inherited home”

**Overturned as a binary by T14-C31.**

Home is plural: origin, citizenship, household, blood relation, memory, military community, and chosen destination can coexist.

## Counterreading 9 — “Remembering the dead is always healthy”

**Narrowed by T14-C33.**

Memory resists erasure, but can become grievance purpose, imitation demand, or role monopoly. The dead must be remembered as persons, not converted into permanent commands.

## Counterreading 10 — “Peace will naturally solve group identity”

**Open/method caution under T14-C41.**

The postwar social world has not yet been shown. Peace could widen choice, but it could also become a new fixed order. The group may discover that war had been supplying repeated reasons to stay together.

# XVII. Final-arc open-question registry for Document 04

The following questions remain **OPEN_FINAL_ARC** and must not be resolved without new primary-source evidence.

### `T14-OQ-21` — Raiden's own future

What does Raiden want beyond stabilizing, commanding, and returning others? V14-L183 supplies an independent return-route seed, but not a complete vocation.

### `T14-OQ-22` — Theo after disability

Can Theo continue military/technical work without adapted usefulness becoming a new duty to justify his standing?

### `T14-OQ-23` — Kurena's independent worth

Can Kurena build durable self-worth not organized around protecting or being useful to loved people?

### `T14-OQ-24` — Anju and Dustin

Can a future commitment coexist with Daiya's memory without narratively demoting Daiya into a trauma hurdle?

### `T14-OQ-25` — Frederica's self-rule

What does being her own king mean once inherited monarchy becomes politically active around her body?

### `T14-OQ-27` — group divergence

How far can Spearhead lives diverge while the group remains a home rather than a role-enforcing identity?

### `T14-OQ-28` — military vocation

How much of continued soldierhood is vocation, habit, conditioning, wartime necessity, or some mixture?

### `T14-OQ-32` — Eighty-Six identity after war

Does “Eighty-Six” remain a chosen durable identity across citizenship, locality, family, and generational change?

These are not minor loose ends. Together they define the unresolved social theory of the series at the V14 boundary.

# XVIII. Final synthesis — home is the relationship that survives a different future

The original Spearhead Squadron begins in one of the most coercively homogenizing institutions imaginable. The Republic gives its members one category, one battlefield function, one disposable future, and one official death.

They resist by making difference legible.

They use names where the state uses numbers. They use art where the state uses propaganda. They use jokes where the state uses fear. They use food and bad coffee where the state gives minimum subsistence. They remember the dead where the state erases them. They criticize each other where the state pretends there are no human soldiers to command. They carry objects, stories, habits, and insults across annihilated units.

That resistance creates pride and family.

But *86* refuses to stop at the emotionally satisfying claim that trauma made an unbreakable family. The later volumes ask the more mature question: **what happens when survival makes people break differently from the group shape that once saved them?**

Theo loses the body that allowed old togetherness.

Kurena remains in love with someone who does not choose her.

Anju acquires a new intimate future without ceasing to love the dead.

Raiden becomes responsible for others and discovers his own stability depended on them.

Frederica's inherited status threatens to become political office again.

Rito develops an ethical authority distinct from Shin and dies defending a principle the group must decide whether to preserve.

Shiden demonstrates that other Eighty-Six can want different social and postwar lives.

Yuto and Chitri show that freely accepted obligations can remain binding without making freedom meaningless.

The resulting social ethic is neither radical independence nor permanent fusion.

It is this:

> **Belonging is ethically durable when the relationship can survive the loss of the function that first made the person useful, the wound that first made the person legible, the role that first made the person admirable, or the sameness that first made the group feel safe.**

This is why Theo's amputation is a more important found-family test than any declaration that the Five will always be together. The group has to know him when he cannot fight as before.

This is why Kurena's unreturned love matters. The group has to know her after the future she wanted does not happen.

This is why Anju's later attachment matters. The group has to let love accumulate rather than demand that memory remain exclusive.

This is why Raiden's V14 crisis matters. The person who has always helped hold the group together must be allowed to need holding.

This is why Frederica matters. A family cannot prove its love by assigning a child the historical role everyone else needs her to perform.

And this is why `T14-OQ-27` must remain open. The series has shown Spearhead surviving enormous differentiation **during war**. It has not yet shown whether the group can survive the more ordinary divergence of peace: different jobs, cities, partners, bodies, citizenship practices, memories, and schedules; long periods in which nobody urgently needs to return to the same battlefield.

The strongest V01–V14 answer is therefore provisional but substantial:

> **Spearhead is not home because its members remain the same people forever. It becomes home because, increasingly, they do not have to.**

That is the right to change.

And the final test of that right will be whether each person can one day say “this is who I am now” without hearing, from the people who love them most, that becoming someone different means they have left the family behind.
