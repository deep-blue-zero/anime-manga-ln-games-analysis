# 05 — San Magnolia: Racism, Genocide, Complicity, and Memory

## Scope and authority note

This document is the primary Phase-6 home for the Republic of San Magnolia's racial order and its afterlife: racial classification, denaturalization, concentration, expropriation, coerced labor and military service, the `unmanned` war fiction, ordinary civilian complicity, dissent, private abuse, survivor elimination, collapse, nonrevenge, nonforgiveness, archival erasure, public memory, and post-collapse responsibility.

It is a **literary-institutional analysis**, not an external international-law memorandum. The term **genocide** is used in the sense established by the locked V2 corpus: a state-organized project directed at the destruction of a racialized population as a continuing political presence, including a policy logic in which surviving witnesses and claimants themselves become a problem to be eliminated. The document does not import an outside legal test or claim more precision than the supplied novels support.

The governing question is:

> **How does a republic turn racial ideology into an administratively ordinary program of disappearance, and what can justice mean after the state that denied the Eighty-Six's humanity physically collapses without morally settling its debts?**

The Phase-5 comparison rule is mandatory throughout. Later structures may reproduce a **Sector relation**—expendability, constrained refusal, blocked return, and political distancing—without reproducing San Magnolia's complete historical genealogy. Structural analogy and historical equivalence are not synonyms.

Detailed comparative analysis of Giad belongs primarily to Document 06; war-level allocation belongs to Document 07; the Fawns as body/personhood problem belong to Document 08; source-language taxonomy belongs to Document 12; and the final comparative matrix belongs to Document 14. They appear here only where they clarify the Republic's architecture or afterlife.

# I. Executive thesis

San Magnolia's central crime is not that every Alba citizen sincerely believed every Colorata body was biologically nonhuman.

Its central crime is that a constitutional republic reorganized **law, territory, property, labor, military doctrine, language, statistics, medicine, public memory, and future time** so that Colorata humanity ceased to have operative consequences.

The state did not need to prove that the Eighty-Six were animals. It needed institutions through which citizens could live comfortably while acting as though Eighty-Six rights, deaths, names, families, wounds, testimony, and possible return did not count.

That is why the opening memoir's brutal compression—`豚に人権を与えぬことを`—is only the first link [V01-L001]. The decisive sequence is institutional:

1. Colorata are converted from citizens into a targeted enemy category.
2. The category is stripped of constitutional and civic standing.
3. People and property are concentrated outside the protected interior.
4. their labor and bodies are requisitioned for the wall and battlefield.
5. military language converts human operators into `情報処理装置`, preserving the fiction of an `unmanned` war [V01-L003–V01-L004].
6. casualty accounting declares a battlefield with `no dead` because the people dying have been excluded from the class of countable deaths [V01-L002].
7. conditional restoration of citizenship is promised while service conditions make return statistically and politically impossible.
8. exceptional survivors become witnesses, potential organizers, and claimants whose existence threatens the Republic's postwar legitimacy.
9. terminal deployment and archive destruction resolve that threat by making the victims, graves, records, and legal evidence disappear [V01-L019–V01-L021].

The mature V2 formulation is therefore:

> **San Magnolia does not merely kill a racialized population. It engineers a world in which their killing can be described as the operation of machines, their labor can be consumed without reciprocal membership, their return can be promised without being permitted, and their disappearance can later be represented as proof that no crime occurred.**

The system is genocidal because the intended endpoint is not simply defeat, discipline, or exploitation. It is the removal of the Eighty-Six as a surviving political subject capable of naming what happened, reclaiming property and citizenship, demanding compensation, or entering history as victims of the Republic.

That architecture produces differentiated responsibility. Policy designers, legislators, military administrators, researchers, private abusers, beneficiaries, silent citizens, active dissenters, children, and later generations do not bear identical guilt. But rejecting collective guilt does not create collective innocence. The Republic persists because many people occupy roles that make the system easier to continue than to confront.

Its collapse does not settle the account. Republic civilians later suffer war, displacement, massacre, hunger, fear, and prejudice. Those injuries are real. They do not retroactively balance the racial order, prove the victims were wrong to condemn it, or turn history into a symmetrical exchange of group suffering.

The Eighty-Six likewise do not become morally pure by being persecuted. Some reproduce internal hierarchies; some use `white pig` language; later, Shin and Raiden recognize their own vulnerability to faceless group categories. This is not moral equivalence with a state genocide. It is the series' anti-essentialist insistence that mechanisms of othering remain humanly reproducible even when power, scale, history, and consequence differ radically.

The post-collapse ethical answer is consequently neither compulsory forgiveness nor inherited revenge.

The survivors may:

- refuse revenge without forgiving;
- rescue people they neither like nor absolve;
- demand legal punishment without making punishment the whole of repair;
- relinquish an offender's claim to organize their future without declaring the offense acceptable;
- preserve names and records without accepting a permanent identity as victims;
- remember a country without restoring its institutions;
- preserve anger without converting anger into collective punishment.

Repair must therefore be plural. It may require adjudication, archival recovery, public truth, restored names, compensation, education, material reconstruction, institutional redesign, and protection of survivor choice. It cannot purchase reconciliation, compel forgiveness, or make suffering into payment.

The document's final thesis is:

> **The Republic's genocide works by making a people disappear from law, space, statistics, memory, and futurity. A just aftermath must reverse each form of disappearance without forcing survivors to remain forever inside the identity of what was done to them.**

# II. Analytical distinctions the Republic requires

Several terms overlap in the novels but must remain separate in synthesis.

| Structure | Meaning in this document | Why the distinction matters |
|---|---|---|
| **Racial classification** | inherited or phenotypic groups are assigned political significance | classification is not yet the whole exterminatory system |
| **Denaturalization** | citizenship and legal membership are removed from a targeted population | legal personhood is materially indispensable, not superficial [T14-C32] |
| **Dehumanization** | people are administered as animals, machines, components, monsters, or inferior kinds | rhetoric matters most when it reorganizes rights, records, and risk |
| **Concentration** | forced spatial separation of the targeted population | spatial isolation permits extraction and shields the interior from consequence |
| **Expropriation** | property is seized and redistributed to beneficiaries of exclusion | racism becomes a political economy, not only an attitude |
| **Coerced service** | labor or combat is demanded while refusal is materially closed | conscription alone is not equivalent to the Sector |
| **Disposability** | continued life is valued less than utility obtained from exposure or death | recognized humans can still be disposable [T14-C28] |
| **Blocked return** | the exposed population lacks a credible route back to ordinary membership | a decisive marker separating risky service from terminal use |
| **Archival erasure** | names, deaths, graves, records, and causal chains are destroyed or suppressed | disappearance continues after bodily death |
| **Extermination logic** | survivors themselves become an administrative and political liability | one of the strongest features distinguishing V01 from generic oppression |
| **Complicity** | participation in, facilitation of, benefit from, or culpable acquiescence to wrongdoing | it is graded by agency, knowledge, role, and causal contribution |
| **Repair** | future-oriented responses to harm: truth, judgment, record, compensation, reform, restoration of standing | repair is not identical to forgiveness or restoration of intimacy |

The category **genocide** in this document names the conjunction. It should not be reduced to insult, discrimination, dangerous military service, or even mass death considered separately. The Republic integrates those practices into a policy architecture that aims at the disappearance of a racialized civic population.

# III. From racial category to administrative disappearance

## 1. The constitutional contradiction is not accidental hypocrisy

San Magnolia continues to speak the language of republican citizenship, equality, and constitutional legitimacy while excluding Colorata from the class protected by those terms.

This is not merely a case of citizens failing to live up to high ideals in private conduct. The state changes who the ideals are allowed to describe. The memoir's pig formulation and the novel's `人間ではないのだから` logic make denial conceptually convenient [V01-L001, V01-L003]. The constitution can remain textually present because the target is reclassified outside the people to whom constitutional protection applies.

The V01 majority/constitution exchange is therefore crucial [V01-L022]. A political majority can preserve democratic forms while using classification to remove a minority from the relevant demos. In that situation, procedure does not restrain atrocity; procedure helps declare the victims politically absent.

This is why Shin's claim that the Republic died nine years earlier is more than bitterness [V01-L016]. The state becomes normatively dead when it abandons the principle that fellow citizens remain persons before the law. Physical collapse comes later. The Legion destroys a polity whose constitutional self-destruction preceded military defeat.

The novels do not infer from this that equality and rights are meaningless. Lena's later political position is almost the reverse. Rights are not self-enforcing facts; they are fragile social constructions that must be maintained by institutions and citizens. San Magnolia demonstrates what happens when a republic uses its power to change the membership boundary rather than submit itself to its professed standards.

## 2. Denaturalization converts neighbors into an administrable outside

The Republic's racial order begins by converting people who lived inside the state into enemy citizens and then into people without citizenship. The breadth of the category exposes the enemy-ancestry rationale as pretext: non-Imperial Colorata are included, while some white people of Imperial origin escape the measure. The operative criterion is not careful attribution of wartime allegiance but a visible and inherited population boundary.

Denaturalization performs several tasks at once.

It breaks reciprocal obligation. The state can demand labor and obedience while denying the target a claim upon the state.

It makes dispossession appear administrative rather than criminal. Property can be seized because the former owner no longer stands as a full member with enforceable rights.

It makes family separation and concentration easier to narrate as security measures. People are not being expelled from their own polity; an enemy presence is supposedly being contained.

It also reorganizes memory. Once the target is officially an enemy outside, later citizens can forget that the Eighty-Six were Republic citizens before they were assigned the name of the district to which they were expelled. V14's family recipe—an Eighty-Six mother who was also a Republic citizen—quietly breaks the false eternal separation [V14-L186].

This is why T14-C32 rejects the claim that citizenship is superficial compared with authentic belonging. Citizenship cannot create affection or home by itself, but its removal changes whether institutions owe a person residence, records, medical care, political voice, protection, and acknowledgment of death.

## 3. Concentration and expropriation make the interior materially invested

The Eighty-Five Sectors are not innocent space. Their security, civilian abundance, and distance from the front are produced through the coerced exterior.

Colorata property is seized. Colorata labor is used on the Gran Mur. Colorata bodies carry the war. The protected population is relieved of much military labor and sheltered from the knowledge that human soldiers die on its behalf.

The arrangement therefore creates **beneficiaries**, not only believers.

A citizen need not wake each morning consumed by racial hatred to receive the advantages of racial policy. The system can persist because it lowers the personal cost of war, transfers danger outward, supplies a reassuring story, and makes opposition socially and materially inconvenient.

That is one reason ordinary complicity cannot be reduced to personal sadism. Many citizens participate through normality: accepting seized comfort, repeating official language, refusing inconvenient evidence, treating the wall as a natural boundary, or permitting others to perform violence they do not wish to see.

Alter.1's kitten episode provides a low-pressure control. An object associated with the Eighty-Six becomes polluted and disgusting in domestic judgment. No official needs to issue an order. The racial hierarchy has become ordinary enough to reproduce in household taste.

The political economy and domestic culture reinforce each other. State policy teaches which lives may be used; everyday disgust teaches why the use feels unsurprising.

## 4. The `unmanned` fiction converts a person into a technical component

The Republic's most famous lie is not merely false information. It is a semantic technology.

`プロセッサー` and `情報処理装置` convert a human operator into part of a machine [V01-L003]. Once the operator is defined as processing equipment rather than a soldier, the Juggernaut can remain officially unmanned. Once the battlefield is unmanned, its human deaths need not enter casualty statistics. Thus `その戦場に、死者はいない` is not a description of physical reality but a rule about which bodies count [V01-L002].

The sequence can be stated precisely:

> **classification → component language → unmanned doctrine → zero-death statistics → absence from public mourning.**

This is why `言葉は、便利だ` is politically serious [V01-L004]. The problem is not that euphemism hypnotizes citizens into sincerely hallucinating machines where children stand. The problem is that euphemism coordinates institutions. It tells officers how to file losses, civilians how to understand the war, researchers how to discuss bodies, and political leaders how to claim legitimacy.

Language becomes an interface through which incompatible facts can coexist:

- human beings visibly pilot the weapons;
- the state officially fights without human soldiers;
- the public can know enough to benefit while not knowing enough to feel accountable.

The V2 conclusion is therefore narrower than “slurs cause genocide.” Slurs, technical nouns, and categories matter because law and bureaucracy make them actionable.

## 5. Medical abandonment reveals the same ontology at the level of the body

Volume 10 retrospectively shows that the Processor body is triaged according to rapid return to combat rather than the ordinary value of continued life. There are no adequate military doctors or field hospitals [V10-L009]. Processors are treated as if they already carry black triage tags before injury because the institution has no intention of investing in futures that do not immediately restore battlefield use [V10-L038].

This is not an accidental shortage considered in isolation. It belongs to the same system that calls the person a component. A damaged component is not rehabilitated into another life; it is abandoned when repair no longer serves current function.

The historical importance of this evidence is that the Republic does not only make the Eighty-Six die. It prevents bodily change from opening another social role. Injury becomes terminal because the person has been denied a civilian future into which to return.

## 6. False restoration promises make hope part of the disposal system

The five-year service promise prevents the Republic from being described simply as a state that openly declares extermination from the beginning.

Citizenship restoration is offered as a conditional future. The promise extracts labor and endurance by preserving the appearance of an exit. Yet long-term survivors are not welcomed back. They become anomalies.

Special Reconnaissance exists to resolve the anomaly: `守らせるためではなく、死なせるために` [V01-L019]. The mission does not merely expose veterans to exceptional danger. It is designed to close the return route.

This makes **blocked return** central to the Republic's genocidal time.

The Eighty-Six are told that service leads back to citizenship, but the institution is structured so that service leads toward disappearance. Hope is not outside coercion. It is one of coercion's instruments.

The later novels repeatedly distinguish high-risk missions from disposal by asking whether institutions materially expect soldiers to come back. San Magnolia's decisive answer is no. It promises return while arranging nonreturn.

## 7. Survivor elimination reveals the political endpoint

The Republic's policy becomes fully exterminatory when surviving Eighty-Six are understood as a postwar problem.

Survivors would carry testimony. They could demand return of property, restoration of citizenship, recognition of the camps, compensation for coerced service, and acknowledgment of the dead. Their continued existence would make the Republic answerable to domestic and international audiences.

The simplest way to preserve the Republic's innocence is therefore to eliminate the people whose lives prove the crime.

This is more than military expendability. It is a witness-elimination logic. The target is not dangerous because of what it might do in battle but because it might remain alive to speak.

The policy reaches memory as well as bodies. Records are destroyed so that the Eighty-Six may be represented as people who never existed—`存在しなかったこと` [V01-L021]. The genocide seeks an endpoint in which no victim, grave, personnel file, casualty number, property claim, or surviving witness remains capable of contradicting the official story.

# IV. The political economy of ordinary complicity

## 1. Complicity is neither one thing nor nothing

The novels resist two easy positions.

The first says every Alba is equally guilty because the Republic acted in their name.

The second says ordinary citizens bear no responsibility because they did not individually design the camps or shoot the Processors.

Both erase the structure by replacing graded responsibility with a binary.

A useful responsibility map is:

| Position | Typical relation to the system | Appropriate analytical judgment |
|---|---|---|
| **Policy architects and senior political decision-makers** | design racial law, concentration, expropriation, terminal service, erasure | highest institutional and causal responsibility |
| **Military commanders and administrators** | allocate bodies, maintain no-return doctrine, suppress evidence, enforce inventory language | direct professional and command responsibility, varying by authority and knowledge |
| **Researchers and technical professionals** | make surveillance, Para-RAID exploitation, classification, or bodily use operational | responsibility for professional participation cannot be erased by technical neutrality |
| **Private perpetrators** | purchase, torture, assault, exploit, or discard rightless children | direct personal culpability enabled but not caused away by the state [V12-L009–V12-L015] |
| **Material beneficiaries and ordinary bystanders** | receive safety/property/war insulation, accept propaganda, decline costly opposition | complicity varies with knowledge and available action; benefit and silence remain morally relevant |
| **Dissenters and resisters** | oppose concentration, preserve persons, use office to reduce harm | individual variation prevents racial essentialism; resistance does not erase the surrounding structure |
| **Children and persons with severely limited agency** | inherit narratives and benefits without meaningful initial control | not bearers of inherited personal guilt; may inherit later duties to learn, repair, and refuse repetition |
| **Later citizens/successor institutions** | receive archives, property orders, public myths, and unresolved consequences | responsibility is prospective: truth, repair, institutional reform, not retroactive personal guilt |

This map follows T08's central rule: responsibility should track agency, knowledge, authority, and causal contribution rather than emotional intensity alone.

## 2. Benefit can sustain atrocity without ideological passion

The Republic's protected interior receives a bargain: other people will carry military danger, and the state will narrate the arrangement as technological progress.

That bargain lowers the psychological and material cost of acquiescence. Citizens do not need to become doctrinal racial theorists when ordinary life rewards not asking questions.

The system also externalizes the costs of knowledge. Handlers can command from safety. Civilians can encounter war through propaganda. The wall converts geography into moral distance. Casualty erasure removes funerals, wounded veterans, bereaved families, and public numbers that might force the interior to recognize the human price of its comfort.

Ordinary complicity therefore operates through **insulation**.

A person may know that the Eighty-Six exist while remaining insulated from their names, bodies, voices, medical needs, families, and deaths. Theo's accusation that Lena never asked their names identifies this problem even in a sympathetic dissenter [V01-L011]. Abstract opposition to racism is not the same thing as particular knowledge or material responsibility.

## 3. Silence is participation, but not omnipotence

Lena's V01 principle—silently permitting a wrong is participation in it—prevents moral comfort through private disapproval [V01-L005]. People inside institutions acquire obligations to use the power they actually possess.

But later volumes qualify the principle. Failure to stop an entire state is not automatically the personal guilt of one officer. Lena's grief over the Republic's destruction does not make her causally responsible for every decision that produced it [V11-L006]. Annette may need reflection without claiming crimes beyond her agency. Aldrecht's real complicity does not become repaired because he destroys himself.

The mature position is therefore:

> **Silence, administration, benefit, and failure to use available authority can create responsibility. They do not create omnipotence, equal culpability, or a moral duty of self-destruction.**

This matters for post-collapse politics. A just account must be capable of saying both:

- many ordinary people helped make the system durable;
- no serious process of justice can proceed by assigning one undifferentiated racial guilt to every Republic person.

## 4. The system destroys the military competence it claims to protect

The Republic's genocide is also strategically self-consuming.

By transferring real war to the Eighty-Six, the Alba military loses knowledge of the enemy, practical competence, honest casualty information, and an institutional culture capable of learning from the front. When the Legion reaches the interior, staff cannot reliably interpret alarms or battlefield reality.

This is not a moral balancing mechanism in which the state “gets what it deserves.” The collapse kills many people, including children and civilians who do not bear equal responsibility. Its significance is institutional: a polity that treats some soldiers as disposable machinery eventually destroys its own capacity to understand the war those soldiers are fighting.

The Republic does not merely betray its constitutional ideals. It hollows out the professional systems required for survival.

# V. Racism reproduced through public language, private practice, and embodied expectation

## 1. Public nonpersonhood authorizes private atrocity

Volume 12 is indispensable because it prevents the Sector from being imagined only as a distant military zone.

Eighty-Six infants are purchased inside the walls, tortured, sexually abused, used through Para-RAID, and discarded [V12-L009–V12-L015]. These acts are private, but their permission structure is public. Once the state declares a population rightless and inferior, households, markets, researchers, and abusers can treat the victim's body as available.

The rescued child's inability to understand that the former master no longer has authority shows how deeply domination survives legal rescue [V12-L011]. Freedom is not restored merely by changing formal status. A person trained never to refuse may lack a usable conception of refusal even after the law recognizes it.

This is one of the Republic's longest afterlives: the state teaches domination into bodies and relationships.

## 2. Pollution logic makes association itself degrading

Alter.1's kitten material is minor in scale and major in diagnostic value. An animal becomes dirty because it was associated with the Eighty-Six.

This is not genocide in miniature. It is evidence of how the racial ontology becomes ordinary. Stigma travels through objects, households, and contact. The state need not be visibly present for citizens to reproduce the hierarchy.

Such scenes matter because genocide does not begin and end at moments of mass killing. It requires an everyday world in which segregation, disgust, avoidance, and differential concern feel normal enough that extraordinary violence can be administered without continuous moral shock.

## 3. Victims can reproduce hierarchy without creating symmetry

Kaie refuses racial moral essentialism: some Alba are good; some Eighty-Six are bad [V01-L009]. The camps themselves contain internal prejudice toward mixed, noble, eastern, southern, or otherwise vulnerable subgroups. Anju's scars and Iska's weaponization of ancestry preserve this evidence.

The conclusion is not that “everyone is equally racist” or that the Republic's state program becomes less serious.

The conclusion is:

> **Oppression does not morally purify its victims, and victim wrongdoing does not retroactively legitimate the oppression.**

This distinction becomes essential when Shin and Raiden later catch themselves using faceless group categories. Similarity of cognitive mechanism does not imply equality of institutional power, historical scale, or consequence.

# VI. Dissent and the limits of the exceptional-good-Alba narrative

## 1. Individual Alba variation is real

The novels provide Alba who dissent, resist, aid the Eighty-Six, preserve their humanity, or refuse the Republic's racial common sense. This prevents `Alba` from becoming a moral essence in the opposite direction.

Lena's political development is one major example. Aldrecht's life among the Eighty-Six is another. Vaclav's prior dissent matters. Republic-born people such as Dustin can accept historical responsibility without pretending to become Eighty-Six. Kaie's formulation explicitly preserves this individual variation.

But exceptional individuals do not refute structural analysis.

A system can be racist even when some members of the dominant category resist it. Indeed, the presence of resistance demonstrates that alternatives were morally and politically imaginable. The wrong was not inevitable because every citizen lacked the conceptual capacity to recognize it.

## 2. Lena shows the difference between innocence and opposition

Lena begins morally opposed to the Republic's racism. She is not personally innocent in the strong sense of standing outside the system.

She is an Alba officer protected by the wall, educated by the state, positioned inside the Handler system, and initially capable of speaking to Processors without knowing their names. Her development consists partly of converting moral conviction into accountable use of office.

This is why she cannot absolve herself merely by being correct. Nor must she claim the whole Republic's guilt as personally hers. Her proper responsibility lies in what she can know, command, refuse, record, protect, and later rebuild.

Document 03 treats that development in full. For this document, Lena demonstrates a larger point:

> **Opposition to an unjust order becomes politically meaningful when it changes the dissenter's relation to privilege, information, risk, and action.**

## 3. Annette and Aldrecht distinguish reflection from self-punishment

Annette's responsibility includes childhood betrayal, later professional inheritance, and participation in Republic research structures. Yet Shin's memory does not exist to complete her penitential narrative. V11's distinction is exact: reflection may be appropriate; claiming as one's crime what was not one's crime is not [V11-L003–V11-L004].

Aldrecht provides the harder case because his complicity is real. He participated in a system that harmed children and failed to prevent what happened. But his response turns guilt into self-condemnation, revenge, and Legion capture. Suffering does not repair the people he harmed. Self-destruction is not compensation.

Together, they establish a crucial post-genocide principle:

> **The moral seriousness of a perpetrator or beneficiary is not measured by how completely that person destroys themselves. It is measured by truthful acknowledgment, acceptance of proportionate consequence, present action, and contribution to a future in which the wrong is harder to repeat.**

## 4. Dissent cannot be used to launder the institution

The Republic may later remember its dissenters as proof that “true San Magnolia” remained virtuous. That would repeat the archive problem.

Individual courage should be preserved. It should not be used to obscure the fact that the state, military, public institutions, and many citizens allowed racial policy to become ordinary.

A just history must hold both truths:

- some Republic people resisted and paid real costs;
- the Republic as an institution organized genocide.

# VII. Collapse is not justice

## 1. The Republic dies normatively before it falls militarily

Shin's V01 declaration that the Republic died nine years earlier identifies constitutional death [V01-L016]. By stripping fellow citizens of humanity, the state abandons the political principle under which it claims legitimacy.

The Legion's later conquest is physical collapse, not the moment the Republic first becomes unjust.

This distinction blocks a providential reading. The Legion is not a moral tribunal. Its violence does not discriminate according to culpability, restore stolen property, honor the dead, or build a just successor order. Mass death cannot perform the work of justice merely because it reaches a guilty state.

## 2. Republic victimization does not create retroactive innocence

When Alba civilians become refugees, casualties, or victims of the Legion, their suffering deserves recognition. Kurena can protect Alba children while remaining unforgiving. Shin can refuse revenge while lacking affection for Republic citizens [V11-L010–V11-L011].

This is an anti-disposability floor: a person does not need to belong to an innocent collective in order to have a claim against arbitrary death.

But the reverse inference is invalid. Being victimized later does not erase earlier benefit or complicity. The Republic's suffering cannot be placed on one side of a scale and treated as payment for the Eighty-Six's persecution.

History is not balanced by exchanging corpses.

## 3. Ideology survives territorial collapse

Volume 4 shows refugee politics preserving racial hierarchy, pure-white nationalism, and claims that the Eighty-Six remain Republic weapon property [V04-L011–V04-L012]. The state can lose territory while its administrative ontology survives in language and political desire.

This matters because “the Republic collapsed” is not the same sentence as “Republic racism ended.” Categories, habits, property claims, memories, and myths travel with refugees and institutions.

Volume 11's evacuation hierarchy makes the same point under extinction. Elite and preferred groups still seek priority. Victimization does not automatically produce reflection.

## 4. `エイティシックスじゃなくてもよかったんだ`

V11's insight that it did not have to be the Eighty-Six is one of the series' most important structural claims [V11-L008]. A society accustomed to making danger bearable by transferring it onto an outside can create a new outside when the original target is absent.

This does not mean the new victims become historically identical to the Colorata. The Republic's original racial program remains specific.

It means the **inside/outside mechanism** has become part of political culture. A frightened society can redraw the boundary through class, ancestry, military status, regional identity, or accusation.

The Republic's afterlife is therefore not only racist doctrine. It is the learned habit of solving common danger by deciding whose life may absorb it.

# VIII. Return without reconciliation: nonrevenge, nonforgiveness, and accountability

## 1. Refusing revenge does not forgive the Republic

The Eighty-Six reject revenge from Volume 1 onward partly because they refuse to let the Republic determine what kind of people injury will make them. That decision is rooted in pride, not affection.

V11 makes the distinction explicit. Shin did not want or choose revenge [V11-L010]. He may not want to save Republic citizens, but he does not want them simply to die [V11-L011]. Ethical restraint does not require warm feeling.

Rito supplies the definitive form: he may never forgive—`まだ赦せない`, perhaps for life—while also reaching `もういいや` [V11-L013–V11-L014]. Release means the offender no longer organizes the survivor's future. It is not a declaration that the offense was acceptable.

T14-C22 therefore rejects the claim that nonrevenge culminates in forgiveness.

## 2. Rescue is not absolution

The final evacuation forces the question whether Republic civilians should be abandoned [V11-L033]. The choice to rescue them can be justified by the rescuers' own ethical commitments, professional obligations, or refusal to imitate the persecutor.

It does not purchase reconciliation.

A rescued citizen does not thereby become innocent. A rescuer does not thereby become affectionate. The act preserves life while leaving historical judgment intact.

This is politically important because humanitarian obligation cannot depend on moral intimacy. If only loved or forgiven people possess a claim against death, personhood has again become conditional.

## 3. Anger remains legitimate

V14 protects anger while criticizing displaced collective violence. Raiden wants the people who killed and demonized Rito to be answered. Frederica does not tell him the anger is impure. She tells him to direct it accurately rather than destroy an entire polity [V14-L100–V14-L101].

This is the mature alternative to revenge:

- name the act;
- identify the responsible persons and institutions;
- demand consequence;
- preserve the victim's name;
- refuse to turn the wrongdoer's category into an undifferentiated enemy people.

Shin can insist that Rito's killer face consequence while refusing to treat every Federacy soldier as a demon [V13-L108–V13-L111, V14-L047–V14-L051]. The same standard applies retrospectively to the Republic.

## 4. Punishment is necessary in some cases and insufficient as repair

T14-C23 rejects legal punishment as sufficient repair.

Adjudication can assign culpability and sanction. It cannot:

- resurrect the dead;
- restore stolen childhoods;
- rebuild every family;
- recover every destroyed record;
- make the victim forgive;
- undo years of coerced service;
- guarantee nonrecurrence;
- create housing, education, medical care, or political voice by itself.

A just order requires punishment where evidence and procedure support it. It also requires forms of repair aimed at the living future.

# IX. Memory against erasure—and against conscription by memory

## 1. The Republic attacks memory because memory creates claims

The prohibition on graves and mourning is political [V01-L006]. The destruction of personnel files is political [V01-L021]. Casualty erasure is political [V01-L002].

A named dead person can have family, property, citizenship, a cause of death, and someone responsible. An unnamed component can be expended without generating the same public claim.

Archive destruction therefore continues genocide beyond bodily death. It tries to remove the relationship between victim, act, institution, and witness.

## 2. Names and fragments are a counter-archive

Shin's fragments, Spearhead's objects, the group photograph, letters, and dead-name practice preserve particular people where the state intends an anonymous absence. Lena's failure to ask names exposes the moral danger of generic sympathy [V01-L011].

But names are not a metaphysical test of humanity. They are a practice of accountability and relation. The person already exists; the name prevents the observer and institution from pretending otherwise.

Volume 10's collapse of personal name, callsign, and internment number shows how thoroughly administration can occupy identity [V10-L019]. Recovered names and records later reopen biography, but they do not compel a survivor to return to an old house or identity.

## 3. Public history requires more than heroic memorials

Lena's memoir and later archival work counter the Republic's official nonexistence. Yet T14-C33 warns that preserving memory is not inherently healthy.

Memory can become another form of conscription when the living are required to:

- imitate the dead;
- avenge forever;
- remain permanent victim-symbols;
- preserve one grief as their only political identity;
- accept a heroic or saintly story that removes ordinary contradiction.

Rito's public vindication is therefore an open problem. Restoring his name is necessary. Turning him into a clean martyr may erase the boy who wanted to learn about whales and the sea [V14-L090]. T14-OQ-26 remains open.

## 4. Recipes, jokes, and ordinary stories are counter-erasure practices

V14's recipes and pirate broadcasts answer monsterization by restoring ordinary particularity. The Eighty-Six laugh, cook badly, preserve family dishes, remember music, and possess preferences [V14-L112–V14-L115].

This is not a sentimental substitute for rights. Cooking stories cannot restore citizenship, adjudicate murder, or redesign military institutions.

Their value is representational. A public category described as monsters is reintroduced as people whose lives contain mundane continuities. The recipe from Shin's mother simultaneously breaks the false opposition between `Eighty-Six` and `Republic citizen` [V14-L186].

T14-OQ-31 asks whether such reciprocal imagination can scale into durable representation and law. The text has not yet answered.

## 5. The archive should point forward

The War History Compilation Room receives `未来の戦史`: plans and knowledge for future survival rather than memorialization as an endpoint [V14-L003].

This offers the strongest current model of an ethical archive:

1. preserve what happened and who was lost;
2. retain causal and operational knowledge;
3. use the record to change future practice;
4. do not require the living to reproduce the dead person's role or endpoint.

The archive refuses erasure without making the past sovereign over the future.

# X. The Sector after the Sector: structural reproduction without historical flattening

## 1. A graded comparison is necessary

The V2 corpus uses the following analytical scale:

| Level | Relation | Typical evidence |
|---:|---|---|
| **0** | thematic resemblance | insult, prejudice, unequal esteem, symbolic othering |
| **1** | exclusionary structure | targeted boundary plus differential burden or access |
| **2** | functionalized other | person or group valued primarily as weapon, sacred role, labor, or risk absorber |
| **3** | disposability structure | constrained refusal, unequal exposure, standing dependent on utility |
| **4** | Sector relation | expendability + constrained refusal + blocked return + political/social distancing |
| **5** | historically specific San Magnolia system | Level 4 plus racial denaturalization, concentration, expropriation, legal/statistical nonpersonhood, sustained coerced service, archive destruction, and survivor-elimination logic |

This scale is analytical rather than a taxonomy stated by the novels. Its purpose is to prevent two opposite errors:

- calling every cruelty another Eighty-Sixth Sector;
- treating the original Sector as so unique that later structural recurrence becomes impossible to name.

## 2. V13 reproduces the relation, not the full genealogy

V13 explicitly describes battlefront separation, closed retreat, compulsory exposure, and rear hostility as a new Eighty-Sixth Sector [V13-L121–V13-L127]. This is not merely an analyst's metaphor.

The conjunction is decisive:

- the exposed force is separated from the protected interior;
- retreat is materially closed;
- refusal is constrained;
- danger comes from the Legion ahead and human hostility behind;
- Lena becomes hostage infrastructure to prevent Eighty-Six resistance [V13-L120].

That is a Level-4 Sector relation.

It is not a Level-5 replay of San Magnolia's nine-year racial program. The Federacy's collapse arises through war exhaustion, refugee resentment, unresolved class and ethnic fractures, information warfare, desertion, emergency coercion, and political regression. Different causes produce a related human-buffer relation.

T14-C27 therefore requires **asymmetric analogy**: name the reproduced mechanism and the absent historical machinery.

## 3. The Fawn program proves that recognition does not prevent disposal

The Fawn project studies recognized human bodies because those bodies are useful. It converts adolescent Eighty-Six girls into weapons intended in part for postwar disposal of surviving Eighty-Six [V13-L019, V13-L023].

This is not the old fiction that the target is literally nonhuman. It is more disturbing in a different way: humanity is understood and treated as technically exploitable.

T14-C28 rejects the claim that recognition prevents disposability. A state can know exactly what a person is and still decide that the person's future should end in service to somebody else's security.

The original Sector teaches that racial dehumanization is one route to disposal. The Fawns teach that it is not the only route.

## 4. Anti-Alba backlash is category substitution, not reversed genocide

Giad prejudice against Alba and white-haired people after Republic crimes become public demonstrates inherited group blame. It is morally serious.

It is not equivalent to San Magnolia's system. Alba in Giad are not racially denaturalized, concentrated, converted into unmanned components, or subjected to a comparable state extermination policy.

The proper conclusion is:

> **The direction of prejudice can reverse without the structure, power, and historical consequence becoming symmetrical.**

Later Alba suffering must be recognized without being used to balance or minimize the Republic's genocide.

## 5. Victims can reproduce categorical erasure

Shin's V13 wish that foolish outsiders might be better absent is a face-erasing `them` category [V13-L086]. Raiden later recognizes that displaced rage can resemble the Republic's transfer of fear onto a weaker minority [V14-L085–V14-L086].

Neither has thereby become equivalent to San Magnolia's architects. They have not built a racial state, stripped citizenship, created camps, or planned extermination.

The comparison operates at the level of cognitive mechanism: diffuse fear and pain become easier to bear when located in a simplified group.

Similarity of mechanism does not imply equality of culpability.

## 6. Blood legitimacy is another inherited hierarchy, not one more name for racism

V14's reactionaries argue that legitimate rule belongs to `正当なる真紅の血統` [V14-L210]. This is hereditary guardianship: selected bloodlines are elevated as uniquely fit to speak, judge, and rule.

San Magnolia's racial system creates a degraded outside to exploit and erase. Blood-legitimist restoration creates an elevated inside authorized to govern.

Both deny political standing through inherited category. They are not the same structure.

T14-OQ-08 remains open because the final arc has not yet fully distinguished race, ethnicity, caste, aristocratic blood legitimacy, and military status in its political settlement.

# XI. What a reparative order would have to do

The novels do not yet present a completed postwar settlement. Any detailed institutional blueprint would exceed the evidence. The corpus does, however, establish the responsibilities a credible repair process cannot ignore.

## 1. Restore legal standing without treating recognition as completion

Citizenship, equal protection, residence, records, political participation, and bodily security are foundational. V01 proves they are not superficial.

But legal restoration alone cannot undo learned inability to refuse, social stigma, loss of family, medical abandonment, or the absence of education and material support. Formal rights must become usable.

## 2. Reconstruct the archive

A reparative archive would need to recover where possible:

- names;
- family relations;
- birth records;
- addresses;
- camp and unit records;
- casualty records;
- property seizure chains;
- command decisions;
- medical and research abuse;
- death circumstances;
- official narratives and destroyed-evidence policies.

Reconstruction must also record uncertainty. Missing evidence should not be converted into exculpatory certainty merely because the Republic succeeded in destroying records.

## 3. Differentiate guilt, responsibility, benefit, and inheritance

A just order cannot proceed through collective racial guilt. It must distinguish architects, direct perpetrators, administrators, beneficiaries, bystanders, dissenters, children, and later generations.

Later generations may inherit property orders, myths, institutions, and duties to repair. They do not inherit personal guilt for actions they did not choose.

Conversely, claiming not to have personally designed the camps cannot erase responsibility for direct participation, material benefit, or culpable silence where meaningful action was available.

## 4. Preserve nonforgiveness

No survivor should be required to forgive as proof of healing or civic maturity.

A state apology may be valid without receiving absolution. Cooperation may occur without reconciliation. Rescue may be obligatory without affection. Public truth may be accepted while private relationships remain broken.

This is not a defect in repair. It is respect for the fact that the harmed person retains authority over the meaning of the injury.

## 5. Provide material compensation and future capacity

Recognition without material support would leave the consequences of expropriation and coerced service with the victims.

The exact form remains open, but the relevant domains include:

- property and housing;
- medical and disability care;
- education;
- family tracing;
- income and employment access;
- transportation and civic infrastructure;
- trauma-informed support without paternalistic custody;
- memorial and burial access;
- representation in the institutions making repair decisions.

Compensation cannot make the harm good. Its purpose is to widen the future options of people whose options were deliberately destroyed.

## 6. Reform institutions rather than rely on moral exemplars

The Republic's failure cannot be solved by waiting for another Lena, another Vaclav, or another uniquely courageous official.

Repair requires institutions that preserve inconvenient evidence, protect dissent, count casualties honestly, constrain military utility logic, review emergency powers, protect return, and allow citizens to challenge classification.

T14-C40 rejects any guarantee that political recoverability follows from belief in human return. Institutions may fail to realize moral possibility.

## 7. Make ordinary particularity politically durable

Recipes, stories, humor, music, and recovered family practices can reopen reciprocal imagination. They cannot substitute for law.

The unresolved task is to connect particular recognition to durable representation: education, archives, public media, civic participation, and institutions capable of hearing named persons rather than only categories.

That is T14-OQ-31.

# XII. What remains after the Republic collapses

The question “what remains?” has several answers operating at different scales.

| Remainder | What survives collapse | Analytical consequence |
|---|---|---|
| **Bodies** | injuries, scars, conditioned obedience, interrupted development, lost family continuity | liberation does not restore the pre-persecution body or childhood |
| **Material history** | seized property, destroyed homes, camps, walls, military infrastructure, missing records | postwar justice requires more than moral acknowledgment |
| **Administrative language** | Processor, asset, inventory and purity categories persist in institutions and factions | regime defeat does not automatically end bureaucratic habits |
| **Social category** | `エイティシックス` remains both imposed history and reclaimed identity | survivors must control whether and how the identity continues |
| **Homeland** | San Magnolia remains birthplace, ruin, grave-space, stolen locality and political warning | return and restoration must remain distinct |
| **Memory** | names, fragments, stories, recipes, resentment, grief and testimony | counter-erasure is necessary but can become compulsory identity |
| **Moral debt** | perpetrators, beneficiaries, institutions and later inheritors occupy different relations to the crime | responsibility must be differentiated without disappearing |
| **Transfer mechanism** | society retains the habit of assigning danger to a marked outside | new target categories can reproduce a Sector relation |
| **Resistance** | pride, names, ordinary culture, nonrevenge, legal claims and public narration | victims retain more than injury; they retain political and cultural agency |

The collapse therefore ends neither the harm nor the need for judgment. It changes the form of the work.

During persecution, the immediate task is survival and resistance.

After collapse, the tasks include:

- reconstruction;
- evidence preservation;
- differentiated accountability;
- restoration of standing;
- protection against category substitution;
- and allowing survivors to become more than permanent evidence of the atrocity.

# XIII. Adversarial counterreadings

## Counterreading 1 — “The Republic's crime is extreme prejudice”

**Rejected as insufficient.**

Prejudice is present, but the decisive object is the integrated system of denaturalization, concentration, expropriation, coerced exposure, casualty erasure, blocked return and survivor elimination. Hatred without that machinery would still be wrong; it would not yet describe the original Sector.

## Counterreading 2 — “Dehumanizing words caused the genocide”

**Narrowed.**

Words matter because institutions materialize them. `豚`, `家畜`, `プロセッサー`, and “unmanned” language make procedures appear coherent. The causal account must still include law, economy, military command, geography, bureaucracy and public benefit.

## Counterreading 3 — “Every Alba citizen is equally guilty”

**Rejected.**

The corpus differentiates architects, implementers, beneficiaries, passive citizens, dissenters, children and later inheritors. Unequal responsibility does not imply the absence of structural complicity.

## Counterreading 4 — “Good Alba characters disprove the Republic's racial structure”

**Rejected.**

Individual variation proves racial essentialism false. It does not disprove laws, camps, expropriation, coerced service or official erasure.

## Counterreading 5 — “Eighty-Six prejudice or later wrongdoing creates moral symmetry”

**Rejected.**

Victims can reproduce dehumanizing cognitive moves. That does not equal control of the state, archive, army, property system or exterminatory policy. Similar mechanism does not mean equal power, history or consequence.

## Counterreading 6 — “The Eighty-Six heal when they forgive the Republic”

**Rejected under `T14-C22`.**

V11 and V14 permit nonrevenge, release and continued nonforgiveness. The ethical achievement is not absolution but recovery of a future no longer governed by the offender.

## Counterreading 7 — “Legal punishment completes repair”

**Rejected under `T14-C23`.**

Punishment is one response. Archive, vindication, compensation, education, reconstruction, property history, institutional reform and social recognition remain distinct tasks.

## Counterreading 8 — “A later Sector comparison means the later polity is historically the same as San Magnolia”

**Rejected under `T14-C27`.**

V13 reproduces the human-buffer relation through constrained refusal and blocked return. It does not reproduce every element of Colorata denaturalization, concentration and archival elimination.

## Counterreading 9 — “Recognizing a target as human prevents disposability”

**Rejected under `T14-C28`.**

The Fawn program recognizes human biology while assigning human bodies a disposal function. Personhood can be acknowledged abstractly and cancelled practically.

## Counterreading 10 — “Citizenship is paperwork beside real belonging”

**Rejected under `T14-C32`.**

The Republic's first decisive act is removal of citizenship. Legal membership is not sufficient for home or equality, but it is materially indispensable.

## Counterreading 11 — “Preserving memory is always morally healthy”

**Narrowed under `T14-C33`.**

Counter-archive resists state erasure. Memory becomes dangerous when it requires permanent mourning, imitation, revenge, victim performance or fixed identity.

## Counterreading 12 — “True images cannot dehumanize”

**Rejected under `T14-C35`.**

Accurate footage can be cropped from cause and chronology, then used to convert a contingent act into a categorical identity.

## Counterreading 13 — “Anti-Alba prejudice in Giad reverses the historical balance”

**Rejected.**

Category substitution and inherited group blame are real. Alba in Giad are not subjected to a state program comparable to the Republic's racial denaturalization and exterminatory system.

## Counterreading 14 — “The Republic's destruction is its punishment and closes the account”

**Rejected.**

State collapse kills many people, including victims and dissenters, while leaving records, property, culpability and survivor needs unresolved. Catastrophe is not a substitute for judgment.

## Counterreading 15 — “The ordinary life of citizens inside the walls proves most were innocent”

**Rejected as inference.**

Ordinary life is partly what the system produces for the protected population by displacing danger. Individual knowledge still varies, but normalcy itself cannot acquit the arrangements that make it possible.

## Counterreading 16 — “Volume 14 resolves the Republic's memory problem”

**Rejected under `T14-C36`.**

Recipes, radio, legal accountability and changed survivor attitudes establish directions of repair. They do not establish the final reparative order.

# XIV. Final-arc open-question registry for Document 05

The following questions remain **OPEN_FINAL_ARC**. They must not be resolved without new primary-source evidence.

### `T14-OQ-07` — postwar reparative order

What compensation, archive, education, reconstruction, property restoration and legal responsibility follow Republic and Federacy wrongdoing?

The current corpus establishes categories of repair, not a completed institutional settlement.

### `T14-OQ-08` — race, ethnicity, caste and blood after V14

How will the final arc distinguish racial classification, ethnicity, aristocratic caste, “true blood” legitimacy and military status?

San Magnolia's degraded racial outside and V14's elevated hereditary inside are related forms of inherited political judgment, but they are not the same structure.

### `T14-OQ-26` — Rito's public memory

Can public vindication restore Rito's name without converting him into a simplified martyr who exists only to prove the polity's eventual moral correction?

The same question applies to countless less narratively visible Eighty-Six dead.

### `T14-OQ-31` — civic repair at scale

Can recipes, music, humor, testimony, archives and education become durable representation and law rather than temporary wartime solidarity?

Particularity can reopen imagination. It still needs institutions capable of preserving standing when sympathy declines.

### `T14-OQ-32` — Eighty-Six identity after war

Does “Eighty-Six” remain a chosen durable identity across citizenship, locality, family and generational change?

A reclaimed identity may preserve history without becoming the only legitimate future of descendants.

## Additional Republic-specific questions retained from T11

These are not separately numbered T14 locks but remain analytically open:

1. How will responsibility be assigned to citizens who participated through fear, benefit or conformity without designing the system?
2. Will a complete Fawn archive identify designers, command chains, records and surviving institutional responsibility?
3. Can postwar institutions prevent Sector logic without assuming prejudice can be permanently abolished?
4. Who controls access to San Magnolia's ruins, graves, property records and memorial sites?
5. Can the Republic's constitutional language be inherited without allowing national redemption to overshadow victim authority?

# XV. Final synthesis — the state that made people disappear

San Magnolia begins by drawing a line.

Inside the line are citizens, rights, schools, homes, property, public grief, statistical existence and a nation that imagines itself defended by technology.

Outside the line are Colorata, camps, coerced labor, Juggernauts, minefields, machine-component vocabulary, uncounted deaths and people promised a return the state intends never to permit.

The line is racial, but it does not remain merely symbolic. It becomes:

- law;
- map;
- budget;
- labor policy;
- military organization;
- medical triage;
- casualty accounting;
- archive policy;
- and postwar strategy.

That integration is the Republic's distinctive horror.

A slur can be contradicted in conversation. A law can seize property. A wall can make neighbors disappear from daily perception. A military file can convert a teenager into an asset. A casualty table can make a death politically unreal. A destroyed archive can make later proof difficult. A final mission can remove the survivor who would force all of those acts back into public history.

The Republic's genocide is therefore not best imagined as one moment when hatred becomes violent. It is a long sequence in which **ordinary institutions are made to agree about who does not count**.

The Eighty-Six answer at first with whatever counter-institutions they can build:

- names on fragments;
- memory carried by survivors;
- jokes and food inside the Sector;
- pride that refuses the Republic's ontology;
- refusal to let the dead become official nothing;
- relations strong enough to preserve particular people where the state preserves only categories.

Those practices are already resistance. They are not yet a complete postwar order.

After the Republic collapses, the moral burden changes.

The survivors must be free not to forgive. They must also be free not to spend the rest of life hating. Dissenters must be remembered without being used to absolve the state. Beneficiaries and implementers must be distinguished without allowing every distinction to become an excuse. Children must not inherit guilt by phenotype. Later institutions must not ask the Eighty-Six to prove humanity through heroism, military usefulness or moving stories. Archives must preserve people without requiring survivors to remain curators of their own wound forever.

The V14 movement toward legal judgment, public record, Rito's vindication, recipes, radio and ordinary particularity begins to sketch such an answer. It is deliberately incomplete.

The strongest V01–V14 conclusion is:

> **A just memory of San Magnolia must do two things at once: preserve the historical specificity of what the Republic did, and prevent that history from becoming a new category that owns every future of the people who survived it.**

Historical specificity requires saying exactly what happened:

- racial denaturalization;
- concentration;
- expropriation;
- coerced labor and war;
- administrative dehumanization;
- no-return service;
- archive destruction;
- survivor elimination.

Future freedom requires refusing several demands:

- that the Eighty-Six forgive in order to be healed;
- that they remain angry in order to remember correctly;
- that they return in order to prove belonging;
- that they remain soldiers in order to justify survival;
- that later Alba inherit guilt merely by blood;
- that national reconstruction turn victims into symbols of reconciliation;
- that every later injustice be called “the same Sector” without historical precision.

The Republic tried to make one category the complete truth of a person and then make the people inside that category disappear.

The ethical answer is not merely to reverse the category.

It is to build law, memory and political life capable of saying:

> this happened to these people;
>
> it was done through identifiable institutions and choices;
>
> the record will remain;
>
> the responsible will not control its meaning;
>
> and the people who survived are not required to remain only what the crime made visible.

That is the difference between memorializing an atrocity and allowing the atrocity to retain jurisdiction over the future.
