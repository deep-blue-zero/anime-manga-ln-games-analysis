# 12 — Japanese Voice, Narration, Terminology, and Translation-Sensitive Findings

## Scope and authority note

**Phase-8 verification note.** The original-Japanese V01–V14 and Alter.1 EPUBs were re-mounted from the canonical Drive source hierarchy and re-hashed against the Phase-5 source lock. All 1,045 Phase-5 locator routes, all 28 Japanese terminology locks, and all 55 high-value Document-12 passage controls were rechecked against current source XHTML. One reference-layer wording defect was corrected: the earlier analytical shorthand **兵科転換** was not a source lexeme and has been replaced by the attested wording `兵科が変わる`. No load-bearing interpretation in this document was overturned.


This document is the primary Phase-6 home for findings that depend materially on the **Japanese wording, grammar, register, address system, narration, or lexical distinctions** of *86*. It is not a second general thematic synthesis. Documents 01–11 remain authoritative for their substantive domains; this document asks when those conclusions become more precise, more limited, or differently shaped once the Japanese is allowed to constrain the English abstraction.

The governing question is:

> **What becomes visible only when the novels are read in Japanese?**

The answer is not that the Japanese contains a hidden, radically different story. The more important finding is methodological. The original prose repeatedly prevents the cleanest English formulations from becoming too clean. English words such as **return**, **home**, **together**, **freedom**, **personhood**, **support**, **forgiveness**, **curse**, **Queen**, and **Death God** can each gather several Japanese systems into one apparently stable concept. If analysis then reasons backward from that smooth English word, it can accidentally invent lexical unity that the novels do not possess.

The controlling rule is therefore:

> **Source-language precision should constrain synthesis rather than decorate it. When English becomes smoother than the Japanese, the Japanese wins.**

The Japanese evidence also changes how character development should be described. Shin does not become linguistically florid as he matures. Lena does not simply move from polite officer to “Queen.” Raiden's roughness does not imply conceptual simplicity. Frederica's archaizing register is not comic garnish. Lerche's retainer speech is both role-coded and individual. Fido's formal first-person voice contributes to artificial subjectivity. Shiden's address choices create a relationship with Lena distinct from both formal staff hierarchy and Shin/Lena intimacy.

Finally, narration itself must be treated as layered evidence. *86* frequently uses close third-person focalization and character-colored narration. A sentence appearing outside quotation marks is not therefore automatically an omniscient metaphysical ruling. The corpus repeatedly places strong interpretations inside Ernst, Frederica, Lena, Theo, Kurena, Myrte, and other perspectives, then supplies counterfocalization, correction, or later evidence. The distinction between **narrated fact**, **focalized judgment**, **character speech**, and **retrospective memoir judgment** is one of the most important source-language controls in the V2 corpus.

The final-arc boundary remains Volume 14. Nothing in this document uses V15+ assumptions to settle open lexical, relational, political, or ontological questions.

# I. Executive thesis — Japanese grammar often tells us whether a claim is final

The strongest Japanese-language finding is not one secret keyword. It is a **network of contrasts**.

The series distinguishes:

- `帰る` from `戻る`, `還る`, `帰還`, and `生還`;
- `故郷` from `祖国`;
- ordinary `一緒に` from the often more elevated or burden-sharing `共に`, without making them a hard binary;
- `誇り` from constructions in which pride becomes the only available ground of selfhood through `しか`;
- `従う` from `支える`, and both from `信じる` and `頼る`;
- `人格`, `疑似人格`, `自我`, `自己同一性`, `意志`, and `意思` rather than supplying one recurring term equivalent to philosophical “personhood”;
- `赦す` from broader `許す` tendencies without making orthography an absolute semantic wall;
- role-address such as `死神`, `葬儀屋`, `アンダーテイカー`, `女王`, `鮮血の女王`, `女王陛下`, `聖女`, and military rank from the persons who bear those names;
- aspirational justice through `たらん` from identity-possession through `である`;
- and, crucially, **not yet** from **never** through small temporal forms such as `まだ`.

These distinctions converge on a larger linguistic pattern. *86* is repeatedly interested in whether a proposition has become exhaustive and final.

Japanese grammar can mark the difference before the thematic essay does.

`もう` plus negation can close an old proposition: Shin no longer says pride is all he possesses [V08-L033]. `まだ` can keep a state open without pretending it is solved: not yet able to return, not yet able to forgive. `また` presupposes recurrence. `いつか` makes an event imaginable without fixing its date. Potential grammar in `戻ってこられる` states that return is **possible**, not guaranteed [V14-L044]. Ordinary departure formulae such as `行ってくる` contain the expectation of coming back within the speech act itself.

This is why the mature V2 vocabulary should prefer **recoverability** over **redemption**, **answer-space** over infinite fluidity, and **role plurality** over simple role rejection. Those are analytical glosses, not source lexemes, but they are better because they preserve Japanese modality rather than erasing it.

A second major conclusion concerns voice. Character development in *86* is often encoded by **what can enter an already stable register**.

Shin's syntax remains compressed. Raiden remains rough. Kurena remains emotionally transparent. Frederica remains archaic. Fido remains formal. Lena retains institutional competence even when speaking intimately. Maturation usually does not require linguistic homogenization. It increases the range of truths a person can say while remaining recognizably themselves.

The source-language thesis can therefore be stated compactly:

> **In *86*, linguistic maturity is usually not the replacement of one voice by a healthier voice. It is the expansion of what the existing voice can truthfully admit, ask, refuse, promise, or leave unresolved.**

# II. Method — five levels of linguistic evidence

Document 12 uses five levels of evidence. They should not be collapsed.

| Level | Description | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Exact lexical/grammatical evidence** | A specific Japanese form directly matters to the claim. | `誇りしか`, `戻ってこられる`, `正義たらん`. |
| **Speech-act evidence** | Mood, request, command, permission, or address distributes agency. | `頼ってください`; Lena asking for Shin's judgment after interruption. |
| **Idiolect evidence** | Pronoun, politeness, syntax, sentence length, or register identifies a speaker and relationship. | Frederica's `わらわ / そなた`; Raiden's rough colloquial `俺`. |
| **Focalization evidence** | Narration is colored by the perspective through which information is being processed. | Ernst's benevolent interpretation of the Five; Theo's V08 worldview. |
| **Analytical gloss** | English synthesis names a repeated structure that has no single source lexeme. | “interruptible interdependence,” “purpose capture,” “exhaustive foreclosure.” |

The last category is necessary and legitimate. The error would be pretending an analytical phrase is a direct translation of one Japanese term.

Three additional rules govern translation-sensitive claims.

**First: no dictionary determinism.** A word's common dictionary gloss cannot replace scene syntax, speaker, register, or discourse function.

**Second: no English back-projection.** If four Japanese return systems become English “return,” one may compare them interpretively, but one may not claim the Japanese repeats one master word.

**Third: no title essentialism.** Address forms are relational acts. `女王`, `少佐`, `レーナ`, `死神`, and `アンダーテイカー` tell us not only who is being addressed but what relation the speaker is activating at that moment.

The Phase-5 Japanese lock contains twenty-eight locked control rows. Those rows are not a complete lexicon. They are the high-value distinctions that later synthesis is forbidden to flatten.

# III. Narration and focalization — the prose distributes epistemic authority

## 3.1 Volume 1: the reader is allowed to know Spearhead before Lena does

Volume 1's formal structure makes the ethical problem of distance visible before Lena fully understands it.

Lena begins with voices and tactical interfaces. Para-RAID lets her hear and command while remaining physically protected. The reader, however, is repeatedly taken into Spearhead's base and shown food, animals, teasing, scars, maintenance, basketball, and private relationships that Lena does not know.

Theo's accusation—`名前さえ、一回だって訊いたことがない`—therefore lands after the narrative has already given the reader the particularity Lena lacks [V01-L011]. The form makes the reader comparatively overinformed, then turns that asymmetry into an indictment of abstract sympathy.

Kurena's focalization performs another correction. Shin is not merely the mysterious, emotionally unreadable Processor encountered by Lena. Through a peer who loves and observes him, he becomes comrade, crush, caretaker, frighteningly competent soldier, and person whose own emotional burdens may have no caretaker. The multiplication of perspectives prevents one relationship from monopolizing his legibility.

The Rei interludes create a different epistemic layer. Republic doctrine, Lena's evolving understanding, Spearhead's field knowledge, Shin's sensory access, and the reader's interlude knowledge are not identical. The novel is structured around **unequal access to reality**.

Finally, the memoir frame adds retrospective authority. Older Lena functions as a second-order historical judge, especially in the Republic indictment. That authority is real but should remain distinguishable from immediate-scene focalization. The memoir is retrospective political testimony, not an excuse to treat every thought of younger Lena as authorial omniscience.

## 3.2 Volumes 2–4: interpretation becomes a contested activity

Volume 2 turns Shin into an interpretive object seen differently by multiple observers. Eugene sees quiet kindness. Marcel sees danger. Ernst sees traumatized children. Frederica sees parallels with Kiriya. Grethe sees a gifted officer. Other soldiers see a monster or blade.

The important formal fact is not which observer “wins.” It is that the novel gives enough Shin interiority to show that **each perspective catches something and misses something**.

Ernst is the clearest warning. His love and material generosity are genuine, but his focalized conclusion that the Five are “no longer Eighty-Six” mistakes visible civilian ease for consent to his preferred identity narrative. Benevolence does not eliminate interpretive asymmetry.

Frederica provides a second control. Her ability gives unusual access to people's pasts and presents, but not perfect authority over meaning. Her diagnoses are often penetrating because she is personally implicated in similar structures of inherited purpose. That implication is precisely why they are not neutral revelation.

Volume 4 makes the distinction explicit around Annette and Lena. Annette's childhood memory reveals a version of Shin he does not narrate for himself, but her memory is organized by guilt. Shin's testimony about the past is authoritative regarding his agency but not necessarily exhaustive regarding every causal structure acting through his body and habits. Lena's long interpretation receives narrative weight without becoming a metaphysical ruling. The later memoir itself can soften certainty.

The V2 rule follows:

> **Observation, inference, generalization, and narratorial endorsement are four different evidentiary states.**

## 3.3 Close third person and free-indirect coloring

The series frequently uses close third-person prose in which evaluative language, rhetorical emphasis, or categories belong to the active focalizer even when no quotation marks appear. This is best treated as a spectrum of **free-indirect coloring**, not as a claim that every such sentence is technically textbook free indirect discourse.

The analytical consequence is more important than the label. A narrated sentence can be:

- objectively descriptive;
- directly narratorial;
- filtered through a character's assumptions;
- or deliberately suspended between narrator and focalizer.

Volume 8's Theo sections are a strong example. His focalization carries the sea, cultural rootlessness, the end of war, inherited pride, the reverse-tower insight, and his changing relation to the dead. The novel does not simply pause so an omniscient narrator can announce what Eighty-Six identity means. It lets that argument emerge through a person whose own identity is under active stress.

Kurena then functions as internal counterevidence. Her continued fear of peace, dependence on Shin, and distrust of a postwar future prevent Theo's development from becoming “what the Eighty-Six now understand.”

Volume 14 maintains the same discipline. Myrte's interpretation of release from Republic hatred is useful but character-colored. Lena's reading of Shin's Rei wound is narratively privileged and produces a real breakthrough, yet she also interrupts a statement about continued hatred and supplies an interpretation from outside. The scene should therefore remain both compassionate and epistemically asymmetrical [V14-L195; V14-L197–V14-L199].

## 3.4 Narrator claims are stronger when the prose explicitly steps outside focalized interpretation

Some passages do provide direct narrator-level propositions. Volume 14 explicitly states that Shin has not forgiven Rito's murderer [V14-L049]. It directly describes the Eighty-Six as ordinary human beings with preferences, pleasures, weaknesses, and hurt [V14-L114–V14-L115]. It explicitly treats morale goods as legitimate military requirements and marks Lyudmila's fear of death as a first, anomalous drive for a Sirin.

These should carry more evidentiary weight than a character's diagnosis.

But even narrator claims should be scoped. A direct statement about Shin's nonforgiveness does not establish a universal theory of forgiveness. A narrator statement about Lyudmila does not automatically generalize to every Sirin. Evidence class controls the width of the claim.

# IV. Shin's voice — stable syntax, expanding semantic admission

Shin's linguistic development is one of the clearest cases where translation can create a false arc.

A loose English paraphrase may make early Shin sound emotionally blank and later Shin sound therapeutically articulate. The Japanese supports something subtler.

His baseline is already recognizable in Volume 1:

- plain syntax;
- low lexical excess;
- restrained emotional marking;
- polite `です/ます` toward Lena in the early Handler relation;
- casual masculine speech with peers;
- emotional force created by omission and brevity.

His early future language is correspondingly sparse. `考えたこともありませんね` and `どこに、ですか？` do not establish absence of all desire; they establish the absence of a practiced positive answer to future questions. The same voice can deliver `先にいきます。少佐`, where rank and brevity preserve distance even inside a deeply personal departure [V01-L028].

The important development is **semantic admission**.

By Volume 3, Shin can admit that the old structure has failed to generate a desire of his own. Yet his syntax remains compact. Raiden's and Frederica's interventions do not teach him a new eloquent register; they make new propositions sayable.

Volume 6 is decisive. Direct abandonment petition enters the voice: `おいていかないでください`. Positive future becomes more concrete through the sea and Lena. The answer `生きてるから` is almost aggressively simple [V06-L055]. Its importance comes precisely from refusing philosophical ornament: continued life is enough to defeat a false forced choice.

Volume 8 marks revision through grammar rather than stylistic transformation. `誇りしかないとはもう言わない` closes an old exclusive proposition [V08-L033]. He can tentatively imagine a nonmilitary field without presenting it as a settled career. The modal softness matters: possibility is not plan.

Volume 9 adds explicit uncertainty about his own command judgment and reciprocal support: `支えてもらってたのはおれも同じだ` [V09-L014]. This is not a newly verbose Shin. It is the same concise speaker permitting dependence to become declarable.

Volume 12 produces the deliberately paradoxical `一人きりじゃ戦えない、弱い死神だから` [V12-L029]. The role survives while solitary grandeur is removed from it.

Volume 13's `おれは──弱くて、臆病で、馬鹿だから` remains linguistically plain even as it performs major self-correction [V13-L089]. Volume 14 likewise makes `戻ってこられる` and then `痛かった` carry enormous developmental weight [V14-L044; V14-L197–V14-L198].

The canonical voice rule is therefore:

> **Do not write mature Shin as more rhetorically elaborate merely because he is more self-aware. His development is the admission of more difficult semantic content into a consistently compressed register.**

### Shin voice profile

| Dimension | Stable tendency | Developmental change |
|---|---|---|
| First person | masculine `おれ` | remains stable; social `おれ達` / group language becomes context-sensitive |
| Sentence shape | short, compressed, low-excess | increasingly carries desire, dependence, pain, and uncertainty |
| Politeness | relation-sensitive; especially polite toward Lena early | intimacy changes address more than it erases discipline |
| Emotion | often indirect or minimally labeled | explicit words such as `重かった`, `弱い`, `痛かった` become admissible |
| Role vocabulary | `死神`, `葬儀屋`, `アンダーテイカー` | role becomes revisable and shareable rather than essence |
| Future | initially under-specified | becomes relational, modal, and eventually politically generalizable |

# V. Lena's voice — institutional fluency, private adolescence, and role switching

Lena's Japanese voice develops differently from Shin's because she begins with much more explicit proposition-making.

Her Volume 1 baseline is earnest institutional politeness. She argues morality and constitutional principle openly. She apologizes. She self-corrects. She gives her full name—`ヴラディレーナ・ミリーゼ`—in a relationship where names have become ethically charged [V01-L012].

The danger in English is to treat increasing forcefulness as a one-way movement from polite idealist to commanding Queen. The Japanese instead preserves several simultaneous registers.

She can speak as:

- officer;
- Handler;
- commander;
- Republic citizen;
- `鮮血の女王`;
- supposed `聖女`;
- friend;
- lover;
- frightened adolescent;
- politically reflective citizen.

Those are not stages in which each new identity deletes the previous one.

Volume 3 gives an especially important grammatical self-correction. `忘れないでと、言われましたから` changes into `忘れたくないから`: from “because I was told not to forget” to “because I do not want to forget” [V03-L044]. The syntactic movement is from external assignment toward owned desire.

Volumes 4–6 expose the risk of interpretive confidence. Lena can speak with great diagnostic certainty about Shin while the narrative preserves gaps between what she observes and what she knows. Her better later register often becomes invitation rather than verdict: if you someday want something, tell me. She also recognizes that belief itself may be normative rather than evidential—wanting to believe that the world can still become different does not mean claiming that the world already deserves trust.

Volume 7 complicates Queenhood. `わたしにはこれしかないんです！` uses the same exclusivity structure that the series criticizes elsewhere. Queenhood can therefore become Lena's version of an enclosing identity. At the same time, the confession sequence exposes private jealousy, fluster, and speech-act incompletion. After being kissed, Lena is acutely aware that she has not yet answered `好き`; physical intimacy has not automatically completed the verbal relationship.

Volume 11 makes role switching explicit. Lena can perform `聖女` and `鮮血の女王` theatrically under emergency conditions [V11-L043], then reject the tragic-saint role through `もう`: `悲壮面の聖女は、もうしません` [V11-L045]. The title is available as political performance without being accepted as exhaustive self.

Volume 13 shows perhaps the richest code-switching in one line: `〈アンダーテイカー〉。ヴラディレーナ・ミリーゼ大佐、指揮に復帰します。──心配かけました、シン` [V13-L082]. Callsign and full military self-identification re-establish command; the final given name restores the private relation inside the same transmission.

Volume 14 continues the duality. Queen-language becomes necessary when command requires the mask [V14-L080], but `その人はわたしのものだ！ 返せ！` is not bureaucratic Queen speech [V14-L177]. It is possessive, intimate, emergency rhetoric. A translation that purifies it into abstract selfless protection loses something real; a reading that converts it into standing ownership loses something equally real.

### Lena voice profile

| Dimension | Stable tendency | Developmental change |
|---|---|---|
| First person | `わたし` | stable across roles; authority shifts through syntax and address rather than pronoun replacement |
| Officer register | explicit, orderly, proposition-rich | grows more decisive while learning to mark uncertainty |
| Self-correction | present from early volumes | increasingly separates duty, guilt, desire, and political judgment |
| Titles | rank, `女王`, `鮮血の女王`, `聖女` | become consciously deployable masks rather than one mature identity |
| Private register | embarrassment, jealousy, food/shopping, romance | remains adolescent and relational alongside command competence |
| Interpretive risk | confidence can sound authoritative | later corpus increasingly requires her to restore another's answer rather than own it |

# VI. Spearhead voices — ethical intelligence does not require philosophical diction

## 6.1 Raiden

Raiden's rough masculine colloquial register is one of the series' strongest controls against equating formal language with conceptual depth.

`俺は、死にたくねえ。それだけだ` gives one of Volume 3's deepest ethical answers in ordinary rough speech [V03-L025]. He does not offer a theory of existential value. He states that not wanting to die is enough.

Later, trust remains similarly plain: `俺はあんたを信頼してるぜ、レーナ。だからあんたも信じてくれよ` [V14-L129]. The sentence is conceptually sophisticated—reciprocal trust under uncertainty—without changing Raiden into an academic moralist.

**Reconstruction rule:** preserve roughness, brevity, and practical framing. Do not make Raiden sound more theoretical simply because analysis can formalize his reasoning.

## 6.2 Theo

Theo begins with cutting directness. His V01 accusation is linguistically important because its force comes from concrete omission: Lena never even asked their names [V01-L011]. His voice later retains sarcasm and critique while gaining gentler forms of accommodation.

His V08–V09 language is especially sensitive to function. He can say he can live as he is, then after injury say he cannot fight in the same way while explicitly separating that from whether he must die. Translation should not allow “cannot fight” to become “has no future” or “is retired forever.”

Theo's analytical intelligence often appears as plain contrast rather than formal thesis. His voice therefore supports the disability synthesis precisely because it is not sanitized into therapeutic language.

## 6.3 Kurena

Kurena's `あたし` and emotionally transparent style remain stable as her judgment matures. This matters because maturation should not be represented as movement toward Lena-like formality or Shin-like restraint.

The V09 progression from `戦うしかない` / `誇りしかない` to named fear of powerlessness and finally `今でも好き` shows increasing precision without abandonment of youthful register. Volume 14 similarly lets `あたし` carry grief, self-assertion, and the statement that she is no longer merely the little sister [V14-L029].

**Reconstruction rule:** emotional transparency is not immaturity by itself. Mature Kurena should remain recognizable as Kurena.

## 6.4 Shiden

Shiden's rough colloquial command register gives her a unique relation to Lena.

`しっかりしろよ女王陛下ァ` is simultaneously irreverent, loyal, commanding, and intimate [V08-L039]. `あんたが信じてやらなくてどうする` follows in the same register [V08-L040]. She can use Queen-address without reproducing deferential court language.

Her shift toward personal `レーナ` in lower-pressure contexts matters because address is a social action. It creates a relation distinct from formal staff subordination and from Shin/Lena romance.

## 6.5 Group pronouns and the danger of `we`

Volume 13 makes collective pronoun drift politically important. `おれ` can become `おれ達`; heterogeneous targets become `あいつら`; “people unlike us” become easier to classify as one opposing category.

The linguistic point should not be overclaimed. Pronouns do not cause persecution. They show **category expansion and responsibility diffusion** in speech at the moment social grievance is becoming collective.

This is one reason translation must preserve when a statement moves from “I” to “we.” The shift may be politically substantive even when English prose would sound smoother by varying pronouns stylistically.

# VII. Frederica, Lerche, Fido, and Vika — marked register as identity evidence

## 7.1 Frederica: `わらわ / そなた` is part of the person

Frederica's archaizing register is not ornamental medieval flavor pasted on top of an otherwise ordinary child voice.

Alter.1 supplies unusually explicit metalinguistic evidence: `言葉の選び方もわらわの一部じゃ`—her choice of words is part of herself. The register therefore has canonical characterization weight.

Her stable `わらわ / そなた` system persists through comedy, fear, grief, accusation, and political reflection. Because the register remains stable while content changes, it becomes an ideal example of V2's broader voice principle: **development happens inside idiolect**.

The archaic style can carry ordinary affection, sharp ethical diagnosis, and self-rule. `わらわ自身の王となろうぞ` would lose considerable character information if flattened into generic modern therapeutic language [V12-L063].

**Reconstruction rule:** preserve archaization consistently enough to mark identity, but do not make every line grandiose. Frederica's voice can be playful, childish, frightened, or practical inside the same historical register.

## 7.2 Lerche: retainer speech is both designed role and individual voice

Lerche's `それがし`, formal negative forms, honorifics, and `死神殿` mark a retainer register. The form is institutionally inherited, but it is also recognizably hers.

This is philosophically useful. Artificial or role-designed origin does not imply absence of individuality. The same speech pattern can signal both constructed provenance and a stable individual style.

Her assertion that she is not the donor person—`それがしは...レルヒェリート殿ではない`—is especially important because the marked retainer voice belongs to the successor subject making the distinction [V05-L027].

V14 preserves `死神殿` within that voice [V14-L075]. A translation that standardizes Lerche into neutral military English would remove part of the evidence that she is a distinct speaker rather than a generic artificial soldier.

## 7.3 Fido: formal interior first person and artificial self-continuity

Fido's Volume 10 interiority uses formal `わたくし`. The voice is stable across technical exposition, affection, guilt, memory, and decision.

The important point is not that formal Japanese proves personhood. It adds one property to the matrix: Fido possesses a linguistically coherent self-model capable of referring to continuity before and after “becoming myself.” His artificial origin and formal diction do not prevent relation-bearing particularity.

**Translation rule:** do not anthropomorphize Fido by making him casually human, but do not erase the formal first-person structure that supports a stable artificial subject voice.

## 7.4 Vika: elevated intellect can collapse into child-simple need

Vika often speaks through aristocratic, technical, or highly intellectual vocabulary. His most revealing lines can nevertheless collapse into elementary motive: he wanted to see someone.

This register contrast is important because it prevents high intelligence from becoming his entire linguistic identity. A translation that makes all of Vika uniformly ornate would erase the moments when extreme conceptual sophistication gives way to emotionally primitive directness.

# VIII. Address is relational action — rank, callsign, title, surname, given name

Japanese permits relationships to change through forms of address without requiring explicit exposition.

A useful address model asks four questions:

1. **What name or title is used?**
2. **Who is speaking?**
3. **What role is being activated?**
4. **What alternatives could have been used but were not?**

## 8.1 `少佐` and military distance

Shin's V01 `先にいきます。少佐` retains rank at a moment of emotional departure [V01-L028]. The address keeps the relationship inside a professional and unequal structure even as the content becomes personal.

Later rank use does not automatically mean the same thing. Once Shin and Lena occupy the same military institution and have a private relationship, rank can function as ordinary protocol rather than Republic Handler/Processor hierarchy.

Diachrony matters.

## 8.2 `アンダーテイカー`

The callsign can be:

- tactical identifier;
- chosen name;
- inherited role marker;
- symbolic office;
- and later something separable from Shin himself.

Volume 13's `〈アンダーテイカー〉。ヴラディレーナ・ミリーゼ大佐...──心配かけました、シン` deliberately moves from callsign to rank to given name within one communication [V13-L082]. It is almost a miniature grammar of role plurality.

## 8.3 `死神 / 葬儀屋 / アンダーテイカー`

English can make these look like one symbol. Japanese keeps several layers.

`死神` is an epithet/image. `葬儀屋` is the ordinary occupation word used to explain the role. `アンダーテイカー` is the stylized callsign/name. `我らが死神` adds collective affection and reliance [V10-L027]. `死神殿` adds Lerche's honorific retainer relation [V14-L075]. `弱い死神` revises the role internally rather than abandoning it [V12-L029]. Volume 10's counterfactual says Shin did not have to remain `死神` at all [V10-L060].

The title is therefore relational and historical, not metaphysical essence.

## 8.4 `女王 / 鮮血の女王 / 女王陛下 / 聖女 / レーナ`

Lena's title network is even more variable.

`鮮血の女王` can be battlefield reputation and self-deployed command identity [V02-L050; V11-L043]. `女王陛下` can be Shiden's deliberately rough, affectionate challenge rather than deferential monarchy [V08-L039]. `聖女` can be public myth, tactical performance, or a role Lena rejects [V11-L004; V11-L045]. Personal `レーナ` can signal intimacy or a relationship that refuses to reduce her to office.

One title therefore cannot be declared her “true mature identity.”

## 8.5 Pronouns and relational stance

Pronouns similarly matter without becoming mechanical personality tests.

- Shin's `おれ` supports a plain masculine baseline.
- Raiden's `俺` participates in a rougher colloquial register.
- Kurena's `あたし` contributes youth and emotional immediacy.
- Lena's `わたし` remains compatible with officer, lover, and political citizen roles.
- Frederica's `わらわ` is marked, archaizing, and self-consciously identity-bearing.
- Lerche's `それがし` marks retainer persona.
- Fido's `わたくし` creates formal artificial interiority.

These forms should inform voice reconstruction, not be converted into deterministic personality labels.

# IX. `帰る`, `戻る`, `還る`, `帰還`, `生還` — English “return” is analytically dangerous

The largest lexical correction concerns **return**.

| Japanese | Dominant tendency in this corpus | Translation-sensitive risk |
|---|---|---|
| `帰る` | homeward, destination-oriented, recipient-oriented | English “return” can hide the person/place one comes back to |
| `戻る` | re-entry, restoration, movement back to prior place/state | can describe recovery **or** reactionary regression |
| `還る` | deathward/metaphysical release | can be falsely merged with romantic/home return |
| `帰還` | operational return from deployment | can be over-metaphorized into psychological recovery |
| `生還` | returning alive | can be flattened into generic mission completion |

## 9.1 `帰る`: return has an address

V03's `帰る故郷はなく、守る家族もなく` shows the absence of both place and people to return to [V03-L011]. V06 then makes Lena a relational return-address without turning her into the only possible home. V08–V10 repeatedly use return within relationships and military life.

The Japanese supports the idea that return is often **recipient-shaped**. It does not support the stronger claim that every return in the series is one metaphysical process of restoration.

## 9.2 `戻る`: recovery and regression share the same broad semantic space

Frederica's `先に進めぬ、ではない。戻れぬ、であろ` distinguishes inability to move forward from inability to go back [V04-L015].

Volume 14 later gives both positive and negative controls. Shin's `戻ってこられる` asserts recoverable possibility [V14-L044]. The reactionary call to return Giad to empire uses the same broad prior-state logic.

This is why “return” itself cannot be the good. What matters is **what one returns to, whether the movement is possible rather than compulsory, and whose judgment defines the prior state as desirable**.

## 9.3 `還る`: the dead passing on is not coming home to Lena

The orthographic choice marks deathward or metaphysical release in Legion/dead contexts. It should not be silently translated into the same thematic bucket as `帰る`.

At a high interpretive level one may compare the structures: both resist indefinite captivity. But the source language itself distinguishes dying into proper rest from going home to a living addressee.

## 9.4 `帰還 / 生還`: military institutions reveal themselves through return expectations

`生還` and `帰還` belong first to operational grammar. The Federacy's determination to bring the Strike Package back alive is institutionally significant [V08-L017]. V13's denial of `帰還` becomes politically important because blocked return changes the ethical structure of military service [V13-L115].

But the military meaning should remain visible before it is converted into metaphor.

## 9.5 `行ってくる / ただいま`: home is embedded in ordinary speech acts

T12's V09 control is especially valuable. `行ってくる` differs pragmatically from a neutral “I'm leaving.” It conventionally contains anticipated return. `ただいま` marks arrival back into a social place.

This is stronger evidence for ordinary relational home than simply counting the noun “home.” The future is encoded in a mundane formula.

# X. `故郷` and `祖国` — lived home and political homeland must remain distinct

English often uses “home,” “homeland,” or “country” flexibly enough to conceal an important Japanese distinction.

`故郷` usually foregrounds lived or remembered place, local origin, or somewhere one imagines returning to. V03 can say the Eighty-Six have no `故郷` to return to [V03-L011]. V13 can make return to a peaceful hometown a concrete lost possibility [V13-L091; V13-L124].

`祖国` is more political-historical. It is the national homeland one may defend, lose, condemn, mourn, or remain historically entangled with. Kiriya can speak of destroying homeland and family [V05-L011]. Lena can grieve a destroyed `祖国` without thereby endorsing restoration of San Magnolia as a desirable polity [V11-L055].

The mature home model therefore cannot be:

> home is chosen rather than inherited.

T14-C31 correctly rejects that binary. A person may simultaneously possess:

- birthplace;
- `故郷`;
- `祖国`;
- citizenship;
- household;
- unit belonging;
- family;
- memorial landscape;
- and a chosen future destination.

Translation should specify which relation is active rather than collapsing them into one emotionally loaded English noun.

# XI. `一緒に` and `共に` — togetherness has register and material prerequisites

`一緒に` and `共に` overlap. They are not a rigid semantic binary.

Still, the corpus supports useful tendencies.

`一緒に` frequently marks ordinary proximity and shared activity. `一緒に生きたい` in Volume 9 sounds relationally immediate [V09-L019]. `一緒に帰る` similarly makes return a shared practical act [V10-L015].

`共に` can carry a more elevated, literary, normative, or burden-sharing quality. Frederica tells Shin to use those who go `共に` as support [V03-L033]. Shin's Volume 7 confession uses `共に` in future-bearing declarations [V07-L053–V07-L054]. Volume 9's co-bearing language again uses the elevated form around shared burden [V09-L017].

The crucial control is Volume 1. Lena suppresses “together” because she can move freely while the Eighty-Six cannot. The narration recognizes that a word of shared experience would be ethically premature where material conditions are radically asymmetric.

The development is therefore not simply increased frequency of “together.” It is that later institutions and relationships sometimes become **materially capable of supporting a word that earlier would have disguised inequality**.

# XII. `誇り`, `しか`, `呪い`, `願い`, `絆`, `祈り` — binding is not one moral category

The source-language correction to the pride thesis is decisive.

Volume 3 says:

> `戦い抜く誇りしか、おれ達にはない` [V03-L012]

The restrictive `しか` matters. The problem is not simply `誇り`. It is **pride-only**: pride as the only admissible support for identity.

Volume 8 later explicitly revises the restrictive proposition: `誇りしかないとはもう言わない` [V08-L033]. The old identity is not discarded. Its exclusivity loses authority.

Volume 9 then provides the lexical taxonomy:

> `誇り。願い。絆。祈り。あるいは──呪い。` [V09-L001]

`呪い` is therefore not a technical word meaning “toxic attachment.” It belongs to a semantic field of binding forces whose valence depends on relation and answer-space.

The same volume can call pride a wound and curse [V09-L053]. Volume 13 broadens the field further: words, wishes, prayers, and emotions can bind, distort paths, and become `呪い`; an accepted binding can nevertheless be called love [V13-L113–V13-L114].

The translation rule is:

> **Gloss `呪い` first as a context-sensitive binding metaphor. Then ask whether the binding is inherited, coercive, sustaining, chosen, destructive, welcomed, or impossible to escape.**

T14-C07 therefore remains mandatory: Eighty-Six pride is not simply a pathology to outgrow.

# XIII. `従う`, `支える`, `信じる`, `頼る` — authority ethics lives in verbs and mood

Volume 12 supplies one of the strongest direct source-language confirmations of the V2 relationship model:

> `従うのではなく支えてくれた` [V12-L067]

Obedience and support are lexically contrasted. The same cluster also distinguishes being demanded from being trusted.

This matters because English “support” is too broad. A protector can:

- obey;
- support;
- trust;
- rely;
- monitor;
- command;
- restrain;
- ask;
- or decide for another person.

Those are not interchangeable merely because the motive is care.

`頼る` is equally important. Early inability to rely on others belongs to Shin's isolation. Later `頼ってください` becomes an explicit invitation to dependence [V09-L017]. Yet the series does not make dependence itself the good. A child may be forced into self-reliance because dependable support is structurally absent; conversely, permanent dependence can be demanded by guardianship.

The correct source-language inference is narrower:

> **Healthy support preserves another person's ability to answer.**

No Japanese noun directly means “interruptible interdependence.” That phrase remains an analytical synthesis. T14-C11 narrows it appropriately: interruption is agency-preserving only when bounded by emergency or incapacity and followed by restoration of judgment.

Speech act matters as much as vocabulary. An order, request, permission, promise, and self-statement distribute agency differently. Volume 13's command recovery is exemplary: after Raiden interrupts Shin physically, Lena seeks the squadron commander's judgment rather than installing her own answer as permanent substitute [V13-L082; V13-L084].

# XIV. `自由`, `平等`, `人権`, `幻想`, `正義` — political vocabulary is contested, not narratorial scripture

Political abstraction in *86* is unusually vulnerable to quotation without speaker context.

## 14.1 `自由`

`自由` can mean:

- formal legal liberty;
- the right to mourn [V01-L006];
- absence of external command;
- burdensome responsibility to choose;
- usable alternatives;
- or an ideological slogan.

Volume 12's `それが『自由』だと押しつけられた` captures the experience of formal emancipation as imposed burden [V12-L024]. The novel does not thereby declare freedom bad. It distinguishes release from coercion from **capability for self-government**.

## 14.2 `幻想`

Volume 13's line that rights, freedom, equality, and justice are `幻想` is easy to mistranslate philosophically [V13-L048].

In context, `幻想` does not mean worthless delusion. The surrounding proposition is that these norms do not exist as self-enforcing physical objects and therefore must be sustained by citizens.

A controlled English gloss is:

> **socially maintained ideal / normative construction**

rather than “fake,” “illusion” without explanation, or “delusion.”

## 14.3 `正義たらん` versus `正義である`

Volume 14 gives the project's strongest grammar-to-politics finding.

The revolutionary motto is:

> `我ら、世界に誇る正義たらん` [V14-L001]

The archaic/literary `たらん` is aspirational or volitional: roughly, **let us be / may we become justice worthy of pride before the world**. The polity stands under a standard.

The reactionary paraphrase becomes:

> `我らはすなわち正義である` [V14-L206]

`である` makes identity declarative. The polity no longer aspires to justice; it identifies itself with justice.

This grammatical shift strengthens the institutional thesis beyond thematic paraphrase. It shows how a corrigible norm becomes self-authenticating political identity.

Earlier controls matter. V11 speaks of the `正義の美名`, the beautiful name/reputation of justice [V11-L035]. V12 can accuse the righteous country of cowardice [V12-L058]. `正義` is therefore contested vocabulary, never a magic narrator label proving institutional goodness.

# XV. “Personhood” is a matrix — `人格`, `疑似人格`, `自我`, `自己同一性`, `意志`, `意思`

The English philosophical word **personhood** is useful for synthesis and dangerous for translation.

The Japanese corpus distributes relevant properties across multiple terms.

| Term | Controlled tendency in this corpus | What it does **not** prove by itself |
|---|---|---|
| `人格` | personality / person-like configuration | full moral standing or autonomy |
| `疑似人格` | artificial/constructed personality provenance | moral falseness or nonpersonhood |
| `自我` | ego/self | stable numerical identity |
| `自己同一性` | identity continuity / self-identity | complete agency |
| `意志` | will/volitional drive | practical ability to disobey |
| `意思` | intention/judgment/volition in context | a fixed metaphysical faculty distinct in every usage from `意志` |

Kiriya's Shepherd description explicitly joins `人格と意志` while also stating inability to disobey superior command [V03-L035]. That is precisely why personality plus will does not equal sovereign autonomy.

Volume 4 separately marks `自己同一性` in the problem of personality-bearing replication [V04-L022]. Volume 6 uses `疑似人格` for recreated artificial personality [V06-L008]. Volume 8 can contain multiple `人格` and `自我` inside one fused system [V08-L038]. Volume 12's `己の意思という夾雑物` presents individual will from the standpoint of a system that treats it as operational impurity [V12-L002].

The source language therefore forces property-by-property ontology.

A later synthesis should never write “the Japanese says X has personhood” when the actual passage says only that X has `人格`, `自我`, memory, will, or continuity. Those findings may contribute to moral-standing analysis, but the philosophical conclusion remains synthesis.

T14-C30 remains mandatory: artificial origin is not evidence of inferior standing. Equally, artificial sophistication is not by itself evidence of full personhood.

---

# XVI. `赦す` and `許す` — orthography helps, context governs

The corpus supports a real but limited orthographic tendency.

`赦す` frequently appears in morally weighty forgiveness or absolution contexts. `許す` is broader and often covers permission, allowance, toleration, approval, or ordinary moral forgiveness.

The error would be to build a hard rule:

> `赦す` = forgiveness; `許す` = permission.

Japanese usage is not that clean.

The correct method is:

1. note orthography;
2. identify syntactic object and discourse context;
3. determine whether moral absolution, permission, tolerance, or another sense is active;
4. then translate.

V14 gives a useful direct narrator control: Shin has not `赦して` Rito's murderer [V14-L049]. V11's `全然、まだ赦せない` similarly marks explicit nonforgiveness. The `まだ` matters as much as the kanji: the state is present and may endure, but grammar does not require analysts to turn it into an eternal essence.

This preserves T14-C22: nonrevenge is not forgiveness.

# XVII. Temporal grammar — `もう`, `まだ`, `また`, `いつか`, `これから`

Small adverbs carry a disproportionate amount of developmental information.

## 17.1 `もう`: strongest when an old proposition is explicitly discontinued

Not every `もう` means growth. Many occurrences simply mean “already” or “anymore.”

The high-value pattern is:

> **old proposition + `もう` + negated continuation**

Shin's `誇りしかないとはもう言わない` is exemplary [V08-L033]. Lena's `悲壮面の聖女は、もうしません` similarly rejects continuation of a performed role [V11-L045].

## 17.2 `まだ`: incompletion without finality

`まだ` is one of the corpus's most important protections against premature closure.

It can mean:

- still;
- not yet;
- remaining;
- open incompletion.

It should not automatically be translated analytically as unresolved pathology. “Not yet” preserves temporal room.

## 17.3 `また`: recurrence as future structure

`また` presupposes another time. This matters in a series where early life is organized by terminal expectation.

A future need not first appear as a grand career plan. It can appear as another meal, another visit, another argument, another deployment from which someone expects return.

## 17.4 `いつか / これから`: projection without certainty

`いつか` makes future possibility imaginable without claiming schedule or guarantee. `これから` establishes a forward orientation from the present.

These forms matter because future can become linguistically real before it becomes institutionally secure.

## 17.5 Potential grammar: possibility is not redemption

The canonical example is `戻ってこられる` [V14-L044]. The potential asserts capacity. It does not promise success.

T14-C40 therefore remains critical: Shin's formulation supports **modal political recovery**, not a guarantee that every person or polity will return to justice.

# XVIII. Institutional and racial language — dehumanization works through administrative vocabulary

The Republic's racial order is linguistic as well as legal.

Several words do political work by changing the category under which violence is counted.

| Term | Institutional function | Translation-sensitive caution |
|---|---|---|
| `エイティシックス` | imposed status for expelled former citizens; later also reclaimed identity | do not treat imposed category and chosen identity as identical historical acts |
| `有色種` | broad racialized Colorata category | internally diverse; not one ethnicity |
| `アルバ` | racial/phenotypic and political privilege category | not synonym for identical moral guilt in every individual |
| `プロセッサー` | information processor replacing soldier/person | technical euphemism is part of administrative dehumanization |
| `ハンドラー` | remote control/command office | later legitimate command use does not erase original asymmetry |
| `無人機` | official fiction of unmanned warfare | depends on defining operators outside counted humanity |
| `家畜 / 豚` | livestock/pig language | overt ontological degradation and later counter-insult |
| `白ブタ` | retaliatory/reclaimed insult toward Republic Alba | does not become neutral demographic vocabulary merely because victims use it |

Volume 1 gives the mechanism compactly: people are declared not human and reclassified as `情報処理装置` [V01-L003]. The memoir epigraph's pig/rights formulation shows the political consequence [V01-L001].

Later vocabulary demonstrates that category can be contested from within. Shin explicitly rejects being livestock or a component of an unmanned machine [V07-L012]. He can simultaneously call Lena `共和国人` and the Eighty-Six's `女王` [V07-L013]. Shiden can distinguish a Republic person from `白ブタ` as a moralized insult [V07-L026].

Translation should therefore preserve the difference among:

- legal/racial classification;
- institutional job label;
- overt slur;
- reclaimed group identity;
- and contextual insult.

The existence of the same English noun across scenes does not guarantee the same social act.

# XIX. Military terminology — function, professionalism, and disposal are encoded differently

Military vocabulary should not be translated as generic action language when institutional distinctions matter.

`撤退` is legitimate tactical withdrawal, not cowardice. `帰還` and `生還` encode organized return and survival. `退役` marks exit from military role rather than disappearance of person. `兵科が変わる` ("if the service branch changes"; V09, `p-014.xhtml ¶033`, source-reverified in Phase 8) marks a change of military branch within continued service. `プロセッサー` is politically loaded because San Magnolia uses it to replace soldierhood with component status, while later military institutions can use the inherited term differently.

Hero vocabulary requires equal caution. `英雄` can signify genuine exceptional achievement while also naming structural dependency. The series is not linguistically anti-heroic in the sense of denying courage. It distinguishes admired action from an institution that requires exceptional people as permanent infrastructure.

The same is true of Shin's `死神` role. Role vocabulary can be militarily functional, socially affectionate, self-authored, inherited, or imposed depending on speaker.

Document 07 remains the primary home for tactical/operational/strategic analysis. Document 12's narrower conclusion is that **translation must preserve whether the Japanese is talking about function, office, status, survival, withdrawal, or mythic role rather than normalizing them into generic “soldier” language**.

# XX. Translation-sensitive negative controls — where smooth English would become analytically wrong

The following formulations are prohibited or require qualification in later synthesis.

| Smooth English formulation | Source-language correction |
|---|---|
| “Return is the master motif.” | At least `帰る`, `戻る`, `還る`, `帰還`, `生還`, plus ordinary departure/return speech acts. |
| “Home is chosen, not inherited.” | Separate `故郷`, `祖国`, citizenship, household, return-address, memorial place, and chosen destination. |
| “Togetherness simply increases.” | `一緒に` / `共に` have overlapping register tendencies; V01 explicitly identifies materially false togetherness. |
| “Pride becomes a curse.” | `しか` exclusivity is often the actual problem; `呪い` is a context-sensitive binding metaphor. |
| “Support means dependence.” | `従う`, `支える`, `信じる`, `頼る`, command, request, and permission distribute agency differently. |
| “Rights are illusions.” | V13 `幻想` means non-self-enforcing normative construction in context, not worthless delusion. |
| “The Federacy believes it is justice.” | Early `正義たらん` is aspirational; V14 `正義である` is the reactionary shift into identity possession. |
| “The Japanese calls X a person.” | Often the source actually supplies `人格`, `自我`, `自己同一性`, `意志`, memory, or behavior—not one personhood lexeme. |
| “Shin becomes expressive.” | He remains low-excess; what changes is semantic admission. |
| “Queen is Lena's true mature identity.” | Queen/Saint/rank/personal-name systems remain relation- and speaker-dependent. |
| “Death God is Shin's essence.” | `死神`, `葬儀屋`, and `アンダーテイカー` are historical role/address systems that can be revised or deputized. |
| “Not yet means unresolved pathology.” | `まだ` often preserves healthy incompletion and temporal openness. |
| “`赦す` and `許す` are two perfectly separate concepts.” | Orthographic tendency only; context governs. |
| “Narration says X, therefore X is objective.” | First identify whether the passage is narrator assertion, close focalization, free-indirect coloring, character claim, memoir judgment, or paratext. |

These are not cosmetic translation preferences. Each can materially change the ethical or philosophical argument.

# XXI. Narratorial authority matrix

A reusable evidence hierarchy for later synthesis is:

| Evidence form | Default authority | Required caution |
|---|---|---|
| Direct narrator fact | high within stated scope | do not universalize beyond entity/scene without support |
| Retrospective memoir claim | high historical testimony, situated narrator | older Lena is authoritative but still a historical subject |
| Character speech | direct evidence of speaker position | truth of proposition remains separate |
| Close focalized narration | strong evidence of experience/interpretation | do not upgrade to omniscient fact |
| Free-indirect coloring | strong evidence of perspective | boundaries may be deliberately porous |
| Technical explanation | high for mechanism when reliable | speaker competence and uncertainty still matter |
| Enemy/recruitment claim | evidence of claimed mechanism/worldview | strategic deception and persuasion remain possible |
| Afterword/paratext | useful authorial/structural evidence | not automatically diegetic fact |
| Translation gloss | analytical convenience | must not overwrite Japanese distinctions |

Several major V2 corrections follow from this matrix.

Frederica is diagnostically powerful but not an oracle. Ernst's compassion does not make his interpretation of recovery authoritative. Lena can be correct and still overstep. Theo's conclusions can be profound and still be Theo's conclusions. No Face's technical access does not make a coercive sales pitch neutral. Narrator-level confirmation carries more weight, but its scope remains bounded.

This epistemic discipline is part of the literary form, not merely an external research precaution. *86* repeatedly asks who gets to name another person's condition—and then complicates the answer by changing focalizer.

# XXII. Character voice reconstruction guide

For future comparative or hypothetical dialogue, the following profiles should govern reconstruction.

| Character | Preserve | Avoid |
|---|---|---|
| **Shin** | terse syntax; plain masculine `おれ`; relation-sensitive politeness; dry humor; hard emotional nouns/adjectives stated simply | therapeutic monologues; ornate introspection; constant stoicism without humor |
| **Lena** | clear propositions; institutional competence; `わたし`; ability to switch between officer and private register; self-correction | treating Queen speech as her only mature voice; making every insight omniscient |
| **Raiden** | rough masculine colloquial speech; practical ethics; concise challenge | academic philosophical diction |
| **Theo** | directness, sarcasm, critique, artistic sensitivity, later accommodation | turning disability into permanent gentleness or passivity |
| **Kurena** | `あたし`; emotional transparency; blunt fear/desire; growing precision | “maturing” her into formal restraint |
| **Shiden** | rough colloquial confidence; irreverent Queen-address; social perception | formal subordinate speech as default |
| **Frederica** | archaizing `わらわ / そなた`; child emotion inside imperial diction | generic fantasy grandiloquence or completely modern speech |
| **Lerche** | retainer `それがし`; formal/honorific service register; distinct artificial individuality | neutralizing her into generic military speech |
| **Fido** | formal `わたくし`; precise technical and relational interiority | casual human slang or emotionless machine prose |
| **Vika** | aristocratic/intellectual complexity with occasional child-simple motive | uniform ornate detachment |

The most important reconstruction rule is negative:

> **Do not translate character development into convergence toward one supposedly mature speech style.**

The series values distinct voices. A person can become more reflective while remaining rough, archaic, youthful, formal, terse, or sarcastic.

# XXIV. T14 adversarial controls for Japanese-language synthesis

Document 12 does not reopen T14. It explains where source language most strongly constrains its dispositions.

- **T14-C01:** `まだ`, potential grammar, and role language do not make endless revision the supreme good. They preserve possibility and nonfinality where the text does so.
- **T14-C04:** one English “recoverability” metaphor must not erase `帰る / 戻る / 還る / 帰還 / 生還` distinctions.
- **T14-C05:** `戻る` can name political regression as well as recovery; return to a prior state is not inherently good.
- **T14-C07:** `誇りしか` makes exclusivity a central problem; `誇り` itself cannot be declared pathological.
- **T14-C11:** “interruptible interdependence” is synthesis, not a source keyword; the Japanese supports it through command/request/support/trust contrasts.
- **T14-C12:** Shin/Lena is not linguistically pure non-possession. V14's `ものだ` possessive syntax must remain visible [V14-L177].
- **T14-C20:** future language and present aliveness do not prove bodily self-care; linguistic progress can coexist with concealment.
- **T14-C22:** `赦す` evidence preserves nonforgiveness separately from revenge.
- **T14-C24:** justice language is grammatically corrigible in `たらん` and politically dangerous when converted to `である`.
- **T14-C30:** `疑似人格` describes constructional provenance, not inferior standing.
- **T14-C31:** `故郷`, `祖国`, citizenship, household, and return-address defeat a chosen/inherited-home binary.
- **T14-C32:** political membership vocabulary prevents citizenship from being treated as superficial paperwork.
- **T14-C37:** No Face/Vaclav language can strongly direct identity while leaving continuity mechanism open.
- **T14-C38:** Lyudmila's language cannot be generalized to all Sirins without further evidence.
- **T14-C40:** `戻ってこられる` is potential, not a guarantee.
- **T14-C41:** future-oriented grammar does not tell us how becoming operates once the war ends.

# XXVI. Translation protocol for final synthesis

The eventual continuous synthesis should follow these rules whenever Japanese distinctions carry analytical weight.

1. **Give the Japanese anchor when the argument depends on it.** Do not sprinkle Japanese ornamentally.
2. **Keep quotations short.** The analytical work should occur in gloss and comparison.
3. **Name the speaker or focalizer.** Political and philosophical nouns are frequently contested.
4. **Distinguish lexical fact from analytical synthesis.** “Purpose capture” may be useful even though no single Japanese expression maps onto it.
5. **Preserve grammatical modality.** Potential, negation, `まだ`, and `もう` can change the claim's truth conditions.
6. **Preserve address transitions.** Rank, title, callsign, surname, given name, and intimate address are relational evidence.
7. **Preserve restrictive particles.** `しか` may matter more than the noun it modifies.
8. **Do not essentialize marked idiolect.** Archaic or rough speech is character evidence, not a total personality description.
9. **Do not upgrade focalized interpretation to narrator truth.** Identify evidence state first.
10. **Do not translate every recurrence consistently if the Japanese is not actually the same word.** Conceptual elegance is subordinate to source structure.
11. **Conversely, do not vary translation decoratively when repetition itself matters.** Repeated forms such as `もう` + negation or an address shift may be analytically significant.
12. **Use gloss notes where English cannot carry all distinctions economically.** One controlled note is better than a misleadingly smooth master term.

A compact convention for the final synthesis can be:

> Japanese anchor — literal/near-literal gloss — contextual analytical gloss.

For example:

> `戻ってこられる` — “can come back” — **recoverability / capacity for return, not guaranteed redemption** [V14-L044].

This makes the inferential step visible rather than disguising it as translation.

# XXVII. Final synthesis

Reading *86* in Japanese does not reveal that its English-visible themes were wrong. It reveals where they were **too unitary**.

“Return” fragments into homecoming, restoration, deathward release, extraction, and survival. “Home” fragments into lived origin, political homeland, citizenship, household, memorial landscape, and a person who waits. “Together” acquires register and material prerequisites. “Pride” becomes dangerous most clearly when grammar makes it the only support available. “Curse” becomes a field of binding rather than a moral verdict. “Support” becomes distinguishable from obedience because the novel itself places the verbs against each other. “Personhood” dissolves into a property matrix because Japanese does not provide one stable lexical shortcut. “Justice” changes political meaning through a shift from aspiration to declarative identity. “Not yet” refuses to become “never.”

The same source-language discipline transforms character reading.

Shin's maturation is not a journey from silence into eloquence. He remains terse. The change is that words such as `弱い`, `戻る`, `生きてる`, and finally `痛かった` become sayable without requiring him to become a different speaker.

Lena's maturation is not a replacement of polite officer by Bloody Queen. She becomes increasingly capable of moving among institutional, commanding, intimate, embarrassed, political, and performative registers while learning that accurate interpretation does not grant ownership of another person's meaning.

Raiden can articulate moral sufficiency in rough speech. Kurena can mature without abandoning `あたし`. Frederica's archaization is explicitly part of herself. Lerche's designed retainer language is also individualized voice. Fido's artificial self is linguistically formal rather than mechanically blank. Shiden can call Lena Queen in a register that is almost the opposite of deferential monarchy.

Narration follows the same plural logic. The novels repeatedly grant strong perspectives without making them absolute. The reader is moved among protected Handler, segregated soldiers, children, commanders, civilians, artificial subjects, enemies, and retrospective historians. A sentence can be narratorial truth, character-colored thought, memoir judgment, technical explanation, or strategic persuasion. The form continually asks the reader to know **who is doing the naming**.

This produces Document 12's mature conclusion:

> **The deepest Japanese-language pattern in *86* is not a secret vocabulary of freedom or return. It is a grammar of nonfinality and situated naming. People and institutions are constantly named—soldier, Processor, Eighty-Six, Queen, Saint, Death God, citizen, weapon, human, monster—and the novels repeatedly ask whether that name describes one relation or is being allowed to consume the whole person.**

The source language does not solve that problem by demanding infinite fluidity. Stable names can remain beloved. `アンダーテイカー` can be chosen. `エイティシックス` can be reclaimed. `女王` can be proudly performed. `わらわ` can be constitutive of Frederica's self. `祖国` can remain mourned. `共に` can name a durable lifelong commitment.

The danger begins when grammar, institution, or relationship makes one name **exhaustive**.

That is why the smallest Japanese forms matter. `しか` can reveal exclusivity. `もう` can close an old claim. `まだ` can preserve incompletion. Potential grammar can keep return possible without promising it. A given name after a callsign can restore a private person inside an office. A declarative `である` can turn a political aspiration into self-certifying ideology. A single plain past-tense adjective—`痛かった`—can admit a wound without turning the wound into an identity.

The final rule for the V2 corpus is therefore simple:

> **Translate relations, modalities, and speakers—not just words.**

When the Japanese keeps several possibilities open, the English synthesis must keep them open too.
