# T12 — Japanese Voice, Address, Temporality, and Key Terms Retrieval

T12 is the source-language control layer for the first eleven Phase-4 dossiers. Its purpose is not to accumulate Japanese quotations as decoration. It asks where English analytical vocabulary has hidden distinctions that the Japanese text preserves through **lexeme choice, orthography, grammar, register, address form, title, and temporal aspect**.

The governing adversarial question is:

> **Which V2 arguments are genuinely supported by repeated Japanese-language structures, and which become too smooth only because several distinct Japanese expressions have been translated into one English abstraction such as “return,” “home,” “together,” “freedom,” “curse,” “personhood,” “support,” or “justice”?**

The principal result is corrective rather than destructive:

> **Most of the major V2 theses survive source-language audit, but several need narrower wording. “Return” is at least four different lexical systems; “home” divides lived place from political homeland; “together” varies in register and relational force; “curse” is a binding-force metaphor rather than a simple synonym for evil; “personhood” is an analytical category assembled from several Japanese terms rather than one stable source word; and Shin's development is best tracked as semantic admission inside a still-terse voice rather than as stylistic emotional expansion.**


# 0. Retrieval method and source-language caution

The machine pass scanned **47,941 Japanese paragraphs** in the locked V01-V14 and Alter.1 EPUB corpus and produced **14,637 lexical-family hit rows across 11,877 unique paragraphs**. Those counts are retrieval aids, not thematic weights. The human-facing ledger retains **247 context-reread items**, combining canonical V2 locators, audited Alter.1 locators, and a small set of fresh direct-source controls.

T12 applies five rules:

1. **No dictionary determinism.** A lexical tendency is not a universal semantic law. Context, syntax, speaker, register, and scene govern.
2. **No English back-projection.** If several Japanese words become one English gloss, later synthesis must not pretend the Japanese repeats one master term.
3. **No orthographic overclaim.** Kanji choice can be evidentiary, but forms such as `赦す` and `許す` overlap in actual Japanese usage.
4. **No title essentialism.** `女王`, `死神`, rank, personal name, and callsign are relationally situated address forms and roles, not metaphysical essence labels.
5. **No frequency-as-thesis.** High counts for future-time or command vocabulary partly reflect genre and narrative form. Retention requires contextual analytical value.

The primary machine families include return, home/homeland, togetherness, pride/curse, support/trust/obedience, freedom/rights/justice, personhood/selfhood, titles/address, command/request, and future-time operators. The evidence ledger preserves both the source locator and the retrieval family so Phase 5 can route language claims without rediscovering them from prose.


# 1. “Return” is not one Japanese master metaphor

The largest T12 correction concerns English **return**. The Japanese corpus distributes the apparent motif across at least four major lexical systems:

| Japanese system | Dominant tendency in this corpus | What English “return” can hide |
|---|---|---|
| `帰る` | homeward/destination/recipient-oriented return | return *to someone/somewhere*, homecoming, being received |
| `戻る` | re-entry, restoration, movement back to a prior place/state | restoration, recovery, return to role/state, political regression |
| `還る` | deathward/metaphysical return to where the dead should go | passing on, cosmic/death ontology, release of trapped dead |
| `帰還` / `生還` | operational return/survival from mission or battle | extraction, military recovery, successful return from exposure |

These overlap, but they are not interchangeable.

## 1.1 `帰る` — destination and recipient

V01 begins the relationship between survival and receiving others through Rei:

> `必ず生きて、帰らないと`

By V03, the absence of a destination is itself part of the problem:

> `帰る故郷はなく、守る家族もなく`

V06 turns that structure explicitly relational:

> `『帰ってこいと、貴女が命じたのでしょう。それなら待っていてください。帰る場所...`

and later:

> `「今日は、帰ります。……待っている人がいますから」`

The language supports T01/T04's claim that a return-address can be a person as well as a place. But the Japanese is stronger if phrased **homeward/recipient return** rather than as a universal philosophy of restoration.

## 1.2 `戻る` — prior state, re-entry, and recoverability

Frederica's V04 distinction is lexical and conceptual:

> `先に進めぬ、ではない。戻れぬ、であろ。`

The problem is not merely inability to advance; it is inability to go back. V14 then gives two radically different uses of the same verb family. Lena says:

> `「わたしは戻れません。わたしには──今、中央にいるわたしには、こちらでやるべきことがある。だから……今はまだ、戻れません」`

while Shin later says:

> `「そうだ。だから、他の奴やつらだって変われる。戻ってこられる」`

The potential construction in `戻ってこられる` matters: it means **can come back / are capable of coming back**, not **will inevitably be redeemed**. T01's “recoverability” is therefore a defensible analytical gloss precisely because it preserves modality rather than converting possibility into destiny.

But V14 also uses `戻る` for reactionary restoration:

> `「……我らギアーデは、再び帝国に戻るべきだ」`

The same broad return-to-prior-state semantic space can underwrite moral recovery or political regression. Return is therefore not the value; the **state or relation returned to** remains normatively decisive.

## 1.3 `還る` — deathward/metaphysical return

Direct source retrieval shows a distinct orthographic system around the trapped dead. V01 describes the Legion dead as wanting to return to the place to which they should pass after death. One exact paragraph states that they seem to desire that each go to the place they ought to go; another describes the dead returning to the world's deepest darkness. V05 applies the same language to beings kept from the death they should have reached, and V06 says, in effect, that the dead should be allowed to return where they belong.

This matters because English can make `還る` look like the same motif as Shin “coming home” to Lena. It is not. The source text marks **deathward release** with a different graph and semantic history. T01 may compare the structures at a higher interpretive level, but Phase 6 must not imply that the novels themselves lexicalize both with one repeated verb.

## 1.4 `帰還` / `生還` — operational return

Military return is still another layer. Mission language repeatedly distinguishes the capacity to come back alive, extraction, and organized return from combat. This is closest to T05's doctrine of recoverable military function. Operational return can become ethically important because an institution that expects return treats soldiers differently from one that expects disappearance, but the vocabulary should remain military first rather than being converted immediately into psychological metaphor.

## 1.5 Departure/return speech acts — `行ってくる` / `ただいま`

V09 supplies an especially valuable relational-linguistic control. The text explicitly notes the choice of `行ってくる` rather than the more polite `行ってきます`, and later gives `ただいま`. These are ordinary Japanese home-departure/home-return formulas whose pragmatics presuppose a social place from which one leaves and to which one expects to return.

This is stronger evidence for **ordinary relational home** than merely counting `帰る`. The future is built into the speech act: “I am going” is grammatically and pragmatically shaped as “I am going and coming back.”


# 2. `故郷` and `祖国` — home and homeland are not the same noun

English “home/homeland” can conceal a crucial distinction.

`故郷` usually foregrounds lived or remembered place, local belonging, origin-space, or somewhere one imagines returning to. V03 can say there is no `故郷` to return to; V13 can recover a desire to go back to a particular home town.

`祖国` is more explicitly political-historical: national homeland, collective origin, inherited polity, the country one protects, mourns, repudiates, or remains historically related to.

The distinction is not absolute—Japanese usage is never that mechanical—but it strongly supports T04's correction that birthplace, citizenship, felt home, and national-historical relation can diverge.

A person can mourn a `祖国` without wanting to live there again. A place can matter as `故郷` without functioning as a political homeland. And a citizenship grant can change legal status without manufacturing either feeling.

The source-language consequence for Phase 6 is straightforward:

> **Translate the relation, not merely the noun. Before writing “home,” specify whether the Japanese scene is about `故郷`, `祖国`, a house/household, a return-destination, citizenship, or a person waiting.**


# 3. `一緒に` and `共に` — two registers of “together”

The corpus repeatedly uses both `一緒に` and `共に`. They overlap semantically and must not be turned into a false binary, but their register tendencies are analytically useful.

`一緒に` often appears in ordinary shared activity, proximity, and conversational relational life. V03's reunion uses it for fighting together; V09 uses it for wanting to live together; V13 uses it for school-going and ordinary companionship.

`共に` often carries a somewhat more elevated, literary, normative, or burden-sharing register. Frederica tells Shin to use those who go with him as support:

> `共に行く者どもを支えとすればよい`

Shin's V07 confession uses `共に` in its high-register future declarations, and V09 uses the form around carrying burdens together.

The strongest negative control appears in V01. Lena suppresses the thought that she wanted to see something “together” with Shin because she can move freely while the Eighty-Six cannot. The narration explicitly recognizes that calling the relationship `一緒に` would falsely imply symmetry under conditions where one party can leave and the others cannot.

So the important V2 development is not simply that “together” becomes more frequent. It is that **the material conditions eventually become capable of supporting a word that V01 correctly identifies as ethically premature**.


# 4. `誇り`, `呪い`, `願い`, `絆`, `祈り` — binding language, not moral labels

The V09 epigraph provides the source-language taxonomy directly:

> `誇り。願い。絆。祈り。あるいは──呪い。`

Pride, wish, bond, prayer, or perhaps curse are presented as neighboring possibilities. The Japanese therefore resists any glossary in which `呪い` simply means “bad attachment.”

V03's pivotal phrase is:

> `戦い抜く誇りしか、おれ達にはない`

The restrictive particle `しか` is crucial. The danger is not pride alone but **pride as the only remaining admissible support for selfhood**. V08 later says the exclusive formulation no longer holds, and V09 can describe pride/wound/curse as overlapping names for a binding structure.

V13 intensifies the same semantic field by treating words, wishes, prayers, and emotions themselves as capable of becoming `呪い`—yet also suggesting that an accepted curse can be called love. This strongly supports T03's model of **binding whose moral quality depends on whether the bearer can reinterpret, accept, refuse, or survive beyond it**.

T12 therefore narrows the English rule:

> **Do not translate `呪い` into a fixed analytical category. Treat it first as the text's metaphor for a force that binds, persists, or constrains; determine from context whether that binding is destructive, sustaining, chosen, inherited, coercive, or ambivalent.**


# 5. `従う` / `支える` / `信じる` / `頼る` — T09's distinctions are source-language visible

One of the strongest confirmations of an earlier Phase-4 thesis is V12's explicit lexical opposition:

> `従うのではなく支えてくれた`

The text itself contrasts **obeying** with **supporting**, and demanding/depending upon a prescribed response with **trusting**. T09's distinction was therefore not imposed from outside the Japanese.

But `頼る` requires a counter-control. V03 can present inability to rely on another as a symptom of isolation; Alter.1 can say that a child who cannot fight without relying on anyone will not survive. The latter is not a celebration of liberal autonomy. It describes an environment where dependable support is structurally unavailable.

Accordingly:

> **The positive term is not “dependence” or “independence” by itself. The linguistic evidence supports a contrast between relations that preserve another person's answer-space and relations that demand obedience, guarantee, or permanent self-sufficiency.**

No single Japanese noun means T09's analytical term “interruptible interdependence.” That phrase remains an **IT/SI-level synthesis** assembled from repeated source-language contrasts and behavior, not a lexical fact.


# 6. `自由`, `平等`, `人権`, `幻想`, `正義` — political abstractions are speaker-contested

The political vocabulary requires strict speaker attribution. `自由` can mean legal liberty, open options, burdensome self-government, escape from inherited role, or ideological slogan depending on scene. `正義` likewise appears as aspiration, propaganda, criticism, and self-identity.

V05 gives the compact freedom paradox:

> `檻の扉は開いた / 何処にも行かないことだって、自由`

An open cage can include the freedom not to leave. V12 then lets characters experience formally granted freedom as an imposed burden. These scenes do not refute freedom; they distinguish **absence of coercion** from **developed capability for choosing**.

V13's famous formulation is especially vulnerable to mistranslation by analysis:

> `人権だとか、自由だとか平等だとか正義だとか。そういうものは、なるほど幻想だ。実在しない。だからこそ市民それぞれが、その幻想の...`

`幻想` here should not be reduced to “delusion,” “fake,” or “meaningless.” The surrounding logic is that rights, freedom, equality, and justice do not exist as self-enforcing natural objects; they require citizens repeatedly to instantiate and maintain the normative construction. T10's institutional argument therefore survives, but its English should say **socially maintained ideal/normative construction** rather than implying that Lena dismisses rights as unreal nonsense.


# 7. V14 justice grammar — `正義` as standard versus `正義` as identity

T12 strongly confirms T10's reading of the Federacy motto.

The aspirational form is:

> `我ら、世界に誇る正義たらん`

The archaic/literary `たらん` construction is volitional/aspirational: roughly, **let us be / may we be / let us become** justice worthy of pride before the world. The community is grammatically positioned as something that must answer to a standard.

V14's reactionary epilogue then performs the key conversion:

> `『我ら、世界に誇る正義たらん』。──つまり、連邦人の我らはすなわち正義である。`

The declarative `である` turns aspiration into identity possession: **we are justice**.

This is not merely a thematic paraphrase. The political transformation is enacted in grammar.

Earlier volumes supply controls: V11 can invoke the Federacy as a country of justice while also warning about the “beautiful name” of justice, and V12 can call the righteous country cowardly. `正義` is therefore never an omniscient narrator's guaranteed label. It is a contested political word whose danger rises when a polity treats the word as self-authenticating.


# 8. “Personhood” is an analytical matrix, not one stable Japanese word

T02's most important source-language correction is confirmed. Japanese distributes the relevant ontology across several terms:

- `人格` — personality/person-like individual configuration;
- `疑似人格` — pseudo-personality, especially artificial construction/provenance;
- `自我` — ego/self;
- `自己同一性` — self-identity/identity continuity;
- `意志` and `意思` — will, intention, judgment, volition in overlapping but nonidentical uses.

V03 explicitly joins personality and will in Shepherd description. V04 separately discusses `自己同一性`, personality as an admixture/noise for reproducible systems, and a high-mobility type lacking personality. V08 can speak of multiple personalities and selves inside one system.

The English philosophical term **personhood** is therefore a synthesis category, not a direct recurring source lexeme. Phase 6 should avoid formulations such as “the Japanese repeatedly calls X a person” when the actual evidence may be about `人格`, selfhood, memory continuity, will, or behavior.

Likewise `疑似人格` should not be treated as moral disqualification. In the Sirin context it describes constructional status. Whether the resulting subject has interests or moral standing remains a separate question.


# 9. Shin — `死神`, `葬儀屋`, callsign, and semantic admission

Shin's role-language is multiple rather than singular.

`死神` is an epithet/role-image. `葬儀屋` is the ordinary Japanese “undertaker/funeral worker” word used to explain the image. `アンダーテイカー` is also the personal/callsign form attached to Shin and his machine. Later there are relational variants such as “our Death God,” honorific role-address such as `死神殿`, and V12's deliberately paradoxical “weak Death God”:

> `一人きりじゃ戦えない、弱い死神だから`

V10's retrospective control is especially important because Shin can say he did not have to remain the Death God. V14 can even treat the Death-God position as something another person temporarily occupies or deputizes. These uses make role and person analytically separable in the source language itself.

The larger voice finding is equally important: **Shin does not develop by becoming florid**. His baseline remains plain, compressed, and low-excess. Development occurs because increasingly consequential words become admissible inside the same syntax.

Examples include:

- V06: > `「生きてるから」`
- V08: the paired `もう言わない / もう思わない` revisions;
- V12: `弱い死神`;
- V14: > `「そうだ。だから、他の奴やつらだって変われる。戻ってこられる」`
- V14: > `『──痛かった、』`

The mature Shin therefore should not be written in comparative scenes as eloquently therapeutic merely because he has become more self-aware. **What changes is the semantic range of what he will state plainly.**


# 10. Lena — `女王`, saint, rank, personal name, and masks of office

Lena's title system is likewise relationally variable. `女王` can appear as enemy slur, battlefield honorific, affectionate rough address, public command identity, or a role she herself deploys. `鮮血の女王`, `女王陛下`, rank terms such as `少佐` / `大佐`, personal `レーナ`, and `聖女` therefore must not be collapsed into one fixed self.

A high-value address development occurs when Shiden moves from Queen-address toward personal `レーナ`. V14 then explicitly frames Queenhood as a strong mask under pressure rather than an exhaustive identity. V13 also shows that the title can become political leverage because “the Queen of the Eighty-Six” is valuable as a hostage.

The methodological rule is:

> **When a title appears, ask who is speaking, what relationship is being activated, whether the title is affectionate, military, hostile, theatrical, political, or self-chosen, and whether the scene permits the personal name instead.**

The same caution applies to rank. V01's `少佐` marks professional and relational distance. Later rank-address can remain ordinary military protocol without reproducing the V01 Handler/Processor hierarchy. Address form must be read diachronically, not assigned one permanent meaning.


# 11. Idiolect as identity: Frederica, Lerche, Fido, Raiden, Kurena, Theo, Shiden

T12 confirms that individual voice is analytically meaningful beyond named keywords.

**Frederica.** Alter.1 directly states that her choice of words is part of herself: the fresh source paragraph `言葉の選び方もわらわの一部じゃ` makes her archaizing `わらわ / そなた` register an explicit identity claim, not a decorative joke. Losing the register would therefore be a characterization loss.

**Lerche.** Her retainer voice (`それがし`, formal negative/polite forms, `死神殿`) simultaneously marks individuality and institutional role. This is useful to T02 because an artificial subject's distinct voice can coexist with role-designed origins.

**Fido.** V10's first-person interiority uses stable polite first-person language, adding linguistic evidence to subjectivity without requiring biological-human speech norms.

**Raiden.** Rough masculine colloquial speech repeatedly carries highly sophisticated ethical judgments in deliberately non-philosophical diction. A comparative reconstruction should not make him sound academically theoretical merely because the analysis can formalize his reasoning.

**Kurena.** Youthful `あたし` and emotional transparency remain part of her voice even as judgment matures.

**Theo.** Directness, sarcasm, critique, and later gentler accommodation coexist; development should not erase the cutting intelligence of his early register.

**Shiden.** Rough colloquial command and personal address produce a distinct relation to Lena that is neither Shin/Lena intimacy nor formal staff deference.

Voice fidelity therefore requires more than vocabulary matching. It requires **sentence length, politeness level, first-person pronoun, habitual compression/expansion, and relation-specific address**.


# 12. Temporal grammar — `もう`, `まだ`, `また`, `いつか`, `これから`

T06's future-time model is strongly supported by small grammatical operators, but they must be read contextually.

## 12.1 `もう` — completion, revision, or simple “already”

Not every `もう` marks development. The high-value cases pair it with negation or explicit self-revision: V08's “I won't say anymore that pride is all I have” and “I don't think anymore that the battlefield is my only place,” V11's `だから──もういいや`, and Lena's refusal to perform the tragic-saint role any longer.

The semantic structure is **old proposition + `もう` + negated continuation**. That is much stronger evidence of revision than a bare occurrence of the adverb.

## 12.2 `まだ` — open incompletion

`まだ` frequently marks a state as not final: still alive, not yet able to return, not yet able to forgive. V14's `今はまだ、戻れません` is particularly important because it means **not now/yet**, not permanent refusal. V11's `まだ赦せない` similarly preserves nonforgiveness without declaring it a metaphysical eternal state, even though Rito also recognizes it may last his lifetime.

## 12.3 `また` — recurrence

`また` is one of T06's quietest future markers. “Again” presupposes a repeatable world. A future can therefore be encoded through another visit, another conversation, another birthday, another deployment from which one expects to return.

## 12.4 `いつか` / `これから` — indefinite and projective future

`いつか` makes an event imaginable without fixing when it will occur. `これから` orients action from now forward. These forms matter because early Shin often lacks a rich positive future noun while later scenes allow him to organize present choices around events located beyond the current crisis.

## 12.5 Potential grammar and recoverability

V14's `戻ってこられる` is grammatically central. It asserts **capacity/possibility**, not inevitability. The narrator immediately extends that potential to the Federacy itself. This is exactly why “recoverability” is more faithful than “redemption” as T01's master English term.


# 13. `赦す` and `許す` — useful orthographic evidence, not a hard semantic wall

T08's forgiveness analysis needs one source-language caveat.

`赦す` frequently appears in morally marked forgiveness/absolution contexts: Shin/Rei, Zelene, Rito's explicit nonforgiveness, and V14's wound discussion. `許す` is much broader and often means permit, allow, tolerate, approve, or “forgive” in ordinary moral language.

The corpus therefore supports a **tendency**:

- `赦す` is often a strong signal that moral forgiveness/absolution is in view;
- `許す` requires much wider contextual disambiguation.

But it does not support a rule that the two kanji forms map perfectly onto two separate English concepts. Japanese authors can use `許す` for moral forgiveness as well. Phase 6 should therefore treat orthography as one evidentiary cue, never as the sole basis for a claim about forgiveness.

V11's explicit nonforgiveness remains secure:

> `全然、まだ赦せないよ`

and V14's pain/forgiveness sequence remains secure because context, not orthography alone, establishes the moral problem.


# 14. Command, request, and permission — relationship ethics often lives in mood rather than nouns

A recurring pattern across the corpus is movement among **orders, requests, promises, permissions, and self-statements**.

Early Handler language necessarily contains military command asymmetry. Later Shin can ask for help or delegate command in his same terse voice. V06's reciprocal return sequence mixes command and request in a relationship where both participants are asserting claims on each other's survival. V13's strongest interruptibility scene works because after physical interruption Lena asks for Shin's judgment rather than issuing the judgment he must adopt.

This is why T09 should not search only for words meaning “support.” The ethical structure often appears in **who uses imperative mood, who asks, who is allowed to answer, and whether the addressee can refuse without exiting the relationship**.

The source-language implication is procedural:

> **Track speech acts, not merely sentiment. “I order you,” “please,” “I want,” “I trust you,” “decide,” and “you may choose” distribute agency differently even when all are spoken from care.**

# 16. Phase-5 routing rules

T12 is retrieval substrate rather than the final Japanese-language specialist essay. Phase 5 should lock the following routing conventions:

1. Every high-value English abstraction must carry its Japanese lexical family when that distinction affects argument.
2. T01/T04 return-home claims should route through the four return systems rather than one “return” bucket.
3. T02 personhood claims must label whether evidence concerns personality, self, identity continuity, will, refusal, embodiment, or moral standing.
4. T03 pride/curse claims should note restrictive grammar such as `しか` when exclusivity is the actual evidence.
5. T09 relationship claims should preserve speech-act form—order, request, trust, delegation, permission—where analytically relevant.
6. T10 justice claims must preserve the `たらん` / `である` grammar.
7. Specialist character documents should preserve idiolect: pronouns, politeness, compression, archaization, rough colloquial register, and role-specific address.
8. Translation/gloss should be marked as analytical gloss when no single English word preserves the Japanese distinction.
9. No cross-volume register-development claim should be promoted to canonical synthesis without at least two longitudinal controls and one negative/counterexample where feasible.
10. Alter.1 remains supplemental: it can establish low-pressure voice and chronological continuity, but must not silently overwrite mainline publication-boundary evidence.

# 17. Final T12 thesis

The Japanese-language evidence substantially strengthens the V2 project, but it also disciplines its cleanest abstractions.

> **The series' recurring concern is not encoded in one master keyword. It is produced through a network of lexical and grammatical contrasts: going home versus returning to a prior state, returning alive versus the dead passing on, homeland versus lived home, ordinary togetherness versus elevated co-bearing, pride versus pride-only exclusivity, obedience versus support, aspiration toward justice versus identifying oneself as justice, and “not yet” versus “never.”**

That network matters because *86* repeatedly asks whether a state, role, wound, relationship, identity, or political failure is **final**. Japanese grammar often answers before thematic exposition does. `もう` can terminate an old proposition. `まだ` can keep a state open. `また` can make recurrence imaginable. Potential `戻ってこられる` can make recovery possible without guaranteeing it. Ordinary `行ってくる` / `ただいま` can make a relationship future-bearing through the grammar of departure itself.

The most important methodological conclusion is therefore:

> **Source-language precision should constrain synthesis rather than decorate it. When English becomes smoother than the Japanese, the Japanese wins.**
