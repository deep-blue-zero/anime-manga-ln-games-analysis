# T04 — Home, Belonging, Citizenship, Exile, and Return Retrieval

This dossier retrieves the evidence needed to answer a deceptively simple question: **where do the people of *86* belong?** The V01-V14 corpus does not permit one answer. Birthplace, `故郷`, `祖国`, legal citizenship, blood family, household, unit, military organization, refugee status, country of residence, remembered origin, chosen destination, and the person who waits for one's return repeatedly separate and recombine.

The governing adversarial question is: **Does the series ultimately replace inherited home with chosen home, or does that formulation erase the continuing force of origin, citizenship, blood relation, homeland grief, refugee displacement, and places a person may value without wanting to live there again?**

The retrieval result is: **home in *86* is plural, layered, and partly material. A person can acquire new homes without invalidating origin; can retain `祖国` while losing `故郷`; can hold citizenship without belonging; can belong socially before feeling at home; can recover family without replacing chosen relationships; and can value a place precisely as somewhere freely revisited rather than somewhere one must permanently return. The political test is not whether a subject has one true home, but whether law, infrastructure, relationships, and military institutions leave that subject meaningful routes to enter, leave, return, and form additional attachments.**

# 1. Executive taxonomy: nine layers later synthesis must keep separate

| Layer | Core question | Key negative control |
|---|---|---|
| **Birth/origin** | Where was this person born; what ancestry/archive can be recovered? | Recovered address does not automatically become remembered home. |
| **`故郷` / lived home** | What place is experienced as home or desired return? | A birthplace may no longer be `故郷`; a later town may become one. |
| **`祖国` / homeland-polity** | What national community/history remains politically or emotionally significant? | Claude can retain `祖国` while denying `故郷`; Shin can reject the Republic as `祖国`. |
| **Citizenship** | What polity legally recognizes and protects the person? | Giad citizenship is real without instantly creating belonging. |
| **Household/family** | Who receives the person in ordinary life? | A house can shelter someone without feeling like home; blood family can be added later. |
| **Unit/comrade belonging** | Who provides current social recognition and mutual obligation? | Same-wound belonging can become enclosing if divergence is treated as betrayal. |
| **Exile/refugee condition** | Has return become materially or politically unavailable? | Citizenship can survive territorial loss while lived home becomes inaccessible. |
| **Return-address** | Is there somewhere/someone to whom departure can lead back? | Waiting can support freedom or become hostage leverage. |
| **Return capability** | Can the person actually leave danger and re-enter ordinary life? | A nominal home is not substantively usable if roads, law, mines, commands, or coercion block return. |

The central correction is therefore: **belonging is not a zero-sum contest among blood, polity, unit, household and chosen relationship.** The corpus increasingly allows these layers to coexist, conflict, weaken, recover, or be supplemented.

# 2. Japanese lexical distinctions

## 2.1 `故郷` is not simply “country of citizenship”

Across the corpus, `故郷` is the most place-and-life oriented term. It can mean remembered birthplace, a lost town, battlefield space that became socially inhabitable, or a newly acquired community. V08's Fleet material is decisive because a displaced speaker can say a later town **is now his `故郷`** after losing the original country. V09 then states directly that family and home need not be received only at birth.

The term therefore supports **acquired home**, but it does not mean inherited origin is irrelevant. Chitri in V13 still wants the old home-town, family and school. New home and lost home can both remain true.

## 2.2 `祖国` is political-historical belonging, not necessarily lived home

`祖国` repeatedly survives where `故郷` does not. V11 gives the cleanest distinction: Claude can look upon a country he cannot experience as home and still call it homeland. Lena can mourn the destroyed Republic as `祖国` while learning that her present return-address lies elsewhere. Conversely, Shin can be Republic-born and once a Republic citizen without ever regarding the Republic as homeland.

Later synthesis should therefore avoid translating every `祖国` occurrence into the affective English word “home.” It often carries national origin, political loyalty, inherited history, shame, duty, or grief instead.

## 2.3 Citizenship is a legal relation whose moral importance is enormous but incomplete

V01 makes citizenship destruction one of the genocide's foundational technologies. The Colorata are not simply insulted; they are legally removed from the civic category that carries rights, residence, recognized death and political standing. V02 then gives the Five real Giad citizenship. That change matters. It provides law, protection, material resources and recognized membership.

Yet V02-V07 repeatedly demonstrate that legal membership does not automatically generate an internal sense of home. This is not an argument that citizenship is superficial. It is an argument that **legal recognition and lived belonging are distinct achievements**.

## 2.4 `帰る` and `戻る` inherit T01's distinction

T01 already established that the two return families cannot be flattened. T04 uses that result at the home/citizenship level. `帰る` often foregrounds homeward orientation or an address that receives the subject; `戻る` often foregrounds re-entry, restoration, or movement back to a prior position/state. The distinction is broad rather than absolute, but it becomes particularly useful in V14, where `戻ってこられる` concerns moral-political recoverability while `再び帝国に戻るべきだ` supplies the reactionary negative control.

# 3. Volume-by-volume home and belonging map

## V01 — citizenship destruction makes home politically removable

V01 begins by making legal status spatial. The Republic identifies Colorata residents as enemy citizens, strips `市民権`, removes them beyond the walls, and turns the former citizen's own country into the agent of exclusion. That means the first home problem in *86* is not psychological. **The state can make a person's existing home unusable by changing who is allowed to count as a citizen there.**

The fake five-year restitution promise deepens the crime. Official rhetoric implies that service can restore citizenship and allow families to return to old houses. The later discovery that survivors are deliberately disposed of reveals citizenship restoration as a narrative technology used to make exterminatory service appear temporary and contractual.

At the same time, some older Eighty-Six continue fighting in order to prove to themselves that *they*, rather than the Alba who abandoned defense, are the true Republic citizens. Citizenship is therefore simultaneously a legal status denied by the state and an identity claim retained by the excluded.

**V01 state:** birthplace, national origin, legal citizenship, current residence and moral membership have been violently torn apart.

## V02 — Giad can grant citizenship faster than it can create home

The Five become Federacy citizens almost immediately. V2 insists this is real legal rescue: they have protection, housing, money, school options and actual rights. T04 rejects the cynical reading that these things are merely decorative.

But the same volume observes that Shin has lost family, blurred childhood memory and no place he thinks of as `故郷`, and initially sees no obvious problem in that absence. Kiriya provides a different version: his birthplace became enemy territory through revolution and war. These controls block a simple equation between legal nationality and emotional home.

V2 also supplies one of the series' most dangerous transitional phrases: the Five call military return `帰ろう。あたし達のいるべきところに`. Their most legible place remains battle. That does not make Giad rescue false. It demonstrates that **institutions can open a home-shaped possibility before the rescued person possesses the habits or desires required to inhabit it.**

## V03 — house, place-to-live and belonging separate explicitly

A Giad officer says the Federation gave the Eighty-Six a house to return to and a place to live. The text then spends the volume proving why those provisions are not sufficient definitions of home.

The Five lack remembered homeland, family, cultural inheritance and durable civilian desire. Shin says there is no `居場所`. Kiriya's trajectory provides the negative endpoint: no place to return, no purpose beyond battle, pride as the only remaining positive identity.

Yet V3 is also careful not to invert paternalism. If someone later chooses military life with genuine alternatives available, that choice may remain legitimate. The question becomes **whether there is somewhere else one could go and still remain a recognized person**.

## V04 — origin-memory cannot be required for present identity

V4 retrieves the home problem through memory. Several Eighty-Six cannot remember birthplace, parents, family faces or inherited culture. The volume asks whether this absence is itself a deficit others are entitled to repair.

Shin's answer is resistant: he remains himself without those memories. That is not a claim that family or homeland are worthless. It is a refusal to make remembered origin the authenticity test for current identity.

The volume also preserves a deferred possibility: perhaps someday someone may want to return to a homeland they cannot currently access. **Not remembering home and not wanting home now cannot be converted into a permanent ban on future return.**

## V05 — battlefield home is real without being sufficient

V5 explicitly summarizes the Eighty-Six's Sector social order as battlefield for home and comrades for compatriots. This is one reason the military identity cannot simply be dismissed as false consciousness: real relationships and real social worlds formed there.

But V5 also broadens displacement beyond the Eighty-Six. Frontline civilians elsewhere abandon houses and home regions as war advances. This comparison is important because it lets the novel discuss common human displacement without erasing the Republic's distinctive racial-civic persecution.

Shin also states he never thought of the Republic as `祖国`. Birthplace and former citizenship therefore do not create a homeland claim automatically.

## V06 — return becomes relational and plural

V6 is the crucial conversion point. On leave, most Eighty-Six still have no family house to return to. Shin can visit recovered blood relatives, but he leaves them because **someone is waiting elsewhere**. Blood-family recovery adds a relationship rather than replacing the social world already built in Giad.

The Shin/Lena exchange makes the return-address explicit: if Lena ordered him to return, she must wait; if the place of return disappears, return itself becomes impossible. This turns home from possession into an ongoing relation between departure and reception.

The coda also anticipates divergence. The Five currently return to the same place, but survival means they may later choose different destinations without the bond thereby ceasing to exist. **Mature belonging must survive differentiated futures.**

## V07 — plural civic identity replaces purification

V7 is the clearest political identity volume before V11. The former Eighty-Six are now Federacy citizens and soldiers. Lena remains a Republic person. Shin refuses the attempt to purify her by denying that origin: she is `共和国人` and simultaneously their Queen.

That is a major T04 rule. A morally compromised national inheritance does not have to be erased in order for a new belonging to become real. Shiden's later correction makes the same point socially: Lena can be Republic-born without being reduced to the Republic's white-pig moral category.

Shin's grandparents provide a positive kinship control. Biological family becomes something he actively practices through letters and visits. The fact that these relationships are blood-based does not make them less chosen; the fact that they are chosen later does not make the blood irrelevant.

## V08 — exile and reconstructed collective home

The Fleet Countries provide T04's strongest collective displacement case. A whole population abandons territory, carries refugees and preserves national symbols because physical return may be impossible for years. `祖国` persists as memory and political-cultural continuity during territorial absence.

But V8 then goes further: another town becomes `故郷`. The later home does not falsify the lost homeland. It becomes an additional place capable of holding ordinary life, memory and future.

This same movement appears at the individual level when Shin says he no longer believes the battlefield is his only `居場所`. The positive model is not replacement—old home bad, new home good—but **addition of viable places from which a future can be lived**.

## V09 — home can be acquired, and same-wound belonging can be too narrow

V9 states the thesis almost directly: family and `故郷` are not things one can receive only at birth; they can be found later. The following passage tells the wounded subject to find another `居場所`, somewhere one can say one is hurting and lean on another person.

Crucially, the suggested belonging must extend beyond people with the same wound. Milmelkoleo demonstrates why. Same-injury solidarity can become a closed social world where healing, role exit or different desire threatens membership.

V9 also introduces formal asylum. A state can offer a new legal home, showing that political belonging remains negotiable. Yet the Eighty-Six's non-native-born Federacy status is simultaneously treated as politically vulnerable by others. **Citizenship is real, but social power can still stratify citizens by ancestry and institutional backing.**

## V10 — ancestral arrival is not homecoming

V10's backward chronology reaches perhaps the strongest origin/home distinction in the series. Shin arrives in the country of inherited blood, a place that might under different history have been his `故国`. The narration does not call that fulfillment.

Other children provide the contrast. Some remember an old house and actively want to return. Others, including Shin and Raiden, no longer remember a usable destination. The text therefore refuses both universal claims:

- everyone naturally longs for birth-home;
- birth-home stops mattering once memory is damaged.

Different histories produce different relations to origin.

## V11 — the Republic can be birthplace, homeland, grief-object, memorial destination, and non-home at once

V11 is T04's central volume. The Republic collapses, its citizens become refugees, and former residents return to a destroyed political-territorial space.

Anju's formulation is the cleanest negative control against chosen-home oversimplification: the Republic can become only **the country where she happened to be born**. She explicitly says the place she wants to live and return to is elsewhere.

Claude produces another distinction: a country may still be `祖国` while no longer being `故郷`. Lena differs from both. She retains deep homeland grief and can still call the Republic home in later V12 material, yet her V11 immediate emotional return-address has shifted. She can choose to come back to the place where she first physically met Shin without making San Magnolia the exclusive center of her future.

The mass-refugee layer matters equally. Federacy railways, hotels, apartments and administrative sorting materially receive displaced Republic citizens. Refugee protection is therefore not a sentimental label. **Belonging has logistical preconditions.**

## V12 — lost homeland remains politically and emotionally active

V12 prevents V11 from becoming a clean “Lena moved on” story. The narration still says the Republic is Lena's `故郷` and its citizens her `同胞`. Bounded guilt does not require emotional denationalization.

At the same time, Republic refugees are recruited as volunteers, raising a structural problem: displacement can reduce bargaining power and make voluntary military incorporation vulnerable to exploitation. Ernst's objections to using such people as frontline shields preserve the distinction between service and disposability.

The Fawn survivors then ask for a road back to their birth-home. That request is critical evidence against a model in which new institutional rescue should automatically redirect every survivor toward Giad. **Some displaced people want the origin that others reject.**

## V13 — new home becomes explicit, then return to it is blocked

V13 names Rustkammer the Eighty-Six's `新たな故郷`. This is perhaps the strongest direct textual confirmation of acquired collective home in the mainline.

The volume immediately makes that achievement politically fragile. Return to the base is forbidden; the final military architecture closes routes backward; displaced populations are proposed as replacement manpower; family-love rhetoric is used to justify shifting danger onto an out-group.

This is why T04 inherits T01's **return-capability** standard. A home is not fully usable if law, coercion, military orders or mines make returning to it impossible. The issue is not sentimental attachment alone; it is the material route through which attachment can be enacted.

Chitri provides the opposite continuity. She wants her original town, parents, teachers and school. V13 therefore holds **new homeland and lost birth-home desire at the same time**.

## V14 — civic belonging can recover, and “return” can also become reactionary

V14 extends home politics in two directions.

First, the Strike Package takes care not to loot civilian homes because residents may come back. This small logistical detail matters: ethical military conduct imagines civilians as future returners rather than abandoned property owners whose ordinary lives no longer count.

Second, the Federacy's civic crisis becomes a contest over who counts as `同胞`. Public address repeatedly invokes `連邦市民`; other citizens explicitly insist the Eighty-Six are the same human beings and compatriots. Legal citizenship alone did not end prejudice, but the public language of citizenship becomes one resource through which prejudice can be contested.

Shin then generalizes return morally: frightened people can change and `戻ってこられる`. The epilogue supplies the required negative control—reactionaries also demand that Giad `再び帝国に戻る`. **Return is a capacity, not a moral destination.** What matters is what one returns *to* and whether the return reopens or closes future agency.

## Alter.1 — archive restores origin without manufacturing home

Alter.1 is unusually useful because it removes crisis pressure. Recovered Republic records can restore names, family information, birthdays and old addresses. Shin can inspect that information and still describe the old house as an unknown house he does not remember.

The supplement also gives a subtle household control. Ernst's residence can function as shelter and gathering place without Shin automatically feeling `帰る家` there. Elsewhere the same Republic is described as non-homeland/non-home to Shin while still being Lena's homeland and nostalgic home.

This is exactly why T04 uses **plural home** rather than “chosen family replaces blood/nation.” Archive, blood, household, polity, unit and chosen partner can all remain meaningful without collapsing into one canonical home.


# 4. Cross-cutting adversarial controls

## 4.1 Citizenship is neither sufficient nor optional

One of the easiest analytical mistakes would be to notice that Giad citizenship does not immediately make the Five feel at home and conclude that legal status is superficial compared with authentic relationship. V01 makes that impossible. The Republic's destruction of Colorata citizenship is precisely what permits forced relocation, confinement, denial of recognized deaths, military disposability, and the disappearance of ordinary legal claims. `市民権` is not bureaucratic decoration; it determines whether institutions are obligated to treat the person as a rights-bearing member of the polity.

Giad's later citizenship grant therefore matters even before the Five know what they want to do with it. It creates a protected field in which later choice becomes possible. Housing, money, education, medical care, lawful employment, access to ordinary infrastructure, enforceable status, and the possibility of leaving military service are not equivalent to belonging, but they are conditions under which belonging no longer has to be improvised entirely against the state.

The opposite error is equally serious. Because legal citizenship is important, later specialists must not infer that every Federacy citizen is socially secure. V7 shows the former Eighty-Six encountering persistent outsider treatment despite citizenship. V9 imagines them as politically less claimable because they are not native-born and lack powerful family constituencies. V14 shows citizens themselves contesting whether the Eighty-Six count as `同胞`. The correct model is therefore layered:

> **citizenship establishes institutional membership; social belonging determines whether that membership is treated as ordinary; home adds affective and practical attachment; none can substitute completely for the others.**

This is also why V14's civic-language struggle matters. `連邦市民の同胞` can be propaganda, but it can also be a normative claim against exclusion. The language of citizenship remains available for political self-correction because the Eighty-Six are not merely guests whom benevolent natives may choose to tolerate. They are citizens whose exclusion contradicts the Federation's own stated order.

## 4.2 Home is additive rather than zero-sum

The Fleet Countries, Shin's grandparents, Lena's Republic attachment, Rustkammer, and Alter.1 all resist a substitution model of home.

A substitution model would say:

> old home is lost or morally compromised -> character finds true new home -> old attachment becomes obsolete.

The corpus repeatedly does something messier.

The Fleet case preserves `祖国`, loss, inherited culture and symbols while allowing another town to become `故郷`. Shin can recover grandparents without treating them as replacements for Raiden, Theo, Anju, Kurena, Frederica, Lena, or the broader life he has already built. Lena can stop treating the Republic's sins as her omnipotent personal guilt while still mourning the country, recognizing Republic civilians as compatriots, and wanting to revisit the place where she and Shin first met. Chitri can want her old town even after being rescued into a different political future. Alter.1 can restore an old address without restoring the emotional experience of that house.

T04 therefore recommends **additive belonging** as the default hypothesis:

> **A later home can become fully real without forcing the earlier home to become unreal. A recovered blood relation can become fully real without demoting chosen relationships. A polity can remain historically significant without retaining exclusive authority over residence, loyalty, or future.**

This is not unlimited accumulation. Attachments can conflict. A destroyed state may be impossible to inhabit. A blood relation may be unwanted or abusive. A unit can become too enclosing. A homeland can demand immoral loyalty. The point is only that change is not analytically equivalent to replacement.

## 4.3 Refugee status exposes the material side of belonging

The refugee material from V8, V11, V12 and V13 is especially valuable because it prevents “home” from becoming purely phenomenological.

A refugee can remember exactly where home is and still be unable to live there. A citizen can retain nationality while becoming territorially dependent on another state. A community can possess traditions and political identity while lacking secure land. A person can be welcomed rhetorically while still lacking housing, transport, employment, documentation, schooling, security, or a safe route back.

V11 makes this unusually concrete. Republic evacuation requires rail prioritization, temporary apartments and hotels, administrative sorting of politically dangerous groups, food, policing, and host-state political decisions about how many people to receive and where to place them. The Federacy's mixed motives do not erase the reality that these systems keep people alive.

V12-V13 then show the danger of converting displaced status into reduced political bargaining power. Refugees can become a convenient pool of volunteer or quasi-volunteer manpower. Public arguments can say that people without secure homes should absorb more danger because established citizens have families and places worth protecting. This produces a recurring hierarchy:

> those with homes are imagined as people for whom survival is naturally valuable; those without secure homes are imagined as people who can be risked.

That hierarchy is one of the deeper links between T04 and the original Eighty-Sixth Sector. The issue is not simply homelessness. It is **politically converting lack of secure return into an argument for greater expendability**.

## 4.4 Household is not identical to home, but ordinary reception matters

The series is interested in surprisingly mundane questions: who is at the door, who cooks, who expects someone back, who writes letters, where leave is spent, where possessions remain, and where a person can be sick or exhausted without immediately becoming useless.

These details matter because a household is one of the mechanisms through which abstract survival becomes repeated ordinary life.

Ernst's house is an instructive control. It can shelter the Five, host friends, provide food and function as an ordinary gathering point while Shin still does not automatically experience it as his definitive `帰る家`. A household can therefore precede the feeling of home.

Shin's grandparents provide the opposite movement. Biological relation exists before intimacy. Through letters, visits and repeated contact, kinship becomes practiced rather than merely genealogical. The later relationship is neither pure inheritance nor pure choice; it is inherited possibility converted into lived relation.

The Shin/Lena waiting language adds another layer. A person can become part of a return-address without owning the returner. This is why T04 cross-links T09 later: healthy relational home must permit departure. The person who waits should make return possible, not make leaving morally impossible.

## 4.5 Return capability turns home into a political property

V13 clarifies something the earlier volumes often imply: a home is politically incomplete if one cannot actually return to it.

This does not mean all valued places require unrestricted physical access. Dead homelands, memorial spaces, enemy-held territory and childhood homes can remain meaningful even when inaccessible. The narrower point is that when an institution itself intentionally closes the road back, attachment becomes a site of coercion.

The original Sector did this through walls, mines, erased military status and terminal deployment. V13's Federacy crisis reproduces a narrower battlefield relation through orders, social coercion and physical minefields. V14's irreversible-transfer problem makes the same logic personal: moving to a state from which one cannot return is ethically different from temporary separation because it closes a future option.

T04 therefore adopts **return capability** as a material dimension of home:

> **If a polity says a person belongs but structures military, legal or social life so that valued destinations cannot be re-entered, belonging is thinner than the formal label suggests.**

This does not imply an unconditional right to enter every place one values. Security, ownership, war and other people's rights can constrain movement. It means that deliberate no-return architecture is an important warning sign when evaluating whether a society treats attachment as the person's own or as leverage available to institutions.

## 4.6 Belonging can protect insiders by creating outsiders

T04 also needs a negative account of belonging itself. Family, homeland and compatriot language are not automatically humane.

V13 is explicit: people invoke love of children, parents, spouses and country in order to argue that somebody else should absorb battlefield risk. The problem is not that family love is false. Its reality is precisely what gives exclusion emotional force. If *my* child must survive, the temptation is to find a category of person whose child can be endangered instead.

The Republic's original system is the most extreme version: citizenship and humanity are narrowed until the Colorata can be made the outside that protects the inside. Giad's later crisis proves the mechanism can recur without reproducing every historical feature of the Republic.

This gives T04 a final caution:

> **belonging is ethically valuable only if membership does not require a disposable nonmember.**

That proposition should feed Documents 05, 06 and 14. It explains why the series can value family, unit, homeland and civic solidarity while remaining suspicious of political orders that prove their internal love by producing an expendable exterior.

# 6. Mature T04 formulation

The strongest formulation after adversarial retrieval is:

> **Home in *86* is not a single authentic place waiting to be recovered. It is a layered relation among origin, legal membership, memory, household, people, practice, political protection and the material possibility of return. A person may retain several of those layers, lose others, acquire new ones, and revise their relative importance without making the earlier attachments unreal.**

The negative institutional form is therefore not simply exile. It is **enforced home foreclosure**: law, war, mines, racial classification, hostage logic or role enclosure making a person's valued destinations unusable or making belonging conditional on remaining in one assigned place.

The positive institutional form is not “give everyone a new home.” It is to preserve enough rights, material capacity, safety, mobility, information, housing and social recognition that people can **form, leave, revisit, combine and sometimes refuse homes themselves**.
