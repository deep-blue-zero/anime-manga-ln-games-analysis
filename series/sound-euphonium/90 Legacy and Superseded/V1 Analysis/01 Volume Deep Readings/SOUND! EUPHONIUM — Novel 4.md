# What kind of volume this is

Volume 4 is not the beginning of Kumiko’s second school year.

It is a fourteen-story anthology that moves backward and sideways through the timeline established by the first three novels, then extends briefly beyond the national competition into the winter succession period.

Its contents are:

1. **「北宇治高校吹奏楽部の日常」**  
   *Everyday Life in the Kitauji High School Concert Band*

2. **「科学準備室の京子さん」**  
   *Kyoko of the Science Preparation Room*

3. **「あの子には才能がある」**  
   *That Girl Has Talent*

4. **「少女漫画ごっこ」**  
   *Playing at Shōjo Manga*

5. **「好きな人の好きな人（前編）」**  
   *The Person My Crush Likes — Part One*

6. **「好きな人の好きな人（後編）」**  
   *The Person My Crush Likes — Part Two*

7. **「犬と猿とおかん」**  
   *Dogs, Monkeys, and Mom*

8. **「背伸び」**  
   *Standing on Tiptoe*

9. **「こんぷれっくす」**  
   *Complexes*

10. **「きみのいなくなった日」**  
    *The Day You Disappeared*

11. **「北宇治高校文化祭」**  
    *The Kitauji High School Cultural Festival*

12. **「新三年生会議」**  
    *The New Third-Year Meeting*

13. **「お兄さんとお父さん」**  
    *The Young Man and Father*

14. **「とある冬の日」**  
    *One Winter Day*

The anthology form changes the kind of truth the series can reveal.

The main trilogy follows large public movements:

- forming a serious ensemble;
- auditions;
- competition;
- institutional conflict;
- national qualification;
- Asuka’s family crisis;
- and the national performance.

Volume 4 listens underneath that public melody.

It reveals:

- the first note Hazuki successfully produces;
- the hidden labor behind SunFes costumes;
- Aoi’s unspoken wish to be asked back;
- Hazuki’s complete romantic perspective;
- the origins of Natsuki and Yuuko’s antagonistic friendship;
- Mizore’s first day without Nozomi;
- Reina’s childhood privilege;
- the private logic behind the next leadership team;
- and Kumiko and Shuuichi’s direct mutual confession.

Volume 4 is therefore best understood as the **countermelody of the opening trilogy**.

The main story tells us what happened.

The secret stories tell us what those events meant to people who were not controlling the narrative.

# Executive thesis

Volume 4 argues that an ensemble’s public sound is made from **private facts the audience cannot hear**.

The audience hears:

- a parade;
- a concert;
- an audition;
- a competition performance;
- or a polished school ensemble.

It does not hear:

- the beginner ashamed that she cannot make a sound;
- the student sewing costumes in a science room;
- the girl who quits partly hoping someone will say she matters;
- the rejected confession beneath a cheerful friendship;
- the insecurity hidden inside a strange name;
- the argument through which two girls express intimacy;
- the economic advantages behind an apparent meritocracy;
- or the phone call that turns childhood familiarity into romance.

That is the meaning of **ヒミツ / secrets** here.

Most of the secrets are not scandalous facts. They are **unseen motives**.

The anthology’s governing principle can be stated as:

> **People are rarely only what they contribute to the visible performance.  
> Every apparently minor member carries an interior story capable of changing how the whole ensemble is understood.**

This also gives Volume 4 a social and musical philosophy:

> **What does not stand out may still be essential.  
> What appears replaceable may still be personally irreplaceable.  
> What looks like conflict may be intimacy.  
> What looks like confidence may rest upon privilege.  
> What looks like kindness may conceal pain.  
> What looks like ordinary daily life may be the very thing everyone later grieves losing.**

The main trilogy repeatedly distinguishes between being **needed** and being **wanted**.

Volume 4 generalizes that distinction across the ensemble.

A tuba is needed even when the audience rarely notices it.

Aoi wants to hear that she is personally wanted, not merely that her instrumental part can be covered.

Hazuki wants Shuuichi even though she knows he wants Kumiko.

Mizore doubts whether Yuuko wants her or merely pities her.

Natsuki and Yuuko are chosen as leaders not because they are interchangeable functionaries, but because their particular conflict makes them capable of balancing each other.

Shuuichi and Kumiko finally stop speaking through circumstances and say that they want each other.

The anthology’s deepest answer is:

> **Belonging cannot be reduced to usefulness.  
> But usefulness, ordinary labor, and unnoticed support are among the ways belonging is made real.**

# 1. Formal architecture: an ensemble novel becomes truly polyphonic

The first three novels remain closely attached to Kumiko’s consciousness even when other characters become central.

Volume 4 shifts among numerous focal characters:

- Kumiko;
- Aoi;
- Midori;
- Hazuki;
- Riko;
- Shuuichi;
- Mizore;
- Takuya;
- and Reina.

This is not merely variety.

It formally proves one of the series’ major claims:

> No one position inside an ensemble can hear the total sound.

Kumiko is perceptive, but she does not know:

- how Hazuki experienced falling for Shuuichi;
- what Aoi wanted from Asuka;
- how Reina first met Taki;
- why Riko interprets Yuuko and Natsuki’s fighting as intimacy;
- what Mizore thought on the day Nozomi disappeared;
- or what Shuuichi feels when he watches Kumiko with Reina.

Events that seemed complete from Kumiko’s perspective acquire new emotional information from another seat in the band.

The effect resembles orchestration.

Each story adds a timbre that was present in the trilogy but not foregrounded:

- Hazuki supplies romantic directness;
- Midori supplies imaginative generosity;
- Aoi supplies resentment and status anxiety;
- Riko supplies relational mediation;
- Mizore supplies compressed abandonment;
- Reina supplies privileged certainty;
- Shuuichi supplies inhibited ordinary masculinity.

Volume 4 is therefore not supplementary in the sense of disposable bonus material.

It is supplementary in the musical sense:

> It fills harmonic space the principal melody left open.

# 2. 「北宇治高校吹奏楽部の日常」  
## The first note and the value of what does not stand out

The opening story returns to the period shortly after Kumiko, Hazuki, and Midori join low brass.

Its scale is deliberately small.

Hazuki cannot make a proper sound.

Asuka teaches her how to form the embouchure, breathe, and produce vibration. Eventually Hazuki manages a note. The section then plays a simple version of “Twinkle, Twinkle, Little Star.”

Nothing competitively important happens.

That is the point.

## The origin of ensemble pleasure

Hazuki has not yet developed:

- technical fluency;
- competitive ambition;
- musical status;
- or even certainty about her instrument.

Yet playing one recognizable melody with other people creates immediate joy.

This is music before hierarchy.

There are no:

- auditions;
- medals;
- rankings;
- difficult repertoire;
- or questions about national-level expressiveness.

There is only the discovery that individual sounds can become something shared.

This scene gives a foundational answer to the later competition volumes:

> Before music becomes judgment, it is coordination and delight.

## The tuba as the anthology’s ethical instrument

Hazuki worries that the tuba is not glamorous.

Asuka explains that an ensemble is made from many small gears. Some instruments receive obvious melodies. Others support the structure that lets those melodies exist.

Her key principle is:

> 「目立つものだけがすべてやないし、目立たへんからといって不要というわけでもない」

What stands out is not everything, and something is not unnecessary merely because it does not stand out.

This applies far beyond instrumentation.

It describes:

- costume preparation;
- section leadership;
- emotional mediation;
- B-ensemble members;
- quiet friendships;
- substitute preparation;
- and the anthology itself.

Volume 4 is dedicated to the social equivalents of inner voices and bass lines.

## Asuka as teacher

The story also preserves an aspect of Asuka that the crisis trilogy can obscure.

She is genuinely good at teaching beginners.

She:

- explains mechanisms;
- demonstrates patiently;
- makes musical history interesting;
- and communicates why an obscure instrument matters.

Her charisma is not merely manipulation. Her competence produces real access for others.

The tragedy of Asuka is not that her warmth is fake.

It is that warmth, strategic control, concealment, and care all coexist.

## Kumiko’s early desire

The story occurs before Kumiko’s full awakening in Volume 1.

She remembers Reina asking whether she was frustrated and tentatively says:

> 「私も、頑張りたいとは思ってるよ」

She thinks she also wants to try hard.

The qualification matters.

This is not yet the girl who cries at nationals. It is the first quiet movement from detached participation toward chosen effort.

The anthology opens at the moment before desire becomes dramatic.

# 3. 「科学準備室の京子さん」  
## Ghosts, costumes, and invisible performance labor

Kumiko, Hazuki, and Midori hear that a ghost named Kyoko haunts the science preparation room.

Their nighttime investigation appears to be a comic supernatural interlude. It becomes a story about the labor hidden behind spectacle.

## The three first-years in comic register

The investigation sharply distinguishes their voices:

- Midori is curious and narratively excited.
- Hazuki is visibly frightened but continues moving.
- Kumiko insists upon rationality while betraying that she is also afraid.

This establishes a recurring point about Kumiko: her cool observational style is not the absence of ordinary vulnerability. She simply dislikes being seen inside it.

## Kyoko is a worker before she is a ghost

The “Kyoko” they find is Kiyama Kyoko, a drama-club student helping create and alter costumes for the Sunrise Festival.

The polished visual identity of the parade depends upon:

- old fabric;
- limited budgets;
- borrowed expertise;
- repeated fittings;
- and students spending evenings in a preparation room.

An audience sees colorful uniforms.

It does not see the people who made them possible.

This story applies Asuka’s tuba philosophy to production labor:

> The least visible component may be supporting the entire image.

## Kaori’s envy

Kaori remarks that low brass seems fun.

Kumiko notices the faint envy and pretends not to hear it.

The moment suggests that instrumental sections are emotional microcultures.

The trumpet section contains:

- competition;
- strong seniority;
- Reina’s exceptional ability;
- Kaori’s symbolic status;
- and Yuuko’s intensity.

Low brass, for all its own tensions, often offers:

- comedy;
- food;
- teasing;
- beginner instruction;
- and a more domestic sense of belonging.

Kaori may admire not the instruments but the kind of relationship their section permits.

## The unresolved ghost

The story does not fully eliminate the supernatural possibility.

Kumiko previously sees a crying schoolgirl whose details do not quite match Kyoko’s later appearance and movements.

The ambiguity is playful, but structurally appropriate.

This is a volume about realities present at the edge of the official account.

Even after the apparent explanation, something may remain unseen.

# 4. 「あの子には才能がある」  
## Aoi, Asuka, and the wish to be wanted

This is one of the anthology’s most important stories.

It revisits Aoi after she quits the band and places her in direct conversation with Asuka.

## The former gifted child

Aoi once believed she was unusually capable.

She studied hard, expected to enter an elite school, and experienced failure as the collapse of a self-image.

At Kitauji, she meets Asuka:

- academically superior;
- musically excellent;
- attractive;
- socially powerful;
- and apparently effortless.

Aoi interprets Asuka as a “real genius” and herself as a background character who mistakenly believed she was the protagonist.

This is not simple jealousy.

It is a crisis of narrative status:

> What kind of life remains when you discover that someone else occupies the role you imagined was yours?

## The timing of Kitauji’s transformation

Aoi resents that Kitauji becomes serious during the year she must prepare for university entrance.

The band now offers precisely the meaningful experience that was absent when participation would have been easier.

She repeats:

> Concert band cannot get you into university.

The sentence protects her decision, but it also reveals the framework trapping her.

An activity must justify itself through future utility.

The possibility that music might matter because it is part of one’s life appears irresponsible.

## Why Asuka refuses the presidency

Aoi asks why Asuka did not become president.

Asuka answers that it would be troublesome.

Aoi assumes that someone with aptitude has an obligation to occupy the role. Asuka rejects that premise:

> Does possessing an aptitude mean one must do everything one could do well?

This is an important challenge to meritocratic thinking.

Talent creates possibility. It does not automatically create duty.

The band’s habit of relying on Asuka partly arises from confusing:

- capacity;
- responsibility;
- and consent.

## “I’m good at hiding what I lack”

Aoi says Asuka is lucky and seems to possess everything.

Asuka answers that she lacks many things but is good at hiding them.

This line retrospectively illuminates Volume 3.

Her apparent completeness is a performed absence of visible need.

Aoi sees the polished result, not the family coercion, debt logic, secret paternal longing, or constant emotional management underneath it.

## Aoi’s hidden request

Aoi says she quit because of study pressure, but the conversation reveals another emotional layer.

Some part of her wanted the band to stop her.

She wanted to hear:

- we need you;
- your absence matters;
- come back.

Asuka instead answers instrumentally.

If a rare oboe or bassoon player quit, the ensemble would face difficulty. There are several tenor saxophones. One absence does not create the same technical problem.

Then she tells Aoi to study hard.

The response is factually rational and emotionally devastating.

## Necessary versus wanted

This story provides the clearest negative version of Volume 3’s central distinction.

Aoi asks, indirectly:

> Do I matter?

Asuka answers:

> Your part can be covered.

She interprets the question through institutional utility because that is the language in which she herself has learned to understand belonging.

Aoi does not need to hear that the band will collapse without her.

She needs someone to say that **her particular presence will be missed**.

The story therefore exposes the limit of Asuka’s intelligence before Kumiko finally crosses it in Volume 3.

Asuka understands systems.

She often fails—or refuses—to answer the human question beneath the functional one.

# 5. 「少女漫画ごっこ」  
## Romantic scripts and bodies that refuse to behave like fiction

Midori’s fascination with shōjo manga generates an experiment in the famous wall-don scenario.

The story initially appears to be pure comedy. It is also a compact examination of how fictional scripts shape adolescent expectations.

## Midori’s narrated world

Midori selected a coeducational high school partly because she imagined the romantic possibilities supplied by popular fiction and film.

She does not merely experience life.

She casts it.

A hallway can become a confession scene.  
A senior can become a heroine.  
A gesture can acquire a genre.

This imaginative capacity is central to her warmth. It lets her perceive possibilities other people dismiss.

It can also cause her to approach real relationships as scenes waiting to happen.

## Hazuki’s resistance

Hazuki prefers shōnen manga and finds the wall-don threatening rather than romantic.

This foreshadows the difference between her and Midori’s approaches to love:

- Midori enjoys romance as narrative possibility.
- Hazuki distrusts the abstraction but eventually falls through concrete bodily experience.

Hazuki does not want a trope.

She wants a specific person after he helps her carry a tuba.

## Failed and successful performances

Takuya performs the gesture toward Riko, but the result is underwhelming.

Their real affection does not automatically become legible through a borrowed dramatic pose.

Asuka then wall-dons Riko and produces a much stronger physical response.

The comedy depends partly upon Asuka’s enormous charisma and partly upon the instability of presumed heterosexual scripts.

The “correct” boyfriend does not necessarily create the strongest charge. Performance, personality, proximity, and surprise matter.

## Asuka’s social imagination

Asuka immediately wants Kaori to perform the gesture toward Yuuko because she knows exactly how powerfully Yuuko would react.

Her humor is inseparable from perceptiveness. She continually sees:

- who adores whom;
- which gesture would destabilize someone;
- and how to turn an emotional structure into theater.

## Reality is not less romantic

Midori briefly concludes that reality cannot match manga.

Then she learns that Riko and Takuya are affectionate in quieter private ways.

The story’s answer is not that fiction is false and ordinary life dull.

It is:

> Real intimacy often refuses the most recognizable pose because it has developed its own language.

# 6. 「好きな人の好きな人」  
## Hazuki’s crush, confession, rejection, and ethical courage

The two-part Hazuki story is essential to understanding her.

The main trilogy largely presents the romantic situation from Kumiko’s side. Volume 4 restores Hazuki as the subject of her own desire.

## Attraction begins through embodied kindness

Shuuichi helps Hazuki carry her tuba down the stairs.

Their hands touch. He smiles. She becomes intensely aware of him.

The scene matters because Hazuki has dismissed romance as an irritating abstraction.

She does not fall because a genre tells her she should.

She falls because:

- her body is exhausted;
- someone notices;
- he helps without humiliating her;
- and his competence makes her feel temporarily less alone.

Her crush begins in an experience of being supported.

## Musical admiration enters the attraction

Hazuki is a beginner and regularly feels left behind.

Shuuichi has switched from horn to trombone, yet already plays capably because he possesses prior musical experience and has worked seriously.

She admires:

- his ease;
- his knowledge;
- and his place inside a world she is struggling to enter.

Romantic attraction and musical aspiration are not fully separate.

## Discovering Kumiko

Hazuki sees Kumiko and Shuuichi together after Aoi’s departure and recognizes their familiarity.

Their intimacy is not spectacular. It lies in:

- shared history;
- casual speech;
- bodily ease;
- and an assumption of continued presence.

Hazuki realizes that she likes someone whose emotional world already includes another girl.

## Midori notices

Midori observes the behavioral changes:

- increased concern with appearance;
- passing near Shuuichi’s classroom;
- and a new sensitivity around him.

Her romantic imagination becomes perceptive care rather than mere meddling.

## The indispensable tuba lesson

Before the confession, Asuka tells Hazuki that the tuba is not glamorous but is essential to the ensemble.

> 「目立つものだけがすべてやない」

The line applies simultaneously to Hazuki.

She is not the obvious romantic heroine within the Kumiko–Shuuichi structure. Her feelings are still real. Narrative centrality does not determine emotional worth.

## The temptation to surrender before acting

Hazuki considers abandoning the crush because Kumiko might like Shuuichi.

She imagines this as loyalty.

Midori rejects that framing.

Using Kumiko as the reason not to act would:

- make Kumiko responsible for Hazuki’s decision;
- treat friendship as too fragile to survive honest desire;
- and allow Hazuki to avoid the risk of rejection while calling the avoidance selfless.

Midori says:

> 「他人を言い訳にすんのは絶対に間違ってると思う」

Using another person as an excuse is wrong.

This is one of Midori’s sharpest ethical insights.

## “Whatever happens, Midori will be with you”

Midori does not promise success.

She offers continuity:

> 「どうなっても緑がついてるから」

Whatever happens, Midori will remain.

That is the secure relational ground that lets Hazuki risk desire.

The structure resembles Yuuko’s later role for Mizore. A person can confront possible rejection when another bond makes rejection survivable.

## Hazuki confesses

Hazuki invites Shuuichi to the Agata Festival and states her feelings.

He rejects her.

She then asks whether Kumiko is the reason and recognizes something Shuuichi himself has not yet fully said.

Kumiko had declined Shuuichi’s invitation partly out of consideration for Hazuki. Shuuichi’s disappointment reveals where his feelings are directed.

## Generosity after rejection

Hazuki offers to help Shuuichi and Kumiko become a couple because Kumiko remains important to her.

This is generous.

It is also painful.

The ending makes clear that their shared laughter is artificial. Neither points it out.

The novel refuses to turn Hazuki’s maturity into painless moral perfection.

She can:

- mean the generous offer;
- still be devastated;
- and perform cheerfulness because she does not yet know what else to do.

## Hazuki’s significance

Hazuki becomes one of the trilogy’s clearest models of courageous desire.

Unlike Kumiko and Shuuichi, she speaks before certainty protects her.

She loses.

Her dignity comes not from victory but from ownership:

> She knows what she wanted.  
> She asked.  
> She received an answer.  
> The answer hurt.  
> Her relationships nevertheless continued.

# 7. Midori Kawashima: romantic imagination with ethical intelligence

Volume 4 substantially deepens Midori.

She is not merely:

- cute;
- wealthy;
- musically talented;
- or obsessed with romance.

Her imaginative excess is paired with unusual moral clarity.

## She sees narratives—and knows when they become excuses

Midori enjoys shōjo scripts, weddings, confessions, and emotional symbolism.

Yet when Hazuki tries to cast self-erasure as friendship, Midori rejects the story.

She distinguishes:

- romantic fantasy;
- from ethical action.

She is not fooled simply because a sacrifice sounds beautiful.

## Support without control

Midori wants Hazuki to confess, but her deepest promise is not:

> Do what I say.

It is:

> I will remain after whatever you choose produces consequences.

That is one of the healthiest forms of support in the first four novels.

## Broad attention as “cuteness”

In 「こんぷれっくす」, Hazuki dismisses Midori’s praise because Midori calls nearly everything cute.

This is a misunderstanding of Midori’s gaze.

For Midori, cuteness is not a scarce competitive ranking. It is a mode of attention.

She notices:

- embarrassment;
- awkward clothes;
- a softened face;
- a person trying;
- and the way Riko makes Takuya smile.

Her affection is broad without being empty.

The story asks whether praise must be exclusive to be credible.

Midori’s answer is no:

> Seeing value in many things does not make each act of seeing false.

# 8. 「犬と猿とおかん」  
## Conflict as intimacy

Riko’s viewpoint recasts the famous Natsuki–Yuuko hostility.

In the present, the two argue loudly over what belongs on a fried egg.

To outsiders, they look like natural enemies.

Riko remembers their first meeting and sees something else.

## Yuuko’s immediate social force

Yuuko approaches Riko directly and begins building familiarity without much hesitation.

She can be abrasive, but she is rarely indifferent. Her relational style is active.

## Natsuki’s resistance

Natsuki is redirected into euphonium after failing to obtain her preferred saxophone placement.

At a shop, Yuuko demands Natsuki’s last croquette sandwich. Natsuki refuses largely because Yuuko has demanded it.

Their pattern begins immediately:

- Yuuko pushes.
- Natsuki resists.
- Neither withdraws.
- Riko mediates.

## Riko as “mother”

Riko solves the conflict through practical exchange and is called motherly.

Her role throughout the ensemble is easy to underestimate. She:

- notices emotional dynamics;
- mediates without humiliating participants;
- and converts social noise into something livable.

She is the interpersonal equivalent of a supporting inner voice.

## Why Riko envies them

Riko realizes that Natsuki and Yuuko can say things to each other that most people suppress.

Their arguments signify trust:

- neither fears the relationship will disappear after irritation;
- neither has to perform constant gentleness;
- and each expects the other to answer honestly.

This complicates a common assumption:

> Low conflict does not necessarily indicate greater intimacy.

Sometimes the ability to fight safely is evidence that a relationship can tolerate the full person.

## Preparing the future leadership pair

The story is not only comic background. It establishes why the later selection of Yuuko and Natsuki as president and vice-president is institutionally intelligent.

Their disagreement is not a defect to be removed.

It is a balancing mechanism.

# 9. 「背伸び」  
## Shuuichi, masculinity, and pretending to be an adult

Shuuichi’s viewpoint gives access to the small male minority inside the concert band.

The story is partly comedy about boys waiting outside while the girls change. It also examines the social performances young men use to conceal uncertainty.

## The male microculture

The boys discuss:

- attractive girls;
- romance;
- bodies;
- and who should initiate relationships.

Some of the conversation is crude. One boy jokes about becoming a teacher to date students, and Takuya immediately identifies the idea as criminal.

The story does not present “the boys” as a single moral type. It distinguishes:

- objectification;
- awkward curiosity;
- inhibited affection;
- and actual predatory fantasy.

## Takuya’s directness

Takuya tells Shuuichi that he confessed to Riko.

His principle is simple:

> When the person you want will not come toward you, you must step forward.

This is the lesson Shuuichi repeatedly fails to apply until the final story.

## Black coffee as performed adulthood

Shuuichi encounters Taki at a vending machine.

Wanting to appear mature, he purchases black coffee despite disliking it. He assumes an adult man such as Taki must prefer bitterness.

Taki instead buys sweet, milky coffee and praises Shuuichi for having adult taste.

Shuuichi is trapped inside his own performance.

The title **背伸び** means both physically reaching upward and trying to act older than one is.

The story’s lesson is gentle:

> Adulthood is not the public consumption of bitterness.

Shuuichi recognizes that a sports drink suits him better and stops forcing the image.

## Taki outside authority

The scene also humanizes Taki.

Outside rehearsal, he is:

- relaxed;
- kind;
- mildly awkward;
- and fond of sweet coffee.

This does not erase his authority. It shows that institutional roles are not total identities.

# 10. 「こんぷれっくす」  
## Names, bodies, and borrowing a kinder gaze

Midori dislikes being named Sapphire.

Her parents intended the name as a declaration of uniqueness and value. She experiences it as conspicuous and embarrassing.

If she had been a boy, they might have called her Diamond.

The parental fantasy of a “special child” has become a social burden she must repeatedly explain.

## Midori’s body complex

She also worries about:

- her height;
- small frame;
- childlike appearance;
- and lack of the maturity she sees in other girls.

Her confident affection toward others coexists with discomfort toward herself.

## Hazuki’s counter-complexes

Hazuki dislikes:

- her loud voice;
- darker skin;
- face;
- and perceived lack of conventional femininity.

She compares herself unfavorably with:

- Reina’s beauty;
- Kaori’s softness;
- and the girls who appear naturally suited to romantic narratives.

The two girls possess opposite surfaces but similar difficulty believing they are desirable.

## The failure to receive praise

Midori repeatedly tells Hazuki that she is cute.

Hazuki dismisses the statement because Midori calls so many people and things cute.

This reveals an important relational problem:

> Praise cannot help if the recipient has already constructed a reason it does not count.

The speaker may be sincere. The listener’s defensive interpretation can make sincerity inaccessible.

## Shopping as embodied care

Midori takes Hazuki to buy more feminine clothes.

The point is not that Hazuki must become conventionally feminine to have value. Midori wants her to experience another possible self and be looked at with delight rather than judgment.

Hazuki’s embarrassment becomes evidence of vulnerability, not failure.

## A thematic echo

The story quietly echoes Shindō’s later praise of Asuka and Nozomi’s praise of Mizore.

People often require another person’s gaze to discover qualities they cannot recognize alone.

The challenge is learning to believe the gaze without becoming wholly dependent upon it.

# 11. 「きみのいなくなった日」  
## Mizore before the Volume 2 crisis

This story returns to the previous school year and shows the day Nozomi quits.

It is one of the anthology’s most painful pieces because the event itself is administratively ordinary.

Mizore arrives.

Nozomi and many others are absent.

An upperclassman casually tells her that they have quit.

The world has changed before Mizore is told that a decision exists.

## Abandonment through noncommunication

Nozomi does not directly reject Mizore.

She fails to include her.

For Mizore, that absence is more destabilizing than an argument would have been.

An argument would prove that she occupied Nozomi’s mind.

Silence allows the fear that she never mattered.

## Mizore freezes

She does not:

- pursue Nozomi;
- demand explanation;
- quit;
- or cry publicly.

Her response is immobilization.

This is consistent with the Mizore of Volume 2. Emotional intensity does not produce visible action. It compresses her until action becomes impossible.

## Yuuko arrives immediately

Yuuko finds Mizore alone and asks whether she intends to quit.

Yuuko says she will remain because:

- Kaori asked her to continue;
- she likes trumpet;
- and she wants music in her future, possibly through university.

This distinction matters:

> Another person may initiate a path, but continuation can become self-authored.

Yuuko begins through Kaori’s influence but has developed reasons belonging to herself.

Mizore has not yet done this with Nozomi and oboe.

## Yuuko sees a future Mizore cannot imagine

Yuuko imagines Mizore:

- attending music college;
- playing professionally;
- or joining an overseas orchestra.

Mizore has no equivalent future image. Her music ends conceptually wherever Nozomi ends.

Yuuko sees possibility in her before she can see it herself.

## Pity and mistrust

Yuuko invites Mizore home.

Mizore assumes the invitation comes from pity.

She does not ask the question haunting her:

> Would you still want me if I were not pitiful?

This is another form of the anthology’s needed-versus-wanted problem.

Mizore can interpret care only as response to damage. She cannot yet believe she might be personally desirable as a friend.

## Reframing Volume 2

Yuuko did not suddenly become important during Mizore’s breakdown.

She had been remaining beside her since the first day of abandonment.

The tragedy is not that Yuuko offered too little.

It is that Mizore’s wound prevented her from recognizing the offer as secure.

# 12. 「北宇治高校文化祭」  
## Costumes, ordinary youth, and relationships at play

The cultural-festival story is the anthology’s largest ensemble comedy.

It temporarily removes the cast from the dominant logic of competition and allows them to inhabit school as:

- classmates;
- customers;
- costumed performers;
- crushes;
- pranksters;
- and friends.

This ordinary social world matters because the national project can otherwise make school appear to consist only of rehearsal and judgment.

## Kumiko and Shuuichi’s missed cues

Shuuichi tries to arrange time with Kumiko.

She repeatedly fails to recognize or answer the invitation as romantic, saying she intends to tour with Reina.

Their relationship remains defined by familiarity without explicitness.

## Kumiko’s small jealousy

Kumiko notices that Midori now calls Reina “Reina-chan” rather than Kōsaka.

She experiences slight discomfort and compensates by moving closer to Hazuki.

This is a revealingly petty social reaction.

Kumiko wants Reina to integrate into the group, but part of her also values the exclusivity of their bond.

The novel does not idealize intimacy into pure generosity.

## The crepe shop

Natsuki gives Yuuko an absurd quantity of whipped cream as “special service.”

Yuuko eats it rather than concede defeat.

Their conflict functions as a mutual performance of familiarity.

Mizore offers Nozomi similar treatment and appears disappointed when she declines. Their relationship has resumed, but Mizore is still learning how to create casual intimacy rather than merely receive Nozomi’s attention.

## Kaori as rabbit

Kaori enthusiastically serves as a rabbit-costumed advertising captain.

This gives her a comic, participatory side that the trumpet-solo conflict often hides.

She is not only:

- elegant senior;
- object of Yuuko’s devotion;
- or Asuka’s quiet admirer.

She can be silly and publicly committed to a joke.

## Asuka as fortune teller

Asuka performs fortune-telling, holds and theatrically examines Reina’s hand, and predicts national gold.

For Kumiko, she predicts ghost possession.

Asuka’s philosophy is that fortune-telling should be enjoyed and convenient results believed when useful.

The scene condenses her relation to performance:

- she understands the fiction;
- she enjoys controlling its effect;
- and she treats belief pragmatically rather than metaphysically.

Haruka arrives to make Asuka work, demonstrating a more balanced relationship than Haruka’s former insecurity allowed.

## The tea shop

Shuuichi becomes visibly nervous around Reina.

Kumiko unconsciously orders the same drink as Reina, and Reina notices her tendency to flow with the choices of people around her.

Even in a light story, Reina identifies Kumiko’s central vulnerability:

> Kumiko often discovers herself by adapting to another person’s current.

## Reina as wingwoman

Reina gives Shuuichi two tickets to her class’s haunted house and orders him to take Kumiko.

This is highly significant for the source’s relationship structure.

Reina is not positioned as a romantic rival sabotaging Kumiko’s possible relationship with Shuuichi. She actively helps it progress.

Her intense intimacy with Kumiko and her support for Kumiko–Shuuichi coexist.

This is another reason simplistic competitive shipping models inadequately describe the novel.

## The haunted house

Kumiko and Shuuichi are both frightened.

The bloodied ghost chasing them turns out to be Reina, who delights in Kumiko’s reaction.

The story transforms several habitual roles:

- Reina becomes mischievous rather than austere.
- Kumiko becomes openly frightened rather than observationally composed.
- Shuuichi becomes equally vulnerable rather than masculine protector.
- School horror becomes social play.

## Costumes as identity experimentation

Across the festival, characters become:

- rabbit;
- waiter;
- ghost;
- fortune teller;
- café worker;
- and customer.

These temporary roles do not conceal a single “true self.” They permit parts of the self to emerge under playful conditions.

The festival is a socially sanctioned space for trying on ways of being.

# 13. 「新三年生会議」  
## Succession and the institutional use of conflict

After nationals, the third-years retire and the new leadership structure is announced.

Yuuko becomes president.

Natsuki becomes vice-president.

Takuya becomes low-brass leader, with Riko as vice-leader.

The story is told largely through Takuya, allowing succession to appear not as ceremonial abstraction but as a redistribution of daily responsibility.

## Yuuko as president

Yuuko is already capable of:

- opening meetings;
- mobilizing people;
- asserting standards;
- and treating the band’s future as personally urgent.

She has many qualities required for leadership.

She also runs emotionally hot.

Her loyalty can become:

- impulsiveness;
- rigidity;
- or escalation.

## Natsuki as vice-president

Asuka claims she selected Natsuki because the result would be entertaining.

Riko provides the more serious explanation.

Natsuki is one of the only people who can restrain Yuuko without being intimidated or absorbed into her emotional momentum.

Their antagonism is institutionally useful.

Yuuko pushes.  
Natsuki resists.  
Yuuko acts.  
Natsuki questions.  
Neither allows the other to settle too comfortably into one extreme.

## Leadership as relationship, not isolated merit

The pair is not selected because each independently ranks highest on a generic leadership scale.

They are selected because their interaction produces balance.

This is a more sophisticated model than appointing the single most talented or charismatic individual.

The institution begins learning the lesson of Asuka’s crisis:

> Leadership should emerge from a structure of mutual correction rather than reliance upon one exceptional person.

## Inherited gestures

Yuuko unconsciously imitates Asuka’s clap and meeting mannerisms.

Natsuki notices and responds with nostalgia.

Institutional culture persists through bodily habits before anyone formally declares tradition.

Yuuko does not become Asuka, but traces of Asuka’s leadership travel through her.

## Takuya and Riko

Takuya and Riko walk home holding hands and discuss becoming third-years.

Their relationship is quiet, physically comfortable, and future-conscious.

Takuya plans to attend an instrument-repair school in Tokyo. The approaching separation gives their ordinary time new weight.

The story shows that succession is not only advancement.

It is the beginning of departure.

# 14. 「お兄さんとお父さん」  
## Reina, talent, privilege, and the origin of Taki’s specialness

This childhood story substantially complicates Reina’s philosophy of excellence.

## A musically privileged household

Young Reina has access to:

- piano;
- violin;
- trumpet;
- multiple instruments;
- a professional-musician father;
- practice space;
- and an environment where artistic ambition is assumed to be legitimate.

She also possesses genuine talent and exceptional work ethic.

The story does not deny either.

It asks what those qualities look like when separated from the resources that cultivate them.

## The piano competition

Reina wins a school accompaniment position over her friend Yuka.

Reina understands the result as:

> I practiced more and performed better.

Yuka sees another truth:

- Reina has instruments at home;
- more opportunities to practice;
- parental knowledge;
- and economic support unavailable to her.

She calls Reina unfair.

## Merit and invisible infrastructure

Reina is shocked because she has not thought of her conditions as advantages.

To her, they are simply the world.

This is a crucial revision of the meritocratic position she articulates in Volume 2:

> Become overwhelmingly good, and judgment will follow.

Volume 4 asks:

> Who receives the time, equipment, instruction, safety, and permission required to pursue overwhelming ability?

Reina’s success remains real.

It is not self-created in isolation.

## Music as emotional escape

The accusation disturbs Reina, but she quickly returns to playing with her father.

Music allows her to leave uncomfortable social interpretation behind.

This resembles her later tendency to convert uncertainty into additional practice.

It is productive and evasive at once.

## Young Taki

Reina meets Noboru Taki as a young man carrying a trombone.

He is connected to her father and to Taki’s own father, the former Kitauji conductor.

Taki says he once resented how much of his father’s attention the band consumed, but later entered music education himself.

Reina’s father suggests that Taki may someday conduct Kitauji.

The child burns his name into memory because her father treats him as significant.

## Before romance

This first encounter is important because Taki initially enters Reina’s world as:

- an admired older male figure;
- a musician recognized by her father;
- and part of a paternal artistic lineage.

Her later romantic attachment does not appear from nowhere.

It develops around:

- authority;
- musical legitimacy;
- continuity with her father;
- and an early association with a future she already values.

The title’s “young man and father” accurately captures the original structure.

Taki is first meaningful because he stands near the father whose judgment helps organize Reina’s world.

# 15. Reina’s meritocracy after this story

Reina remains one of the series’ strongest advocates for effort and excellence.

Volume 4 does not expose her as a fraud.

It makes her philosophy incomplete.

## What remains true

Reina:

- practices intensely;
- chooses difficult goals;
- accepts judgment;
- and refuses to hide behind excuses.

Her ability is not merely purchased.

## What becomes visible

Her effort occurs inside a support structure that includes:

- money;
- instruments;
- knowledgeable parents;
- early access;
- and permission to prioritize music.

Yuka’s accusation may be emotionally crude, but it identifies a real inequality.

## Why this matters for Kitauji

Kitauji’s meritocratic auditions are more just than seniority-based placement.

But a fair audition cannot equalize every condition preceding the audition.

Students arrive with different:

- middle-school programs;
- private lessons;
- instruments;
- family resources;
- and available time.

The novels are gradually constructing a nuanced position:

> Merit-based judgment may be necessary.  
> Merit is never produced under perfectly equal conditions.  
> Recognizing structural advantage does not require denying individual effort.  
> Recognizing effort does not require pretending structure is irrelevant.

# 16. 「とある冬の日」  
## Kumiko and Shuuichi finally speak

The final story moves beyond nationals into winter.

It gives Shuuichi the viewpoint and resolves the romantic ambiguity accumulated across the first three novels.

## Watching Kumiko and Reina

Shuuichi sees Kumiko with Reina and feels jealousy.

Reina notices him looking back and responds with an almost smug awareness.

This triangle is not treated as a conventional contest where one girl must defeat the other.

Reina knows Shuuichi’s feelings and has already helped him during the cultural festival.

She also clearly recognizes the intimacy she herself occupies with Kumiko.

## Shuuichi’s coming responsibility

Shuuichi has been selected as a first-year representative or emerging leader for the next band structure.

He worries that he lacks the interpersonal competence for it.

Riko reassures him indirectly by pointing to the coming transition:

- the current first-years will become seniors to new students;
- instruction will become part of their responsibility;
- and Kumiko possesses more social skill than she realizes.

The group is moving out of the protected beginner position.

## Riko’s warning about time

Riko speaks from her own approaching separation from Takuya.

Daily proximity feels permanent while school continues. It is not.

Graduation turns:

- shared routes;
- casual meals;
- and ordinary conversations

into finite opportunities.

She tells Shuuichi that fear of confession does not stop time.

This is the anthology’s mature development of the advice Takuya gave in 「背伸び」.

## Kumiko initiates the meeting

Kumiko unexpectedly calls and asks Shuuichi to meet.

She gives him a miniature trombone charm as a return gift for the white-sunflower hair ornament.

She has purchased a tuba charm for herself because no euphonium version was available.

The detail is comic and thematically apt.

Even in miniature merchandise, the euphonium remains obscure and folded into a broader low-brass category.

Kumiko’s instrument resists easy recognition.

## Instrumental tokens

The exchanged objects encode their relationship:

- Shuuichi gives Kumiko a flower with romantic resonance.
- Kumiko gives Shuuichi his instrument.
- She keeps a low-brass counterpart for herself.

Their intimacy is rooted not only in childhood but in the musical identities they now inhabit together.

## The hair ornament

Shuuichi places the ornament in Kumiko’s hair.

The gesture is physically intimate and visually evaluative.

Kumiko says it may be too cute for someone like her. Shuuichi’s gaze provides a different answer.

Like Midori’s praise of Hazuki, this scene asks whether a person can borrow another’s affectionate perception long enough to inhabit a version of herself she would otherwise reject.

## The confession

Shuuichi finally speaks directly:

> 「好きやねんけど」  
> 「お前を！」

He likes her.

Kumiko’s body reacts before she can form an answer. She crouches under the intensity of her heartbeat.

Then she whispers:

> 「私も、秀一のこと好きだよ」

She likes him too.

## Why the ordinary setting matters

The confession does not occur:

- onstage;
- after a medal;
- during a crisis;
- or in a spectacular festival scene.

It happens on an ordinary winter day.

That is consistent with their relationship.

Reina and Kumiko’s bond repeatedly receives:

- mountaintops;
- festival nights;
- heightened visual beauty;
- and extraordinary emotional language.

Kumiko and Shuuichi’s relationship is built from:

- neighborhood continuity;
- irritation;
- missed invitations;
- instrument cases;
- small gifts;
- and the expectation of seeing each other tomorrow.

The modest setting is not proof of lesser importance.

It is their form.

# 17. Hazuki, Kumiko, Shuuichi, and the ethics of romantic overlap

The anthology makes the romantic structure substantially clearer.

## Hazuki acts first

Hazuki recognizes her feelings and confesses despite uncertainty.

She is rejected but receives a definitive answer.

## Kumiko delays

Kumiko cares about Hazuki and declines Shuuichi’s festival invitation partly to avoid hurting her.

This is compassionate, but it also allows Kumiko to postpone facing her own feelings.

## Shuuichi delays

Shuuichi likes Kumiko but repeatedly uses:

- teasing;
- practical conversation;
- and childhood familiarity

to avoid direct speech.

## Midori rejects false sacrifice

Midori argues that friendship does not require pretending desire never existed.

This is the ethical center of the structure.

The problem is not that two friends can like the same person.

The problem would be:

- manipulation;
- concealment that denies others agency;
- or treating another person as an excuse for one’s own fear.

## Hazuki’s continuing dignity

Kumiko and Shuuichi’s eventual relationship does not render Hazuki’s feelings narratively pointless.

Her confession:

- clarifies Shuuichi’s feelings;
- forces Kumiko to recognize the situation;
- and demonstrates a courage both of them lack.

She is not a failed route.

She is a full participant whose action changes the relational field.

---

# 18. Kumiko and Reina after the explicit romance

Volume 4 is useful because it confirms that Kumiko’s romantic movement toward Shuuichi does not dissolve her intense bond with Reina.

Reina:

- notices Shuuichi;
- understands his feelings;
- encourages him;
- teases Kumiko;
- and remains physically and emotionally close to her.

The novel therefore refuses a simple equation:

> If Kumiko likes Shuuichi romantically, every intimate moment with Reina must have been nonmeaningful.

Nor does it require the inverse:

> If Kumiko and Reina possess intense yuri-coded intimacy, Shuuichi must be narratively irrelevant.

The more accurate portrait is that Kumiko’s emotional life is plural.

Shuuichi represents:

- continuity;
- ordinariness;
- irritation;
- embodied familiarity;
- and romantic possibility.

Reina represents:

- exceptional aspiration;
- aesthetic fascination;
- intimate disclosure;
- challenge;
- and a bond that frequently exceeds ordinary friendship conventions.

Adolescence can contain several kinds of attachment before every boundary has been permanently named.

# 19. Ordinary youth as a serious value

The main trilogy’s competitive structure can make every hour appear subordinate to nationals.

Volume 4 restores the value of experiences that do not improve a contest score:

- ghost hunting;
- wall-don games;
- food arguments;
- clothes shopping;
- culture-festival costumes;
- bad coffee;
- phone charms;
- and walking home while holding hands.

These moments matter for two reasons.

## They make the ensemble worth preserving

A band is not only a performance machine.

It is also:

- a social world;
- a source of ordinary pleasure;
- a place where identities form;
- and a collection of days people later miss.

When third-years cry at retirement, they are not grieving only the loss of competition.

They are grieving these routines.

## They protect artistic ambition from totalization

Kitauji’s seriousness is admirable.

It could become destructive if every relationship were evaluated solely by its contribution to gold.

The anthology quietly resists that reduction.

A day can matter without improving technique.

A friendship can matter without stabilizing the ensemble.

A student can matter even when another player could cover the same part.

# 20. Hidden labor

Across the stories, performance depends upon work that official narratives rarely celebrate.

Examples include:

- Kyoko and the drama students altering costumes;
- Riko mediating conflicts;
- Midori supporting Hazuki before and after confession;
- Yuuko finding Mizore;
- Natsuki restraining Yuuko;
- Takuya and Riko accepting section leadership;
- students preparing festival cafés;
- and parents funding early musical access.

This produces a broader institutional argument:

> Public excellence is assembled from unequal mixtures of visible talent and invisible support.

A soloist receives applause.

Behind that solo may be:

- family money;
- patient instruction;
- a friend who stayed;
- a costume worker;
- a substitute who prepared;
- or a section member carrying the harmony.

The anthology does not diminish the soloist.

It restores the ensemble around her.

# 21. Visibility, replaceability, and personal value

The stories repeatedly separate three concepts that are often confused.

## Visibility

Who attracts attention?

Examples:

- Reina’s trumpet;
- Asuka’s charisma;
- Kaori’s beauty;
- or a romantic protagonist in manga.

## Functional necessity

Whose absence creates a practical problem?

Examples:

- a single oboist;
- a tuba supporting the ensemble;
- or Natsuki restraining Yuuko.

## Personal irreplaceability

Whose particular presence matters to another person, even when the practical role could be filled?

Examples:

- Aoi wanting to be missed;
- Hazuki wanting Shuuichi;
- Yuuko wanting Mizore’s trust;
- Shuuichi wanting Kumiko;
- and Kumiko wanting Asuka in Volume 3.

The anthology’s argument is that these categories overlap but are not identical.

A highly visible person may be emotionally isolated.

A functionally replaceable member may be deeply loved.

An unnoticed player may be structurally essential.

A necessary person may still long to be wanted for reasons beyond necessity.

# 22. Conflict as a relationship form

Several stories complicate the assumption that affection should always look harmonious.

## Natsuki and Yuuko

Their fighting allows directness and mutual correction.

They are selected to lead together partly because neither is overawed by the other.

## Kumiko and Shuuichi

Their irritation is an old form of proximity. Yet it also enables avoidance; conflict can conceal desire as easily as express trust.

## Asuka and Aoi

Their conversation is direct but not safe. Asuka’s truthfulness becomes cruelty because she answers a different question than the one Aoi is asking.

## Hazuki and Midori

Midori sharply rebukes Hazuki rather than gently validating her proposed self-sacrifice.

The confrontation is supportive because Midori is willing to risk Hazuki’s temporary displeasure in order to defend her agency.

The collection’s position is therefore nuanced:

> Conflict can be intimacy when the relationship can survive truth.  
> Conflict becomes harm when one person uses superior power or interpretive control to erase the other’s real question.

# 23. Scripts, roles, and performed identity

Volume 4 is filled with people acting according to social scripts.

## Shōjo romance

Midori imagines wall-don as a standardized route to emotional intensity.

Reality responds unpredictably.

## Masculine adulthood

Shuuichi drinks black coffee to perform mature masculinity.

Taki’s sweet coffee punctures the image.

## Main character and background character

Aoi interprets Asuka as the protagonist and herself as a replaceable extra.

This narrative hierarchy intensifies her shame.

## Meritocracy

Young Reina imagines effort and result as a complete explanation of success.

Yuka introduces material conditions omitted by that script.

## The cultural festival

Everyone consciously performs temporary identities.

Because the performance is acknowledged as play, it can reveal rather than merely conceal.

## The good friend

Hazuki initially imagines that a good friend should erase her own crush.

Midori rejects the role as self-serving theater.

The volume does not argue that all performance is false.

It asks:

> Does the role help someone express a possibility, or does it prevent her from admitting what she wants?

# 24. Speaking styles and viewpoint language

The anthology strongly confirms how well differentiated the cast’s voices are.

## Aoi

Aoi’s narration is organized around:

- comparison;
- academic status;
- protagonist/background hierarchies;
- and resentment disguised as realism.

She interprets people according to how naturally they appear to occupy valued positions.

## Hazuki

Hazuki’s voice is concrete, bodily, and skeptical of romantic abstraction.

She notices:

- weight;
- touch;
- heat;
- embarrassment;
- and immediate social reactions.

Her eventual confession sounds direct because circumlocution does not suit her basic temperament.

## Midori

Midori narrativizes experience through:

- manga;
- cuteness;
- romantic possibility;
- and affectionate symbolic language.

Yet her ethical judgments can be startlingly firm.

## Riko

Riko reads relationships structurally.

She observes:

- who provokes whom;
- who calms whom;
- and what apparent hostility is accomplishing socially.

Her viewpoint makes her ideal for the succession stories.

## Mizore

Mizore’s language remains compressed and suspicious of motive.

She experiences Yuuko’s invitation but immediately translates it into pity.

Her inner voice shows how defensive interpretation can prevent care from being received.

## Reina

Child Reina’s viewpoint is absolutist and materially naïve.

Practice, ability, and result appear straightforward until another child names inequality.

Her discomfort is genuine, but she lacks the social framework to metabolize it.

## Shuuichi

Shuuichi’s narration combines:

- ordinary adolescent masculinity;
- embarrassment;
- longing;
- jealousy;
- and a strong desire not to appear ridiculous.

He recognizes many of his evasions but still struggles to move beyond them.

## Kumiko from outside

One of the anthology’s great pleasures is seeing Kumiko as others see her.

She can appear:

- oblivious;
- socially capable;
- frustratingly passive;
- kind;
- attractive;
- and much more emotionally influential than she imagines.

Her viewpoint is no longer permitted to define the whole cast’s experience of her.

# 25. Visual paratext

## Original cover

The original cover uses a warm orange background and crowds much of the ensemble into overlapping proximity.

Asuka, Kaori, Reina, Midori, Hazuki, Shuuichi, and a partly obscured Kumiko occupy the image without a dominant performance setting.

The composition differs meaningfully from the first three covers.

There is no clear visual narrative of:

- competition;
- solo crisis;
- or one central instrument.

Instead, the emphasis is social density.

Faces overlap.  
Bodies crowd one another.  
Expressions imply embarrassment, amusement, or attention to nearby people.

This is appropriate for a collection about secret interpersonal lives.

Kumiko’s partial visual obscurity is especially fitting. She remains important, but the anthology no longer allows her to dominate the frame.

## Promotional wrapper

The anime promotional wrapper overlays the lower portion with Kyoto Animation’s Kumiko holding the euphonium.

This recenters the work around:

- the recognizable protagonist;
- the title instrument;
- and the anime brand.

The contrast is revealing:

- the original cover promises ensemble side stories;
- the wrapper reasserts Kumiko as the franchise’s primary entry point.

## Chapter-title plates

Each story receives a minimalist musical title page.

These do not provide narrative illustration, but they create formal equality among the stories.

Aoi’s pain, the ghost comedy, Hazuki’s confession, leadership succession, and Reina’s childhood are each presented as distinct movements within one larger score.

# 26. Adaptation baseline

The following material is especially important when comparing the anthology with anime adaptations or omissions.

## 1. Aoi and Asuka’s confrontation

This is indispensable for understanding:

- Aoi’s resentment;
- her desire to be needed;
- Asuka’s utilitarian cruelty;
- and the needed-versus-wanted distinction later resolved by Kumiko.

Removing it makes Aoi simpler and Asuka less morally difficult.

## 2. Hazuki’s full romantic perspective

The two-part story preserves:

- how she falls for Shuuichi;
- her beginner insecurity;
- Midori’s ethical intervention;
- the direct confession;
- the rejection;
- and the pain beneath her generosity.

Reducing this to a comic crush would significantly diminish Hazuki.

## 3. Midori’s seriousness

Her promise to remain beside Hazuki and her rejection of false self-sacrifice are central evidence that she is more than an eccentric cute-girl archetype.

## 4. Yuuko and Natsuki’s origin

Their first food conflict and Riko’s interpretation establish that their antagonism is a form of durable intimacy.

This materially supports their later president–vice-president pairing.

## 5. The leadership succession

The new-third-year meeting is the actual bridge between the first-year trilogy and the next institutional phase.

It explains why:

- Yuuko becomes president;
- Natsuki becomes vice-president;
- and Takuya and Riko inherit low-brass responsibility.

## 6. Mizore’s day of abandonment

This story establishes how early Yuuko begins supporting Mizore and how Mizore’s mistrust prevents her from believing she is wanted.

It strengthens the entire Volume 2 arc.

## 7. Reina’s childhood privilege

The piano conflict is essential counterevidence to an uncomplicated meritocratic reading of Reina.

It demonstrates that the series itself recognizes unequal access beneath competitive judgment.

## 8. Reina and Taki’s first meeting

The childhood encounter places her later romantic fixation within:

- family admiration;
- musical lineage;
- and paternal recognition.

## 9. Reina’s support for Kumiko and Shuuichi

The cultural-festival tickets are important because they prevent a simplistic romantic-rival framing.

## 10. The explicit winter confession

The final story confirms that Kumiko and Shuuichi mutually confess before the next school-year phase.

Any adaptation that delays, removes, or ambiguates this event changes the relational baseline.

## 11. Taki’s sweet coffee

The vending-machine scene is small but valuable characterization. It distinguishes the teacher’s public authority from his ordinary tastes.

## 12. The first low-brass ensemble

Hazuki’s first successful note and simple group performance preserve music’s precompetitive pleasure.

This is important thematic counterweight to the national narrative.

# 28. Character reference matrix after Volume 4

# 29. Provisional questions for the next mainline phase

## Kumiko as mentor

The first story teaches that unnoticed support can be essential. Kumiko is about to become a senior.

Can she recognize beginners whose needs resemble:

- Hazuki’s technical embarrassment;
- Aoi’s desire to matter;
- or Mizore’s inability to ask?

## Yuuko and Natsuki as leaders

Their mutual correction is promising, but leadership magnifies personal habits.

Will:

- Yuuko’s passion become coercive?
- Natsuki’s restraint become disengagement?
- or will each prevent the other’s weakness from controlling the band?

## The inheritance of Asuka’s style

Yuuko already reproduces Asuka’s gestures unconsciously.

Will Kitauji preserve:

- Asuka’s theatrical command;
- her utilitarianism;
- her concealment;
- or only the useful parts?

## Reina and structural awareness

The childhood story exposes unequal access behind talent.

Will Reina ever integrate this into her explicit philosophy, or will she continue treating excellence as principally individual will?

## Mizore’s autonomous future

Yuuko can imagine music college and an orchestra for her.

Can Mizore develop a future that is not merely a continuation of Nozomi’s approval?

## Kumiko and Shuuichi after confession

The relationship now exists explicitly.

Can they communicate directly in ordinary life, or will they continue relying on irritation, implication, and third-party assistance?

## Kumiko and Reina after the romantic baseline changes

Reina supports the relationship, but her bond with Kumiko remains unusually intense.

How will these attachments coexist once the characters become more conscious of their categories and futures?

## Hazuki’s seniority

Hazuki’s beginnerhood and rejection have developed unusual empathy.

Will she become the kind of senior who makes an inexperienced newcomer feel that an unnoticed instrument—and an unnoticed person—still matters?

## Takuya and Riko

Their future contains geographical separation.

Can their quiet, stable relationship survive the end of daily proximity that Riko already fears?

## Aoi after Kitauji’s continuing success

She has left, but the band continues becoming the institution she once wanted.

Will she interpret that development as:

- confirmation that quitting was necessary;
- permanent exclusion;
- or something she can remember without self-punishment?

# Final assessment

Volume 4 is not as dramatically unified as the first three novels, but it is indispensable to the series’ social world.

Its achievement is not a single great climax.

It is **redistribution of narrative dignity**.

The anthology turns toward people and experiences that the main competitive narrative could easily treat as secondary:

- the beginner’s first note;
- the costume maker;
- the quitting student;
- the rejected girl;
- the mediator;
- the substitute leader;
- the lonely oboist;
- the privileged prodigy’s childhood friend;
- and the ordinary boy trying to say something sincere.

The title’s “secrets” are not primarily hidden scandals.

They are the truths that disappear when a person is described only by her public role.

Aoi is not only the senior who quit.

Hazuki is not only the girl Shuuichi rejected.

Midori is not only the eccentric prodigy.

Yuuko and Natsuki are not merely comic enemies.

Mizore is not only the silent genius.

Reina is not only talent created by will.

Shuuichi is not only Kumiko’s familiar childhood friend.

The band itself is not only the fifty-five or fifty-six people visible on a competition stage.

It includes:

- past members;
- B-ensemble players;
- costume workers;
- parents;
- teachers;
- friends;
- and the ordinary days that give performance emotional meaning.

The collection’s most important principle is first stated through the tuba:

> What stands out is not everything.  
> What does not stand out is not therefore unnecessary.

Volume 4 applies that principle to human beings.

Aoi’s story then sharpens it:

> Being unnecessary to the mechanics of an ensemble does not mean one’s absence is emotionally meaningless.

Hazuki’s story adds:

> A feeling does not become worthless because it is not reciprocated.

Mizore’s story adds:

> Care cannot help if the wounded person believes she is wanted only because she is wounded.

Reina’s story adds:

> Excellence is personal labor built upon conditions the performer may not see.

The succession story adds:

> Institutions endure not by finding another singular genius, but by building relationships in which people correct and support one another.

The winter confession closes the volume by moving from collective secrets to personal declaration.

Kumiko and Shuuichi have spent years living inside mutual familiarity. Their bond is visible to almost everyone except the two people most responsible for naming it.

On one ordinary winter day, they finally do.

That ending is appropriate because Volume 4 repeatedly argues that ordinary life is not filler surrounding the meaningful performance.

Ordinary life is where meaning accumulates.

The national audience may never know:

- who made the costumes;
- who cried after a rejected confession;
- who bought the wrong coffee;
- who asked a lonely girl to come home;
- who chose the president’s necessary counterweight;
- or who received a tiny trombone charm in winter.

But those private events shape the people who later lift their instruments.

Volume 4’s place in the series can therefore be summarized as:

> **The first trilogy teaches Kitauji how to sound together.  
> The secret stories teach us what each person was carrying into that sound.**
