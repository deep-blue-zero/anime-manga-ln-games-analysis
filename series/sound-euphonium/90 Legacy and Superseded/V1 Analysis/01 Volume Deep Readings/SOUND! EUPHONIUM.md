# SOUND! EUPHONIUM  
## Novel 1: *Welcome to the Kitauji High School Concert Band*

### Literary deep dive and series-analysis baseline

**Japanese title:** 『響け！ユーフォニアム 北宇治高校吹奏楽部へようこそ』  
**Author:** Takeda Ayano / 武田綾乃  
**Scope:** Novel 1 of the ten-volume corpus  
**Spoiler level:** Complete Volume 1 spoilers

> **Volume 1 is not fundamentally about a weak band becoming good. It is about a girl who has survived by not wanting anything too openly learning to desire something strongly enough that failure can hurt.**

# I. Executive thesis

*Sound! Euphonium* Volume 1 is a novel about **the moral and emotional difficulty of wanting**.

Kumiko Oumae enters high school hoping for a reset. She has deliberately chosen a school where few people know her. She selected Kitauji partly because its sailor uniform is cute. She is uncertain whether she even wants to continue concert band. Her dominant strategy is to avoid commitments that might expose her:

- she goes along with groups;
- softens her opinions;
- lets other people decide first;
- keeps her ambitions hypothetical;
- avoids saying whom she supports;
- and treats disappointment as something preventable through lowered expectations.

This does not make Kumiko apathetic. She is observant, musically experienced, emotionally responsive, and more attached to the euphonium than she initially admits. What she lacks is not feeling. She lacks the willingness to **claim** her feeling.

Reina Kōsaka represents the opposite condition. Reina is prepared to want something so intensely that defeat becomes unbearable. She wants to reach nationals. She wants to become special. She wants to play the trumpet at a level that separates her from other people. She loves Taki. She wants the solo. She neither apologizes for these desires nor disguises them as casual possibilities.

The prologue therefore establishes the novel’s governing contrast:

> Reina loses and cries because she truly expected to win.  
> Kumiko does not cry because she protected herself from expecting anything.

Reina’s accusation—「アンタは悔しくないわけ？」, “Aren’t you frustrated?”—becomes the question that the entire volume gradually answers.

By the final performance, Kumiko thinks, without qualification, that she wants to win. In the epilogue, she cries after Kitauji advances to the Kansai competition. She can finally tell Reina that she is genuinely happy because the result now belongs to her emotionally.

The external transformation is:

> a disorderly band becomes a competitive ensemble.

The deeper transformation is:

> **Kumiko moves from participating without ownership to wanting without an alibi.**

# II. Narrative architecture

The novel consists of a prologue, four main chapters, and an epilogue:

| Section | External movement | Kumiko’s internal movement |
|---|---|---|
| **Prologue** | Middle-school competition result | She discovers the gulf between her own detachment and Reina’s commitment |
| **1. よろしくユーフォニアム** | Entrance into Kitauji and the band | She attempts a new life but returns to the instrument she cannot abandon |
| **2. ただいまフェスティバル** | Sunrise Festival and interpersonal entanglement | Performance pleasure returns; Reina exposes the self beneath Kumiko’s “good girl” skin |
| **3. おかえりオーディション** | Intensive training and auditions | Old humiliation returns, but so does Kumiko’s desire to improve |
| **4. さよならコンクール** | Audition fallout, solo dispute, Kyoto competition | She says goodbye to competition as something emotionally external |
| **Epilogue** | Kitauji advances to Kansai | Kumiko finally shares Reina’s tears and desire |

The sequence of chapter titles is significant:

- **よろしく** — a greeting or request for a good relationship;
- **ただいま** — “I’m home”;
- **おかえり** — “welcome back”;
- **さよなら** — “goodbye.”

The first volume is structured as a cycle of meeting, homecoming, return, and farewell. What exactly returns changes with each chapter:

- Kumiko returns to concert band.
- She returns to the physical pleasure of performance.
- Her old audition trauma returns.
- Reina’s middle-school question returns.
- The euphonium returns as something she chose rather than something assigned to her.

The “goodbye” in the fourth chapter cannot simply mean farewell to competition, because the novel ends by extending the competitive journey. A stronger interpretation is that Kumiko says goodbye to **her previous relation to competition**: the position from which she could perform, receive a result, and remain emotionally untouched.

## The mirrored result-board scenes

The prologue and epilogue deliberately repeat one another.

Both scenes contain:

- hundreds of people facing one direction;
- summer heat;
- a posted competition result;
- Kumiko’s racing heart;
- Reina crying;
- and the question of what those tears mean.

In the prologue, Kumiko initially assumes Reina must be crying from happiness. When Reina explains that she is devastated, Kumiko asks whether she seriously believed they could reach nationals. Kumiko’s response is not malicious, but its unfiltered honesty humiliates both girls. Reina is exposed as someone who wanted too much; Kumiko is exposed as someone who did not want enough.

In the epilogue, Reina again embraces Kumiko in tears. Kumiko briefly fears that Kitauji has failed. Instead, Reina announces that they have advanced to Kansai. This time Kumiko’s body gives way. Her vision blurs, her throat tightens, and she cries with Reina.

The same image has acquired an opposite emotional meaning because Kumiko has changed.

The epilogue is not merely a happy reprise. It provides the proof that the novel’s internal arc has succeeded:

> **Kumiko can now be hurt or delighted by a result because she has finally entrusted part of herself to the effort.**

# III. Kumiko Oumae: detachment learning to desire

## 1. Kumiko’s central defensive mechanism

Kumiko is frequently described as passive, but “passive” does not capture her precision. Her primary trait is **defensive noncommitment**.

She is good at remaining adjacent to desire without becoming accountable for it.

She can say:

- it would be nice to reach nationals;
- she might continue band;
- Reina is probably better;
- Kaori’s situation is sad;
- Shuuichi is only a childhood friend;
- she is helping Hazuki because that is what a friend should do.

What she avoids is saying:

- I want to reach nationals.
- I want to play.
- I support Reina.
- I am jealous.
- I want Shuuichi’s attention.
- I care how this turns out.

This is not simple cowardice. Kumiko has learned that declared desire makes a person vulnerable to several forms of pain:

1. **Failure:** wanting nationals makes a “dud gold” devastating rather than acceptable.
2. **Social punishment:** succeeding in an audition once made an older student hate and bully her.
3. **Moral responsibility:** choosing one side means accepting that another person may be hurt.
4. **Self-knowledge:** admitting jealousy or ambition would damage her preferred identity as an easygoing, kind person.

Her journey is therefore not from indifference to passion. It is from **disavowed passion to acknowledged passion**.

## 2. The “good girl” skin

Kumiko has internalized the idea that she is kind. People have praised her as a good girl, and she has gradually organized her social behavior around preserving that description.

Reina is fascinated by the gap between this surface and Kumiko’s less presentable interior. During their festival-night excursion, Reina tells her that she wants to peel away her “good girl” skin. She identifies the middle-school question—“Did you really think we could make nationals?”—as the moment Kumiko became interesting to her.

Kumiko had not intended cruelty. That is precisely why Reina noticed her.

The sentence revealed a person beneath the socially edited version:

- skeptical;
- unsentimental;
- blunt;
- slightly mean;
- and capable of seeing the absurdity in another person’s idealism.

Reina does not demand that Kumiko become nicer. She wants Kumiko to become **less falsified**.

This is crucial to their relationship. Reina is not merely the passionate friend who motivates a passive protagonist. She is the person who grants Kumiko permission to possess an unattractive interior without ceasing to be worthy of attachment.

When Reina jokingly calls this an “愛の告白,” a confession of love, the joke works because the encounter already carries intimacy. Reina is declaring attachment not to Kumiko’s public virtue, but to the less flattering person behind it.

## 3. Kumiko’s observational intelligence

The novel uses close third-person narration so thoroughly aligned with Kumiko that her perception shapes nearly every character before that character can define herself.

Kumiko notices:

- body types and clothing;
- social seating patterns;
- false smiles;
- who is looking at whom;
- shifts in group atmosphere;
- suppressed resentment;
- instrumental weaknesses;
- the difference between competent and exceptional playing;
- and the tiny gestures by which people contradict what they say.

This makes her a strong analyst of other people and a poor interpreter of herself.

She recognizes that Aoi is lying about having no regrets because Aoi’s fingers are digging into her own arm. She sees the coldness behind Asuka’s smile. She notices Shuuichi trying to find someone with whom to celebrate his audition result. She understands that Kaori’s second audition is not really an attempt to overturn the result but an attempt to make defeat emotionally undeniable.

Yet Kumiko repeatedly fails—or refuses—to recognize her own:

- attachment to Shuuichi;
- envy of Hazuki;
- attraction to Reina;
- investment in the audition;
- love of the euphonium;
- and desire to win.

Her intelligence initially flows outward because outward interpretation is safer than inward admission.

## 4. Her unflattering interior is part of her literary strength

Kumiko is not idealized.

She experiences jealousy when Hazuki pursues Shuuichi, even while helping her. She feels relief when Shuuichi rejects Hazuki, then recognizes the ugliness of that relief. She judges other people’s appearances. She notices sexualized bodies and imagines physical textures. She avoids taking sides when friends argue because neutrality protects her comfort. She is sometimes relieved when another subject interrupts a difficult conversation.

These details prevent her from becoming a generic “ordinary girl” protagonist. She is ordinary in the more rigorous sense: she is contradictory, vain, kind, cowardly, perceptive, selfish, embarrassed, and capable of growth.

The novel’s ethical position is not that Kumiko’s concealed thoughts are secretly virtuous. It is that meaningful commitment begins only once she stops confusing social pleasantness with the whole of her character.

## 5. The old audition wound

Kumiko’s unease around auditions has a concrete origin.

In middle school, she defeated an older euphonium player and entered the competition ensemble. The senior responded by ignoring her, accusing her of mockery, and physically mistreating her instrument. This experience teaches Kumiko several destructive lessons:

- competence can make other people hate you;
- official fairness does not protect you socially;
- seniority remains powerful even after merit has supposedly decided;
- and success can isolate the person who earned it.

When Kumiko defeats Natsuki in the Kitauji audition, she expects the old pattern to repeat.

Instead, Natsuki responds with generosity. She congratulates Kumiko, supports the A ensemble, and refuses to convert personal disappointment into punishment of a junior. This is one of the novel’s quietest but most important acts of kindness.

Natsuki does not erase the pain of losing. She proves that disappointment does not have to become cruelty.

Her behavior gives Kumiko a corrective experience:

> **Meritocratic loss can wound without morally authorizing abuse.**

## 6. The euphonium as chosen attachment

Kumiko was originally assigned the euphonium when she wanted to play trombone like her older sister. She describes it as obscure, unglamorous, and visually unimpressive. She has imagined switching instruments.

Yet she always returns to it.

Near the competition, she recognizes that she has spent seven years with the euphonium. Its warm sound, supporting function, weight, and bodily familiarity have become part of her. Her realization is not merely that she “likes” it:

> she loves it.

This matters because the euphonium parallels Kumiko herself.

Both are:

- easy to overlook;
- situated between foreground and foundation;
- less glamorous than the trumpet;
- difficult to explain to outsiders;
- and essential to the ensemble’s body.

Kumiko does not need to become Reina to become serious. Her development does not require transformation into a spectacular soloist. It requires recognizing the worth of the musical position she already inhabits.

The title’s imperative—*Resound, Euphonium*—can therefore be read personally as well as musically:

> **The instrument must sound, but so must the girl who has habitually muted her own desire.**

## 7. Kumiko’s decisive movement

Several moments chart her change:

1. She returns to concert band despite intending to escape it.
2. She experiences the pleasure of improvement under Taki.
3. After Shuuichi speaks hopefully about nationals, she says she wants to become better.
4. She practices seriously for the audition.
5. She begins supporting Reina rather than merely observing the solo dispute.
6. She tells Asuka that the Kyoto competition will not be their last performance because they are going to nationals.
7. Onstage, she thinks: **I want to win.**
8. In the epilogue, she cries.

The culmination is not the medal. It is the sentence inside Kumiko before the performance:

> 「勝ちたいと、久美子は心の底から思う。」

She wants to win from the bottom of her heart.

That thought would have been impossible for the Kumiko of the prologue.

# IV. Reina Kōsaka: desire without apology

## 1. Reina as Kumiko’s opposite and possible future

Reina is introduced through emotional disproportion.

Everyone else celebrates a gold award. Reina cries because it does not lead to the next level. Her response appears excessive only if one accepts the group’s reduced expectations. From Reina’s perspective, the group is celebrating failure because calling it success is more comfortable.

Her defining phrase is:

> 「アタシはさ、特別になりたい。」

She wants to become special.

Reina does not mean that she wants vague self-esteem. She wants:

- praise;
- distinction;
- exceptional skill;
- and separation from the mass of ordinary people.

The trumpet is the means by which she intends to produce that distinction.

Her desire is aesthetically serious and socially dangerous. She is willing to bear isolation in exchange for excellence, but she is not immune to the isolation. The novel repeatedly shows that her apparent hardness has costs.

## 2. Talent and social illegibility

Reina’s playing is not merely somewhat better than Kaori’s. The second audition makes the qualitative difference audible even through prose.

Kaori’s performance is:

- polished;
- delicate;
- technically excellent;
- and visibly improved through desperate effort.

Reina’s performance changes the atmosphere of the hall. Its power, beauty, continuity, and residual heat produce an involuntary collective response.

The novel’s position is clear: Reina deserves the solo.

But the social conflict remains real because institutional legitimacy requires more than a technically correct decision. The band knows:

- Kaori is a beloved senior;
- this is her last competition;
- she has worked for years;
- Reina is a first-year student;
- Reina knows Taki;
- and Reina’s manner does little to reassure people that she respects the emotional order she is disrupting.

Reina responds to complaints with the severe logic that anyone who objects should become better than she is. That is artistically coherent and socially merciless.

The novel admires her clarity without treating it as a complete philosophy of communal life.

## 3. Reina’s vulnerability

Reina is frightened before the second audition. Her fingers tremble. She takes deep breaths. After the accusations of favoritism, she privately breaks down against Kumiko and asks whether she is wrong.

This is an important correction to the image of effortless genius.

Reina is not fearless. She has chosen a value system that requires acting despite fear:

> the better performance should win, even when she herself must endure the hostility produced by winning.

Her relationship with Kumiko gives her somewhere to express what her public identity cannot safely contain.

## 4. Reina, Kumiko, and intimacy

The novel already gives Kumiko and Reina a highly charged relationship.

Their festival-night sequence includes:

- deliberate separation from the crowd;
- a white dress chosen for the encounter;
- physical closeness;
- touch along Kumiko’s face;
- playful “love confession” language;
- discussion of concealed selves;
- Reina’s admission that she wants to be special;
- and the experience of silence as something comfortable rather than empty.

Kumiko also notices Reina sensually: her ears, scent, skin, hair, clothing, and physical movements. This should not automatically be collapsed into a single declared romantic category, but neither should it be dismissed as ordinary exposition.

The novel distinguishes several layers:

- Reina explicitly describes her feelings for Taki as romantic love.
- Kumiko’s relationship with Shuuichi contains repressed heterosexual attraction.
- Kumiko and Reina share intense emotional, aesthetic, and bodily intimacy.
- Reina frames her attraction to Kumiko as attraction to the concealed self beneath her social performance.

The important literary point is not to force these relationships into mutually exclusive boxes. Kumiko is an adolescent whose desires are less settled than her excuses.

Reina functions as:

- friend;
- artistic ideal;
- object of fascination;
- moral challenge;
- and intimate witness.

## 5. The limits of “specialness”

Reina’s philosophy is powerful because it gives form to effort. It is incomplete because no ensemble can consist entirely of isolated exceptions.

She wants to stand apart, but her trumpet exists inside collective music. Her solo can lead the piece only because dozens of other players give it harmonic, rhythmic, and emotional context.

Volume 1 does not defeat Reina’s philosophy. It places it inside a larger problem:

> **How can exceptional ambition remain exceptional without treating everyone else as scenery?**

That question remains open at the end of the volume.

# V. Taki Noboru: pedagogy, meritocracy, and the engineering of atmosphere

## 1. Taki does not merely teach technique

Taki’s most important early act is to ask the students what they want.

He gives them a choice between pursuing nationals and treating the club as a more relaxed activity. This appears democratic. It is also strategic.

Once the students vote for nationals, Taki gains a basis for holding them accountable:

> this is not a standard he imposed from outside; it is the goal they themselves selected.

He repeatedly returns their own decision to them. If they want nationals, rehearsal habits, attendance, auditions, and standards must correspond to that desire.

His pedagogy is therefore built around the conversion:

> **declared choice → obligation → labor.**

This mirrors Kumiko’s personal arc. Wanting is meaningful only when the person accepts the work and risk implied by the desire.

## 2. Taki as an architect of “air”

Natsuki offers one of the novel’s sharpest interpretations of Taki. She suspects that the first disastrous ensemble rehearsal was partly staged as a demonstration.

Taki allows the students to hear:

- how bad they are;
- how far they are from their stated goal;
- and how quickly proper instruction can produce improvement.

This establishes both the band’s deficiency and his authority.

Taki understands that a school ensemble is governed not only by rules but by **空気**, the social atmosphere that determines what behavior feels normal.

The previous year’s atmosphere made apathy normal. Serious first-years were treated as disruptive because their effort implicitly judged the seniors.

Taki creates a new atmosphere in which:

- practice is expected;
- questions are legitimate;
- auditions matter;
- mediocrity becomes embarrassing;
- and reaching nationals begins to feel conceivable.

This is effective leadership. It is also a form of social manipulation.

The novel does not present manipulation as automatically sinister. It asks what kind of manipulation is necessary to redirect a group whose existing norms preserve failure.

## 3. Meritocracy as justice

Taki’s auditions attack an old institutional injustice: automatic senior privilege.

Under his system:

- first-years can defeat seniors;
- position is not guaranteed by age;
- every player must demonstrate ability;
- and the ensemble is selected for performance rather than sentiment.

For Reina and Kumiko, this is protective. It allows talent to become institutionally legible.

It also forces the band to confront the human cost of its stated goal. “We want nationals” sounds harmless until it means:

- a senior loses her final opportunity;
- a friend sits in the B ensemble;
- a first-year becomes socially hated;
- and years of service carry less weight than several minutes of superior playing.

Taki’s meritocracy is not portrayed as false. Reina really is better. Kumiko really does deserve her seat. But procedural fairness does not dissolve grief.

## 4. Taki’s emotional reserve

Taki generally appears controlled, courteous, and difficult to read. His measured voice can make severe judgments feel almost impersonal.

Yet several moments complicate the image:

- he tells Kumiko he genuinely believes this group can reach nationals;
- he praises the final rehearsal as perfect;
- he smiles with confidence before the competition;
- and his eyes are moist after Kitauji advances.

The epilogue prevents him from becoming a mere technician. He has also wagered emotionally on the group. He simply expresses investment through structure rather than confession.

# VI. Kitauji as a social institution

## 1. The band inherits an unresolved moral injury

The current band cannot be understood solely through its visible members.

The previous year, ambitious first-years attempted to make the ensemble practice seriously. Older students ignored, ostracized, and suppressed them. Many of the serious students quit. The unusually small second-year class is the visible residue of that conflict.

The central wrong was not merely “the band was lazy.” It was that senior students used hierarchy and group cohesion to punish people whose effort exposed their inadequacy.

The surviving members carry different relationships to that history:

- **Aoi** feels complicit and cannot participate in sudden collective enthusiasm without recognizing the hypocrisy.
- **Haruka** feels that she failed to protect younger students.
- **Natsuki** entered partly because the club appeared relaxed and initially had little competitive commitment.
- **Asuka** remained socially functional with everyone while refusing intervention.
- the new first-years enter without fully understanding what happened.

Taki can improve the band, but improvement does not retroactively repair those who were driven out.

## 2. Aoi and the refusal of false renewal

Aoi quits to prioritize examinations, but her reasons are not purely academic.

She admits that voting against nationals gave her an alibi. She knew she might leave and did not want to perform enthusiasm she could not sustain. She also resents the ease with which the club now embraces seriousness after punishing serious students the previous year.

Aoi’s position challenges the narrative of inspirational renewal:

> **Can an institution call itself transformed when it has never properly answered for the people it harmed?**

Her later claim that she has no regrets is contradicted by her body. Kumiko sees her grip her own arm hard enough to leave marks and chooses to pretend she believes her.

That moment encapsulates both characters:

- Aoi needs the lie to remain functional.
- Kumiko recognizes the lie but avoids forcing truth upon her.
- neither resolution nor condemnation is available.

## 3. Haruka: leadership without charisma

Haruka believes Asuka should have been president. She is burdened by comparison, uncertain of her authority, and wounded by Aoi’s departure.

Yet the novel gradually shows the value of her apparently ordinary leadership.

Haruka:

- worries about members;
- feels responsible for failures;
- remains emotionally reachable;
- and speaks to the group not from dazzling superiority but from shared vulnerability.

Before the competition, Taki asks her to address the band. Her speech is awkward but sincere: everyone has worked, and now they must place that work into the limited performance time.

The band answers her.

Haruka’s leadership is not Asuka’s command of attention. It is the legitimacy of someone who is visibly carrying the same fear as everyone else.

## 4. Collective atmosphere is morally neutral

One of the novel’s most sophisticated claims is that group unity is not inherently good.

The same collective force can produce:

- last year’s ostracism of earnest members;
- this year’s contempt for Reina;
- contagious apathy;
- contagious effort;
- shared rehearsal discipline;
- and the bus-wide singing before the competition.

The “air” of the room does not become virtuous simply because everyone agrees.

Volume 1 therefore avoids a simplistic club-anime equation:

> unity = goodness.

Its stronger formulation is:

> **Collective energy amplifies whatever value currently governs the group.**

A healthy institution needs more than harmony. It needs a worthy standard and mechanisms that prevent harmony from becoming coercion.

# VII. Asuka Tanaka: charisma without declared allegiance

Asuka is perhaps the volume’s most deliberately unresolved character.

She is:

- exceptionally skilled;
- academically brilliant;
- knowledgeable about the euphonium;
- socially magnetic;
- comically theatrical;
- and capable of controlling the emotional temperature of a room.

She appears to be the band’s natural leader, but she refused the presidency and reluctantly accepted the vice presidency.

The reason becomes increasingly legible: Asuka wants access to music without accepting full responsibility for other people’s emotional demands.

## 1. Asuka’s neutrality

During the previous year’s conflict, Asuka did not participate in the bullying, but she did not defend its targets. She practiced and maintained workable relations with both camps.

Natsuki calls her “special” partly because Asuka seems able to exist outside the needs governing everyone else.

When Kumiko asks privately whether she supports Kaori or Reina, Asuka eventually answers that she does not care who plays the solo. She considers the conflict itself trivial.

This answer is genuinely cold. It also may not be the complete truth.

Asuka:

- brings Kaori a drink;
- protects her private practice;
- understands that she needs emotional closure;
- and returns to her after dismissing the entire issue.

Her actions show attachment while her stated philosophy rejects attachment as a valid basis for judgment.

The contradiction suggests that neutrality may be another form of performance.

## 2. The ethical ambiguity of nonintervention

Asuka’s neutrality allows her to remain effective. She is not consumed by factions. She can stabilize Haruka and continue playing.

But neutrality also protects her from responsibility.

Her position raises one of the series’ recurring questions:

> **When does refusing to take sides preserve fairness, and when does it merely preserve oneself?**

Kumiko is drawn to Asuka because she recognizes a more perfected version of her own defense: acute perception without declared investment.

Asuka is what Kumiko might become if she learned to make detachment charismatic rather than abandoning it.

---

# VIII. Kaori and the dignity of losing

Kaori’s solo conflict could easily have been reduced to sentimental seniority versus objective merit. The novel makes it more psychologically exact.

Kaori knows Reina is better.

What she lacks is not information. She lacks a form of defeat she can emotionally inhabit.

She continues practicing the solo in private because merely being told she lost leaves open several painful possibilities:

- perhaps the audition was unfair;
- perhaps Taki favored Reina;
- perhaps she could have won with more time;
- perhaps her final chance was taken without adequate recognition.

The public second audition gives her what Asuka calls **納得**: not happiness, but a result she can internally accept.

Kaori’s improved performance is excellent. Reina’s is still decisively stronger.

When Taki asks whether Kaori wants to play the solo, he gives her the opportunity to seize it despite what everyone has heard. Kaori refuses. Her statement that she cannot play is both a relinquishment and a moral achievement.

The narration recognizes the contradiction:

- she sincerely believes Reina should play;
- she sincerely wishes the truth were different.

Her acceptance does not purify the pain.

This is one of Volume 1’s most mature judgments:

> **A dignified choice does not require the person making it to stop wanting the alternative.**

Kaori becomes admirable not because she ceases to desire the solo, but because she refuses to make the ensemble pay for that desire once the musical truth is undeniable.

# IX. Natsuki, Hazuki, Midori, and Shuuichi

## Natsuki: disappointment without retaliation

Natsuki initially represents the club’s relaxed culture. She joined partly because Kitauji did not seem demanding and does not begin with strong competitive investment.

Her audition loss reveals more character than an easy victory would have.

She is disappointed but does not punish Kumiko. She participates in the B ensemble, supports the competition members, and helps make collective success possible. Her maturity provides a contrast both to Kumiko’s middle-school senior and to the broader band’s treatment of Reina.

Natsuki demonstrates a form of solidarity not dependent on getting what one wants.

## Hazuki: beginnerhood and emotional directness

Hazuki begins with little musical knowledge and gravitates toward visually glamorous instruments. She ends up on tuba, learns through bodily strain, and discovers the less visible labor of low brass.

Her romantic storyline with Shuuichi also serves a thematic purpose.

Hazuki can:

- like someone;
- admit it;
- confess;
- be rejected;
- grieve;
- and continue living.

Kumiko finds this terrifying because Hazuki accepts the very vulnerability Kumiko avoids.

Hazuki’s rejection is painful but clarifying. Kumiko’s refusal to name her own desire preserves ambiguity at the expense of everyone involved.

## Midori/Sapphire: excellence without Reina’s severity

Midori comes from an elite school and is already an excellent contrabassist. She loves concert band so thoroughly that even painful practice never made her hate it.

She offers two essential counterpoints.

First, she embodies pride without defensive embarrassment. Before the competition, she advises Kumiko to think not “I must not fail” but essentially:

> behold my extraordinary technique.

Second, her experience at an elite school prevents the novel from equating competitive success with human flourishing. She has seen national-level excellence coexist with:

- ostracism;
- resentment;
- brutal auditions;
- and broken relationships.

During the solo dispute, she initially wonders whether allowing Kaori to play might preserve something more important than the marginal competitive advantage.

Hazuki rejects that solution because it would unfairly punish Reina.

Neither girl is foolish. They represent two valid ethical concerns:

- **Midori:** music is not the only human good.
- **Hazuki:** compassion cannot require the best performer to surrender what she earned.

Kumiko’s reluctance to answer shows that choosing fairly often means injuring a value one also recognizes.

## Shuuichi: familiarity, embarrassment, and reciprocal avoidance

Shuuichi is more than a conventional romance marker.

He and Kumiko share:

- childhood history;
- musical history;
- mutual knowledge;
- attraction;
- resentment;
- and an ability to soothe one another through ordinary irritation.

Both avoid directness. Kumiko helps Hazuki pursue him while suppressing jealousy. Shuuichi rejects Hazuki because his interest lies elsewhere, then avoids Kumiko rather than clarifying matters.

At the competition, when Kumiko nearly panics, Shuuichi interrupts the spiral. He tells her that she has practiced enough and that she will be fine, even though his own fingers are trembling. Their physical contact restores both of them to the present.

This relationship provides a different kind of intimacy from Kumiko and Reina:

- Reina draws Kumiko toward exceptional desire and concealed identity.
- Shuuichi gives her continuity, bodily familiarity, and ordinary mutual dependence.

The novel does not require one relationship to invalidate the other.

# X. Major themes

## 1. Desire versus alibi

Many characters create alibis that protect them from the consequences of wanting:

- Kumiko says “if we can” rather than “I want.”
- Aoi votes against nationals partly to justify leaving.
- Asuka invokes official neutrality.
- Kaori initially allows supporters to articulate the challenge she cannot comfortably demand herself.
- Kumiko helps Hazuki rather than acknowledge her own feelings for Shuuichi.
- the old band treated competitive apathy as a social norm so no one had to confront failure.

Taki’s regime strips away these alibis. Once the group chooses nationals, it must encounter what that choice costs.

## 2. Effort as exposure

Effort is often praised as inherently noble. Volume 1 understands why people avoid it.

To work seriously is to produce evidence of what one wanted. If the effort fails, the person cannot claim she never cared.

Reina’s tears are possible because she tried.  
Kaori’s second audition hurts because she practiced desperately.  
Kumiko’s panic is possible because the result now matters.  
Aoi’s withdrawal protects her from one danger while exposing another regret.

The novel’s position is not “effort guarantees success.” It is:

> **Effort makes the self visible—to others and to itself.**

## 3. Meritocracy and human dignity

Volume 1 supports meritocratic selection more strongly than it opposes it. Reina and Kumiko deserve their positions.

But it refuses the idea that a correct ranking resolves every moral problem.

Meritocracy can:

- correct seniority-based injustice;
- reward skill;
- improve the ensemble;
- and protect talented younger players.

It can also:

- humiliate;
- isolate;
- intensify rivalry;
- and encourage people to treat the disappointed as obstacles to efficiency.

The solo re-audition works because it joins two forms of legitimacy:

- the musical result becomes publicly audible;
- Kaori receives agency in accepting it.

Fairness requires truth, but dignity requires a process by which human beings can live with that truth.

## 4. Specialness and ensemble dependence

Reina wants to become special. Kumiko plays an instrument whose beauty lies largely in support.

The novel does not force a choice between these positions.

A great ensemble needs:

- exceptional individuals;
- reliable supporting bodies;
- disciplined group coordination;
- and people capable of recognizing excellence without disappearing before it.

The musical form itself supplies the argument. Reina’s solo is special because the ensemble creates the conditions in which it can emerge.

## 5. Belonging without self-erasure

Concert band is both refuge and threat.

It gives characters:

- friends;
- purpose;
- bodily routine;
- artistic pleasure;
- and collective achievement.

It also generates:

- hierarchy;
- surveillance;
- factionalism;
- enforced atmosphere;
- and fear of exclusion.

Kumiko’s growth does not consist of dissolving into the group. She must learn to belong while retaining enough selfhood to judge, choose, and want.

## 6. Music as social truth

The novel repeatedly gives sound evidentiary power.

Characters can argue about:

- fairness;
- seniority;
- effort;
- loyalty;
- and favoritism.

When Reina and Kaori play consecutively, the difference becomes something the room cannot honestly deny.

Sound does not eliminate interpretation, but it constrains bad faith.

At the same time, music is not only a ranking mechanism. The final rehearsal produces a collective bodily pleasure that cannot be reduced to competition. Kumiko experiences the ensemble locking together and thinks simply that playing is fun.

The novel preserves both truths:

> music can expose hierarchy, and music can temporarily abolish it.

# XI. Prose, voice, and Japanese-language characterization

## 1. Close third-person and free indirect style

The narration is formally third-person but functionally saturated with Kumiko’s consciousness. Descriptions slip quickly between external fact and her private judgment.

This produces several effects:

- comic timing without a separate narrator;
- unreliable self-interpretation;
- immediate social analysis;
- bodily adolescent perception;
- and gaps between what Kumiko notices and what she understands.

The reader often recognizes Kumiko’s jealousy, attraction, or investment before she does.

## 2. Standard Japanese inside a Kyoto social world

Kumiko speaks standard Japanese because her family previously lived in Tokyo and continues using it at home. Many classmates speak in Kansai/Kyoto-inflected language.

This makes her simultaneously local and slightly foreign.

She knows Uji’s geography, schools, and customs, but her voice marks a degree of distance. The distinction complements her narrative function: she belongs enough to understand the world and stands apart enough to observe it.

Reina’s emotionally forceful Kansai speech gives her declarations an especially immediate quality. Midori’s cute self-reference and regional cadence, Hazuki’s energetic dialect, and Asuka’s playful register also distinguish their relations to social space.

Because the OCR frequently damages particles and punctuation in dialogue, exact register analysis should later be checked against the images. The larger pattern is nevertheless unmistakable.

## 3. Embodied musical prose

The novel does not describe music only through abstract beauty. It repeatedly returns to:

- lips and buzzing;
- breath entering the instrument;
- aching arms;
- instrument weight;
- fingerings;
- tuning;
- overtones;
- valves and slides;
- saliva;
- shaking hands;
- bloodied fingers;
- stage heat;
- and the physical pressure of sound.

This makes artistic development material rather than mystical.

The students improve because bodies are trained into repeatable action. Even transcendental moments of ensemble unity are built from accumulated muscle memory.

## 4. Adolescent embodiment and gaze

The opening pages immediately establish Kumiko as conscious of bodies, appearance, breasts, legs, clothing, beauty, and social comparison. Similar attention continues across male and female characters.

This is partly genre texture, but it also serves characterization. Kumiko does not perceive people as abstract moral subjects. Her reactions are sensory and sometimes embarrassing. Attraction appears before she has a stable language with which to categorize it.

The result is a protagonist whose interior life feels less curated than her public behavior.

## 5. Uji as emotional geography

The novel’s locations are not interchangeable scenery.

Schools, stations, riverbanks, bridges, slopes, temples, festival routes, and the ascent of Daikichiyama give emotional encounters physical form.

Especially important is upward movement:

- climbing removes Kumiko and Reina from the crowd;
- physical effort precedes confession;
- elevation turns the festival into distant light;
- and artistic aspiration becomes spatially imaginable.

The local environment makes the novel feel rooted in a lived community rather than an abstract school-anime stage.

# XII. Volume 1 as the adaptation baseline

When the anime corpus is later analyzed, the following points should be tracked carefully rather than assuming that the adaptation merely reproduces them.

## 1. Kumiko’s narrative sharpness

The novel’s Kumiko is unusually candid internally:

- judgmental;
- bodily conscious;
- jealous;
- socially evasive;
- and frequently amused by other people.

The anime must externalize this without continuous prose. Voice acting, framing, pauses, and facial movement will therefore be doing interpretive work rather than simply presenting the same material.

## 2. Shuuichi’s structural weight

Shuuichi is woven into Kumiko’s first-volume development:

- he shares her middle-school history;
- contributes to her first declaration that she wants to improve;
- becomes the object of concealed jealousy;
- and steadies her before the performance.

Any reduction or redistribution of this material changes Kumiko’s emotional architecture.

## 3. Reina and Kumiko are already intense in the novel

The adaptation does not begin with a neutral friendship that it must wholly reinvent. The prose already contains:

- sensual visual attention;
- physical touch;
- “love confession” language;
- chosen isolation from the festival;
- fascination with concealed identity;
- and emotionally privileged silence.

The appropriate comparative question is not whether the anime “invented” intimacy, but **which latent elements it foregrounded, amplified, or reorganized**.

## 4. Asuka’s coldness

Asuka’s charisma in the novel is inseparable from her unnerving refusal of emotional obligation. Her admission that the solo conflict is meaningless to her should remain a major comparison point. A warmer or more mysterious presentation would materially alter her early characterization.

## 5. Taki’s institutional manipulation

The novel gives enough evidence to read Taki as someone who deliberately engineers atmosphere and commitment. His pedagogical success should not be flattened into pure inspirational mentorship.

## 6. Midori’s argument against pure competitive logic

Midori’s elite-school experience and hesitation over Reina versus Kaori complicate the novel’s apparent meritocracy. Omitting or reducing this argument would make the story’s ethical structure simpler than the source.

## 7. The competition performance

The novel suspends the result and gives the performance itself an extended embodied climax. The actual result arrives only in the epilogue.

An audiovisual adaptation can provide the music the prose evokes, but it will also have to choose:

- whose reactions to emphasize;
- how much imperfection remains audible;
- whether the performance feels objectively excellent;
- and how to translate Kumiko’s internal transition into visible form.

# XIV. Provisional series questions established by Volume 1

Volume 1 resolves its immediate arc but leaves several structural questions open.

## 1. Can Kumiko sustain desire after the first success?

Crying over advancement proves that she now cares. It does not prove that she knows how to live with prolonged ambition, defeat, responsibility, or leadership.

## 2. Can Reina become special without becoming alone?

Her talent legitimizes her confidence. Her social abrasiveness and exceptionalism may also isolate her from the ensemble that makes her music possible.

## 3. Is Taki’s meritocracy sufficient?

It corrects seniority and raises performance quality. It does not automatically repair humiliation, historical injury, or unequal emotional stakes.

## 4. What does Asuka actually want?

Her love of playing is clear. Her relation to leadership, attachment, responsibility, and the future remains deliberately obscured.

## 5. Has Kitauji repaired itself or merely become successful?

The new atmosphere produces results. The abandoned students of the previous year remain absent. Institutional achievement and moral repair are not the same thing.

## 6. What kind of relationship will Kumiko build with music?

She has discovered that she loves the euphonium. The larger question is whether that love becomes:

- vocation;
- identity;
- community;
- competition;
- or some unstable combination of all four.

# XV. Final assessment

Volume 1 succeeds because it understands that a school band is not merely a collection of lovable characters pursuing a shared dream. It is an institution in which incompatible goods collide:

- excellence and kindness;
- seniority and merit;
- individual distinction and ensemble unity;
- honesty and social peace;
- commitment and self-protection;
- competition and musical joy.

The novel supports Kitauji’s transformation, but it does not sentimentalize it. The same process that gives Kumiko purpose:

- makes Aoi leave;
- humiliates Kaori;
- isolates Reina;
- places Natsuki in the B ensemble;
- and reopens the band’s unresolved history.

Its deepest achievement is Kumiko. She does not become a different personality. She remains evasive, observant, awkward, wry, and conflicted. What changes is that these traits no longer prevent her from making a claim.

At the beginning, Reina asks whether Kumiko feels frustrated.

At the end, Kumiko no longer needs to be asked.

She loves the euphonium.  
She wants to improve.  
She wants Kitauji to win.  
She cries when it does.

The first novel’s complete movement can therefore be condensed to:

> **A girl enters high school hoping to escape the shame of having wanted too little.  
> She meets a girl who wants too much, a teacher who makes choice binding, and a band whose beauty and cruelty cannot be separated.  
> By the time the euphonium sounds onstage, she has accepted the danger of caring.**

That is the genuine beginning of *Sound! Euphonium*.
