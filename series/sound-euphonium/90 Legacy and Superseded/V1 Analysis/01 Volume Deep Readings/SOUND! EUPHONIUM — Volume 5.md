# What kind of volume this is

Volume 4 looked backward and sideways through the first-year timeline, exposing the private lives beneath the main trilogy.

Volume 5 moves forward.

Its two novellas occur after:

- Kitauji’s national bronze;
- the retirement of Haruka, Asuka, Kaori, and the other third-years;
- Yuuko and Natsuki’s succession as president and vice-president;
- Kumiko and Shuuichi’s mutual confession;
- and the transformation of Kitauji from obscure school band into nationally recognized ensemble.

This makes Volume 5 the true bridge between the first-year trilogy and Kumiko’s later life as an upperclassman.

It asks what happens **after the climactic competition story**.

Kitauji has achieved the improbable.

Now it must answer more difficult institutional questions:

- What does it play when judges are no longer choosing the repertoire’s meaning?
- What does it want an audience to feel?
- Can the new leadership create rather than merely inherit?
- What becomes of retired seniors?
- How do students distinguish admiration from self-subordination?
- Can Kumiko stop defining herself as the ordinary player beside extraordinary people?
- What kind of senior is she becoming?

Both novellas end immediately before or at the beginning of their public performances.

That is a deliberate structural choice.

The dramatic climax is not:

> Did the audience applaud?

It is:

> **Did the students learn what they wanted to play, why they wanted to play it, and with whom they wanted to stand?**

# Executive thesis

Volumes 1–3 were organized around competitive selection.

A judge, instructor, audition, or result decided:

- who played;
- who received the solo;
- which school advanced;
- and where Kitauji stood among other ensembles.

Volume 5 changes the governing verb.

It moves from **being selected** to **selecting**.

The students must now choose:

- a program;
- a concert theme;
- musical priorities;
- visual presentation;
- soloists;
- collaborators;
- and the kind of band they want Kitauji to become.

Its central question is therefore:

> **What happens when a group that learned seriousness through competition must develop artistic authorship beyond competition?**

The answer emerges across the two novellas.

## In *Winter-Colored Rhapsody*

Kitauji must turn dozens of individual preferences into one coherent concert.

The band learns that artistic democracy does not mean mechanically including every suggestion. Someone must:

- listen;
- discriminate;
- arrange;
- refuse;
- and create a form through which private wishes can become a public experience.

Mizore, once unable to name why she played or what she wanted from music, becomes the person who makes those decisions.

Her theme is simple:

> **楽しいこと — something enjoyable.**

She wants children and adults alike to leave thinking that concert band is fun.

That apparent simplicity represents extraordinary development.

## In *Star-Colored Serenade*

Kumiko confronts a more personal form of authorship.

She admires Reina so intensely that she has made Reina categorically unreachable. When Azusa stands beside Reina as an equal musical partner, Kumiko experiences jealousy, exclusion, and shame.

The problem is not that Reina has betrayed her.

The problem is that Kumiko never permitted herself to want the same position.

She had converted admiration into an excuse:

> Reina is special.  
> I am ordinary.  
> Therefore I cannot demand to stand beside her.

Azusa breaks that logic.

Kumiko finally says:

> 「吹きたいです」  
> I want to play.

And, more importantly:

> **I want to become special too.  
> I want to stand proudly beside Reina.**

Volume 5’s deepest development can therefore be summarized as:

> **Volume 1 taught Kumiko to want victory.  
> Volume 3 taught her to say that she personally wanted Asuka.  
> Volume 5 teaches her to want artistic equality.**

She no longer wants only to remain near exceptional people.

She wants to become someone capable of answering them in sound.

# 1. The post-competition problem

Competition gave Kitauji an unusually clear structure.

The objective was:

> nationals.

The methods were:

- rehearsal;
- auditions;
- technical improvement;
- external evaluation;
- and increasingly unified effort.

After nationals, that clarity disappears.

The new leaders inherit a band that knows how to chase a result but has not yet fully learned how to author an experience.

## National recognition creates a new audience

Kitauji’s regular concert now attracts people because the school reached nationals.

This creates expectation:

> A national-level band should sound impressive.

The audience is not a neutral crowd. It arrives with an image of Kitauji already formed.

The students must decide whether to:

- demonstrate difficulty;
- reproduce competition prestige;
- display versatility;
- entertain families;
- honor their own history;
- or satisfy the musical desires of individual members.

The problem resembles Asuka’s old critique of the band.

A group can perform every requested surface gesture and still lack an inner reason.

## Success becomes an obligation

Asuka warned that once Kitauji crossed a high threshold, outsiders would begin treating that threshold as normal.

Volume 5 shows the beginning of that burden.

Kitauji can no longer present itself as:

- inexperienced;
- rebuilding;
- or unexpectedly ambitious.

Its previous miracle has become its current reputation.

The new leadership must preserve prestige without merely imitating the ensemble that Haruka and Asuka helped create.

# 2. 「冬色ラプソディー」  
## From collecting preferences to making an artistic decision

The first novella follows Kitauji’s preparation for its regular concert from autumn into winter.

Yuuko is now president.  
Natsuki is vice-president.  
Mizore is placed in charge of concert planning.  
Kumiko is assigned to assist her.

The pairing is significant.

Mizore has exceptional musical ability but historically struggled to articulate desire.

Kumiko is skilled at:

- observing;
- mediating;
- asking questions;
- and moving among different social groups.

Together, they must transform dozens of opinions into one program.

---

# 3. Kumiko’s first step into seniority

Kumiko has already been given responsibility for guiding new students in the coming spring.

Because no new students have yet arrived, she initially experiences the role as abstract. Assisting Mizore becomes her first practical rehearsal for seniority.

She must now do what Asuka, Natsuki, Yuuko, and others once did for her:

- listen to uncertain people;
- explain musical choices;
- organize work;
- recognize hidden wishes;
- and help another person arrive at a decision without making it for her.

## From recipient to organizer

In the earlier novels, Kumiko entered structures created by older students.

Now she helps create the structure others will perform inside.

This is an important change in narrative position.

She is still not president.  
She is still not the loudest voice.  
She is still inclined toward observation.

But observation is becoming responsibility.

The question is no longer only:

> What is everyone really feeling?

It is:

> What should be done with what I have noticed?

---

# 4. Mizore as curator

Mizore’s selection as planning leader initially seems counterintuitive.

She is:

- quiet;
- socially compressed;
- uncomfortable with broad communication;
- and historically dependent upon other people to give music emotional direction.

That is precisely why the assignment matters.

## The old Mizore

In Volume 2, Mizore could execute music at an extraordinary technical level but could not answer:

- whether she liked the oboe;
- what she wanted her solo to express;
- or why she continued playing.

Nozomi’s praise finally gave her performance a listener and emotional purpose.

## The new task

Planning a concert requires a different kind of authorship.

Mizore must decide:

- what belongs;
- what does not;
- what order creates meaning;
- how contrasting pieces relate;
- and what the audience should experience.

She can no longer merely respond correctly to someone else’s request.

She must produce the request.

## The survey problem

The band collects suggestions from members.

The resulting list contains:

- popular music;
- film music;
- competition repertoire;
- instrument features;
- personal favorites;
- serious concert works;
- comic selections;
- and pieces tied to memories.

A naïve democratic approach would simply include the most popular items.

Asuka objects.

She argues that stacking everyone’s preferences together produces only a shallow sampling of surfaces. The concert requires a point of view.

Her central question is:

> What do **you** want?  
> What are **you** unwilling to surrender?

This continues the first trilogy’s argument about desire.

Listening to everyone is not the same as possessing no preference.

A leader who refuses to choose does not avoid power. She merely conceals the fact that incoherence has become the choice.

---

# 5. Haruka and the danger of disappearing into consideration

Haruka reinforces Asuka’s argument more gently.

She admits that she once focused so intensely upon other people’s wishes that she lost track of her own.

Her former leadership anxiety made her constantly ask:

- What would Asuka do?
- What does the band expect?
- How can I avoid disappointing everyone?
- Who is more suitable than I am?

By retirement, she can say:

> She is different now.

This matters for Mizore and Kumiko alike.

Both girls are highly responsive to others.

Their danger is not selfish domination.

It is self-erasure disguised as sensitivity.

Haruka’s mature lesson is:

> **A leader must hear other people without becoming acoustically absent from her own decision.**

---

# 6. The program as portrait of Mizore’s development

Mizore ultimately constructs a varied concert program including:

- **Star Puzzle March**
- **Hooray for Hollywood**
- a **Harry Potter** symphonic suite
- a **Mission: Impossible** medley
- **Crescent Moon Dance**
- **East Coast Pictures**
- **Canterbury Chorale**
- first- and second-year feature pieces
- **Japanese Folktale Rhapsody**
- an instrumental-introduction number
- **South Rampart Street Parade**
- **Disco Kid**
- and lively encore material.

The exact pieces matter less than the logic connecting them.

## Variety without randomness

The program contains:

- cinematic familiarity;
- serious concert repertoire;
- Kitauji’s recent history;
- student features;
- humor;
- accessible melodies;
- and rhythmic spectacle.

Mizore does not choose one narrow definition of musical seriousness.

Her standard is whether the whole event can communicate pleasure.

## “Something enjoyable”

Mizore states the concert’s theme:

> 「楽しいこと」

She wants both children and adults to find it enjoyable.

This is a profound transformation from the girl who once asked what competition meant and concluded that she hated it.

Her music is no longer oriented solely around:

- correct execution;
- Nozomi’s approval;
- or an institutional result.

She is imagining an unknown audience.

That is an expansion of relational capacity.

## Nozomi remains present—but not alone

Mizore includes music Nozomi values. Nozomi’s preferences still matter intensely.

But the program also incorporates:

- Kumiko’s suggestion;
- Hazuki’s desire;
- senior memories;
- younger performers;
- and broad audience accessibility.

Mizore’s growth is not the elimination of Nozomi.

It is the pluralization of the world around her.

The series repeatedly rejects a crude model of independence in which maturity requires ceasing to need people.

Maturity means that one relationship no longer has to carry the entire weight of existence.

---

# 7. Hazuki and the erased wish

Hazuki initially suggests a Harry Potter selection.

She also writes **“Twinkle, Twinkle, Little Star”**—the first melody she played with the low-brass section—then erases it because it seems too simple.

This is one of the novella’s most important small actions.

## Self-censorship before judgment

No one rejects Hazuki’s wish.

She rejects it in advance.

She assumes that a simple beginner piece cannot belong in the concert of a nationally recognized band.

This shows how prestige changes internal culture.

The band does not need to tell its less experienced members that their memories are childish. They may begin telling themselves.

## The return of the melody

A more sophisticated arrangement built around the familiar tune enters the final program.

Hazuki’s private wish is therefore heard without being included in its original literal form.

This is a model of good artistic leadership:

> The leader does not merely reproduce the first request.  
> She identifies the feeling beneath it and finds a form capable of carrying that feeling publicly.

What Hazuki wants is not necessarily the exact elementary arrangement.

She wants recognition of:

- where she began;
- the joy of producing sound with others;
- and the fact that beginnerhood belongs to Kitauji’s history too.

## From invisible beginner to rhythmic foundation

Hazuki is assigned a major responsibility in the younger-member feature.

Without a conductor or full percussion structure, her tuba must stabilize the ensemble’s pulse.

The instrument that seems least glamorous becomes the condition under which the featured trumpets can sound secure.

This develops Volume 4’s opening principle:

> What does not stand out may be carrying everything that does.

---

# 8. Hazuki’s performance anxiety

Hazuki does not fear only that she will embarrass herself.

She fears that her weakness will damage Reina’s sound.

This reveals both humility and danger.

## Admiration becomes self-subordination

Reina’s playing appears special to Hazuki.

Hazuki worries that someone like her has no right to support it.

The same logic appeared in Kumiko’s relationship with Reina:

> Reina is exceptional.  
> I am ordinary.  
> My job is not to interfere.

Volume 5 repeatedly challenges this hierarchy.

An ensemble does not ask whether the tuba player is spiritually worthy of the trumpet player.

It asks whether both performers will assume responsibility for the music.

## Reina’s criticism

Reina critiques Hazuki sharply.

She explains that:

- the foundation cannot drift;
- the ensemble will rely upon Hazuki’s pulse;
- and good intentions cannot substitute for rhythmic stability.

The criticism is severe but musically legitimate.

Reina treats Hazuki as someone capable of carrying real responsibility.

That is more respectful than protecting her with artificially low expectations.

## Taki’s choice

Taki tells Hazuki that Riko can replace her if the part is beyond her current ability.

Hazuki refuses to surrender it.

Taki then makes the stakes explicit:

> The piece depends upon her.

This is the difference between desire and fantasy.

Hazuki says she wants the role.

Taki tells her what wanting the role obliges her to do.

## Recognition

After Hazuki improves, Reina tells her directly.

The praise affects Hazuki profoundly because it comes from the person whose sound she feared she might damage.

Reina’s ability to give that recognition is also character development.

She has always been able to identify quality. She is learning that naming another person’s growth can matter as much as naming inadequacy.

---

# 9. The regular concert as music without a judge

The concert differs from the competition in several fundamental ways.

## The students select the music

They are not given required pieces or constrained by a competitive program’s strategic demands.

Their choices reveal what they believe concert band can be.

## The audience chooses Kitauji

People attend specifically to hear the school.

The musicians are no longer trying to convince a panel that they deserve continuation.

They are responding to trust already offered.

## Entertainment becomes an artistic obligation

“Fun” is not treated as the opposite of musical seriousness.

The program’s accessible selections, costumes, visual effects, narration, and instrument introductions all demand craft.

Making a novice audience feel welcome is a difficult form of musical communication.

## The students build the entire event

They manage:

- program selection;
- publicity;
- posters;
- visual presentation;
- costumes;
- staging;
- scripts;
- and audience expectations.

The concert reveals that a performance is not only the sound produced after the conductor raises the baton.

It is an institution of preparation.

## The novella stops at the curtain

The story ends as the regular concert begins.

This refuses the conventional validation scene.

We do not need prolonged applause to know whether Mizore grew.

Her decisive act occurred when she selected the program and named its purpose.

---

# 10. Yuuko and Natsuki’s new regime

The regular concert is the first major event created under the new president and vice-president.

Their leadership demonstrates that Kitauji has partially learned from its dependence upon Asuka.

## Yuuko as president

Yuuko possesses:

- emotional force;
- initiative;
- loyalty;
- willingness to speak;
- and a strong sense of what the band owes its members and audience.

Her opening speech asserts:

- this is the first event under the new structure;
- the audience should see Kitauji’s seriousness;
- and the ultimate objective remains national gold.

She does not imitate Haruka’s hesitant leadership or Asuka’s theatrical detachment.

She leads through conviction.

## Natsuki as vice-president

Natsuki:

- notices pressure;
- tempers escalation;
- encourages juniors;
- and prevents Yuuko’s intensity from becoming the only available emotional temperature.

She has become far more engaged than the girl who originally joined because concert band seemed easy.

## Productive conflict

They continue to quarrel.

Mizore cleverly places music associated with both of them in the encore, preventing the program from becoming another battleground.

Their conflict is not evidence that the succession failed.

It is part of the structure.

The institution no longer depends upon one supposedly complete person. It depends upon incomplete people who correct each other.

---

# 11. Kumiko and Shuuichi after confession

Volume 5 depicts Kumiko and Shuuichi as an actual couple rather than an unresolved possibility.

Their relationship remains awkward and deliberately understated.

## Secrecy

They limit visible interaction at school partly because they fear:

- gossip;
- section complications;
- Hazuki’s feelings;
- and disturbance within the band.

This is compassionate, but it also reflects their familiar tendency to protect relationships through concealment.

## Hazuki already knows

Hazuki recognizes what has happened and directly signals her support.

She does not need Kumiko to preserve a false reality for her sake.

This quietly repeats Midori’s earlier lesson:

> Do not use another person as the excuse for avoiding ownership of your choice.

Hazuki’s pain was real.

So is her capacity to continue.

## Ordinary meetings

Kumiko and Shuuichi meet during winter evenings, travel together to distribute publicity materials, and revisit their former middle school.

Their romance is built from:

- shared errands;
- familiar routes;
- small jokes;
- and practical encouragement.

It remains distinct from the heightened, aestheticized intimacy Kumiko shares with Reina.

## The former teacher

Their middle-school teacher explains that local students now aspire to Kitauji because it has become strong.

Kumiko has become part of the image that once influenced her from outside.

She says she is glad she continued.

That statement carries the full first-year arc:

- indifference;
- frustration;
- competition;
- defeat;
- and inherited responsibility.

Music is no longer merely an activity she happens to have retained.

It is part of the life she is grateful not to have abandoned.

---

# 12. Retired seniors and life beyond the competitive ensemble

The former third-years return during concert preparation, but their roles have changed.

This is a major theme of both novellas.

## Haruka

Haruka intends to retain the saxophone as a hobby.

Her relationship with music does not have to choose between:

- professional career;
- and total abandonment.

This is an important corrective to the adolescent assumption that an activity must become one’s vocation to remain meaningful.

## Kaori

Kaori remains socially connected to the ensemble and to Asuka.

The guidebook profile material even lists visiting cafés with Asuka as one of her hobbies, extending their relationship into ordinary post-band life.

## Asuka

Asuka is occupied by examinations and cannot simply return whenever Kitauji wants her.

This is healthy.

The band must learn that inheritance does not mean permanent access to its former leaders.

## Presence after retirement

The seniors’ visits demonstrate that graduation changes relationship rather than erasing it.

They can:

- advise;
- tease;
- encourage;
- and preserve memory

without retaking control of the institution.

# 13. 「星彩セレナーデ」  
## Collaboration, illumination, and the desire to stand beside someone

The second novella follows a joint concert between Kitauji and Rikka High School at the opening of an illuminated public attraction.

The planned repertoire includes:

- **Merry-Go-Round**
- **Noah’s Ark**
- **Sing, Sing, Sing**

The event differs sharply from both the competition and regular concert.

It is:

- cross-school;
- theatrical;
- physically staged;
- connected to marching and color-guard culture;
- and designed for a public spectacle of light and movement.

The central musical problem is no longer whether Kitauji can defeat another school.

It is whether students from two different traditions can create one performance together.

---

# 14. Rikka as another valid band culture

Rikka is famous for marching.

Its students possess:

- strong bodily discipline;
- athletic rehearsal culture;
- aggressive greetings;
- visual precision;
- pride in movement;
- and a highly audience-oriented performance philosophy.

Kitauji’s first-year transformation was organized around seated concert-band excellence and competition repertoire.

Rikka demonstrates that musical seriousness can take another form.

## Music as showmanship

For Rikka, visual presentation is not superficial decoration placed around “real” music.

Movement, spacing, costume, gesture, and audience address are part of the musical work.

This expands Kitauji’s conception of performance.

## Different measures of prestige

Rikka’s students may be less uniformly confident about conventional seated concert-band ranking than they are about marching identity.

That distinction prevents the novel from flattening schools into one universal hierarchy.

A band may be elite according to one artistic system and less secure according to another.

## Collaboration rather than conquest

Kitauji and Rikka remain sources of mutual comparison, but the joint concert converts rivalry into exchange.

Each school must borrow capacities from the other:

- Kitauji’s concert precision;
- Rikka’s movement and spectacle;
- and the distinct personalities of individual performers.

---

# 15. Azusa Sasaki: the alternative Kumiko

Azusa is one of the most important figures in the second novella.

She is Kumiko’s old friend and a major character in the Rikka branch of the series.

Her function here is not merely to create jealousy.

She embodies a way of inhabiting ambition that Kumiko has not learned.

## Azusa says what Kumiko thinks

Takeda’s interview explicitly distinguishes their narrative voices:

- Kumiko often produces sharp internal observations.
- Azusa is more likely to say such things aloud.

That difference extends to desire.

Azusa does not wait for certainty before imagining herself in a featured role.

She announces that she wants the solo.

She assumes that aspiration itself is legitimate.

## Visible development

Kumiko remembers Azusa’s earlier playing.

A year at Rikka has transformed her:

- stronger sound;
- greater physical confidence;
- improved control;
- and an ability to enter intense musical exchange without hesitation.

Her growth is evidence of what one year inside a demanding institution can produce.

## Not a villain

Azusa does not attempt to take Reina away from Kumiko.

She is:

- friendly;
- ambitious;
- musically serious;
- and naturally willing to stand in the space Kumiko has refused to claim.

Her existence becomes painful because it reveals Kumiko’s self-limitation.

---

# 16. The Noah’s Ark soli

The opening of **Noah’s Ark** contains featured lines appropriate for contrasting instrumental voices.

Kumiko wants to play with Reina.

She initially does not fully admit this.

## The old hierarchy

Kumiko has long understood Reina as:

- exceptional;
- singular;
- beautiful;
- and headed toward a musical future beyond ordinary high-school players.

That admiration has motivated Kumiko.

It has also protected her.

If Reina is categorically special, Kumiko never has to risk comparison.

She can remain the devoted nearby witness.

## Reina’s direct desire

Reina tells Kumiko that she wants to play the soli with her.

This matters enormously.

Kumiko is not merely trying to attach herself to Reina’s brilliance.

Reina actively wants Kumiko’s sound in dialogue with her own.

The relationship is musically reciprocal before Kumiko is psychologically capable of believing in equality.

---

# 17. The riverside practice

Kumiko and Reina practice by the Uji River after graduation.

Their two sounds form an intimate world.

Kumiko experiences the moment almost absolutely:

> Even if everything else disappeared, Reina’s presence would seem sufficient.

This is not a casual friendship scene.

Music allows the emotional structure between them to become spatial:

- two bodies;
- two instruments;
- one shared phrase;
- and an imagined world temporarily stripped of everyone else.

Then Azusa arrives.

## Azusa and Reina

Azusa joins Reina in the storm section.

Their sounds meet with:

- confidence;
- force;
- immediate responsiveness;
- and something like competitive pleasure.

Kumiko experiences exclusion.

The pain is not merely that Azusa is talented.

It is that Azusa can do what Kumiko has imagined herself too ordinary to attempt:

> stand beside Reina without apology.

## Jealousy as information

Kumiko’s jealousy is unattractive but valuable.

It tells her what she wants.

She does not only want:

- Reina’s friendship;
- private affection;
- or proximity.

She wants musical legitimacy in Reina’s world.

The jealousy converts vague admiration into a specific goal.

---

# 18. From idolization to aspiration

Kumiko’s relationship with Reina has always contained the language of specialness.

Reina wants to become special.

Kumiko has often treated Reina as already belonging to another category of person.

Volume 5 identifies the moral and developmental danger in that attitude.

## Idolization can become refusal

Calling someone special can sound like praise.

It can also mean:

> I will never demand equality.  
> I will remain safely beneath you.  
> Your greatness excuses my own smaller ambition.

This is structurally similar to the band’s old treatment of Asuka.

Making someone exceptional can isolate her while absolving everyone else of responsibility.

## Kumiko’s new desire

Kumiko finally thinks:

> 「久美子だって、特別になりたい」  
> Kumiko wants to become special too.

And:

> She wants to stand beside Reina with her head held high.

This does not mean becoming identical to Reina.

Reina’s trumpet voice is:

- brilliant;
- declarative;
- forward;
- and cutting.

Kumiko’s euphonium voice is:

- warm;
- rounded;
- connective;
- and internal to the ensemble.

Equality does not require sameness.

It requires that Kumiko stop treating her own kind of sound as inherently lesser.

---

# 19. Shuuichi’s role in Kumiko’s musical awakening

Kumiko turns to Shuuichi after the riverside encounter.

His response is understated but crucial.

He says that he is also practicing for a solo even though he may not receive it.

The possibility of failure does not make desire foolish.

## Belief without abstraction

Shuuichi tells Kumiko that she can do it.

He does not deliver a grand speech about destiny or specialness.

He believes in:

- the work he has seen;
- the person he knows;
- and her capacity to improve.

His physical reassurance—touching or patting her head—places the support inside their ordinary embodied familiarity.

## Different relational functions

Reina awakens Kumiko’s artistic desire.

Shuuichi gives her a secure place from which to risk acting upon it.

These functions are not interchangeable.

Nor does one invalidate the other.

The source’s relational architecture is strongest when it refuses to turn every intimacy into a zero-sum contest.

---

# 20. Saying “I want to play”

Kumiko tells Natsuki directly:

> 「はい。吹きたいです」

Yes. She wants to play.

The simplicity of the line should be understood against four volumes of hesitation.

Kumiko once:

- drifted into euphonium;
- avoided naming ambition;
- declined emotional risk;
- and treated strong desire as something belonging to Reina or Asuka.

Now she asks for a featured musical position.

## No moral disguise

She does not claim:

- the band needs her;
- the part objectively belongs to her;
- or someone else would be unsuitable.

She says she wants it.

That is the mature form of the speech she gave Asuka in Volume 3.

Personal desire no longer needs to hide behind collective necessity.

## Natsuki’s response

Natsuki smiles and supports her.

This is another moment in Natsuki’s development as a senior.

She does not treat Kumiko’s ambition as arrogance.

She recognizes that saying it aloud is growth.

# 21. Asuka as mentor after graduation

Asuka returns in an unexpected role.

She does not reclaim her old euphonium seat.

She participates through:

- baton and guard work;
- visual performance;
- and private instruction for Kumiko.

This is one of Volume 5’s strongest images of inheritance.

## Continuation without repetition

Asuka does not need to remain “the euphonium senior” forever.

She can:

- learn a new skill;
- enter a different performance language;
- and assist the next player without occupying her place.

Tradition survives because the predecessor changes roles.

## Diagnosing Kumiko

Asuka identifies Kumiko’s primary obstacle as psychological and bodily tension.

Fear of failure causes:

- tightened lips;
- hardened sound;
- unstable fingers;
- and forced phrasing.

Kumiko’s emotional habit appears directly in her playing.

She braces against possible error.

Asuka advises her to pursue the euphonium’s natural beauty rather than attempt to overpower anxiety through force.

## A distinctly euphonium solution

Kumiko does not need to imitate Reina’s aggressive trumpet declaration.

Her instrument’s strength lies in:

- warmth;
- breadth;
- stability;
- and resonance.

Her route toward equality with Reina is not to become trumpet-like.

It is to make the euphonium fully itself.

This continues the series’ instrument–person analogy:

> Kumiko becomes special by inhabiting her own form, not by borrowing Reina’s.

---

# 22. Asuka and Kaori after graduation

Kaori arrives with food while Asuka is instructing Kumiko.

Asuka jokingly refers to her as her cute girlfriend or uses similarly provocative language. Kaori blushes.

The exchange is playful, but the guidebook reinforces that their relationship continues through ordinary activities such as visiting cafés together.

## What has changed

During the first trilogy, Kaori often approached Asuka through yearning and uncertainty.

After graduation, their relationship appears more relaxed.

Asuka still controls tone through teasing, but she no longer seems to require total distance.

## Yuri-coded continuity

The source continues to frame them through:

- sensual joking;
- embarrassment;
- domestic care;
- chosen leisure time;
- and mutual post-school contact.

It does not formally label them as a couple.

It deliberately supplies the texture from which such a reading emerges.

---

# 23. Kumiko wins the soli

Taki selects Kumiko and Reina for the featured opening.

Kumiko’s reaction is explosive joy.

The result matters because she has not merely been granted proximity through friendship.

Her playing has been judged capable of carrying the part.

## The reward is relational and artistic

Kumiko is proud that the opening contains their two sounds.

Reina later says that she is happy they can play together.

The scene confirms what Kumiko feared to believe:

> Reina does not merely tolerate her nearby.  
> Reina actively values her as a musical partner.

## Merit without emotional emptiness

The choice is musically earned.

It is also emotionally meaningful.

The novel does not insist that fair judgment must be stripped of relationship. The important condition is that relationship does not substitute for capability.

Kumiko’s effort has made the desired relationship artistically possible.

# 24. The illumination scene

Before the joint performance, Kumiko and Reina retreat to a private viewing place.

At the designated time, the illumination activates:

- lights;
- fountain effects;
- color;
- and a transformed public landscape.

Reina says she wanted Kumiko to see it.

She acknowledges that Kumiko may later return with Shuuichi.

Then she says:

> 「それでも久美子と見たかってん」  
> Even so, I wanted to see it with Kumiko.

And:

> 「久美子の初めては、アタシのやから」  
> Kumiko’s first belongs to me.

Kumiko replies that the line sounds erotic.

The exchange recalls the Volume 1 festival-night “love confession” joke, but the emotional stakes are now far more established.

## Possessive intimacy without exclusivity

Reina knows Kumiko is dating Shuuichi.

She does not demand that the relationship end.

She claims a different kind of priority:

> This first shared experience is ours.

The novel permits overlapping emotional territories rather than forcing one relationship to erase another.

## Wordlessness

They hold hands.

Kumiko reflects that words are unnecessary and that being together is enough.

For that instant, she experiences herself as Reina’s and Reina as hers.

This is among the strongest yuri-coded passages in the novels so far.

The text does not formally redefine their relationship as conventional romance.

It does depict:

- mutual possession;
- bodily closeness;
- private beauty;
- exclusivity of a first experience;
- and satisfaction in simply being together.

Any serious relational analysis must preserve that intensity rather than reducing it to generic friendship.

## Azusa’s interruption

Azusa later discovers them still holding hands.

Her presence connects the musical and emotional parts of the novella.

She is the person whose playing forced Kumiko to claim equality with Reina. She now witnesses the intimate bond that already existed beneath that ambition.

# 25. Romance, yuri coding, and relational plurality

Volume 5 makes the source’s refusal of clean exclusivity especially explicit.

## Kumiko and Shuuichi

They are canonically dating.

Their relationship includes:

- mutual confession;
- private meetings;
- small acts of physical reassurance;
- ordinary companionship;
- support for musical goals;
- and anxiety about public disclosure.

## Kumiko and Reina

Their relationship includes:

- artistic aspiration;
- profound aesthetic attention;
- handholding;
- mutual possession;
- emotionally exclusive first experiences;
- intense private language;
- and the desire to perform as equals.

## Reina’s position

Reina helps Kumiko’s relationship with Shuuichi progress while also making possessive claims upon Kumiko.

This is not accidental incoherence.

It suggests that Reina does not understand all forms of relational priority through one conventional romantic hierarchy.

## Kumiko’s position

Kumiko does not appear to experience her attachment to Reina as a threat to her relationship with Shuuichi.

Her emotional world has different rooms:

- Shuuichi is home, continuity, and grounded belief.
- Reina is aspiration, beauty, intensity, and artistic transcendence.

The boundaries remain ambiguous because adolescence has not yet forced every experience into a permanent adult label.

## Analytical conclusion

A responsible reading should avoid both reductions:

> Kumiko dates Shuuichi, therefore the Reina intimacy is meaningless.

And:

> The Reina intimacy is intense, therefore the Shuuichi relationship is false.

The novels support neither.

They depict a protagonist capable of multiple sincere attachments whose emotional grammars differ.

# 26. Music as hospitality

The first-year trilogy emphasized music as:

- ambition;
- ranking;
- self-revelation;
- and institutional struggle.

Volume 5 adds **hospitality**.

## Regular concert

Kitauji asks:

> What can we give an audience that came because it trusts us?

Mizore’s answer is enjoyment.

## Joint concert

Kitauji and Rikka ask:

> How can two traditions create a spectacle neither could produce alone?

The answer includes:

- shared movement;
- visual design;
- public space;
- illumination;
- and featured dialogue between instruments.

## Hospitality is not servility

Playing for an audience does not mean mechanically satisfying every preference.

Good hospitality requires authorship.

The musicians must decide what experience they are offering.

This mirrors interpersonal care:

> Listening to another person does not require erasing oneself.  
> Giving something meaningful requires possessing a form from which to give.

# 27. Rhapsody and serenade

The two titles encode the volume’s structure.

## Rhapsody

A rhapsody is associated with freedom, episodic variety, and the assembly of contrasting material.

The regular concert becomes a social rhapsody:

- many wishes;
- many styles;
- old memories;
- younger players;
- comedy;
- seriousness;
- and one curator creating coherence.

The “winter color” suggests the season after competitive heat, when the band must discover another palette of musical life.

## Serenade

A serenade is conventionally music addressed toward someone.

The joint-concert novella repeatedly asks who is playing toward whom:

- Kumiko toward Reina;
- Reina toward Kumiko;
- Azusa toward the challenge before her;
- Kitauji toward Rikka;
- both schools toward the public;
- and the illuminated setting toward everyone gathered there.

The stars and colored lights externalize relational address.

The performance is not only sound produced in space.

It is sound offered.

# 28. Midori and the inheritance of knowledge

Asuka assigns Midori the role of preserving low-brass knowledge.

The “knowledge bank” may partly be one of Asuka’s playful inventions, but the transfer is genuine.

Midori begins explaining:

- music history;
- composers;
- instrument facts;
- and repertoire context.

## Why Midori fits the role

Midori possesses:

- elite musical education;
- enthusiasm;
- verbal expansiveness;
- and a desire to share what she loves.

She will not reproduce Asuka’s exact teaching style.

Asuka teaches through:

- theatrical authority;
- teasing;
- and strategic control.

Midori teaches through:

- delight;
- association;
- and contagious enthusiasm.

This is another example of inheritance without imitation.

The role survives because a new personality transforms it.

# 29. The author interview as interpretive evidence

The long Takeda Ayano interview is unusually valuable because it confirms several structural intentions visible in the novels.

It should not be used to eliminate all interpretive ambiguity. A work can mean more than an author’s concise retrospective explanation.

It does clarify the design process.

## The interior viewpoint of band life

Takeda wanted to depict concert band from inside the student ensemble rather than through:

- a teacher;
- a distant observer;
- or a simplified inspirational framework.

Her own band memories remained close enough to support details of:

- motivation gaps;
- instrument assignment;
- sectional cultures;
- social conflict;
- and the peculiar combination of artistic and athletic labor.

## The title and euphonium

She chose **響く / hibiku**, resonance or sounding, because it fit the character of the euphonium’s tone.

The instrument also signals insider specificity.

A trumpet or flute title would carry more immediate glamour. The euphonium points toward:

- supporting sound;
- obscurity;
- warmth;
- and the experience of people inside the ensemble rather than only its visible stars.

## Kumiko versus Reina as protagonist

Takeda initially considered Reina as the protagonist of the solo conflict.

She rejected that structure because it risked becoming a straightforward story in which a gifted first-year defeats unmotivated seniors.

Kumiko’s cooler, partially detached perspective creates moral and social complexity.

She can see:

- Reina’s merit;
- Kaori’s pain;
- Yuuko’s protest;
- and the institutional stakes

without fully belonging to one side at the outset.

## Characters as complementary parameters

Takeda describes building characters through contrasts and complementary pairs.

Examples include:

- Kumiko and Reina as cooler and hotter dispositions;
- Midori and Hazuki as experienced and beginner musicians;
- other pairs balancing qualities that one person alone could not contain.

The interview’s phrase that two characters may together form “one” helps explain the series’ relational architecture.

Characters are rarely developed as isolated psychological units.

They are revealed through friction with a counter-voice.

## Kumiko’s difficulty

Takeda notes that Kumiko can be difficult to write because she is:

- passive;
- hard to read;
- and less likely than more active characters to generate movement openly.

This is not an accidental weakness.

It is the engine of her development.

The series repeatedly constructs situations in which Kumiko must move from:

- perception;
- to judgment;
- to speech;
- to action.

## Taki’s limitations

Takeda describes Taki as technically persuasive but poor at handling students and still immature as an advisor.

This supports the novels’ refusal to treat him as an infallible inspirational teacher.

His bluntness may be effective musically while producing:

- uncertainty;
- emotional distance;
- or avoidable conflict.

The realism lies in separating professional expertise from total interpersonal competence.

## Volume 2 and incomplete understanding

Takeda explains that Volume 2 concerns relationships in which people remain friends or lovers without fully understanding each other.

Nozomi believes she understands Mizore.

She does not.

This confirms that the reconciliation was never intended to produce perfect symmetry.

The relationship continues despite interpretive gaps.

## Volume 3 and Kumiko’s shell

Takeda identifies Kumiko’s decisive intervention in the Asuka crisis as the act that breaks her shell.

This supports the reading that the climax is not simply “Kumiko persuades Asuka.”

It is:

> Kumiko finally assumes the interpersonal risk she habitually avoided.

## Characters acting beyond the outline

Takeda leaves endpoints loose enough for characters to act in ways she did not initially plan.

Takuya and Riko, for example, began dating without a heavily predetermined authorial scheme.

This creative method helps explain the cast’s sense of social autonomy. Relationships can emerge from established personalities rather than only from rigid plot assignment.

## The series in one sentence

Takeda describes the series as:

> 「目標に向かって頑張る人たちの物語」

A story about people working toward a goal.

The plural is important.

It is not the story of one genius or one hero.

It is about people whose goals:

- overlap;
- conflict;
- change;
- and become possible only through shared labor.

# 30. The Rikka material and the alternative narrator

The interview also discusses the Rikka spin-off and Azusa’s design.

Takeda felt much of Kitauji’s initial story had been fully expressed after Volume 3 and the short-story material. Rikka allowed her to explore marching culture and another ensemble logic.

## Kumiko and Azusa as narrative opposites

Takeda describes a useful distinction:

- Kumiko often thinks the sharp or troublesome remark internally.
- Azusa is more likely to say it aloud.

Their observational emphases also differ:

- Kumiko’s descriptions often attend to legs and embodied presence.
- Azusa pays greater attention to hands, eyes, and hair.

This confirms that narrative gaze is part of characterization.

The world does not look the same through every protagonist.

## Why Azusa affects Kumiko

Azusa is not merely more extroverted.

She externalizes ambitions Kumiko may possess but suppress.

Her presence in *Star-Colored Serenade* is therefore almost metanarrative.

An alternative protagonist enters Kumiko’s novel and demonstrates another way to occupy the same world.

# 32. The concert-band guide as cultural framing

The latter sections explain basic concert-band and marching concepts for readers unfamiliar with the activity.

These pages have limited literary authority, but they reveal how the volume imagines its audience.

The franchise has become large enough that readers may arrive through:

- anime;
- character interest;
- romance;
- or school drama

without understanding band culture.

The guide therefore translates the institution behind the fiction.

Its broad framing emphasizes concert band as something like an **athletic cultural club**:

- physically demanding;
- repetitive;
- hierarchical;
- collective;
- schedule-intensive;
- and built upon disciplined bodily technique.

That description is important to the series’ genre identity.

*Sound! Euphonium* is not merely a performing-arts story where emotion automatically becomes beauty.

It is about bodies trained until emotion can pass through technique.

# 34. Adaptation baseline

The following elements are especially important for any eventual animated adaptation or comparison.

## 1. Mizore as concert planner

The story depends upon seeing her move from passive technical excellence toward active curation.

The final program should not appear to have been created anonymously by the club.

## 2. Asuka’s critique of consensus

Her argument—that merely piling together everyone’s suggestions produces shallow incoherence—is central to the novella’s theory of leadership.

## 3. Hazuki’s erased “Twinkle, Twinkle”

The quiet self-censorship and later restoration of its emotional meaning are indispensable.

## 4. Hazuki’s rhythmic responsibility

Her part should be heard and felt as structural rather than treated as comic background beneath Reina’s featured sound.

## 5. Reina’s eventual praise

The praise matters because it follows precise criticism and validates Hazuki’s actual improvement.

## 6. Kumiko and Shuuichi as a discreet couple

The relationship is already established. An adaptation that reverts them to wholly unresolved ambiguity would alter the novel’s baseline.

## 7. The former middle-school teacher

Kumiko’s statement that she is glad she continued is an important retrospective marker of her first-year development.

## 8. Mizore’s theme of enjoyment

“Something fun” must not be delivered as naïve simplicity. It represents her expansion from one private listener toward a public audience.

## 9. Rikka’s different performance culture

The joint concert should make the contrast visible and audible:

- bodily discipline;
- stage movement;
- showmanship;
- and audience orientation.

## 10. Azusa and Reina’s musical chemistry

Kumiko’s jealousy only makes sense if the two sound genuinely matched and responsive.

## 11. Kumiko’s self-limitation

The conflict is not merely fear of losing a featured part. She has made Reina unreachable through admiration.

## 12. Shuuichi’s support

His desire for his own solo and immediate faith in Kumiko are essential to the romantic relationship’s function.

## 13. Asuka’s new role

She should not simply return as the old euphonium savior. Her baton participation and mentorship demonstrate post-graduation transformation.

## 14. Kumiko’s bodily tension

Asuka’s technical diagnosis should connect psychology and sound.

## 15. The soli selection

Kumiko’s joy needs to register as the result of both artistic recognition and relational equality.

## 16. The illumination dialogue

Reina’s claim over Kumiko’s “first,” the erotic joke, handholding, and wordless mutual possession are central source material, not incidental fan-service interpretation.

## 17. Ending before the performance

Both novellas deliberately make preparation and chosen relationship the dramatic resolution. An adaptation may add concert spectacle, but it should not displace the actual climax.

# 37. Provisional questions for the next mainline phase

## Kumiko’s senior identity

Kumiko has learned to speak her desire in high-pressure moments.

Can she:

- guide first-years;
- make routine decisions;
- and sustain directness when there is no dramatic crisis forcing honesty?

## The danger of Reina-centered ambition

Wanting to stand beside Reina is a powerful engine.

But if Kumiko’s worth depends upon Reina’s recognition, she risks reproducing Mizore’s earlier structure around Nozomi.

Can Kumiko develop ambition that remains hers even when Reina is absent, ahead, or moving abroad?

## Kitauji’s artistic identity

The regular concert demonstrates versatility and entertainment.

The competition goal remains national gold.

Can Kitauji integrate:

- audience hospitality;
- internal joy;
- technical excellence;
- and competitive discipline

without allowing one value to consume the others?

## Yuuko’s leadership

Yuuko’s passion can inspire.

It can also create a strong emotional climate in which dissent becomes difficult.

How will she respond to new students who do not immediately share the inherited national ambition?

## Natsuki’s balancing role

Natsuki is frequently valuable because she restrains others.

Will she be permitted to want something beyond being the person who makes Yuuko and the institution more manageable?

## Hazuki’s audition future

She is improving and has now carried meaningful responsibility.

Will her work translate into A-ensemble selection, and how will she respond if it does not?

## Midori as teacher

Midori possesses knowledge and enthusiasm but can be socially overwhelming.

Can she calibrate explanations to students who do not yet share her joy?

## Mizore after school

Her program demonstrates genuine authorship.

Does she want:

- music college;
- professional performance;
- another ensemble;
- or simply continued connection?

The answer has not yet become fully her own.

## Shuuichi and Kumiko’s public relationship

Their secrecy protects the band from gossip but also preserves old habits of emotional concealment.

At what point does discretion become another form of avoidance?

## Reina and Kumiko’s unclassified intimacy

The illumination scene intensifies their mutual possession without resolving its category.

As the characters mature and make larger life choices, can that relationship remain productively ambiguous, or will one or both eventually demand a more explicit definition?

# Final assessment

Volume 5 is one of the most important transitional works in the early *Sound! Euphonium* corpus.

It does not possess the concentrated dramatic force of Asuka’s crisis or the national competition.

Its achievement is different.

It demonstrates that the story can survive its original climax.

Kitauji’s meaning was never limited to:

> Can this weak band reach nationals?

Once that question is answered, the deeper work begins.

The students must decide:

- what kind of musicians they are;
- what kind of audience they imagine;
- what inherited traditions deserve continuation;
- how former leaders remain present without remaining in control;
- and whether admiration will produce growth or permanent hierarchy.

The two novellas form a deliberate pair.

## *Winter-Colored Rhapsody*

The first moves inward.

Kitauji must combine its own many voices.

Mizore’s task is orchestral and institutional:

> How do private wishes become one public shape?

Her answer is not technically grandiose.

She wants the concert to be fun.

That simplicity contains enormous growth. The girl who once understood music almost entirely through one friend now imagines unknown children and adults receiving joy from a program she has chosen.

## *Star-Colored Serenade*

The second moves outward.

Kitauji encounters another school, another performance tradition, and another girl capable of standing beside Reina.

Kumiko’s task is personal:

> Will admiration remain an excuse for inferiority, or become a demand upon herself?

Azusa does not steal Kumiko’s place.

She reveals that Kumiko has not claimed one.

Kumiko responds with the most important artistic desire she has voiced:

> She wants to play.  
> She wants to become special too.  
> She wants to stand beside Reina proudly.

Asuka’s instruction then provides the correct solution.

Kumiko cannot become equal by forcing herself to sound like someone else.

She must release the fear tightening her body and allow the euphonium to produce its own beauty.

That is the mature form of the title’s imperative:

> **響け — sound, resonate.**

Not:

> become louder than the trumpet.

Not:

> imitate the senior.

Not:

> remain safely in the harmony.

But:

> Let your own tone become strong enough to answer another person without disappearing beside her.

The romantic and yuri-coded structures reinforce that artistic argument.

Shuuichi tells Kumiko that she can do it. His love gives her stability.

Reina tells Kumiko that she wants to play with her. Her desire gives Kumiko a horizon.

Neither relationship replaces the other.

One helps Kumiko remain grounded enough to risk failure.

The other makes her want to exceed the self she previously accepted.

The illumination scene then expresses their relationship outside ordinary categories. Reina knows Kumiko may return with Shuuichi and still claims the first experience for herself. Kumiko recognizes the erotic charge, holds her hand, and experiences a moment of mutual possession requiring no further language.

This is not incidental ambiguity.

It is consistent with the series’ larger philosophy:

> People can matter to one another in ways that exceed the roles available to describe them.

The guidebook interview confirms that Takeda builds the series through such complementary relationships. Characters become legible not as isolated profiles but as voices answering one another:

- Kumiko and Reina;
- Midori and Hazuki;
- Yuuko and Natsuki;
- Mizore and Nozomi;
- Kumiko and Shuuichi;
- Kitauji and Rikka.

Volume 5’s place in the series can therefore be summarized as:

> **The first trilogy taught Kitauji to pursue a goal.  
> Volume 4 revealed the private lives carried into that pursuit.  
> Volume 5 asks the students to become authors of what comes next.**

They are no longer only playing music selected by adults, competitions, inherited crises, or institutional momentum.

Mizore selects a concert.

Kumiko selects an ambition.

Asuka selects a new role.

Hazuki selects responsibility.

Reina selects Kumiko as her musical partner.

And the band begins to learn that continuation is not repeating the performance that once made it special.

Continuation means choosing again.
