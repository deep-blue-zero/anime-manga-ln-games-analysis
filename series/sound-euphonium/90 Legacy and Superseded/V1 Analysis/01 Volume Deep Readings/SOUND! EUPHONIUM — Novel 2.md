# Executive thesis

Volume 1 was about the moral and emotional difficulty of **wanting something openly**.

Volume 2 is about what happens when people want incompatible things—and when both institutions and relationships must decide **who is chosen**.

Its central problem is ranking.

A competition ranks performances.  
An audition ranks players.  
A band ranks effort against seniority.  
A person ranks friends according to emotional importance.  
A student decides which teacher is special.  
A musician imagines one particular listener.  
A national competition permits only three schools to continue.

The novel’s deepest conflict is not simply Mizore’s fear of Nozomi or Kitauji’s attempt to reach nationals. It is the pain created when different systems of value fail to align:

> **The person who is first in your heart may regard you as one friend among many.  
> The effort that defines your life may receive no institutional reward.  
> The player who deserves a place today may have been excluded under yesterday’s rules.  
> The performance that feels miraculous to you may still lose to someone else’s ordinary excellence.**

Volume 2 is therefore a novel of **asymmetrical first places**.

Mizore regards Nozomi as her single indispensable person. Nozomi sincerely cares for Mizore, but not with the same exclusivity. Natsuki also builds part of her musical identity around admiration for Nozomi. Yuuko loves Kaori and fiercely protects Mizore, while knowing she may never occupy the emotional position Nozomi does. Reina wants Taki to choose her romantically, wants musical distinction, and increasingly treats Kumiko as a private emotional exception. Kumiko wants Kitauji to be selected, even when selection requires equally serious students elsewhere to lose.

The volume’s title quietly contains this logic. **いちばん / ichiban** means “number one” or “most.” This is “the hottest summer,” but the novel continually asks who or what becomes **first**:

- the best school;
- the best soloist;
- the most important friend;
- the special teacher;
- the person for whom one plays.

Its answer is not that ranking is inherently evil. Nor does it claim that merit resolves every problem. Its stronger and more uncomfortable conclusion is:

> **People need judgment, standards, and choices—but no judgment can measure the total meaning that music or another person has accumulated inside a life.**

# 1. Volume 2 as a revision of Volume 1

Volume 1 ended triumphantly. Kitauji adopted meritocratic auditions, rejected seniority-based entitlement, produced its best available ensemble, and advanced through the Kyoto competition. Kumiko finally wanted victory enough to cry.

Volume 2 does not repudiate that victory. It complicates nearly every principle that made it possible.

## Meritocracy remains necessary—but proves insufficient

Reina deserved the trumpet solo. Volume 2 never suggests otherwise. Yuuko herself eventually acknowledges that if Kitauji seriously wants nationals, Reina is the appropriate player.

But the novel now asks what happens around a correct decision:

- Kaori’s grief remains real.
- Yuuko’s anger arises from years of watching worthy players denied by the opposite system.
- Mizore’s technically impeccable oboe still lacks communicative life.
- Nozomi was talented and serious but arrived one year too early for merit to be rewarded.
- The students who stayed in the corrupted band now benefit from reforms that came too late for those who resisted first.

Correct ranking does not automatically produce justice, because justice also has a history.

## Effort is not guaranteed retroactive meaning

The students who quit the previous year fought for exactly the band Kitauji later becomes under Taki. Their aspirations were not foolish. They were institutionally mistimed.

Nozomi’s pain is therefore not simply regret over an impulsive resignation. It is the discovery that the world she demanded now exists without her:

> She was not wrong about what the band should become.  
> She was merely unable to make it become that in time.

The current ensemble’s success is genuine, but it cannot reimburse the students whose effort was punished under the old regime.

## Desire becomes morally crowded

In Volume 1, Kumiko’s desire to win was an awakening.

In Volume 2, she discovers that everyone else wants just as intensely. When she hears a powerhouse, part of her wishes its players would make mistakes. She despises herself for it, but she cannot stop wanting Kitauji to be chosen.

Her ambition is no longer emotionally innocent.

That is maturation rather than corruption. Kumiko is beginning to understand that serious desire creates conflict precisely because other people’s desires are also real.

# 2. Narrative architecture

## Prologue: competition as meaninglessness

The prologue transfers the opening perspective from Kumiko to Mizore.

At their middle-school competition, South Middle has practiced intensely and expects success. It receives silver. Nozomi openly grieves and asks why Mizore does not seem disappointed. Mizore cannot make the emotional response Nozomi expects.

Her governing question is:

> 「コンクールってなんなんだろう」  
> What is a competition, anyway?

Her provisional answer is hatred.

The scene parallels Volume 1’s opening, where Reina confronts Kumiko for not being properly frustrated by a dud gold. But the emotional structures are different.

For Kumiko, insufficient frustration reveals that she has not yet admitted she wants victory.

For Mizore, muted reaction does not mean she lacks feeling. It reveals that she cannot accept the system by which effort is converted into a result. Competition seems to take months of life, reduce them to a medal, and then allow that medal to reorganize friendship.

Volume 1 asks whether Kumiko can learn to care.

Volume 2 asks whether caring is safe when an external judgment can make the entire experience feel worthless.

## Chapter 1: 「フルートの来襲」

Nozomi’s flute is an “invasion” in several senses.

She reenters the social field from which she resigned.  
She brings the suppressed institutional past into the successful present.  
She threatens Asuka’s carefully managed stability.  
Her sound penetrates Mizore’s body before Mizore can intellectually defend herself.

The word **来襲** is stronger than a neutral “arrival.” Nozomi is not malicious, but her return functions like an assault upon the band’s buried memory.

The chapter establishes:

- Kitauji’s rapidly intensifying national ambition;
- Nozomi’s desire to rejoin;
- Asuka’s unexplained refusal;
- the history of the previous year’s mass resignation;
- Mizore’s panic response;
- Kumiko and Reina’s expanding intimacy;
- Kumiko’s fear that special relationships may become ordinary;
- Natsuki’s hidden admiration for Nozomi;
- and the first major evidence that Asuka’s neutrality is strategic rather than passive.

## Chapter 2: 「トランペットの本心」

The obvious “true feeling” belongs to Reina. Her love for Taki becomes an object of insecurity when she suspects Niiyama may be romantically connected to him. The usually declarative Reina becomes frightened, jealous, and dependent upon Kumiko’s reassurance.

But the title operates more broadly.

The chapter is centrally concerned with getting an internal feeling into sound:

- Hashimoto criticizes Mizore’s technically flawless but emotionally uncommunicative solo.
- Niiyama asks Mizore to like the oboe rather than treat it as obligation.
- Reina embodies the opposite problem: her playing communicates an almost excessive self-assertion.
- Taki’s own emotional past—the death of his wife—is disclosed to Kumiko by Hashimoto.
- Yuuko articulates her true feelings about competition, effort, Kaori, and the previous year.
- Kumiko and Reina physically hold on to each other in the dark.

The trumpet represents declaration. Reina’s problem is rarely that she cannot produce a clear musical or verbal statement. Her difficulty is admitting uncertainty without losing the identity of the exceptional girl.

## Chapter 3: 「オーボエの覚醒」

The oboe awakens when Mizore finds a concrete addressee.

Adults repeatedly tell her that her playing needs expression. This is musically sound but emotionally unusable advice. “Play with more feeling” cannot help someone who does not know what she is playing for, whether she likes the instrument, or why she remains in the band.

Nozomi’s praise supplies the missing relational form:

- she has always liked Mizore’s sound;
- Mizore’s playing makes her feel excited;
- she is looking forward to hearing the solo;
- she believes Mizore will perform well.

Mizore can now transform the abstract instruction “express something” into:

> **Play so that Nozomi can hear what her presence means.**

Her awakening is not the discovery of a self wholly independent from others. It is the discovery that relational feeling can become musical form.

## Epilogue: the answer changes because the experience changes

After Kitauji is selected for nationals, Kumiko asks Mizore whether she still hates competitions.

Mizore smiles fully for the first time and answers:

> 「……たったいま、好きになった」  
> “Just now, I started liking them.”

This is emotionally satisfying and philosophically unstable—and that instability is deliberate.

Nothing about competition has become objectively fairer. Other schools still lose. A powerhouse substitute still makes a painful mistake. South Middle’s old silver remains unchanged. Nozomi’s earlier exclusion is not repaired.

What has changed is Mizore’s lived relationship to the event.

This time:

- she is not abandoned;
- Yuuko has affirmed her place;
- Nozomi has heard her;
- her solo communicates what she could not say;
- the ensemble’s effort receives public continuation;
- and competition becomes the site of connection rather than separation.

The ending does not establish that competition was always good. It establishes that institutions acquire meaning through the relationships and memories attached to them.

Yuuko’s earlier argument is vindicated: people often love contests after success and hate them after failure. The novel does not mock that contingency. It treats it as an honest part of human experience.

# 3. The novel’s central structure: ranking music and ranking people

The competition plot and the friendship plot are not separate.

Mizore’s fear can be described in competitive language:

> She has ranked Nozomi first, but fears she does not place highly in Nozomi’s emotional world.

Her crisis is not that Nozomi hates her. Hatred would at least confirm significance. Her fear is that she is **interchangeable**.

She tells Kumiko:

> 「私にとっての希美と、希美にとっての私は、全然違う」  
> The Nozomi who exists for me and the person I am to Nozomi are completely different.

That is the emotional thesis of the volume.

Competition publicly declares that some performances continue and others do not. Mizore imagines friendship as an unspoken version of the same process. If she ceases to play well, will Nozomi stop needing her? If Nozomi can leave without telling her, was Mizore ever selected at all?

The novel then multiplies this pattern.

## Nozomi and Asuka

Nozomi calls Asuka special because Asuka alone tried to stop her from resigning. Asuka later claims she intervened because Nozomi was talented and might be useful.

Nozomi interprets the action emotionally.  
Asuka explains it instrumentally.

Both readings may contain truth.

## Natsuki and Nozomi

Natsuki entered band partly because she admired Nozomi’s capacity to care enough to fight and cry. Nozomi possessed the intensity Natsuki felt she had wasted her middle-school years avoiding.

Nozomi is therefore not only Mizore’s formative person. She is also Natsuki’s.

## Yuuko and Mizore

Yuuko knows she is not Nozomi. Her late conversation with Natsuki contains real pain: after a year beside Mizore, she still cannot “beat” the absent Nozomi.

Yet the novel rejects the premise that emotional importance must be zero-sum. Yuuko may not replace Nozomi, but she is the person who prevents Mizore from collapsing.

## Reina, Taki, and Kumiko

Reina wants to be special to Taki. At the same time, she reveals private insecurity almost exclusively to Kumiko. She jokes that she will perform for Kumiko and waits for her after the crisis.

The person she romantically desires and the person with whom she is becoming emotionally safest are not necessarily the same.

## Kitauji and the judges

Kitauji wants to be chosen by the competition. Its students know that their own conviction cannot grant them a national berth. They must become legible to external evaluators.

The entire volume is haunted by the wish:

> **Please recognize that what matters to me has value.**

# 4. Kumiko Oumae: from detached observer to morally compromised participant

Kumiko remains the viewpoint consciousness, but Volume 2 changes what observation means.

In Volume 1, observation often protected her from commitment. She could diagnose others while avoiding a clear statement of her own desire.

In Volume 2, she begins using perception as a form of intervention.

She:

- questions Natsuki about Nozomi;
- listens to Nozomi’s account of the previous year;
- speaks to Mizore about competition;
- challenges Asuka’s refusal;
- learns Yuuko’s position;
- promises to help Nozomi;
- searches for Mizore during the crisis;
- witnesses the reconciliation;
- and rejects Asuka’s attempt to reduce Yuuko to an emotional “insurance policy.”

She is no longer merely standing outside the social machinery.

## Her intervention is limited—and the novel knows it

Kumiko does not resolve the central crisis.

Yuuko reaches Mizore emotionally.  
Nozomi supplies the praise that changes the solo.  
Asuka arranges the forced encounter.  
Natsuki provides the historical and emotional context.

Kumiko’s contribution is listening, carrying accounts between social worlds, and remaining present long enough for connections to emerge.

That is appropriate to her instrument and character. The euphonium rarely dominates the ensemble, but it can bind musical layers together. Kumiko performs a similar narrative function.

The novel avoids making its protagonist the magical healer of every wound. Sometimes another person has the right relationship, history, or voice for the task.

## Curiosity and care remain entangled

Kumiko wants to help, but she is also intensely curious. She repeatedly approaches truths that are not entirely hers to possess.

The novel never turns this into a moral indictment, because curiosity is part of her genuine attentiveness. But it prevents her kindness from becoming self-flattering.

Her “good girl” identity remains incomplete. She can care and intrude at the same time.

## Her competitive desire acquires an ugly side

Volume 1 allowed Kumiko to discover that she wanted to win.

Volume 2 makes her confront what winning means.

While listening to the elite university-affiliated school, she inwardly wishes for errors. When the E-flat clarinet soloist falters, Kumiko receives the mistake she had wanted—and immediately has to confront the human beings inside the rival institution.

Outside the hall, she overhears that the soloist was a last-minute substitute for an injured senior. The substitute is devastated; the senior comforts her. Their ambitions, admiration, guilt, and effort are no less real than Kitauji’s.

Kumiko realizes that every school has its own narrative.

This is one of the volume’s most important ethical developments:

> **Empathy does not remove competition. It makes competition harder to enjoy innocently.**

Kumiko still wants Kitauji selected. The novel does not demand saintly impartiality. It demands that she stop imagining rivals as faceless obstacles.

## Kumiko’s relation to performance has deepened

During Kitauji’s performance, she thinks that she wants the music to continue. She wants the audience to keep hearing them. The stage is no longer merely the place where a result will be obtained.

That is a significant development from the opening of Volume 1.

She has moved from:

> I happen to play euphonium

toward:

> I want our sound to exist in other people’s experience.

This is an artistic desire, not only a competitive one.

## Her emotional relationships diversify

Kumiko’s bond with Reina becomes more intimate, but Shuuichi remains meaningful.

Shuuichi listens to the competition music privately, notices Kumiko’s anxiety, reassures her, and produces a bodily self-consciousness she does not experience in exactly the same way around other people.

Reina, meanwhile:

- invites Kumiko to the fireworks;
- displays expressions reserved for her;
- holds her hand during emotional uncertainty;
- waits for her after the Mizore incident;
- brushes dust from her clothes;
- and says she may perform for Kumiko.

Volume 2 does not require these relationships to cancel each other. Kumiko’s emotional life is becoming more complex precisely as Mizore’s crisis demonstrates the danger of allowing one person to become the sole condition of existence.

# 5. Mizore Yoroizuka: technical perfection without a reason to speak

Mizore is the volume’s central character.

Her surface is defined by absence:

- minimal expression;
- minimal speech;
- little visible reaction to success or failure;
- no clear statement that she likes the oboe;
- no obvious reason for remaining in the club;
- and technically immaculate playing that instructors experience as emotionally empty.

The novel gradually reveals that this emptiness is not a lack of feeling.

It is feeling that has been compressed until it can no longer circulate.

## Her relationship to objective evaluation

Mizore enjoys rhythm games and has perfectly cleared their songs. This is not incidental characterization.

A rhythm game offers:

- explicit timing;
- visible inputs;
- measurable accuracy;
- repeatable conditions;
- an unambiguous perfect score.

Musical competition looks numerical from outside, but its judgments remain subjective. Hashimoto can tell Mizore her solo is boring despite her technical accuracy. A judge may prefer one interpretation over another. Months of practice can become a silver medal without a comprehensible causal explanation.

Mizore is comfortable with systems in which correctness is visible. She is terrified by systems in which the criteria are partially hidden.

Her artistic crisis is therefore also an epistemological crisis:

> **How do I know what “good” means when correct notes are not enough?**

## Nozomi as origin

Mizore did not begin music through independent fascination. Nozomi spoke to an isolated girl, invited her into band, and transformed her daily life.

That origin matters because Mizore comes to treat three things as inseparable:

- Nozomi;
- friendship;
- music.

When Nozomi leaves, Mizore continues practicing not because the instrument independently sustains her, but because she fears that musical usefulness is the only remaining reason Nozomi might keep her.

Her statement is devastating:

> If she becomes bad at playing, Nozomi may decide she is no longer needed.

The oboe is not merely an instrument. It is perceived proof of relational worth.

## The shame of unequal attachment

Mizore understands that her dependence is asymmetrical. That self-awareness does not free her; it makes her ashamed.

She knows Nozomi has many friends.  
She knows Nozomi did not deliberately injure her.  
She knows her own reaction is disproportionate to Nozomi’s intent.

She therefore directs disgust inward:

> What kind of person becomes this attached to a friend?

The novel refuses to cure this by calling Mizore irrational and instructing her to become independent. Instead, it shows that the problem is not attachment itself. It is the collapse of an entire emotional world into one relationship.

## Yuuko’s intervention: pluralizing dependence

Yuuko’s decisive question is not “Why do you need Nozomi?”

It is:

> **What am I to you?**

She confronts Mizore’s assumption that the first person who rescued her must remain the only person who counts. Yuuko insists that later relationships are not inferior merely because they began through sympathy or after a wound.

This does not replace Nozomi.

It expands Mizore’s world enough that Nozomi’s possible rejection would no longer mean absolute solitude.

That is why Mizore can finally face her. Asuka describes Yuuko cynically as a kind of insurance. Functionally, Asuka sees something real: Yuuko’s presence makes the confrontation survivable. But her description strips the relationship of its moral substance.

Yuuko is not merely a backup asset. She has spent a year noticing, protecting, arguing with, and remaining beside Mizore. Security is not evidence of manipulation. It is one of the things real relationships provide.

## The reconciliation does not equalize the relationship

Nozomi explains that she did not invite Mizore to resign because Mizore had continued practicing seriously. Inviting her to abandon that effort would have been disrespectful.

This resolves the factual misunderstanding.

It does **not** make their emotional investments equal.

Nozomi apologizes lightly because she had no idea what her silence meant to Mizore. Kumiko recognizes that Nozomi will probably never understand the full intensity of Mizore’s attachment.

The novel preserves that residue deliberately. Mizore’s happy resolution is not:

> Nozomi secretly loved me with equal exclusivity.

It is:

> Nozomi did value me, even if she valued me differently.

That is a more mature and more painful resolution.

## The awakened oboe

Nozomi tells Mizore that she has always loved her sound. She describes it as producing excitement and joy. She asks to hear the competition performance.

Mizore then plays with the emotional force her instructors had been unable to elicit.

This is not a magical cure. It reveals something important about expression:

> **Feeling becomes artistically communicable when it is given direction.**

“Play emotionally” is abstract.

“Let Nozomi hear what her presence means” is concrete.

The resulting solo is sweet, sorrowful, vivid, and unbroken. Its emotional complexity comes from the relationship itself: joy, fear, gratitude, dependency, grief, and relief occupy the same phrase.

Mizore’s oboe awakens because Mizore finally has something she is willing to let another person hear.

# 6. Nozomi Kasaki: sincere light that fails to notice the shadows it casts

Nozomi is neither villain nor uncomplicated victim.

She is:

- talented;
- charismatic;
- socially abundant;
- impulsive;
- hardworking;
- sincere;
- and often poor at imagining how intensely other people experience her.

Her tragedy is partly temporal. She fought for standards one year before the institution was willing to enforce them. Under Taki, the same seriousness that made her troublesome would have made her valuable.

## The injury of arriving one year too early

The old Kitauji system rewarded seniority and treated improvement as socially disruptive. Serious first-years from South Middle challenged the upperclassmen and were told to preserve harmony.

Nozomi could have waited for the seniors to graduate. She could not tolerate the waste.

Her decision to quit was rash, but it was not based on trivial impatience. She had been asked to accept that:

- ability would not determine placement;
- committed practice was socially aggressive;
- and the people damaging the ensemble had institutional protection.

The successful present therefore wounds her. The band now shines under rules she wanted, while she watches from outside.

## Her self-narration contains blind spots

Nozomi describes her motives with confidence, but Kumiko notices inconsistencies.

She says Mizore would not have understood the resignation movement. Elsewhere it becomes clear that jealousy, exclusion, or assumptions may also have influenced the failure to include her.

Nozomi does not deliberately lie. She has produced a coherent story about herself and lives inside it.

That is one reason she contrasts so strongly with Mizore:

- Mizore doubts every feeling.
- Nozomi converts feeling into action before fully examining it.

## Her affection is real, but nonexclusive

Nozomi cares for Mizore. She remembers her old musical expressiveness, worries about her solo, praises her sound, and agrees immediately to listen.

The problem is not false affection.

The problem is scale.

Nozomi’s social world contains many people. Mizore’s has been built around one. Nozomi experiences friendship expansively; Mizore experiences it concentrically.

The novel does not make either style morally wrong. It shows why they can become dangerous when neither person understands the mismatch.

## Nozomi as an origin figure

Nozomi affects more than Mizore.

Natsuki admires her.  
Yuuko resents and envies her importance.  
Asuka regarded her as potentially useful.  
The resigning students organized around her initiative.  
Her return forces the band to confront its past.

She is a person whose energy generates other people’s stories, often without her realizing it.

That is charisma in one of its morally complicated forms.

# 7. Yuuko Yoshikawa: abrasive loyalty and the volume’s clearest moral action

Volume 1 allowed Yuuko to appear unreasonable. She challenged Reina’s solo partly because of intense devotion to Kaori and because she could not emotionally accept a result that hurt someone she loved.

Volume 2 reveals that the same qualities which made her disruptive also make her indispensable:

- she notices suffering;
- she refuses emotional euphemism;
- she runs toward people in crisis;
- she says aloud what gentler characters evade;
- and she treats attachment as a claim requiring action.

## Her view of competition

Yuuko offers the volume’s most psychologically accurate theory of contests.

People who receive good results tend to love them.  
People who receive bad results tend to hate them.

This is not a sophisticated abstract defense of competition. It is better: it is a recognition that institutional meaning is lived through outcomes.

Yuuko knows that an unfair system injured Kaori and the previous first-years. She also knows that Reina is the player Kitauji needs. She does not reconcile these truths by pretending one cancels the other.

## Her confrontation with Mizore

Yuuko does not console Mizore gently. She asks whether every shared day, every competition, and every friendship besides Nozomi truly meant nothing.

Her key move is to reject retrospective erasure.

Mizore fears that enjoying Kitauji’s present success betrays the students who quit. Yuuko tells her that the joy is still legitimate.

The current band’s happiness does not prove the past was just.  
The past injustice does not require Mizore to refuse all later happiness.

This is one of the volume’s most mature ethical propositions.

## Yuuko’s own wound

After Mizore reconnects with Nozomi, Yuuko admits that she cannot surpass Nozomi’s place in Mizore’s heart.

Her joke is protective coating. The pain is real.

Natsuki’s response matters: Mizore would have broken much earlier without Yuuko. Emotional importance is not measured solely by occupying first place.

Yuuko may never be Mizore’s origin. She is nevertheless part of the reason Mizore can continue.

# 8. Natsuki Nakagawa: admiration, guilt, and the life not lived seriously enough

Natsuki’s casualness is another defensive style.

She claims to dislike effort and joined concert band because it was reputed to be easy. Yet her history reveals that she was drawn to Nozomi precisely because Nozomi possessed an intensity she lacked.

At middle-school graduation, Natsuki watched Nozomi and the band members cry even though many would attend the same high school. She initially found it ridiculous, then recognized that she envied them.

They had something worth grieving.

Natsuki’s attraction to band begins as attraction to **commitment itself**.

## Guilt as belated action

Natsuki did not adequately support Nozomi during the previous year’s conflict. She did insult the cruel upperclassmen, but she remained largely outside the struggle.

Helping Nozomi return becomes a form of atonement.

Her “crime” is not active betrayal. It is spectatorship.

This makes her an important mirror for Kumiko. Both girls are perceptive and inclined to stand at a slight distance from emotionally expensive conflict. Natsuki shows what it feels like to realize, after the fact, that nonparticipation was itself a choice.

## Her relationship with Yuuko

Their hostility is both genuine and affiliative.

They insult each other, compete, and irritate one another. Yet Natsuki understands Yuuko’s importance to Mizore and offers exactly the reassurance Yuuko needs.

The volume repeatedly shows that warmth need not look gentle. Natsuki and Yuuko’s verbal aggression can coexist with trust.

# 9. Asuka Tanaka: neutrality revealed as management

Volume 1 presented Asuka as socially brilliant, musically exceptional, emotionally unreadable, and unwilling to invest herself openly in factional conflict.

Volume 2 reveals that she is not neutral in the sense of inactive.

She is constantly managing outcomes.

## Why she excludes Nozomi

Asuka understands that Nozomi’s presence destabilizes Mizore, Kitauji’s only oboist and a crucial soloist. She therefore refuses Nozomi’s return until the competition ends.

Her logic is institutional:

- Nozomi adds no immediate competitive value because the A ensemble has already been selected.
- Mizore’s destabilization creates immediate competitive risk.
- Therefore Nozomi should remain outside.

This is rational, effective, and emotionally brutal.

## Her selective truth

Asuka withholds the real reason from Nozomi to avoid injuring her. She allows Nozomi to interpret the refusal as Asuka’s personal stubbornness.

This resembles compassion, but it also preserves Asuka’s control. She decides:

- who may know the truth;
- when they may know it;
- which relationship must be sacrificed temporarily;
- and what level of pain is tolerable for institutional stability.

Her neutrality is not the absence of judgment. It is judgment concealed behind theatrical lightness.

## The “shock treatment”

Once the accidental confrontation occurs, Asuka changes strategies. She sends Nozomi after Mizore carrying the oboe, deliberately converting crisis into forced exposure.

She describes the successful result as “shock therapy” and praises Yuuko as the real MVP.

This flexibility demonstrates why she is so powerful. She does not remain loyal to a principle; she remains loyal to producing a workable outcome.

## Her view of human attachment

Asuka describes Yuuko as Mizore’s “insurance”: once Mizore knows another person will remain, she can risk confronting Nozomi.

This is functionally perceptive and ethically impoverished.

She sees how relationships operate. She resists describing why they matter.

Kumiko’s rejection of this interpretation marks important growth. She no longer accepts Asuka’s intelligence as automatically morally complete.

## The hidden musician

The volume also gives us Asuka practicing alone around four in the morning and playing a melody whose title she refuses to disclose.

This is crucial setup.

Asuka appears to be the person who can reduce everyone else’s feelings to useful mechanisms. Yet she privately makes time for music before the rest of the world awakens, and she protects the meaning of at least one piece from inquiry.

Her detachment is itself a performance.

## Shadow leadership

At the competition, Asuka delivers the speech that truly organizes the band’s emotions. She then returns the formal ritual to Haruka, asking the actual president to lead the cheer.

That gesture is characteristic:

- Asuka governs the atmosphere.
- Haruka occupies the office.
- Asuka preserves the appearance of proper structure while remaining its emotional center.

Volume 2 does not resolve whether this is generosity, avoidance of accountability, or both.

# 10. Reina Kousaka: self-directed music and private vulnerability

Reina remains the novel’s clearest advocate for excellence.

Her position on competition is severe:

- a truly overwhelming performance will be recognized;
- criticizing competition before reaching that level sounds like excuse-making;
- the existence of subjective preference does not eliminate obvious differences in quality;
- and performing before a large audience is itself an opportunity.

She represents faith that ability can eventually compel recognition.

The volume does not prove her entirely right. It does show why her philosophy is psychologically powerful: it transforms uncertainty into work.

## The trumpet’s declarative ethic

Reina’s music says:

> Listen to me.

Hashimoto contrasts her solo with Mizore’s. Reina’s playing contains an almost aggressive assertion of existence. It is not merely technically correct; it expects the world to acknowledge its speaker.

This quality appears in her ordinary dialogue as well. She uses concise declarations, strong first-person language, and minimal hedging.

## Her emotional exception

Her fear that Niiyama may be Taki’s romantic partner exposes the limits of this self-sufficiency.

She cannot resolve romantic uncertainty through practice. Musical excellence cannot compel Taki to choose her.

In the dark, she holds Kumiko’s hand and allows herself to be reassured. Her voice becomes less absolute because Kumiko is becoming a space where uncertainty does not equal weakness.

## “For myself” and “for Kumiko”

After Mizore’s crisis, Kumiko asks whether Reina plays for another person.

Reina answers that she plays because becoming better is enjoyable:

> 「アタシはアタシのために楽器吹いてる」  
> She plays for herself.

This is the strongest counterpoint to Mizore.

Yet immediately before the performance, Reina jokes that she may play for Kumiko. The contradiction is productive.

She does not need to choose between:

- self-directed artistic ambition;
- romantic feeling for Taki;
- and emotional intimacy with Kumiko.

Her identity is sturdy enough to contain several motivations. That is precisely what Mizore has lacked.

---

# 11. Kumiko and Reina as a counterpoint to Mizore and Nozomi

Volume 2 places the two relationships in quiet dialogue.

Both involve:

- music as a medium of closeness;
- one girl experiencing another as special;
- fear that the relationship may not remain exceptional;
- physical and aesthetic attention;
- and uncertainty about the exact category of intimacy.

Kumiko worries during the fireworks that she and Reina may someday become ordinary acquaintances. This fear is a moderated version of Mizore’s terror.

The critical difference is plurality.

Kumiko has Hazuki, Midori, Shuuichi, her family, and the band.  
Reina’s musical identity does not depend entirely upon Kumiko.  
Both girls can move toward and away from each other without immediate psychic collapse.

Mizore has treated Nozomi as the sole bridge between herself and the social world.

The comparison suggests that intimacy becomes dangerous not because it is intense, but because no other structure exists around it.

Both relationships are strongly available to yuri interpretation, particularly through bodily attention, exclusivity, blushing, touch, and music addressed to another girl. The novel does not label either relationship as romance. Reina’s romantic love for Taki is explicit. The more accurate reading is that Volume 2 allows adolescent attachment to remain categorically crowded: aesthetic attraction, friendship, dependency, admiration, and romantic feeling need not yet possess clean borders.

---

# 12. Taki, Hashimoto, and Niiyama: three theories of musical adulthood

The adults become substantially richer in Volume 2.

## Taki: judgment without ontological ranking

Before the Kansai performance, Taki tells the students that a disappointing result would not prove their music intrinsically inferior. It would mean that other performances received higher evaluations from those judges on that day.

Likewise, victory should not be interpreted as proof that Kitauji is essentially superior to everyone else. It grants another opportunity to perform.

This is a sophisticated position:

> **Evaluation is real and consequential without being metaphysically absolute.**

It differs from Reina’s belief that overwhelming excellence can force consensus, but the two positions are not mutually exclusive. Reina supplies the drive to become undeniable. Taki supplies humility about what institutional recognition can establish.

## Taki’s grief

Hashimoto reveals that Taki’s wife died five years earlier. Taki withdrew from music and became emotionally hollow. Taking over Kitauji marks his return to active life.

This places Taki inside the same thematic structure as Mizore.

Both use music in relation to a person who is no longer available in the old form. Neither can be “fixed” by simply finding a replacement.

Hashimoto and Niiyama could not replace Taki’s wife; they could only remain near him. Yuuko cannot replace Nozomi; she can still give Mizore a relationship in which life continues.

The volume repeatedly argues that later bonds do not erase formative ones. They make survival beyond them possible.

## Hashimoto: expression as human presence

Hashimoto’s outward style is comic, expansive, and informal. His musical standards are severe.

He tells Mizore that technical perfection is not enough. Her solo sounds boring because he cannot hear a person inside it. He asks what she wants the solo to be and forces her to produce an image: a crescent moon.

His central pedagogical claim is:

> Music must communicate an authored experience, not merely reproduce notation.

He also treats the students as musicians rather than children. His bluntness is a form of respect for their capacity.

## Niiyama: pedagogical humility

Niiyama initially works through patient repetition, but eventually recognizes that she has unconsciously placed a ceiling on Mizore because Mizore is a high-school student.

She apologizes.

That apology is significant. An instructor admits that the limitation may have been in the teacher’s expectation rather than the student’s ability.

She asks Mizore not merely to execute the oboe but to like it. This does not immediately produce a solution, yet it identifies the deeper problem correctly: obligation cannot permanently substitute for relationship with the art itself.

# 13. Competition as a polyphonic argument

Volume 2 does not appoint one character to explain what competition means. It gives several incompatible views legitimate weight.

## Mizore

Competition converts effort into opaque judgment and creates pain. She initially wants it abolished.

## Reina

Competition is a rare opportunity to be heard. Subjectivity exists, but extreme ability can overcome it. Complaining before reaching that level is evasion.

## Yuuko

Competition’s emotional meaning is outcome-dependent. Good results can release people from old wounds; bad results can make the entire system feel hateful.

## Taki

Judgment is consequential but limited. A result reflects evaluators’ preference, not the absolute worth of the music or people.

## Midori

Excellent music can be admired regardless of whether it threatens her own group. Quality does not become ugly merely because it belongs to a rival.

## Kumiko

She cannot maintain Midori’s detachment. She wants rivals to fail, then despises that desire.

## Hashimoto

Technique is necessary but cannot alone create communicative music.

## The rival substitute

Her story demonstrates that one audible error may sit atop courage, admiration, emergency responsibility, and intense preparation. The mistake is real. It is not the whole truth of the performance.

The novel’s position emerges from the tension among these voices:

> **Competition can motivate artistic development, create occasions for public performance, and give effort a socially recognized form. It can also disguise subjectivity as objective hierarchy, make others’ failure useful to us, and retroactively rewrite how people remember their own labor.**

The institution is neither pure nor meaningless. It is a human structure whose value depends partly upon how people inhabit it.

---

# 14. Kitauji’s “miracle” and the difference between emerging and established excellence

Kumiko regards Kitauji’s Kansai performance as a miracle: one of those rare occasions when every individual produces something beyond the ensemble’s ordinary consistency.

That description matters.

Kitauji is now genuinely good, but its excellence remains fragile. Its best performance may occur once in ten or once in a hundred attempts.

The established powerhouse demonstrates a different quality:

> stability.

Its players make the hardest passages sound routine. Even after a conspicuous solo error, the ensemble immediately restores the musical structure.

Kitauji’s achievement is not that it has already become equivalent in institutional depth. It is that, on the right day, intense collective concentration allows it to enter the same competitive field.

This keeps the national qualification from becoming proof that Taki has instantly transformed Kitauji into an invincible school.

They have produced an extraordinary result. They have not yet built an ordinary culture of extraordinary performance.

---

# 15. The institutional past: success does not equal repair

The previous Kitauji regime was built around a corrupt understanding of harmony.

“Getting along” meant:

- serious first-years should stop challenging seniors;
- placement should protect status rather than reflect ability;
- committed practice should not make others uncomfortable;
- and people harmed by ostracism should absorb it quietly.

The story is careful here. Collective harmony is not automatically virtuous. An ensemble can be socially unified around mediocrity and coercion.

Taki changes the rules. But institutional reform arrives after casualties.

Nozomi and the other resigned students cannot recover the lost year. Their resistance helped expose the problem, yet the students who remained receive the benefit of the corrected system.

Mizore’s guilt over celebrating Kitauji’s success reflects this unresolved debt. Yuuko’s answer is emotionally necessary: refusing happiness will not compensate the people who left.

But the moral account remains open.

> **A reformed institution should be celebrated without pretending reform makes its victims whole.**

# 16. Music as relation

Volume 2 presents several answers to the question: **For whom do you play?**

| Character | Primary answer in Volume 2 |
|---|---|
| Mizore | For Nozomi, because music is the bond through which she first entered life with others |
| Reina | Primarily for herself and the pleasure of becoming better; sometimes imaginatively for Kumiko or Taki |
| Kumiko | Increasingly for the ensemble and for an audience she wants to keep listening |
| Natsuki | Initially because she followed Nozomi’s intensity; increasingly because the band has become her own |
| Taki | Music and teaching as a return to life after bereavement |
| Nozomi | Because she loves band and wants access to the collective experience she lost |
| Yuuko | Through loyalty: music is inseparable from the people whose effort she has witnessed |
| Midori | For the delight and seriousness of music itself |

The novel does not privilege purely self-originating motivation as the only authentic kind.

Natsuki begins through admiration.  
Mizore begins through attachment.  
Taki returns through grief.  
Kumiko grows through other people’s desire.

A motive can begin outside the self and still become real.

The danger appears when the external person becomes the sole guarantor of value. The answer is not isolation. It is a broader network of reasons, relationships, and pleasures.

# 17. The chapter instruments as psychological forms

## Flute: movement and social circulation

Nozomi’s flute corresponds to her personality:

- mobile;
- bright;
- socially agile;
- forward-moving;
- and able to penetrate spaces before others are prepared.

Her “invasion” is not physical aggression. It is the return of an energy the band’s present structure had excluded.

## Trumpet: declaration and distinction

Reina’s trumpet asserts a self into public space.

Its solo says:

> Attend to me.  
> Recognize me.  
> I am here.

That is why the chapter about “true feelings” belongs naturally to her. The trumpet’s challenge is not generating a voice. It is allowing that powerful voice to admit fear.

## Oboe: exposed singularity

The oboe is especially appropriate for Mizore.

Its solo is:

- solitary;
- exposed;
- timbrally distinctive;
- technically delicate;
- and impossible to hide inside the ensemble.

Mizore’s psychological defense is to minimize visible individuality. Her musical role demands the opposite.

The oboe awakens when she accepts exposure before a specific listener.

## Euphonium: mediation and connective tissue

Kumiko’s instrument continues to mirror her narrative role.

It is not usually the obvious star. It supports, thickens, connects, and sometimes receives difficult internal lines that hold larger structures together.

Kumiko’s growth is not toward becoming trumpet-like. It is toward inhabiting the euphonium role deliberately rather than using support as an excuse for invisibility.

# 18. Character voices and speaking styles

Volume 2 strengthens the voices established in the first novel and introduces several especially clear relational idiolects.

## Kumiko

Her outward standard Japanese remains hesitant and qualified. Her internal voice is sharper, more judgmental, and increasingly morally self-critical.

She becomes more direct with Asuka and Natsuki, showing that the gap between thought and speech is narrowing.

## Mizore

Mizore’s ordinary speech is:

- standard Japanese;
- short;
- literal;
- fragmentary;
- emotionally flattened;
- and often stripped of conventional social padding.

Her crisis changes the rhythm. Repetition, negation, and increasingly long confessional sentences appear as emotional pressure overwhelms her normal compression.

The change in syntax makes the breakdown audible even without vocal performance.

## Nozomi

Nozomi speaks in energetic, socially fluent Kansai Japanese.

Her rhythm is:

- expansive;
- quick;
- explanatory;
- confident;
- and lightly humorous.

Even her apology remains buoyant. This is not callousness so much as evidence that she does not experience the event at Mizore’s scale.

Their speech rhythms embody the asymmetry before the content explains it.

## Yuuko

Yuuko’s language is interrogative and confrontational:

- direct second-person address;
- commands;
- repeated questions;
- accusations;
- emotionally forceful Kansai forms;
- and very little protective ambiguity.

Her speech attempts to force hidden relationships into declaration. “What am I to you?” is the purest example.

## Natsuki

Natsuki uses relaxed, ironic Kansai speech, often coating sincerity in dismissive phrasing or jokes.

Her confession that Nozomi was an object of admiration temporarily strips away that slouching register. The change reveals how much effort her casualness conceals.

## Asuka

Asuka remains the great register-switcher.

She can sound:

- theatrical;
- comic;
- flirtatious;
- analytical;
- managerial;
- cruel;
- or intimate.

She often keeps a bright vocal surface while reducing a painful relationship to function. Her control of tone is part of her social power.

## Reina

Reina’s declarative voice remains unmistakable. Her strong first-person **アタシ**, compact judgments, and minimal hedging express a coherent self.

Around Kumiko, the voice gains playfulness and vulnerability without losing its precision.

## Taki

Taki speaks in controlled standard Japanese, often through formal qualification. He is careful not to convert competition results into absolute declarations of human or artistic worth.

His emotional reserve makes the rare hints of personal investment more significant.

## Hashimoto

Hashimoto’s rapid, humorous Kansai speech creates informality, but his artistic criticism is exact. He uses comedy to establish access, then demands adult-level seriousness.

## Niiyama

Niiyama’s voice is gentle, patient, and precise. Her apology to Mizore carries authority because it does not abandon standards; it revises the teacher’s own assumptions.

# 19. Relational intensity and yuri-coded ambiguity

Volume 2 is saturated with intense relationships among girls.

Mizore’s fixation upon Nozomi includes:

- exclusivity;
- bodily panic;
- fear of abandonment;
- blushes;
- the desire to play for her;
- and a need to be recognized as special.

Yuuko’s relationship with Mizore contains:

- physical closeness;
- fierce protective behavior;
- jealousy toward Nozomi;
- and the direct demand to have their bond named.

Kumiko and Reina share:

- private invitations;
- handholding;
- visual and bodily fascination;
- emotional exclusivity;
- intimate teasing;
- and music imaginatively addressed from one to the other.

Natsuki’s admiration for Nozomi is formative enough to redirect her high-school life.

These relationships are unmistakably available to yuri reading. The novel does not settle them into a uniform romantic category. That ambiguity is not evidence that the intensity is unreal.

Its more interesting claim is that adolescent attachment often exceeds the vocabulary available to classify it. Friendship, artistic admiration, romantic attraction, dependency, envy, and identification can occupy the same relationship simultaneously.

The ethical issue is not whether a bond is “really” romantic. It is whether the people inside it can survive the difference between what each person believes the bond means.

# 20. Prose, bodies, and heat

The subtitle’s “hottest summer” appears materially throughout the prose:

- sweat;
- crowded halls;
- sunlight;
- heated instruments;
- flushed faces;
- feverish competition;
- rapid breathing;
- and bodies physically reacting before language catches up.

Emotion repeatedly becomes physiological.

Mizore vomits or nearly vomits at Nozomi’s sound.  
Reina’s romantic anxiety produces physical tension.  
Kumiko’s competition fear tightens her chest.  
Yuuko arrives disheveled and breathless after searching.  
The stage lights feel almost unbearable.  
The ensemble’s shared concentration is felt through skin.

This is important because the characters often lie, hedge, or misunderstand themselves verbally. Their bodies reveal stakes their speech cannot yet organize.

The novel’s adolescent gaze also remains present. Kumiko notices:

- legs;
- skin;
- hair;
- hands;
- clothing;
- scent;
- posture;
- and physical proximity.

These details do not function only as decorative sensuality. They make social attention bodily. Kumiko understands people partly through the forms they occupy in space.

# 22. Adaptation baseline

When the anime adaptation is eventually examined, the following points should be tracked closely.

## 1. Mizore’s interiority

The novel directly explains:

- why she began playing;
- why she fears becoming musically useless;
- why Nozomi’s departure represented proof of insignificance;
- and why she hates herself for dependence.

An audiovisual adaptation may externalize this through silence, blocking, sound design, and bodily reaction. The question will be how much of the explicit psychological structure survives.

## 2. The incomplete reconciliation

The novel does **not** establish equal attachment between Mizore and Nozomi. Kumiko explicitly recognizes that Nozomi may never understand Mizore’s full emotional investment.

Any adaptation that presents the reconciliation as total mutual understanding would simplify the source.

## 3. Yuuko’s centrality

Yuuko, not Kumiko or Nozomi, performs the decisive emotional intervention.

Her argument about later friendships, competition joy, and permission to celebrate success is essential. Reducing her to comic hostility would materially weaken the arc.

## 4. Natsuki’s admiration for Nozomi

Natsuki’s backstory explains:

- why she joined;
- why she assists Nozomi;
- and why her casualness conceals guilt.

This is important for making her more than Yuuko’s comic opponent.

## 5. Asuka’s utilitarianism

The adaptation should preserve that Asuka blocks Nozomi primarily to protect Mizore’s competitive functionality.

She is not simply mysterious or teasing. Her social intelligence is tied to a willingness to treat people as ensemble variables.

## 6. Kumiko’s morally unattractive thoughts

Her wish that the rival school fail is crucial. It turns competition from abstract inspiration into ethically crowded desire.

The anime must decide whether to voice this explicitly or reveal it through reaction.

## 7. Reina’s insecurity

Reina’s fear surrounding Niiyama and Taki reveals a less invulnerable private voice. It also strengthens Kumiko’s role as her emotional confidante.

## 8. Taki’s wife and emotional return

The timing and framing of this disclosure matter. In the novel, Hashimoto interprets Kitauji as part of Taki’s reentry into music and life.

## 9. The adult instructors

Hashimoto’s expression critique and Niiyama’s apology are both central to the novel’s theory of artistic education. They should not be treated as disposable training-camp material.

## 10. Kitauji’s miracle versus powerhouse stability

The novel distinguishes an exceptional one-time performance from the repeatable excellence of an established institution. An adaptation’s sound production could either preserve or blur that distinction.

# 25. Provisional questions for later volumes

## Asuka

What does she actually want?

Why does she create time to practice at four in the morning?  
What is the unnamed piece?  
Why does she insist upon explaining attachment in instrumental terms?  
What would happen if the ensemble’s needs conflicted with her own?

Volume 2 makes her the largest unresolved character.

## Mamiko and abandoned music

Kumiko’s older sister dismisses band as useless for university and adulthood. Her attitude suggests another version of the series’ central question:

> What happens when music once mattered but no longer fits the life one is expected to construct?

Her presence may become a warning or future mirror for Kumiko.

## Mizore beyond Nozomi

The solo breakthrough is real, but it remains addressed primarily to Nozomi.

Can Mizore develop a relationship with the oboe that survives independently of one listener?  
Can Yuuko’s friendship become something she values without ranking it against Nozomi?  
Can she preserve intensity without returning to total dependence?

## Nozomi’s restored membership

Returning to the band does not erase the previous year.

How will Nozomi live beside students who stayed?  
Will her charisma again generate conflict?  
Can she accept supporting rather than leading?

## Kitauji after the miracle

The national berth is extraordinary. Can the band reproduce its performance?

The difference between rare collective transcendence and institutional excellence remains enormous.

## Kumiko’s ambition

Kumiko now wants victory strongly enough to wish for rivals’ failure.

Can she develop an artistic ethic that accommodates ambition without dehumanizing competitors?

## Reina and Taki

Reina has learned that Niiyama is not Taki’s girlfriend, but she does not yet know the full history of his wife through direct relationship with him.

How will her idealized love respond to the adult complexity of grief?

# Final assessment

Volume 2 is a substantial deepening of *Sound! Euphonium*.

Volume 1 built a compelling ensemble and gave Kumiko a clean emotional movement from detachment toward desire. Volume 2 takes the potentially simple triumph of that conclusion and subjects it to historical, relational, and ethical pressure.

Its strongest achievement is that it refuses easy villains.

Nozomi hurts Mizore without intending to.  
Mizore’s dependence is painful without being contemptible.  
Yuuko is abrasive because she cares enough to act.  
Natsuki’s passivity contains admiration and guilt.  
Asuka’s decision is rational and emotionally dehumanizing at once.  
Taki’s meritocracy is preferable to the old system but cannot repair the past.  
Kumiko’s ambition is admirable until it requires another girl’s failure.  
Competition is cruel, motivating, subjective, exhilarating, and socially meaningful at the same time.

The Mizore–Nozomi resolution is especially strong because the novel does not manufacture reciprocity that was never there. Nozomi cared. Mizore mattered. But the two girls did not experience each other with equal intensity.

Mizore survives that truth not by ceasing to love Nozomi, but by discovering that Nozomi is not the only relationship through which she exists.

That is the volume’s deepest emotional answer:

> **The first person who gives you a world does not have to remain your entire world.  
> A later bond is not lesser because it began after the wound.  
> And music can carry an unequal feeling honestly even when ordinary language cannot resolve it.**

The final competition victory does not settle the novel’s philosophical argument. Mizore likes competition “just now” because this particular contest has given her connection, expression, recognition, and continuation.

The same institution once meant abandonment.

That contradiction is not a flaw in the ending. It is the point.

Volume 2’s “hottest summer” is therefore not only the summer in which Kitauji reaches nationals. It is the summer in which the characters learn that wanting to be chosen—by a friend, a teacher, an audience, or a panel of judges—can make them more alive and more vulnerable at exactly the same time.
