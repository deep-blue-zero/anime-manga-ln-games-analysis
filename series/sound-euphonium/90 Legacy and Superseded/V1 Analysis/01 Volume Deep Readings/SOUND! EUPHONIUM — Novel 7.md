# Executive thesis

Volume 6 asked whether a formally fair audition system was enough to make the people inside it feel safe.

Volume 7 asks a still more difficult question:

> **What happens when improvement is real, effort is sincere, the performance succeeds—and someone else is still better?**

Kitauji plays better than it did during its national-qualifying year.

It receives gold at Kansai.

It does not advance.

The result is not produced by obvious catastrophe. No one commits an unforgivable error. The ensemble does not collapse. Mizore’s oboe reaches an astonishing level. Nozomi succeeds in supporting it. Kumiko performs her solo. The students give what they recognize as their best performance.

They still lose their place at nationals.

At the interpersonal level, the same structure governs Mizore and Nozomi.

Mizore has become better than Nozomi imagined possible.

Her musical growth does not bring the two closer automatically. It forces Nozomi to confront:

- inferiority;
- envy;
- shame;
- and the possibility that the person who once followed her has become capable of flying somewhere she cannot follow.

The volume’s central reversal is therefore:

> **Nozomi thought she was the blue bird who left Mizore behind.  
> She discovers that Mizore is the blue bird—and that she may be the one standing on the ground.**

The first half of the second movement centered upon Kanade’s fear that superior ability would make her socially hated.

The second half turns that structure around.

Nozomi loves music more consciously, has fought harder to remain part of an ensemble, and considers herself the person who brought Mizore into music.

Yet Mizore possesses the greater talent.

Nozomi’s pain asks:

> Why should the person who appears to need music less be capable of going further?

This does not make Nozomi cruel or false.

It reveals that admiration is rarely pure.

To admire someone can mean:

- wanting her to succeed;
- wanting to remain necessary to her;
- wanting to share her level;
- and fearing the moment her success proves that she no longer needs you.

Volume 7’s emotional achievement is not perfect mutual understanding.

Mizore tells Nozomi directly:

> 「私、希美のこと、大好き」  
> I love you, Nozomi.

Nozomi answers:

> 「私も、みぞれのオーボエ大好き」  
> I love your oboe too.

The reply is affectionate and unmistakably asymmetrical.

The novel does not manufacture identical feelings merely because symmetry would be comforting.

Instead, it gives Mizore a more durable transformation:

> 「希美がいなくても、私、オーボエを続ける。音楽は、希美が私にくれたものだから」  
> Even without Nozomi, I will continue playing oboe. Music is something Nozomi gave me.

Mizore does not stop loving Nozomi.

She stops requiring Nozomi’s continued physical presence for the gift to retain value.

The volume’s deepest principle can therefore be stated as:

> **Maturity is not ceasing to need other people.  
> It is allowing what they gave you to continue even when they cannot remain beside you in the same form.**

That principle governs:

- Mizore and Nozomi;
- the retiring third-years and Kitauji;
- Yuuko and the presidency;
- Natsuki and her euphonium juniors;
- Tomoe and the performing ensemble;
- Kumiko and Shuuichi;
- and the institutional inheritance that becomes “Team Oumae.”

# 1. Volume 7 as the completion of the second movement

The two halves of the second-year arc form a single developmental argument.

## Volume 6: forming the ensemble

Part One concerns selection.

It asks:

- Who deserves an A-ensemble place?
- Can a first-year defeat a senior without becoming socially hated?
- What counts as effort?
- Can Kitauji distinguish musical judgment from personal affection?
- Can Kumiko recognize what a junior needs before trying to solve the visible conflict?

Kanade’s intentional failure becomes the climax because she does not trust Kitauji to live ethically with a fair result.

The volume ends by creating the ensemble:

- three euphoniums;
- a stronger low-brass section;
- new first-year talent;
- and fifty-five selected players.

## Volume 7: living with the ensemble’s limits

Part Two asks what the selected ensemble can actually become.

The questions shift:

- Can Mizore reveal her full sound without destroying the balance around her?
- Can Nozomi respond when the person she believed she supported surpasses her?
- Can Yuuko distinguish humane leadership from avoidance of necessary pressure?
- Can Yume perform before people whose reactions she can see?
- Can Kitauji accept that being better than last year does not guarantee the same result?
- Can seniors leave without requiring their successors to remain copies of them?

Volume 6 establishes procedural fairness.

Volume 7 exposes relative reality.

A fair audition can select Kitauji’s best ensemble.

It cannot guarantee that Kitauji’s best is among Kansai’s three national representatives.

# 2. Narrative architecture

## Prologue: Nozomi’s private aversion

The prologue enters Nozomi’s consciousness.

This is crucial because previous volumes largely present her through:

- Mizore’s devotion;
- Natsuki’s admiration;
- Yuuko’s jealousy;
- Kumiko’s observation;
- and Nozomi’s socially fluent public voice.

Internally, Nozomi is less uncomplicated.

When Mizore’s gaze settles upon her, Nozomi experiences something like an instinctive, difficult-to-name aversion:

> as though an inner surface had been scrubbed too harshly.

This does not mean she hates Mizore.

It means Mizore’s attention is heavy.

Nozomi senses that she is being looked at with a degree of emotional significance she cannot comfortably return.

She enjoys:

- Mizore’s company;
- their shared solo;
- being important to her;
- and the idea that she alone can support Mizore’s oboe.

She does not want the full responsibility contained in Mizore’s gaze.

## Nozomi’s first interpretation of the fairy tale

Nozomi sees herself as the blue bird.

She previously left Kitauji while Mizore remained. In her interpretation:

- Mizore is Liz;
- Nozomi is the bird who departed;
- and the tragedy can be softened because a free bird can return occasionally.

She believes separation need not be absolute.

This interpretation is sincere and self-protective.

It lets Nozomi imagine that she can preserve:

- freedom;
- affection;
- and relational significance

without being permanently bound to Mizore’s emotional needs.

She does not yet consider that the person with the greater capacity to fly might be Mizore.

---

## Chapter One: 「憧憬にアウフタクト」

An **Auftakt** is an upbeat or pickup—the note or notes that occur before the first full measure.

Admiration functions as the pickup to the entire volume.

People move because they admire:

- juniors admire national-level Kitauji;
- Kumiko admires her seniors;
- Kanade admires and tests Kumiko;
- Motomu admires Midori;
- Mizore admires Nozomi;
- Nozomi once enjoyed being admired by Mizore;
- and Kitauji looks toward the established national powers.

Admiration gives movement its first impulse.

It does not determine where the phrase will resolve.

The chapter follows Kitauji through the Kyoto competition.

The institution’s transformation is immediately visible:

- Kanade and Natsuki now rehearse together productively;
- Mirei is accepted as “Micchan”;
- the low-brass ensemble has stabilized;
- Kumiko holds a short euphonium solo in the required piece;
- both the A and B ensembles receive gold;
- and the A ensemble becomes a Kyoto representative.

The chapter also introduces Ryūsei as a rising competitive force.

Ryūsei resembles the previous year’s Kitauji:

- historically underestimated;
- newly energized by an exceptional conductor;
- and treated as an exciting disruption of the established hierarchy.

Kitauji has moved from underdog to incumbent without fully realizing it.

The school that once embodied unexpected ascent must now watch another school claim the same story.

---

## Chapter Two: 「独白とタチェット」

A **monologue** is spoken interiority.

**Tacet** instructs a player to remain silent.

The title captures the chapter’s governing contradiction:

> Everyone possesses an internal monologue.  
> The most consequential parts remain unspoken.

Nozomi speaks to Kumiko about:

- uncertainty over music university;
- whether she truly wants a professional career;
- whether she loves flute itself or the collective work of ensemble;
- the expense of private music education;
- and the possibility of attending an ordinary university with Natsuki and the others.

She has not said these things to Mizore.

Mizore continues:

- piano preparation;
- music theory;
- private oboe lessons;
- and application planning

under the belief that Nozomi is pursuing the same school.

Nozomi’s silence has become an active force in Mizore’s future.

## The ethics of an uncorrected statement

Nozomi did not invent every part of her original music-university claim.

At one point she considered it.

But she continued allowing the statement to function after her certainty disappeared.

This turns temporary aspiration into relational deception.

The lie is not a single false sentence.

It is a silence maintained because correcting the misunderstanding has become increasingly difficult.

## Love as unstable classification

The chapter’s conversations about romantic feeling reinforce this problem of category.

Reina tries to map relationships through something like overlapping circles:

- liking;
- love;
- friendship;
- romance;
- and exceptional attachment.

Kumiko resists the clean geometry.

Her relationships do not fit a simple chart.

She loves Shuuichi in one way.  
She is attached to Reina in another.  
She continues to experience Asuka and her seniors through inherited emotional force.

The chapter’s point is not that categories are meaningless.

It is that a label can fail to capture the total relational experience.

This prepares the reader for Mizore’s confession and Nozomi’s incomplete reply.

## Chosen difficulty

Mamiko offers another relevant insight.

A difficult life is not necessarily an unhappy life.

A person may choose a hard path because the path is hers.

That difficulty differs from pain imposed by someone else’s decision.

This distinction governs:

- music university;
- professional ambition;
- club leadership;
- performance pressure;
- and Kumiko’s later decision to suspend her relationship with Shuuichi.

---

## Chapter Three: 「彼女のソノリテ」

**Sonorité** refers to the distinctive character, quality, or resonance of a sound.

The possessive “her” is deliberately unstable.

It can refer to:

- Mizore’s newly revealed sound;
- Nozomi’s expressive flute;
- the particular sonority formed between them;
- or the individual voice that cannot be reduced to technique.

This chapter makes musical sound the medium through which hidden relationship becomes undeniable.

---

## Chapter Four: 「未来へフェルマータ」

A **fermata** asks the performer to hold a note or rest beyond its ordinary duration.

The fourth chapter holds the second year at the point of ending.

Kitauji’s competitive season has stopped before nationals.

The result is prolonged through:

- grief;
- self-examination;
- the final all-member performance;
- third-year retirement;
- leadership selection;
- and the suspension of Kumiko and Shuuichi’s romance.

A fermata is not necessarily the end.

It delays the next entrance.

Volume 7 closes with several relationships and institutions held in this state:

- Mizore and Nozomi will follow different possible futures.
- Yuuko and Natsuki have finished leading but remain emotionally present.
- Kumiko and Shuuichi are no longer a couple but have not erased the possibility of returning.
- The national-gold objective has failed for one generation and passes to the next.
- Kitauji pauses before its final movement under Kumiko.

---

## Epilogue: Team Oumae

The epilogue announces:

- Kumiko as president;
- Shuuichi as vice-president;
- Reina as independently appointed drum major.

Leadership is now explicitly distributed among three people.

The new structure responds to the lessons of:

- Asuka’s overcentralization;
- Yuuko’s overwork;
- the growing size of the band;
- and the need to separate organizational, interpersonal, and musical authority.

The second movement ends where the third must begin:

> Kumiko is no longer the person observing Kitauji’s leaders.

She is the person at the lectern.

# 3. Mizore and Nozomi: the relationship after reconciliation

Volume 2 repaired a misunderstanding.

It did not equalize the relationship.

Volume 7 makes that distinction unavoidable.

## What Volume 2 resolved

Mizore learned that Nozomi had not excluded her from the resignation movement because she considered her worthless.

Nozomi had believed that asking Mizore to abandon serious practice would be disrespectful.

Mizore received proof that she had mattered.

## What remained unresolved

Mizore still constructed:

- music;
- future;
- school choice;
- and emotional security

around Nozomi.

Nozomi still enjoyed being significant without understanding the total burden of that significance.

The two returned to friendship while continuing to inhabit different versions of the relationship.

## Nozomi’s discomfort with being worshipped

Nozomi’s prologue reaction clarifies that Mizore’s devotion is not merely flattering.

It can feel invasive.

To be another person’s entire world is a form of power.

It is also pressure.

Nozomi senses that ordinary choices may become catastrophic inside Mizore’s interpretation.

She therefore continues using:

- brightness;
- humor;
- social expansion;
- and partial silence

to prevent the relationship from becoming too heavy.

Mizore experiences those same behaviors as evidence that Nozomi may disappear.

# 4. The original mismatch in their playing

At camp, Hashimoto and Niiyama describe their duet as musically cramped.

## Mizore’s initial sound

Mizore plays with extraordinary accuracy.

Her solo resembles a perfect reference recording:

- repeatable;
- technically complete;
- controlled;
- and emotionally predetermined.

She can execute every note.

She does not yet produce an interpretation that unmistakably belongs to her.

## Nozomi’s initial sound

Nozomi’s flute is more individually expressive.

She plays with:

- movement;
- emotion;
- spontaneity;
- and a recognizable personality.

Her weakness is the inverse of Mizore’s.

She sometimes becomes so expressive that she stops listening to the oboe she is supposed to support.

## Water and oil

Hashimoto describes the two as something like incompatible substances.

Mizore is too fixed.

Nozomi is too self-directed.

The musical problem reproduces the emotional one:

- Mizore adjusts herself around Nozomi.
- Nozomi assumes her natural movement can be accommodated.
- Neither fully listens to the other as an independent musical subject.

---

# 5. Reina’s accusation: humane compromise or loss of hunger?

Reina challenges both the ensemble and Mizore.

She believes Kitauji is accumulating small compromises.

Her central example is Yume.

Taki initially assigns Yume to play first trumpet beside Reina. Yume cannot tolerate the pressure and asks to move to third. Yuuko permits the change.

Reina understands the organizational logic:

- a terrified Yume may not perform at her actual level;
- prolonged pressure may break her;
- and a stable ensemble requires attention to psychological capacity.

She still feels that something has been lost.

## Reina’s fear

The first-year ensemble became stronger through open conflict:

- Reina defeating Kaori;
- merit defeating seniority;
- and everyone recognizing that comfort would not determine placement.

The second-year ensemble is friendlier.

Reina worries that friendliness has become a brake.

## Her accusation toward Mizore

Reina tells Mizore that she is holding back.

She believes Mizore does not trust Nozomi to follow her full sound.

Mizore corrects the precise diagnosis:

> It is not simply that she distrusts Nozomi’s playing.

She cannot emotionally become Liz.

She cannot release the blue bird.

The issue is not lack of artistic thought.

It is that the required emotion contradicts the relationship she wants.

## The limits of Reina’s model

Reina is right that Mizore possesses more sound than she is revealing.

She is less able to accept that forcing expression before a person possesses a livable emotional framework can become destructive.

Her philosophy privileges:

- truth;
- excellence;
- and confrontation.

Yuuko’s leadership privileges:

- continuity;
- stability;
- and protection.

The novel does not fully endorse either extreme.

Their conflict becomes one of Kitauji’s governing tensions.

---

# 6. Niiyama’s intervention: changing the role rather than forcing the feeling

Mizore tells Niiyama that she cannot understand Liz.

She would not release the person she loved.

Niiyama does not shame her.

She first validates the attachment.

## More than one kind of special person

Niiyama explains that a life’s special person need not belong to one category.

A person may experience formative attachment toward:

- family;
- friend;
- lover;
- teacher;
- or another figure

without every intensity becoming identical.

This is one of the series’ clearest statements about relational plurality.

It supports the work’s refusal to reduce every emotionally intense bond to either:

- conventional romance;
- or ordinary friendship.

## Reversing the interpretive viewpoint

Niiyama asks Mizore to stop imagining herself as Liz.

What would the blue bird feel?

Mizore can answer.

The blue bird:

- does not want to leave;
- is hurt by Liz’s decision;
- and nevertheless accepts it because Liz’s wish matters.

Mizore understands this form of love.

She may be unable to release Nozomi voluntarily.

She can imagine accepting Nozomi’s chosen departure because Nozomi’s happiness matters more than possession.

## The blue bird’s agency

The reversal is important because it prevents Mizore’s liberation from being interpreted as abandonment by a wiser protector.

The blue bird also chooses.

She flies because:

- the cage is open;
- the person she loves has asked;
- and she possesses a world beyond the house.

Mizore begins to imagine herself as the person with wings.

# 7. Mizore’s full sonorité

Niiyama tells Mizore to play at full power without adjusting to the ensemble.

The result is overwhelming.

## Technical accuracy remains intact

Mizore does not violate:

- tempo;
- notes;
- rhythm;
- or written instruction.

Her performance remains faithful to the score.

Yet its emotional force completely alters the ensemble around it.

## The ensemble collapses

The other musicians cannot follow.

They listen instead of playing.

Nozomi stops producing sound and begins crying.

Accompanimental balance disintegrates.

Mizore continues, apparently unaware that the ensemble has fallen apart.

## Artistic awakening is not automatic ensemble success

This is a crucial complication.

Mizore has finally produced unmistakably personal sound.

The first result is musical destruction.

Self-expression alone is not enough for ensemble art.

The individual voice must later be integrated with:

- other players;
- balance;
- timing;
- and shared structure.

The band must become capable of responding to Mizore rather than requiring her to remain small enough for its old balance.

## The adults’ response

Hashimoto recognizes that something extraordinary has been uncovered.

Niiyama praises Mizore.

Taki immediately begins revising the ensemble around the new reality.

The correct response is not:

> Return to the safer, flatter version.

It is:

> Rebuild the collective form so that the stronger individual truth can exist inside it.

This is one of the series’ most sophisticated models of institutional growth.

---

# 8. Nozomi hears the distance

Nozomi’s tears do not arise from aesthetic beauty alone.

She has heard the difference between them.

## Love, effort, and entitlement

Nozomi admits that she always knew Mizore was talented.

She resisted the full implication.

Nozomi believes:

- she consciously loves music more;
- she has suffered more for ensemble;
- she fought the old Kitauji;
- she quit and struggled to return;
- and music occupies a more articulated place in her identity.

Part of her therefore wanted to believe she should be the better musician.

Mizore practices almost as naturally as breathing.

Her exceptional ability does not appear to come with Nozomi’s visible ideological struggle.

This feels unfair.

## The Volume 6 effort problem reversed

Kanade feared that visible effort would be rewarded over superior ability.

Nozomi represents the person whose visible passion and struggle do not produce the superior result.

She asks, implicitly:

> If I love this more consciously, why is she the one selected by talent?

Neither volume claims that effort is irrelevant.

They reject the conversion of effort into entitlement.

## Niiyama’s invitation

When Niiyama gives Mizore music-university material without first giving the same material to Nozomi, Nozomi interprets the gesture correctly.

Niiyama has recognized something exceptional in Mizore.

Nozomi has not received the same unsolicited recognition.

The pain is not that Niiyama would refuse to help her.

Nozomi knows she could ask.

The pain is that Mizore was noticed without asking.

She was selected by another musician’s ear.

---

# 9. Nozomi’s music-university claim

Nozomi eventually admits that her claim about music university was partly an attempt to preserve equality.

She saw Mizore’s brochure.

She realized Mizore had been recognized as someone with professional possibility.

She then said she would also attend music university.

## Symbolic equality

Nozomi does not literally believe that applying to music school proves equal skill.

Emotionally, the shared destination would allow her to preserve the idea that they stand on the same plane.

If both are music-university candidates, Mizore has not entered a category unavailable to her.

## The lie grows through inertia

Nozomi later questions whether she wants professional music.

She may prefer:

- ensemble;
- collective effort;
- and flute as a lifelong activity

without converting performance into occupation.

These are legitimate possibilities.

The ethical failure lies in continuing to let Mizore organize her life around the old statement.

## Kumiko’s response

Kumiko does not absolve Nozomi.

She says that lying to Mizore was cruel.

She also says she cannot hate her.

This is an important maturation of Kumiko’s ethical voice:

> Understanding a motive does not require declaring the action harmless.  
> Condemning the action does not require abandoning the person.

Nozomi wanted punishment because self-condemnation had become another form of control.

Kumiko refuses both:

- easy comfort;
- and total rejection.

---

# 10. Why Natsuki sends Kumiko

Natsuki asks Kumiko to follow Nozomi after the disastrous rehearsal.

She does not go herself because she knows she would be too gentle.

Yuuko is similarly partial toward Mizore.

This reveals how well Natsuki understands the emotional field.

## Affection can obstruct truth

Natsuki loves Nozomi as:

- former inspiration;
- friend;
- and object of guilt.

She cannot provide the judgment Nozomi is seeking.

Kumiko occupies another position.

She cares, but not with the same history.

## Kumiko as an ethical listener

Kumiko has become capable of holding several truths:

- Mizore’s sound is extraordinary.
- Nozomi’s inferiority hurts.
- Nozomi’s envy is ugly.
- Envy does not erase affection.
- The lie was wrong.
- Nozomi is not reducible to the lie.

This is the mature form of the Oumae Consultation Office.

Kumiko is not merely good at discovering secrets.

She is learning to respond without converting a complicated person into one final moral category.

---

# 11. Nozomi’s new relationship to Mizore’s sound

Nozomi concludes that she must support Mizore perfectly.

She describes this as the only way to stand as Mizore’s equal.

The statement contains several motives.

## Musical humility

Nozomi accepts that the oboe is the primary voice.

She must listen rather than dominate.

## Competitive pride

She refuses to become irrelevant inside Mizore’s greatest performance.

If she cannot equal the oboe’s technical or emotional power, she will become the flute player capable of responding to it.

## Affection

Whatever else she feels, she wants Mizore’s solo to succeed.

## Resistance to inferiority

Supporting Mizore is also Nozomi’s way of refusing passive defeat.

Her role remains real.

The duet becomes the place where envy can be transformed into disciplined listening.

---

# 12. The roles reverse

Nozomi finally says:

> Mizore was the blue bird.

The realization changes the fairy-tale mapping.

## Nozomi as Liz

Nozomi gave Mizore an initial home in music.

She:

- approached the isolated girl;
- invited her into band;
- and created a shared world.

But the person brought into that world possesses wings Nozomi did not anticipate.

## Mizore as the blue bird

Mizore’s talent contains possibility beyond:

- Kitauji;
- Nozomi;
- and their original friendship.

Niiyama sees:

- conservatory;
- elite instruction;
- and professional potential.

The cage is not only Mizore’s dependence.

It is also Nozomi’s need to remain the person who defines Mizore’s musical world.

## Release from both sides

Mizore must allow Nozomi to choose another life.

Nozomi must allow Mizore’s talent to exceed the relationship through which it began.

The fairy tale no longer assigns one mature liberator and one passive creature.

Both girls must release a version of the other.

---

# 13. The confrontation before Kansai

Mizore finally speaks without waiting to be invited.

This is the interpersonal culmination of the volume.

## “For me, it is still now”

When Nozomi treats the old resignation as distant history, Mizore answers:

> 「昔じゃない。私にとっては、ずっといま」  
> It is not the past. For me, it has always been now.

Trauma has not moved at calendar speed.

Nozomi experienced:

- resignation;
- return;
- and reconciliation

as sequential events.

Mizore continued inhabiting the moment of disappearance.

## Mizore names the totality

She says she:

- followed Nozomi;
- continued oboe because of Nozomi;
- practiced to avoid being abandoned;
- and wanted to reach nationals with her because of their old promise.

Nozomi reacts angrily.

She hears Mizore reducing years of remarkable musical labor to one person.

## “The oboe is not light. Nozomi is too heavy.”

Mizore’s answer is one of the volume’s defining lines:

> 「オーボエが軽いんじゃない。希美が、重すぎるの。私にとって」  
> The oboe is not light. You are simply too heavy, Nozomi—for me.

Mizore is not saying music means nothing.

She is explaining the scale at which Nozomi has organized its meaning.

This is both love and pathology.

The line does not romanticize the weight.

It finally makes it visible.

---

# 14. The “I love you” and the unequal answer

Mizore initiates the “big love hug” she previously watched from the margins.

This is major growth.

She no longer waits to be selected.

She opens her arms.

## Mizore’s declaration

She thanks Nozomi for:

- speaking to her;
- becoming her friend;
- giving her music;
- and drawing her into a life that had previously felt empty.

Then she says:

> 「私、希美のこと、大好き」  
> I love you, Nozomi.

The phrase can exist within friendship, romantic feeling, dependency, or several categories at once.

The text does not force one classification.

Its emotional force is unambiguous.

## Nozomi’s pause

Nozomi does not answer immediately.

The silence matters.

She then says:

> 「私も、みぞれのオーボエ大好き」  
> I love your oboe too.

She does not say:

> I love you too.

## Honest boundary or emotional evasion

The reply can be understood in more than one way.

It may be:

- the most honest affection Nozomi can offer;
- an attempt to affirm what she genuinely admires;
- an inability to match Mizore’s intensity;
- or a retreat from the category Mizore has opened.

These possibilities are not mutually exclusive.

The novel’s restraint is crucial.

Nozomi is not transformed into the reciprocal answer Mizore wants merely because the story has reached an emotional climax.

## What Mizore accepts

Mizore does not demand another formulation.

She nods.

The relationship can continue without pretending that the girls occupy the same emotional position.

This is more mature than false symmetry, but it remains painful.

---

# 15. “Music is what Nozomi gave me”

After the competition, Mizore tells Kumiko she will continue playing oboe even if Nozomi is absent.

This is the resolution of her second-year arc.

## Not independence through denial

Mizore does not claim:

- Nozomi never mattered;
- she played only for herself;
- or her attachment was childish and false.

She preserves the origin.

## Gift instead of chain

Music becomes something received rather than something requiring continuous repayment through proximity.

Nozomi gave Mizore the doorway.

Mizore can now walk beyond the doorway without declaring the gift invalid.

## Inheritance as the series’ mature relational form

This parallels:

- Shindō giving Asuka the euphonium;
- Asuka giving Kumiko the notebook;
- Mamiko inspiring Kumiko to begin music;
- Taki inheriting his wife’s dream;
- and the third-years giving Kitauji their accumulated experience.

The person may leave.

The sound continues.

---

# 16. Kitauji at Kansai: a successful performance that loses

Kitauji’s Kansai performance is not narrated as collapse.

It is genuinely strong.

## Mizore’s integrated sound

The oboe retains the force discovered at camp.

At Kansai, however, the surrounding ensemble can respond.

Nozomi’s flute enters.

The balance no longer disintegrates.

Mizore’s individual sonorité has been absorbed into a revised collective sound.

## Kumiko’s solo and ensemble role

Kumiko performs her required-piece solo under national-level expectation.

She is no longer the hesitant first-year hidden in inner harmony.

Her sound occupies an identified public position.

## The final movement

The ensemble follows the blue bird into the open sky.

The music reaches its intended conclusion:

- release;
- scale;
- freedom;
- and residual sorrow.

The performance is not invalidated by the later result.

## Gold without advancement

Kitauji receives gold.

The national representatives are:

- Meisei Technical High School;
- Shūtō University Affiliated High School;
- Ryūsei Academy Senior High School.

Kitauji is not selected.

Neither is Osaka Tōshō, one of the assumed perennial powers.

The result destabilizes the whole regional hierarchy.

---

# 17. Ryūsei as Kitauji’s mirror

Ryūsei occupied the position Kitauji held one year earlier.

It was:

- underestimated;
- newly transformed;
- guided by a renowned conductor;
- and treated as a dark horse.

Its national selection demonstrates that Kitauji’s story was never exclusive property.

## The former underdog becomes the establishment

Kitauji’s students unconsciously assume:

- they went last year;
- they sound better this year;
- therefore they should return.

Ryūsei breaks the implied progression.

Improvement is relative.

Other institutions are moving too.

## No permanent narrative privilege

Kitauji cannot claim nationals because its journey has been emotionally compelling.

Every school contains:

- effort;
- sacrifice;
- talent;
- and its own internal story.

The judges hear sound, not narrative deservingness.

## Genichirō Tsukinaga

The celebrated former Meisei conductor now associated with Ryūsei bears Motomu’s surname.

A Ryūsei student also attempts to reconnect Motomu with the school and its people.

The precise family relationship remains unrevealed in this volume, but the connection is clearly significant.

Midori does not force disclosure.

She accepts Motomu’s statement that the matter is not his current identity.

This is another mature version of care:

> Respecting someone does not always mean obtaining the complete explanation.

---

# 18. The result and Kumiko’s delayed grief

Reina immediately breaks down.

She says:

> 「悔しくって、死にそう」  
> I am so frustrated I feel like I could die.

Kumiko embraces her.

Kumiko herself cannot cry yet.

## Different tempos of disappointment

Reina’s emotion remains immediate and declarative.

Kumiko’s arrives later, in private, beside Shuuichi.

This difference reflects their established voices:

- Reina experiences and states.
- Kumiko observes, contains, and later understands what she contained.

## Kumiko’s question

She asks why effort was not enough.

The seniors:

- practiced;
- improved;
- played well;
- and did nothing obviously wrong.

Why did they not receive the desired continuation?

The answer is not moral.

They were not punished.

Other ensembles received higher evaluation.

This is competition’s hardest truth:

> Failure does not always identify an error.

---

# 19. Shuuichi’s comfort—and why it frightens Kumiko

Shuuichi tells Kumiko:

- the seniors were not wrong;
- Kitauji was excellent;
- his mother thought it was the best performance;
- and an adverse result does not erase what they did.

Kumiko finally cries.

His kindness gives her permission to stop holding herself upright.

## Comfort as safety

Shuuichi is one of the few people before whom Kumiko can collapse without needing to explain the full structure first.

Their ordinary familiarity makes the comfort possible.

## Comfort as danger

Kumiko also fears it.

She worries that being accepted so easily will make it harder to stand again.

This fear helps explain her later decision to suspend their relationship.

She associates leadership with:

- discipline;
- concentration;
- and refusal of dependence.

The decision is self-authored.

It may also be an overcorrection.

---

# 20. Yuuko after defeat: public strength and private grief

Yuuko responds to the loss in two distinct registers.

## Publicly

She tells the band:

- they gave their best performance;
- their sound was real;
- they should return proudly;
- and the result does not make their supporters’ work meaningless.

She turns the day into the first day of the next competitive year.

This is excellent leadership.

She protects the ensemble from converting disappointment into shame.

## Privately

Shuuichi later reveals that he saw Yuuko crying away from the others.

Her public strength is not emotional immunity.

She performs leadership because the group needs a form through which to survive the moment.

Unlike Asuka’s masks, Yuuko’s public performance does not deny private feeling indefinitely. It temporarily organizes it.

---

# 21. Yuuko’s diagnosis: compromise, care, and retrospective responsibility

Two weeks later, Yuuko writes **national gold** on the board and analyzes the failure.

She concludes:

- Kitauji improved;
- the judges’ comments were positive;
- other schools improved more;
- the band assumed last year’s national berth made this year’s return likely;
- and she prioritized cohesion and protection over competitive friction.

## The Yume decision

Yuuko permitted Yume to move away from the first-trumpet role Taki originally assigned.

She now wonders whether this was:

- humane leadership;
- avoidance of pressure;
- or self-satisfaction disguised as protection.

## The novel does not prove care caused the loss

This distinction is important.

There is no objective evidence that forcing Yume into the first part would have sent Kitauji to nationals.

A frightened player might have performed worse.

Yuuko’s interpretation is a leader’s retrospective attempt to convert defeat into actionable cause.

It should not be treated as the narrator’s final verdict that kindness weakened the band.

## The real institutional question

The issue is whether Kitauji can distinguish:

- accommodation that enables a player’s best;
- from accommodation that permits fear to govern permanently.

Yume’s later solo provides a better answer than either simple force or indefinite retreat.

---

# 22. Yume and Tomoe: choosing one visible listener

Yume’s performance arc is one of the volume’s most successful secondary stories.

She possesses ability.

Her body fails under observation.

## The glasses strategy

Tomoe initially advises Yume to remove her glasses while playing.

If she cannot see individual faces, she feels less judged.

The strategy allows partial functioning by reducing the audience to abstraction.

It does not resolve the fear.

## The “Tequila” solo

Yuuko assigns Yume a public solo.

Yume repeatedly fails.

She asks to be replaced.

The surrounding pity makes the problem worse:

- perhaps the role is cruel;
- perhaps Reina should take it;
- perhaps protecting Yume means removing her.

This reproduces the institutional question Yuuko is trying to solve.

## Tomoe’s speech

Tomoe first addresses the whole ensemble.

She says she wanted to congratulate them after Kansai.

She believed their gold was extraordinary.

She remained silent because everyone was staring at the national berth they had missed.

As manager and sole rehearsal audience, Tomoe occupies a different evaluative position.

She hears:

- the eighty-eight performers;
- their growth;
- and the privilege of being close to their sound.

## “Play for me”

Tomoe then confronts Yume.

She asks:

- Has she ever abandoned Yume after a mistake?
- Has she stopped believing in her?
- Where is the audience that will reject her?

Tomoe offers herself as a concrete addressee.

Yume no longer has to perform for an abstract mass of possible disappointment.

She can play toward one trusted face.

## Putting the glasses back on

Yume asks for her glasses.

She wants to see Tomoe’s happiness.

This reverses the original strategy.

Growth is not the permanent blurring of other people.

It is the ability to look at someone and survive being looked at.

## A healthier relational address

The structure parallels Mizore’s breakthrough through Nozomi.

But Yume’s relation to Tomoe is less totalizing.

Tomoe is:

- the trusted audience for this performance;
- not the sole reason Yume’s musical life exists.

The relationship directs expression without consuming identity.

---

# 23. Tomoe’s “Bravo” and the alternative judge

At the final rehearsal, all eighty-eight players perform *Liz and the Blue Bird*.

Tomoe is the only audience member.

After the final note, she shouts:

> 「ブラボー！」  
> Bravo!

This moment offers a counterpoint to Kansai.

## The competitive judges

They grant Kitauji gold but do not grant continuation.

Their judgment is real and consequential.

## Tomoe’s judgment

Tomoe has heard the ensemble from inside its daily labor.

She knows:

- mistakes;
- improvement;
- exhaustion;
- and the people behind the sound.

Her “Bravo” does not overturn the competition result.

It recognizes a dimension the competition cannot measure.

## The whole band

A and B distinctions temporarily disappear.

The final performance includes:

- selected players;
- unselected players;
- beginners;
- seniors;
- and everyone whose work produced the year.

The third-years’ last *Liz* belongs to the institution rather than only the competition ensemble.

---

# 24. Yuuko’s reform: the ensemble contest

Yuuko decides that Kitauji will begin participating in the ensemble contest.

Her reasoning is that Kitauji already possesses:

- a capable conductor;
- adequate instruments;
- facilities;
- and collective ambition.

The next weakness is individual depth.

## Continuous competition

The ensemble contest prevents the period after summer from becoming a competitive vacuum.

Small groups must develop:

- independence;
- listening;
- exposed technique;
- and responsibility without a full band hiding weaknesses.

## A possible risk

The reform also extends competitive pressure deeper into the year.

Kitauji’s solution to losing is more structured competition.

This may produce:

- growth;
- stronger individual players;
- and a more sustainable national program.

It may also make the band increasingly unable to imagine music outside evaluation.

Volume 5’s regular concert offered music as hospitality and enjoyment.

Volume 7 responds to loss through intensified preparation.

The tension remains unresolved.

---

# 25. Natsuki’s final summer

Natsuki’s third-year arc concludes quietly but powerfully.

Before the Kansai performance, she tells Kumiko and Kanade that she wanted to resemble Asuka but never became that kind of senior.

What she did receive was juniors.

## A different senior ideal

Natsuki is not:

- the most talented euphonium player;
- the central leader;
- or the section’s unquestioned authority.

She is:

- approachable;
- honest about weakness;
- willing to learn from Kanade;
- protective without demanding worship;
- and capable of refusing an unearned place.

Her seniority is relational rather than mythic.

## Wanting more time

She says she wants to play with them longer and asks them to reach nationals together.

The wish is not fulfilled.

That failure does not make the relationship incomplete.

## Natsuki and Kanade

Kanade’s earlier fear centered upon displacing Natsuki.

By the end, they have:

- rehearsed together;
- corrected each other;
- competed honestly;
- and entered the same ensemble.

Natsuki’s retirement leaves Kanade with an inherited model of seniority that does not require hiding superiority or weakness.

---

# 26. Reina: the uncompromising ear becomes institutional authority

Reina’s role in Volume 7 prepares her appointment as drum major.

## She hears the brakes

Reina identifies:

- Mizore’s restraint;
- Nozomi’s failure to listen;
- Yume’s retreat;
- and Kitauji’s reduced competitive friction.

Her diagnosis is often musically accurate before others are willing to voice it.

## She lacks a complete pastoral method

Reina can say:

> Play fully.

She is less naturally equipped to show a frightened person how to become capable of doing so.

Tomoe reaches Yume where Reina cannot.

Niiyama reaches Mizore through interpretive redirection rather than command.

## The correct new role

Making Reina vice-president would waste her abilities on:

- mediation;
- paperwork;
- and everyday emotional coordination.

Making her an independent drum major allows her to concentrate on:

- sound;
- standards;
- musical discipline;
- and performance leadership.

The new structure recognizes that musical truth and organizational care need not reside in the same person.

---

# 27. Kumiko as the next president

Yuuko reveals that Kumiko’s selection was not impulsive.

She had been preparing Kumiko throughout the year.

## The Oumae Consultation Office as leadership training

By directing younger students toward Kumiko, Yuuko helped her build:

- trust;
- visibility;
- cross-sectional relationships;
- and influence among the next generation.

Kumiko thought she was being used as a convenient problem-solver.

She was also being trained.

## Why Kumiko

Yuuko’s criteria include:

- sufficient musical ability to retain credibility;
- the capacity to understand relationships;
- influence among juniors;
- and reliable people capable of stopping or supporting her.

Kumiko is not chosen because she resembles Yuuko, Haruka, or Asuka.

She is chosen because Kitauji now needs her particular form of leadership.

## Leadership through hearing

Kumiko’s likely strength is not command.

It is the ability to hear:

- what someone says;
- what she avoids saying;
- and the difference between the two.

The risk is that listening to everyone may make decisive leadership difficult.

Haruka and Mizore have already demonstrated that sensitivity can become self-erasure if the person never identifies her own preference.

---

# 28. Kumiko and Shuuichi: a self-authored separation

After accepting the presidency, Kumiko returns the Italian White hair ornament to Shuuichi.

She says she wants to focus exclusively on her final year and the band.

If, one year later, he still wants to be with her, she asks him to return it.

The narration states plainly:

> On that day, Kumiko and Shuuichi ceased to be lovers.

## What makes the decision admirable

Kumiko:

- states her limitation;
- accepts responsibility;
- does not blame her parents or Shuuichi;
- and chooses the difficulty she believes the presidency requires.

This differs from Mamiko’s old obedience or Asuka’s coerced surrender.

## What makes the decision troubling

Kumiko assumes:

- leadership and romance are incompatible;
- accepting comfort may weaken her;
- and the responsible choice is to remove one source of emotional dependency.

She decides unilaterally before testing whether the relationship could adapt.

This resembles a recurring *Sound! Euphonium* pattern:

> A character preemptively sacrifices something and calls the sacrifice maturity.

Kumiko once challenged Asuka for doing exactly this.

## The hair ornament as suspended promise

She does not discard the ornament.

She returns it with a possible future instruction.

The relationship enters a fermata.

Its duration is held, not completed.

## Shuuichi’s response

Shuuichi calls her difficult, then supports her.

He says:

> 「部長、頑張れよ」  
> Do your best, President.

And:

> 「俺らの代で、今度こそ全国で金取ろう」  
> In our year, let us finally win national gold.

He does not demand that she choose him over the band.

His acceptance is loving.

It also permits Kumiko’s assumption to go unchallenged.

---

# 29. The irony of Shuuichi becoming vice-president

Kumiko breaks up partly to create distance between romance and leadership.

The epilogue appoints Shuuichi as vice-president.

The separation cannot produce the clean division she imagined.

They must now:

- work together;
- make decisions together;
- manage younger students;
- and pursue the goal that prompted the breakup.

This is an elegant institutional irony.

Kumiko attempted to simplify her emotional world.

Kitauji immediately makes Shuuichi part of her governing structure.

The next phase will test whether:

- emotional maturity means removing attachment;
- or learning to work responsibly beside attachment without allowing it to control the institution.

---

# 30. Team Oumae

The final leadership structure consists of three complementary figures.

## Kumiko — president

Her strengths are:

- relational awareness;
- listening;
- mediation;
- broad trust;
- and the ability to recognize hidden motives.

Her risks are:

- indecision;
- self-doubt;
- overinvolvement;
- and sacrificing her own needs under the language of duty.

## Shuuichi — vice-president

His strengths are:

- patience;
- ordinary approachability;
- kindness;
- practical reliability;
- and a willingness to support without demanding the center.

His risks are:

- hesitation;
- excessive accommodation;
- and difficulty voicing conflict directly.

## Reina — drum major

Her strengths are:

- musical authority;
- exceptional ear;
- ambition;
- directness;
- and refusal of complacency.

Her risks are:

- severity;
- impatience with fear;
- and treating every problem as something effort should overcome.

## A distributed successor to Asuka

No single person reproduces Asuka.

Together, the three divide qualities she once concentrated:

- Kumiko reads people.
- Shuuichi stabilizes.
- Reina drives the sound.

“Team Oumae” is therefore the institutional answer to Asuka’s curse.

# 31. Care versus hunger

One of Volume 7’s most difficult questions is whether Kitauji became too gentle.

The novel does not offer a simple answer.

## The case for care

Yuuko prevented:

- frightened juniors from collapsing;
- conflicts from consuming the ensemble;
- and the second-year band from repeating the previous generation’s cruelty.

The band became socially healthier.

Kanade could be selected without being ostracized.

Mirei could defeat Hazuki without losing her friendship.

## The case for hunger

Reina believes some players were never forced to discover their full capacity.

Yume retreated from first trumpet.

Mizore held back.

The ensemble assumed its existing improvement would be enough.

## The synthesis

The later Yume story suggests the necessary model:

> Do not force a frightened person into exposure without support.  
> Do not let support become permanent escape.

Tomoe gives Yume:

- safety;
- a concrete listener;
- and a reason to risk visibility.

Yume then performs rather than being replaced.

The solution is not cruelty.

It is care capable of demanding action.

---

# 32. Effort and result after Volume 6

Volume 6 asked how effort should be recognized inside auditions.

Volume 7 scales the problem up to whole institutions.

Kitauji worked hard.

Ryūsei also worked hard.

Meisei and Shūtō worked hard.

Osaka Tōshō worked hard and still failed to advance.

Effort cannot determine a unique winner when all competitors possess it.

## Effort as cause, not entitlement

Practice may:

- increase capacity;
- deepen expression;
- and make excellence possible.

It does not create a moral debt the judges must repay.

## The emotional necessity of belief

Even knowing this, people need to believe work matters.

Yuuko’s response is to make the loss actionable:

- individual development;
- ensemble contest;
- inherited technique;
- and immediate preparation for the next year.

This protects effort from meaninglessness without pretending the result was unfair.

---

# 33. The retiring third-years and the problem of legacy

The third-year ensemble does not receive the ending it wanted.

That makes the transfer of leadership more consequential.

## Yuuko

She inherits the national-gold dream and fails to reach nationals.

She nevertheless leaves Kitauji:

- larger;
- socially more stable;
- structurally more distributed;
- and capable of diagnosing its next weakness.

## Natsuki

She reaches A ensemble, performs at Kansai, and becomes the senior she never imagined becoming.

Her legacy is present most strongly in Kanade.

## Nozomi

She finally performs the national-path duet with Mizore but must confront that Mizore’s future may exceed hers.

Her legacy is not professional instruction.

It is the gift of music itself.

## Mizore

She is preparing to continue beyond Kitauji.

Her graduation is less an ending than the beginning of an independent musical life.

## Tomoe

She does not perform at Kansai, but her managerial work and final “Bravo” define how the band understands its own achievement.

The volume refuses to rank these legacies according to stage visibility.

# 34. The chapter-title progression as psychological score

## Auftakt — admiration before the measure

Admiration begins motion.

It is:

- the junior’s first impulse;
- the follower’s orientation;
- and the underdog’s image of what she wants to become.

But an upbeat is incomplete until the full measure arrives.

Admiration must eventually become self-authored action.

## Tacet — what is not played

Nozomi’s uncertainty remains unspoken.

Mizore’s knowledge remains unspoken.

Yuuko’s fear remains partly unspoken.

Kumiko’s grief waits.

The silences alter the music as powerfully as spoken notes.

## Sonorité — the irreducible personal sound

Mizore’s full sonorité makes compromise impossible.

Nozomi must hear who Mizore has become.

The ensemble must change around the sound rather than treating the individual as infinitely adjustable.

## Fermata — holding before release

The result, retirement, confession, leadership transfer, and breakup are all held moments.

They are neither fully concluded nor immediately continued.

The future begins only after the characters remain long enough inside the ending to recognize what is being passed forward.

# 35. Character voices and speaking styles

Volume 7 derives much of its emotional force from the contrast between internal and external voice.

## Nozomi

Nozomi’s public speech remains:

- bright;
- quick;
- socially fluent;
- lightly humorous;
- and full of easy forward momentum.

Her internal voice contains:

- aversion;
- envy;
- shame;
- status comparison;
- and fear of inferiority.

The gap is not simple falseness.

Her brightness is a practiced way of remaining socially mobile.

## Mizore

Mizore’s language remains sparse and literal.

When she finally speaks at length, the simplicity makes the declaration more forceful.

She does not create elaborate justification.

She says:

- the past is still present;
- Nozomi is too heavy;
- she loves Nozomi;
- and she will continue music without her.

Each sentence is almost physically direct.

## Kumiko

Kumiko’s interior voice remains observant and occasionally caustic.

Her external speech has become more morally direct.

She can now say:

- Nozomi’s action was cruel;
- she still does not hate her;
- and understanding is not absolution.

## Yuuko

Yuuko possesses the strongest public rhetoric in the volume.

She can transform:

- despair into pride;
- guilt into reform;
- and a room of passive listeners into a group with a next action.

Her private crying prevents this rhetoric from becoming evidence of emotional simplicity.

## Natsuki

Natsuki’s casual speech creates safety.

When she becomes serious, the change carries unusual weight.

She understands where her own affection would make her an inadequate messenger and deliberately sends Kumiko instead.

## Reina

Reina’s voice remains declarative and uncompromising.

Even when apologizing, she does not retract the underlying musical judgment.

Her language treats hidden potential as something almost morally urgent.

## Yume

Yume’s speech is apologetic and self-erasing.

She repeatedly describes herself as:

- incapable;
- troublesome;
- or unworthy of the role.

When she asks for her glasses, the syntax changes.

She issues a request and names what she wants to see.

## Tomoe

Tomoe often speaks in comic, casual Kansai language.

Her public speech retains that ordinary register while becoming morally authoritative.

She does not imitate Taki or Yuuko.

Her credibility comes from having listened every day.

# 36. Relational intensity and yuri-coded material

Volume 7 contains some of the source’s most consequential same-gender relational material.

## Mizore and Nozomi

The relationship includes:

- formative rescue;
- singular attachment;
- jealousy;
- musical address;
- physical embrace;
- direct “I love you” language;
- and anxiety over a shared future.

It is strongly available to yuri interpretation.

The asymmetrical reply should not be erased.

Mizore’s declaration and Nozomi’s response are not equivalent.

## Kumiko and Reina

Their conversations continue to include:

- possessiveness;
- fear of separation;
- private musical address;
- and resistance to reducing their bond to a conventional friendship category.

The swimsuit and love-classification discussions make the question explicit without providing one final label.

## Asuka and Kaori

They attend Kansai together in coordinated formal dress.

Their post-graduation intimacy continues to be visually and socially foregrounded.

Asuka remains playful and difficult to classify; Kaori remains openly attentive.

## Yuuko and Natsuki

Their leadership partnership culminates in mutual acknowledgment.

The relationship is argumentative, physically familiar, and emotionally indispensable even when not conventionally romantic.

## Analytical caution

The source does not require all these bonds to mean the same thing.

Its recurring proposition is that adolescent and artistic intimacy often exceeds the labels available to contain it.

# 37. Visual paratext

## Original cover

The cover places:

- Mizore at left with oboe;
- Kumiko in the center holding a metronome before her face;
- Nozomi at right with flute.

The composition is an unusually precise map of the volume.

## Mizore and Nozomi occupy different axes

Mizore’s oboe is held vertically.

Nozomi’s flute extends horizontally.

Their instruments do not visually meet.

The duet requires two different orientations to form one phrase.

Kumiko sits between them without holding her euphonium.

She holds the metronome.

Throughout the volume, she mediates:

- tempo;
- information;
- emotional distance;
- and the moment when truth must finally enter.

The metronome also evokes the countdown Mizore fears.

Every beat moves the girls toward:

- competition;
- graduation;
- and possible separation.

## The covered mouth

The metronome obscures part of Kumiko’s face.

This suits a character whose role remains partly observational and whose greatest challenge is deciding when to speak.

## Contrasting expressions

Mizore looks withdrawn.

Nozomi smiles outwardly.

Kumiko appears alert and caught between them.

The cover does not present a harmoniously resolved trio.

It presents three distinct internal tempos.

## Promotional wrapper

The wrapper explicitly asks about:

- Mizore;
- Nozomi;
- and the result of the competition.

It correctly frames the two plots as one question.

The relationship’s resolution and the competitive outcome are both concerned with whether improvement guarantees continuation.

The anime advertising below still foregrounds material from the previous animated phase, visually demonstrating the franchise’s overlap between an adapted first-year story and a novel series already moving through the second year.

# 38. Adaptation baseline

The following material is especially important for a later anime comparison.

## 1. Nozomi’s prologue viewpoint

Her instinctive discomfort under Mizore’s gaze is essential.

Without it, Nozomi can appear merely oblivious rather than pressured by an attachment she cannot match.

## 2. Nozomi’s original blue-bird interpretation

She believes she can leave and return casually.

This explains the relational model Mizore experiences as intolerable.

## 3. Reina’s compromise argument

The adaptation should preserve both sides:

- Reina’s fear of lost hunger;
- and Yuuko’s rational concern for player sustainability.

## 4. Mizore’s inability to become Liz

Her restraint is not generic lack of emotion.

She cannot imagine voluntarily releasing Nozomi.

## 5. Niiyama’s relational pluralism

The statement that a special person need not belong to one emotional category is central to the work’s philosophy of attachment.

## 6. The blue-bird interpretive reversal

Mizore’s breakthrough depends upon changing roles, not forcing herself to feel as Liz would.

## 7. The first full oboe performance

The ensemble must visibly and audibly collapse around Mizore’s sound.

Her awakening is initially too powerful for the existing balance.

## 8. Nozomi’s tears and silence

She does not merely feel moved.

She cannot produce her own sound after hearing the distance between them.

## 9. Nozomi’s envy confession

The adaptation must preserve:

- resentment of Mizore’s talent;
- the belief that greater love and struggle should have made Nozomi better;
- and the music-university statement as an attempt to preserve equality.

## 10. Kumiko’s moral response

She calls the lie cruel without rejecting Nozomi as a person.

## 11. Nozomi’s recognition that Mizore is the blue bird

This is the conceptual reversal at the center of the volume.

## 12. The pre-Kansai confrontation

Key lines include:

- the past remaining present for Mizore;
- Nozomi being “too heavy”;
- the initiated hug;
- Mizore’s explicit declaration;
- and Nozomi’s narrower response about the oboe.

## 13. The performance integration

Mizore’s full sonorité must remain extraordinary while the ensemble learns to support it.

## 14. Kitauji’s non-advancing gold

The performance should sound genuinely excellent.

The result loses meaning if the adaptation implies obvious failure.

## 15. Ryūsei as mirror

Its ascent should be framed as another valid underdog transformation, not a convenient antagonist.

## 16. Yuuko’s two faces after the result

Her public speech and private crying are both essential.

## 17. Yuuko’s later self-critique

The compromise argument should remain an interpretation, not objective proof that humane leadership caused defeat.

## 18. Tomoe’s speech

Her recognition of Kansai gold provides an alternative evaluative viewpoint.

## 19. Yume requesting her glasses

The visual metaphor depends upon her choosing to see Tomoe’s face.

## 20. The all-member *Liz and the Blue Bird*

The final performance dissolves the competition ensemble’s exclusions.

## 21. Mizore’s statement about continuing oboe

This is the actual resolution of her dependency arc.

## 22. Leadership selection

The Oumae Consultation Office must be revealed as part of Yuuko’s longer succession plan.

## 23. Kumiko and Shuuichi’s breakup

The source is explicit that they cease being lovers.

Delaying or omitting this changes Kumiko’s leadership psychology.

## 24. Shuuichi as vice-president

The appointment immediately complicates Kumiko’s attempt to separate romance from responsibility.

## 25. Team Oumae

The independent drum-major role is an institutional response to previous leadership overload.

# 41. Provisional questions for the third-year movement

## Kumiko as president

Can Kumiko make decisions before crisis forces certainty?

Can she:

- listen without becoming paralyzed;
- intervene without reproducing Asuka’s instrumentalism;
- and preserve her own desires while carrying everyone else’s?

## Shuuichi as vice-president

Will working closely together expose the breakup as unnecessary, or confirm that the emotional pause was useful?

Can they establish professional trust without pretending the former romance never existed?

## Reina as drum major

How will Reina respond when her musical standard conflicts with Kumiko’s relational judgment?

Can “Team Oumae” survive a disagreement comparable to Reina and Yuuko’s conflict over Yume?

## National gold as inherited obligation

The new generation frames its goal partly as revenge for the seniors.

Will that inheritance motivate them—or make their final year feel owned by someone else’s disappointment?

## Kitauji’s expanded competitive culture

Will ensemble-contest participation strengthen individual skill?

Or will continuous evaluation make music increasingly difficult to experience outside ranking?

## Kanade without Natsuki

Natsuki’s presence allowed Kanade to test whether a kind senior could respect merit.

What happens when Kanade herself must become that senior?

## Yume without Tomoe

Can Yume find an internal or broader relational reason to remain visible after her chosen listener retires?

## Motomu and Ryūsei

What exactly links Motomu to Genichirō Tsukinaga and the rising rival school?

Will Kitauji’s competition with Ryūsei force a family history he has chosen not to disclose into public view?

## Mizore and Nozomi after graduation

Mizore can now continue music without Nozomi.

Can Nozomi build a future without defining herself against Mizore’s talent?

Can their relationship survive when musical and educational paths diverge?

## Kumiko and Reina

Kumiko’s presidency and Reina’s drum-major role institutionalize their partnership.

Will musical ambition bring them closer, or will Reina’s likely professional trajectory make the countdown toward separation more severe?

# Final assessment

Volume 7 is a powerful conclusion to Kumiko’s second-year movement because it refuses three comforting rewards.

It refuses to reward effort automatically.

It refuses to make love symmetrical.

It refuses to let institutional growth become permanent security.

Kitauji is better than it was.

That is not enough.

Mizore loves Nozomi more openly and understands herself more clearly.

That does not make Nozomi love her in exactly the same way.

Yuuko leads well, the new members develop, and the ensemble performs successfully.

That does not preserve the national berth.

These refusals give the volume its maturity.

The central *Liz and the Blue Bird* arc initially appears to be about Mizore learning to release Nozomi.

That is only half the movement.

Mizore must accept that Nozomi may choose a life elsewhere.

Nozomi must accept that Mizore possesses a life and talent capable of going somewhere beyond her.

The second release is harder for Nozomi because she had imagined herself as the free one.

She thought she was the blue bird who could leave, return, and remain important.

Mizore’s full oboe sound destroys that arrangement.

The person who followed has become the person with wings.

Nozomi’s tears are therefore not merely admiration.

They are grief for an identity:

> the talented, beloved origin figure who alone could support Mizore.

Her envy is ugly, honest, and recognizable.

She believes she loved music more.

She struggled more visibly.

She fought for ensemble.

Why, then, is Mizore the person Niiyama selects?

The novel gives no moral equation that resolves the unfairness.

Talent is not distributed according to visible devotion.

Nozomi’s meaningful action is to stop demanding that reality satisfy the equation.

She listens.

She adjusts.

She decides that if Mizore’s oboe is greater than she imagined, her own task is to become the flute capable of answering it.

Mizore’s development is equally unsentimental.

She finally initiates the hug she once watched other girls perform.

She tells Nozomi the truth.

Nozomi’s answer does not match.

Mizore survives.

That survival is the proof of her growth.

She no longer requires perfect reciprocity in order to continue existing.

She can keep the music even if she cannot keep Nozomi beside her.

The competition plot enacts the same lesson collectively.

Kitauji performs well and receives gold.

It does not receive the continuation it wants.

The performance nevertheless remains real.

Tomoe’s “Bravo” becomes the volume’s alternative final judgment—not a rejection of competitive standards, but recognition that one evaluation cannot contain the complete value of the sound.

Yuuko’s response is characteristic of the series at its best.

She does not claim the judges were wrong.

She does not tell the students that medals are meaningless.

She refuses to let one result convert their effort into shame.

Then she changes the institution.

The ensemble contest, stronger individual training, and distributed leadership are attempts to make the loss productive without pretending it was deserved punishment.

The presidency transfer gives Kumiko the result of everything she has learned.

She entered Kitauji as a girl who avoided wanting strongly enough to be hurt.

She becomes president because she has learned to hear how other people are hurt.

Yet her first act after accepting leadership reveals that development is not complete.

She gives Shuuichi back the flower.

She treats love as one more thing that might distract her from responsibility.

The girl who told Asuka not to surrender her desire in the name of maturity now risks performing the same sacrifice herself.

Shuuichi’s appointment as vice-president ensures the contradiction cannot remain abstract.

Kumiko cannot lead by removing every emotionally complicated person from her field.

She must lead among them.

That is why “Team Oumae” is an apt endpoint.

The next Kitauji will not be built around one invulnerable Asuka.

It will be built from three incomplete people:

- Kumiko hearing;
- Shuuichi supporting;
- Reina demanding.

Their success will depend not upon eliminating those differences, but upon making them resonate.

Volume 7’s place in the larger series can therefore be summarized as:

> **The first movement taught Kitauji to want.  
> The second taught it that wanting, improving, and deserving are not guarantees of being chosen.  
> Its final lesson is that the sound must continue anyway.**

Mizore keeps the oboe.

Nozomi keeps the flute.

Yuuko gives up the lectern.

Natsuki leaves her juniors.

Tomoe listens from the empty hall.

Kumiko returns the flower but inherits the band.

A fermata holds the final note.

Then the next movement waits for her to lower her hand.
