# Executive thesis

Volume 1 was about learning to want.

Volume 2 was about discovering that different people want incompatible things—and that the person one ranks first may not return that ranking.

Volume 3 is about **claiming a desire as one’s own, even when doing so is inconvenient, irrational, costly, or impossible to justify as “best for everyone.”**

Its central conflict is often described as Asuka’s possible departure from the band. That is correct at the plot level, but incomplete.

The deeper crisis is that Kitauji has built itself around a fantasy:

> **The exceptional person does not need to be supported.**

Asuka is brilliant, musically indispensable, academically exceptional, socially commanding, funny, beautiful, and apparently invulnerable. Because she looks capable of managing everything, the band allows her to manage everything. Because she rarely voices personal need, everyone assumes she has none. Because she is “special,” ordinary obligations of care seem not to apply to her.

Her disappearance reveals that this is not admiration alone.

It is also abandonment.

The band has converted Asuka into:

- its emotional regulator;
- low-brass authority;
- technical standard;
- social entertainer;
- shadow leader;
- crisis manager;
- and symbol of stability.

Almost no one has asked what she herself wants.

The novel’s emotional resolution comes when Kumiko stops arguing that Asuka is **necessary** and tells her that she is **wanted**:

> It does not matter what is most rational for the band.  
> Kumiko wants to stand onstage with her.  
> Kumiko wants to hear her euphonium.  
> Kumiko does not want Asuka to erase her own desire and call that maturity.

That distinction is the heart of Volume 3:

> **To be necessary is to have a function.  
> To be wanted is to be recognized as a person.**

Asuka has spent much of her life making herself necessary because necessity feels safer than love. A useful person can justify her place. A person who simply wants something risks refusal.

Kumiko’s intervention offers a different kind of relation. She crosses the boundary Asuka has maintained around herself and says something childlike, selfish, and emotionally indefensible:

> **I want you there.**

It is the most important thing Kumiko has said in the series so far.

# 1. Volume 3 as the culmination of the opening trilogy

The first three novels form a coherent developmental sequence.

## Volume 1: desire must be admitted

Kumiko begins by insulating herself from disappointment. She participates, observes, judges, and drifts, but avoids openly wanting.

Reina’s question—whether Kumiko feels frustrated—exposes that defense.

By the end, Kumiko wants Kitauji to win and cries when it advances.

## Volume 2: desire enters conflict

Once Kumiko wants victory, she must confront the desires of others.

Nozomi wants to return.  
Mizore wants to remain special to Nozomi.  
Yuuko wants Kaori’s effort recognized.  
Reina wants exceptional musical and romantic recognition.  
Rival schools want the same qualification Kitauji wants.

Desire is no longer a private awakening. It creates hierarchy, exclusion, jealousy, and pain.

## Volume 3: desire must become ownership

Volume 3 asks whether a person can still say “I want this” when:

- parents disapprove;
- the institution might operate more smoothly without her;
- another performer has prepared to take her place;
- the choice might be selfish;
- success cannot be guaranteed;
- and acting means assuming responsibility for failure.

Asuka, Mamiko, Taki, Reina, Shuuichi, Haruka, and Kumiko all face versions of this question.

The novel’s answer is not “always follow your dream.”

Its answer is more disciplined:

> **A life chosen by others does not become yours merely because their reasons are sensible.  
> But choosing for yourself also means accepting risk, cost, grief, and the possibility that your choice will not be rewarded.**

Mamiko may fail as a hairstylist.  
Asuka may return and still receive bronze.  
Taki may carry his wife’s dream and not fulfill it this year.  
Reina may confess and not be understood.  
Kumiko may cross another person’s boundary and be rejected.

Agency does not guarantee success.

It makes the outcome genuinely belong to the chooser.

# 2. Narrative architecture

## Prologue: the euphonium arrives before Asuka can name it

The novel opens with a young Asuka receiving:

- a black instrument case;
- a worn notebook;
- and the name **Shindō Masakazu**.

She has almost no memory of the sender. She opens the case and discovers an unfamiliar silver instrument. She searches a catalogue to learn its name:

> ユーフォニアム

The prologue establishes several governing ideas.

### Music arrives as a secret inheritance

Asuka’s euphonium does not emerge from school, her mother, or ordinary family continuity. It comes from the absent father whom her mother has excluded from her life.

Music becomes:

- a forbidden connection;
- a second lineage;
- a material message from someone unavailable;
- and a part of Asuka’s identity that does not belong to her mother.

### Asuka first encounters the instrument before understanding the relationship

She can touch the euphonium before she can understand what her father wants from her.

That is characteristic of the volume’s larger treatment of inheritance. People often receive:

- dreams;
- wounds;
- instruments;
- expectations;
- and unfinished emotional business

before they possess language for them.

### The euphonium reflects her distorted image

When young Asuka looks into its curved surface, her face appears stretched and unfamiliar.

That is a fitting first image for her character. The instrument will eventually become the medium through which she finds a truer self, but it first reflects a self distorted by family history.

---

## Chapter 1: 「唸れ！トランペット」

The first chapter brings conflict into public sound.

Kitauji has qualified for nationals and has become almost unrecognizable compared with the apathetic ensemble of spring. Students now move without Taki’s constant instruction. The band possesses momentum, prestige, and a common objective.

Then the apparently stable structure is struck at its weakest point.

Asuka’s mother enters the school, demands that her daughter quit, and publicly exposes the violence beneath Asuka’s competence.

The chapter also introduces or develops:

- Mamiko’s wish to leave university;
- Kumiko’s old admiration for her sister;
- Taki’s relationship with his deceased wife;
- Italian White sunflowers;
- Asuka’s precarious participation;
- Haruka’s assumption of genuine leadership;
- and the band’s dependence upon a supposedly invulnerable member.

The trumpet is appropriate to this chapter because its dominant action is **declaration**.

Asuka’s mother declares ownership.  
Taki declares that Asuka’s own will matters.  
Asuka declares that she does not want to quit.  
Haruka declares that the band created the myth of Asuka’s specialness.  
The ensemble must learn to answer loudly enough to exist without one person speaking for it.

---

## Chapter 2: 「轟け！トロンボーン」

The trombone chapter turns toward abandoned paths, family history, and the futures students are being asked to choose.

Mamiko’s trombone first inspired Kumiko to begin music. Mamiko later abandoned it because she followed the academically approved route.

Now she announces that she wants to abandon university and pursue hairstyling.

The chapter also includes:

- the station-building concert;
- Kitauji’s encounter with Seira Girls’ national-level performance;
- Haruka’s unexpectedly powerful baritone saxophone solo;
- Hashimoto’s warning that Kitauji’s anxiety has made its music miserable;
- Natsuki preparing as Asuka’s substitute;
- Mamiko confronting her parents;
- Taki explaining his wife’s dream;
- and students beginning to speak about life after high school.

The trombone is carried by both Mamiko’s history and Shuuichi’s present. It links:

- the admired older sister who quit;
- the childhood friend who continues;
- the path Kumiko entered through imitation;
- and the question of whether music remains part of adulthood.

“轟け” suggests a sound that travels with force. The consequences of old choices now thunder into the present.

---

## Chapter 3: 「響け！ユーフォニアム」

The final chapter moves from institutional crisis toward intimate truth.

Kumiko visits Asuka’s house. Asuka reveals:

- Shindō is her father;
- her mother prohibited contact;
- the euphonium and notebook were sent secretly;
- she had to remain academically perfect in order to keep playing;
- she initially cared only about having access to the instrument;
- she wanted nationals because Shindō might judge the high-school division;
- and she manipulated the Nozomi–Mizore crisis to protect Kitauji’s competitive chances.

Kumiko then crosses the boundary Asuka has always accused her of respecting.

She tells Asuka to stop pretending that surrender is maturity.

The chapter culminates in:

- Asuka’s return;
- Natsuki’s “welcome home”;
- the national performance;
- Kitauji’s bronze award;
- Shindō’s private message to the euphonium player;
- and Asuka’s unguarded admission that she loves the euphonium too.

The series title finally becomes literal as the unnamed composition receives its name in the epilogue.

---

## Epilogue: sound becomes inheritance

At graduation, Asuka gives Shindō’s notebook to Kumiko.

The chain is now:

> Shindō → Asuka → Kumiko → future students

Asuka tells Kumiko to play the piece for her own juniors.

The title **響け！ユーフォニアム** therefore becomes more than the name of a composition. It is an imperative:

> Sound.  
> Resonate.  
> Carry this forward.  
> Let a quiet supporting instrument reach someone who has not arrived yet.

The trilogy closes not on victory but on transmission.

# 3. The “greatest crisis”: the exceptional person as a single point of failure

Kitauji’s greatest crisis is not simply that its strongest euphonium player may miss nationals.

The larger problem is that Asuka has become a **single point of institutional failure**.

When she is absent:

- low-brass practice becomes uncertain;
- ensemble confidence collapses;
- students obsess over her rather than their music;
- technical balance deteriorates;
- leaders struggle to control the atmosphere;
- and the whole band plays as though one missing person has removed permission to be good.

Taki directly criticizes this fragility. Concern for a member is understandable, but an ensemble cannot disintegrate whenever one person is absent.

The band must learn the difference between:

- caring about Asuka;
- and using Asuka as a substitute for collective responsibility.

## “We made her special”

Haruka gives the volume’s clearest institutional diagnosis.

She admits that everyone relied upon Asuka because Asuka appeared capable of handling anything. Haruka herself considered resigning as president because she felt inferior to her.

Then she rejects the myth:

> Asuka was not inherently some untouchable being.  
> The band made her special.

This does not deny Asuka’s talent. It identifies what the band has done with that talent.

They have used excellence as an excuse not to support the excellent person.

That insight revises Reina’s Volume 1 philosophy of specialness.

For Reina, becoming special means distinguishing oneself from the undifferentiated crowd through ability and will.

For Asuka, being made special means being denied ordinary dependency.

> **Specialness can be aspiration.  
> It can also be social isolation disguised as admiration.**

---

# 4. Kumiko Oumae: crossing the final boundary

Volume 3 completes the most important phase of Kumiko’s early development.

She has always been perceptive. She has often intervened. But Asuka correctly identifies a limitation in those interventions:

> Kumiko approaches other people’s emotional boundaries, but often stops before crossing them.

She helps facilitate outcomes.  
She listens.  
She gathers information.  
She encourages others to speak.

But she avoids placing her own vulnerable desire directly at stake.

## Asuka’s attack is accurate

During their confrontation, Asuka dismantles Kumiko’s institutional arguments.

Kumiko says everyone wants Asuka back.

Asuka asks:

> Who is “everyone”?

Can Kumiko know Natsuki’s true feelings?  
Can she know that people are not simply reading the atmosphere?  
Can she claim intimacy with people whose innermost motives she has never risked asking about?

Asuka then names Kumiko’s pattern:

- she helped with Kaori and Reina;
- she became involved with Mizore and Nozomi;
- she wants people to reconcile;
- but she fears both hurting and being hurt;
- so she observes from the safe side of the final boundary.

The criticism nearly defeats her because it is true.

## Haruka’s insight gives Kumiko a way out

Kumiko almost submits to Asuka’s logic. Asuka is more intelligent, more informed, and more experienced. It would be easy to accept that Asuka knows best.

Then Kumiko remembers:

> **Asuka is not special in the sense of being infallible.**

That recognition permits disagreement.

Kumiko no longer treats Asuka’s superior reasoning as moral authority.

## “Correctness does not matter”

Kumiko’s decisive speech is emotionally radical because it abandons defensibility.

She admits Asuka is more knowledgeable about the band. Then she says that all of that is irrelevant.

She wants Asuka at the performance.

She attacks Asuka’s performance of adulthood:

- Why are you pretending to understand everything?
- Why are you acting as though you alone are mature?
- Why are you turning your own surrender into the supposedly optimal outcome?
- Why are you erasing the desire to let your father hear you?
- Why are you choosing regret before exhausting the possibility of action?

Kumiko calls herself a child and refuses to be ashamed of it.

That is not anti-adult romanticism. Mamiko has just taught her something important:

> A teenager does not become mature by prematurely swallowing every desire adults find inconvenient.

False maturity is often fear wearing the language of realism.

## From “the band needs you” to “I want you”

Kumiko’s strongest line is not that Asuka is the superior player.

It is that she wants to hear Asuka’s euphonium in that hall.

This matters because Asuka has already prepared Natsuki. She has made herself technically replaceable.

A functional argument can therefore be answered:

> Natsuki can cover the part.  
> The ensemble can continue.

A personal argument cannot be resolved that way:

> Natsuki is not Asuka.  
> Kumiko wants Asuka herself.

This is the first time Kumiko so forcefully makes another person’s existence—not merely her usefulness—the object of desire.

## Growth remains uneven

Kumiko can now confront Asuka directly, but she remains indirect elsewhere.

With Shuuichi, she says:

> “We’re only friends.”

She does not mean this neutrally. She waits for him to contradict it. He fails to do so, retreating into the same safe label.

Kumiko becomes angry because she wants him to cross the boundary she herself refuses to cross.

The contrast is revealing:

- With Asuka, crisis forces her into direct speech.
- With Shuuichi, ordinary romantic vulnerability still produces provocation and evasion.

Growth is relationally uneven rather than a single permanent transformation.

# 5. Asuka Tanaka: competence as armor

Volume 3 turns Asuka from the ensemble’s most fascinating unresolved figure into one of its most psychologically complete characters.

Her central structure is:

> **She makes herself useful so that she never has to ask whether she is loved.**

She is not emotionally empty. She is extraordinarily defended.

## The child who regulates the parent

The confrontation in the staff room reveals an inverted family structure.

Asuka’s mother:

- calls Asuka her possession;
- claims the right to determine her future;
- demands that she quit;
- strikes her when she voices refusal;
- accuses her of playing euphonium to punish her;
- then collapses into guilt and self-condemnation.

Asuka responds by:

- controlling the room;
- stopping others from intervening;
- calming her mother;
- minimizing the assault;
- apologizing to the adults;
- reassuring her mother that she is not hated;
- and escorting her home.

The daughter performs the parental role.

When her mother asks whether Asuka must hate such a terrible mother, Asuka immediately protects her from that fear.

> “You aren’t bad.”  
> “I will never hate you.”  
> “It’s all right.”

These are not merely affectionate statements. They are part of a long-established emotional survival pattern.

Her mother becomes volatile.  
Asuka stabilizes her.  
Her mother collapses.  
Asuka reassures her.  
Ordinary life resumes.

This helps explain Asuka’s extraordinary social control. She learned early that managing atmosphere prevents disaster.

## Love as debt

At home, Asuka describes her upbringing through the language of obligation.

Her mother spent:

- money;
- time;
- labor;
- and emotional energy

raising her alone.

Asuka therefore imagines compliance as repayment.

This is not secure gratitude. It is an internal accounting system:

> I have received investment.  
> I owe a return.  
> My life must justify what was spent upon me.

That framework makes personal desire feel immoral. Wanting something her mother opposes becomes defaulting on a debt.

## Her mother’s map of happiness

Asuka recognizes that her mother genuinely believes she is protecting her.

The desired future is conventional:

- good university;
- good marriage;
- respectable adult life;
- no risky or distracting commitments.

That sincerity makes the control harder to resist.

If the mother were merely malicious, rejection would be simpler. Instead, love and possession are entangled.

Asuka’s insight is devastating:

> Her mother’s map of happiness is a cage precisely because it is presented as love.

## The euphonium as forbidden selfhood

The instrument comes from the father her mother cannot tolerate.

To continue playing it, Asuka must maintain excellent grades. Academic perfection becomes the price of musical existence.

Her achievement is therefore not merely natural brilliance.

It is survival labor.

> 「好きなことを続けるためには、勉強するしかなかった」  
> To continue doing what she loved, she had no choice but to study.

Asuka’s competence has been coerced into being.

If she failed academically, she could lose the euphonium.  
If she became troublesome, she could lose access.  
If she needed too much, the entire arrangement could collapse.

She therefore becomes the girl who never visibly needs anything.

## Her austere room

Asuka’s bedroom contains little of ordinary adolescent self-expression.

There are:

- study materials;
- the euphonium;
- practical objects;
- and very little evidence of relaxed personal life.

Her world has been compressed into the two things her arrangement permits:

1. academic excellence;
2. music, under conditions.

Her charisma at school is expansive. Her private space is almost barren.

## Why she wanted nationals

Asuka initially presents herself as someone who does not care about competitions, factions, or emotional drama. She only wants to play.

Taki changes that.

Once nationals becomes plausible, she learns that Shindō may be judging the high-school division. Her private objective becomes:

> Let him hear me.

She has almost no ordinary father–daughter relationship. She does not claim conventional affection. She calls him “Shindō-san” rather than naturally inhabiting the word “father.”

Yet she wants her sound to reach him.

Music becomes the only relationship available between them.

## Using the band

Asuka admits she delayed resolving the Mizore–Nozomi conflict because Nozomi’s return might destabilize Mizore and reduce Kitauji’s chance of reaching nationals.

She says:

> 「そのために、うちは部活を利用した」  
> For that purpose, I used the club.

This is an important confession because it reframes her earlier neutrality.

She was not detached.

She was strategically invested.

Her refusal to let Nozomi return was both:

- defensible ensemble management;
- and service to her private desire.

The novel does not condemn her as a hypocrite. It shows that pure neutrality never existed.

Asuka’s problem is not that she has personal motives.

It is that she believes having them makes her morally contaminated.

## Self-punishment as fairness

Once her family crisis makes attendance uncertain, Asuka decides that withdrawing is the correct punishment.

Her reasoning is internally elegant:

- she excluded Nozomi because Nozomi threatened ensemble stability;
- she now threatens ensemble stability;
- therefore she must apply the same rule to herself.

But this apparent fairness also provides a familiar escape.

She can surrender before asking her mother again.  
She can avoid hoping.  
She can avoid failing publicly.  
She can convert pain into principle.

Kumiko correctly identifies this as self-satisfaction disguised as sacrifice.

## The piece she claims not to like

Asuka says she does not particularly like Shindō’s composition.

Kumiko immediately notices the contradiction:

> Why repeatedly play a piece one dislikes?

Asuka admits she may have wanted Kumiko to criticize it—to destroy its emotional hold over her.

Instead, Kumiko loves it.

She calls it warm and bright and asks Asuka to play it immediately.

Asuka complies.

This scene gives Kumiko a form of access no one else has received. Asuka allows her to hear the private sound connecting father, daughter, and euphonium.

The music communicates what Asuka cannot admit:

> The piece matters.  
> The instrument matters.  
> The absent father matters.  
> Being heard matters.

## Her return

Asuka earns a top-thirty national result on the mock examination and uses it to bargain with her mother.

Her return is not a miraculous liberation. The swollen cheek indicates that resistance still has a cost.

But she arrives carrying the black instrument case.

Kaori embraces her.  
Haruka cries.  
The entire band surrounds her.

Natsuki approaches.

Asuka tries to apologize. Natsuki refuses to permit it.

> 「先輩、おかえりなさい」  
> Welcome home.

Asuka answers:

> 「ただいま！」  
> I’m home.

This is one of the novel’s most important linguistic moments.

Asuka normally speaks through:

- wit;
- performance;
- qualifiers;
- analysis;
- and strategic control.

“ただいま” is simple, domestic, and childlike.

The band briefly becomes a place she can return to rather than a system she must manage.

## “I like it too”

After nationals, Shindō sends his message:

> “You did well to continue this far. It was a beautiful tone.”

Asuka’s composure breaks into an unguarded smile.

She then asks Kumiko:

> Do you like the euphonium?

Kumiko answers immediately:

> Yes.

Asuka responds:

> 「ふふ、うちも」  
> I do too.

This may be the most emotionally honest line Asuka speaks in the novel.

Not:

- it is useful;
- it was sent to me;
- I happen to play it;
- the band needs it;
- I have continued too long to stop.

Simply:

> I like it.

For Asuka, that is an act of self-recognition.

# 6. Asuka’s mother: protection transformed into possession

The novel does not provide her mother’s independent interiority, so interpretation should remain bounded by what is shown.

What the text clearly establishes is a coercive relationship in which love is expressed through ownership.

She believes:

- she raised Asuka alone;
- sacrifice gives her decision-making authority;
- academic success is the safest route;
- music threatens the future she has designed;
- and disagreement signifies personal rejection.

Her statement is explicit:

> “This child is mine.”

That grammar reduces Asuka from daughter to property.

## Disagreement becomes emotional abandonment

When Asuka refuses to quit, her mother does not treat it as a difference over priorities. She interprets it as evidence that Asuka hates her and wants to make her suffer.

This structure makes independent choice emotionally expensive.

To choose music, Asuka must appear to reject her mother’s entire life of sacrifice.

## Violence followed by dependency

After striking Asuka, the mother quickly becomes remorseful and requires reassurance.

This places Asuka in an impossible position:

- she is the injured party;
- but she must comfort the person who injured her.

The scene explains why Asuka later reduces relationships to function. Ordinary emotional dependency has become associated with being consumed by another person’s instability.

## The novel avoids effortless emancipation

Asuka does not simply denounce her mother and walk away.

She bargains.

She produces academic proof.  
She promises continued excellence.  
She wins limited permission.

This is frustrating, but psychologically credible. Her relationship with her mother cannot be dissolved by one adolescent speech.

Volume 3 grants Asuka a victory, not total liberation.

# 7. Haruka Ogasawara: becoming the president she thought she could not be

Haruka receives one of the volume’s strongest developments.

She has always been formally president, but Asuka has often appeared to be the real center of authority. Haruka knows this and feels inferior.

Asuka’s absence forces her to stop measuring herself against Asuka’s style.

## Haruka’s leadership is not charisma

She cannot become Asuka.

She does not possess:

- the same verbal speed;
- theatrical confidence;
- intellectual aggression;
- or effortless social command.

Her leadership emerges from a different resource:

> honest vulnerability joined to responsibility.

She tells the band that she has considered quitting. She admits jealousy and insecurity. She acknowledges dependence upon Asuka.

Then she turns confession into a collective demand:

> Stop waiting for Asuka to save you.  
> Support her by becoming capable yourselves.

## “Asuka is not special”

This is not an insult.

It is an act of humanization.

Haruka rejects the assumption that exceptional ability removes ordinary need. She also rejects the band’s use of Asuka as an excuse for its own weakness.

The speech allows the ensemble to become more distributed.

## Her baritone saxophone solo

At the station-building concert, Haruka’s performance reveals a self that ordinary conversation hides.

Socially, she is timid and hesitant.

Musically, she is vivid, funky, forceful, and entertaining.

This matters because it proves that leadership does not require one continuous personality across all contexts. Haruka can be uncertain in speech and authoritative in music.

Her solo is not merely a pleasant surprise. It is evidence that the band has underestimated her for the same reason it overestimated Asuka: it mistakes visible social style for total personal capacity.

## Before and after nationals

Haruka gives the pre-performance speech without retreating behind Asuka. After the bronze result, she speaks honestly:

- Kitauji performed to its actual ability;
- everyone tried fully;
- the surrounding schools were better;
- and next year’s students must return.

She does not protect them with false consolation.

Her tears interrupt the speech, and Asuka steps in—but this no longer erases Haruka’s authority. The two finally function as complementary leaders rather than “real leader” and substitute.

---

# 8. Natsuki Nakagawa: the substitute who refuses to become a grievance

Natsuki’s role is quietly devastating.

Asuka and Taki have prepared her in advance as the substitute euphonium player. Her score contains Asuka’s detailed annotations. She knows exactly what her opportunity means:

> She may perform at nationals only because Asuka’s life is falling apart.

Natsuki wants the stage. She is frightened, underprepared compared with Asuka, and aware that this may be an extraordinary chance.

Yet she asks Kumiko to bring Asuka back.

## Self-sacrifice without self-erasure

Natsuki’s decision should not be simplified into “she does not really want to perform.”

She does want it.

That is what makes her choice meaningful.

She judges that Asuka is currently the better player and that Kitauji’s strongest performance includes her. She accepts that ensemble responsibility may conflict with personal opportunity.

This extends the maturity she displayed after losing the Volume 1 audition.

## Her practice is not wasted

The novel directly confronts the idea that unused preparation is meaningless.

When Asuka returns, Natsuki’s national opportunity disappears. But the work remains:

- she is stronger;
- her commitment is proven;
- the section is less fragile;
- and she has become a credible future A-ensemble player.

At breakfast before nationals, Takuya tells her that next year she should perform with them. Natsuki is visibly affected.

Her preparation changes her future even though it does not produce the immediate reward.

## Rejecting Asuka’s apology

When Asuka returns, she begins to apologize.

Natsuki stops her sharply.

An apology would define Natsuki primarily as someone injured by Asuka’s return. Natsuki refuses that role.

She does not want guilt.  
She wants Asuka home.

Her “おかえりなさい” releases both of them:

- Asuka does not need to make herself small to compensate;
- Natsuki does not need to pretend she was never disappointed.

This is mature affection without sentimental dishonesty.

---

# 9. Kaori Nakaseko: devotion at the edge of Asuka’s boundary

Kaori’s role is quieter than in Volume 1, but emotionally intense.

She is the person most visibly distressed by Asuka’s disappearance.

She:

- brings chestnut manju intended for Asuka’s mother;
- accompanies Kumiko partway to Asuka’s home;
- asks Asuka directly not to disappear;
- kneels to tie Asuka’s shoelace;
- monitors news of the mock examination;
- and embraces Asuka when she returns.

Her care is practical, bodily, and almost ritualistic.

## The shoelace scene

Asuka’s undone shoelace becomes an opportunity for Kaori to lower herself physically and care for something Asuka has neglected.

The intimacy is striking.

Asuka watches with an emotionally unreadable expression and then thanks her. She does not reciprocally disclose herself.

Kaori can care for Asuka’s body more easily than she can enter Asuka’s interior world.

## Desire without access

Kaori’s devotion is sincere, but Asuka repeatedly controls the distance between them.

Earlier she gently removes Kaori’s hand from her uniform. At home, she jokingly tells Kumiko that Kaori’s neck makes her want to bite it, a line with clear sensual and yuri-coded charge. Yet comedy also lets Asuka avoid naming what Kaori means to her.

The relationship remains asymmetrical:

- Kaori offers visible emotional priority.
- Asuka offers affection, teasing, protection, and selective physical warmth.
- She does not grant Kaori the confessional access she grants Kumiko.

This does not mean Kumiko is simply “more important.” The relationships serve different needs. But it does show that Asuka chooses Kumiko as the person before whom she can risk being interpreted rather than worshipped.

## The embrace

When Asuka returns, Kaori finally abandons restraint and embraces her.

Asuka is startled but accepts the contact, stroking her back.

For a brief moment, Kaori’s devotion is not deflected into humor.

---

# 10. Mamiko Oumae: the good daughter who outsourced her life

Mamiko is the volume’s principal mirror to Asuka.

Both are academically capable older daughters whose futures have been heavily shaped by parental approval.

Both learn to perform maturity.

Both sacrifice music.

Both become resentful toward someone who appears freer.

But their responses differ.

## Admired origin

Young Kumiko first wants to play music because she sees Mamiko performing trombone.

Mamiko is:

- impressive;
- older;
- musically capable;
- and the model Kumiko wants to follow.

The novel restores this buried affection before showing the sisters’ estrangement.

Their hostility is therefore not original. It is accumulated disappointment.

## The approved path

Mamiko leaves music, studies, enters a respectable university, and follows the future expected of her.

She repeatedly tells Kumiko:

> Club activities will not get you into university.

The statement is partly a repetition of the ideology governing her own life.

If Mamiko’s sacrifice was necessary, Kumiko’s freedom appears irresponsible. If Kumiko can play seriously and still construct a future, Mamiko must confront the possibility that she surrendered too easily.

## Envy

Mamiko admits that she envied Kumiko.

She saw:

- a younger sister doing what she liked;
- parents who seemed more permissive;
- and someone allowed to remain adolescent while Mamiko performed the exemplary daughter.

Kumiko, meanwhile, believed Mamiko was the favored child.

Both sisters interpreted the other as receiving what they lacked.

This mutual envy is one of the novel’s most realistic family insights.

## Avoiding responsibility through obedience

Mamiko’s confession is unusually self-critical.

She followed her parents partly because obedience gave her someone to blame.

If the path failed, she could say:

> Mother told me to do it.  
> This was not my decision.  
> I am not responsible.

Her realization is not merely that her parents controlled her.

It is that she cooperated with the control because ownership frightened her.

This makes her decision to pursue hairstyling morally serious. She accepts that future failure will now be hers.

## The family confrontation

Mamiko tells her parents that she wants to leave university and attend a hairstyling school in Osaka.

Her father does not offer easy inspirational approval. He tells her that if she is serious, she must accept material consequences:

- leave home;
- support herself;
- pay the costs;
- and stop treating the choice as a symbolic rebellion.

This response contains both severity and recognition. He treats her as an adult chooser.

The mother resists more strongly, but eventual conversation allows reconciliation.

## Cooking as failed but sincere repair

Mamiko attempts to prepare dinner and burns the pot.

The scene is comic, but symbolically apt.

She cannot instantly perform the domestic harmony she wants to restore. Her attempt is clumsy and damaging. Kumiko takes over the meal while Mamiko scrubs the pot.

The sisters repair the relationship through shared labor rather than a polished emotional ritual.

## “Do not regret it”

Mamiko’s advice to Kumiko—live without choosing regret—directly precedes Kumiko’s confrontation with Asuka.

Mamiko cannot recover the years she spent suppressing herself. She can prevent Kumiko and Asuka from repeating the same pattern.

## Hearing Kumiko

Mamiko listens to the band’s CD and attends nationals.

This completes an important reversal.

Kumiko began music by watching Mamiko.

Now Mamiko returns to hear Kumiko.

The younger sister’s sound reaches the person whose old sound created her.

# 11. Taki Noboru: inherited desire made voluntary

Volume 3 gives Taki his most significant interior context so far.

His wife:

- attended Kitauji;
- revered the band’s earlier golden age;
- became a music teacher;
- wanted to return her alma mater to nationals;
- and dreamed of winning national gold.

She died before realizing that future.

Taki eventually takes responsibility for Kitauji and carries the dream forward.

## Carrying another person’s dream

This creates a deliberate parallel with Asuka.

Asuka plays to reach her absent father.  
Taki teaches to continue his deceased wife’s dream.

Neither motive originates as a purely autonomous desire.

The novel does not treat that as inauthentic.

A desire received from another person can become one’s own through chosen commitment.

The contrast with Asuka’s mother is crucial:

- Asuka’s mother imposes a future and denies Asuka’s agency.
- Taki receives his wife’s dream after her death and freely assumes responsibility for it.

Inheritance becomes oppressive when it replaces the inheritor’s will. It becomes meaningful when the inheritor chooses to continue it.

## Italian White

The white sunflower is associated with the message:

> 「あなたを想い続けます」  
> I will continue thinking of you.

Taki gave the flower when proposing to his wife and buys it on the anniversary of her death.

The symbol later returns through Shuuichi’s gift to Kumiko, where it acquires a second, less consciously solemn romantic meaning.

The same image can carry:

- bereavement;
- enduring devotion;
- adolescent affection;
- and the continuity of memory across different relationships.

## Defending Asuka’s agency

Taki refuses to accept a withdrawal request that does not reflect Asuka’s will.

His language remains controlled, but Kumiko recognizes his anger.

He makes an institutional principle explicit:

> The student’s decision matters.

This is one of his strongest moral actions, especially because the school administration wants to appease the parent.

## Fear of imposition

After the bronze result and Reina’s public support, Taki admits that he has sometimes wondered whether he is imposing his own desire upon the students.

This complicates the inspirational-teacher role.

He knows:

- his goal emerged partly from grief;
- his methods are demanding;
- not every student initially shared his ambition;
- and charisma can convert personal desire into collective obligation.

Reina reassures him that the students respect him.

The novel does not fully resolve the ethical issue, but Taki’s doubt distinguishes him from Asuka’s mother. He asks whether his power over the students is legitimate.

## Fifty-six performers

Before nationals, Asuka observes that most sports separate coach and players during competition.

Concert band does not.

Taki stands on the same stage. The performance consists of all fifty-six people, conductor included.

This transforms Taki from external evaluator into participant.

The national performance is not simply the students carrying his dream. He must risk judgment alongside them.

---

# 12. Reina Kousaka: strength confronted by grief and uncertainty

Reina continues to embody direct desire, but Volume 3 reveals further limits to her self-image.

## Kumiko’s protective concealment

Kumiko learns about Taki’s wife and does not tell Reina because she does not want to hurt her.

When Reina discovers this, she is angry.

Her response is important:

> She understands why Kumiko hid it.  
> She still wanted to be told.

This develops their friendship beyond protective management.

Kumiko cannot decide which truths Reina is strong enough to bear. Doing so repeats, in smaller form, the paternalism the volume criticizes elsewhere.

## “I thought I was stronger”

Reina is shaken not only by the existence of Taki’s wife but by her own reaction.

She thought of herself as someone who could withstand difficult truth through force of will. Romantic grief reveals that she is not exempt from ordinary vulnerability.

At Daikichiyama, she physically leans upon Kumiko and admits weakness.

Kumiko embraces her.

The mountain again functions as the place where Reina’s public confidence gives way to private truth.

## Adopting Taki’s dream

After learning the history, Reina wants national gold partly to fulfill Taki’s dream.

Her ambition expands:

- personal excellence;
- romantic devotion;
- loyalty to Taki;
- and collective victory

become intertwined.

This is emotionally sincere but contains risk. Reina may be turning another person’s grief into a task she believes excellence can solve.

The bronze result teaches that devotion cannot guarantee fulfillment.

## National performance

Reina is nervous because the performance now carries Taki’s history.

Kumiko reassures her with apparently circular logic:

> Reina can do it because she is Reina.

The line works because it expresses faith rather than analysis. Reina laughs, and they style each other’s hair.

This domestic intimacy counterbalances the large public stakes.

## The public confession

Reina tells Taki directly:

> She likes him.  
> She chose Kitauji because he was there.  
> She truly likes him.

Taki interprets the declaration as gratitude and admiration from a student.

The scene is comic, but not meaningless.

Reina does what Kumiko and Shuuichi repeatedly cannot: she speaks.

The result is not recognition, but the desire at least exists publicly.

## Kaori comforts Reina

After Kitauji receives bronze, Kaori strokes Reina’s head and tells her that reaching nationals was possible because of her.

Reina apologizes. Kaori rejects the need.

This quietly resolves the solo conflict of Volume 1.

Kaori can acknowledge Reina’s indispensable contribution without denying the pain of losing the solo. Reina can receive kindness from the senior whose place she took.

Competition no longer requires them to remain enemies.

## Kumiko and Reina

Their bond becomes even more physically and emotionally intimate:

- shared ascent of Daikichiyama;
- handholding;
- embraces;
- hair touching;
- mutual styling;
- Reina sleeping against Kumiko;
- Kumiko stroking her hair;
- and repeated declarations of trust.

Hazuki’s observation—Reina really likes Kumiko—is played as teasing, but it is plainly true.

The novel simultaneously preserves Reina’s explicit romantic love for Taki. It does not demand that every form of intense attachment resolve into a mutually exclusive category.

---

# 13. Shuuichi Tsukamoto: ordinary intimacy and the failure to speak

Shuuichi remains structurally important in the novel.

He is not as symbolically central as Reina or Asuka, but he anchors Kumiko in:

- childhood;
- ordinary neighborhood life;
- shared musical labor;
- and a possible future outside the heightened dyads of the band.

## “Only friends”

Kumiko tells Shuuichi that after graduation they may rarely see each other because they are only friends.

The line is a test.

She wants him to reject the category.

He nearly speaks, then retreats:

> “Right. We’re friends.”

Kumiko is disappointed because he reproduces her own defensive method—protecting the relationship by refusing to define its desire.

The scene forms a useful contrast with her later speech to Asuka. Kumiko can tell Asuka what she wants only after emotional urgency becomes intolerable. With Shuuichi, both remain trapped within implication.

## The national-eve conversation

Neither can sleep before nationals.

They admit fear, exhaustion, and the strange grief of reaching the goal. Kumiko says she is afraid of the competition ending more than of making a mistake.

Shuuichi correctly identifies this as development. Middle-school Kumiko never cared enough to fear burning out.

Their hands touch accidentally, and both recoil.

The physical reaction confirms what their speech avoids.

## Italian White hair ornament

Shuuichi remembers Kumiko’s birthday and searches for a gift associated with a flower she mentioned.

He chooses a white sunflower hair ornament with Midori’s help.

Only afterward does Kumiko reveal that Taki gave the same flower to his wife when proposing. Shuuichi panics and insists that no special implication was intended.

His denial is excessive enough to expose the implication he cannot yet state.

By the graduation epilogue, Asuka asks whether the ornament came from Kumiko’s boyfriend, and Kumiko reluctantly answers yes. The relationship has therefore moved beyond its earlier stalemate by that point, even though the novel’s primary emotional climax remains elsewhere.

## The trombone link

Shuuichi plays the instrument Mamiko abandoned.

He represents a possible alternative continuity:

- music does not have to end with childhood;
- ordinary romance can coexist with artistic commitment;
- and the path Mamiko left can still remain present in Kumiko’s immediate world.

---

# 14. Kitauji as an institution: from charismatic dependence to distributed responsibility

Kitauji’s first year under Taki succeeds spectacularly, but Volume 3 shows that success creates new dangers.

## The transformed band

By the opening:

- members organize themselves;
- rehearsal norms have become internalized;
- national qualification has increased commitment;
- and the school recognizes the band’s success.

Taki no longer needs to dictate every movement.

This is genuine institutional development.

## Dependence upon exceptional individuals

Yet the ensemble still relies heavily upon:

- Taki’s charisma;
- Asuka’s social management;
- Reina’s solo ability;
- Mizore’s oboe;
- and the emotional labor of a few highly capable members.

Asuka’s absence exposes this concentration.

## Substitution as institutional health

Natsuki’s preparation matters beyond the immediate plot.

A healthy institution cannot require one irreplaceable body.

This does not mean people are emotionally interchangeable. The novel carefully separates:

- technical substitution;
- from personal replacement.

Natsuki can play Asuka’s part.  
She cannot become Asuka.

That is exactly why both preparation and Kumiko’s personal plea matter.

## Haruka’s distributed model

Haruka’s speech shifts the band toward collective responsibility.

Members must:

- practice without Asuka’s constant direction;
- support one another;
- and stop treating leadership as a magical property possessed by one gifted person.

## Success creates expectation

After bronze, Asuka warns the younger students:

> Once a high hurdle has been crossed, outsiders begin treating that level as the minimum.

Kitauji will no longer be an obscure underdog.

Next year it will face:

- expectation;
- comparison;
- pressure to reproduce success;
- and the possibility that equivalent effort will produce worse results.

The institutional challenge is no longer transformation alone.

It is continuity.

---

# 15. The station-building concert: encountering mature excellence

The station concert introduces Seira Girls before nationals and forces Kitauji to hear the difference between emerging and established excellence.

## Same instruments, different world

Seira performs with:

- overwhelming control;
- expressive depth;
- stability;
- and a level of communication that makes Hazuki cry.

The instruments and written notes are not fundamentally different from Kitauji’s.

The difference lies in what the ensemble can make them mean.

This continues Volume 2’s argument that technical correctness is not the whole of music.

## Hazuki as listener

Hazuki cannot yet judge every technical detail, but she feels the performance’s force.

Her tears demonstrate that musical communication does not require specialist vocabulary. A listener can understand before she can explain.

Her position outside the A ensemble also matters. She remains part of Kitauji’s musical culture through listening, supporting, and aspiring.

## Haruka’s solo

Haruka’s solo reveals that Kitauji also contains forms of excellence hidden by ordinary social roles.

The concert therefore does not merely humiliate Kitauji. It expands its imagination:

> This is how far ensemble expression can go.  
> This is also evidence that people we underestimate may contain performance selves we have never seen.

## Joyless improvement

After hearing Seira, Kitauji initially responds by becoming tense and joyless.

Hashimoto criticizes them:

- they look miserable;
- their sound has hardened;
- and they have become so frightened of inadequacy that they no longer communicate pleasure.

The encounter with greatness produces both aspiration and paralysis.

This is another central principle:

> **A standard can guide development or destroy spontaneity, depending on how the performer metabolizes comparison.**

# 16. The national performance: partial hearing and collective transcendence

The novel’s national-performance sequence is one of its strongest pieces of musical prose.

## The performer does not hear what the audience hears

Kumiko notices that her acoustic experience differs from the audience’s:

- nearby brass dominates;
- distant woodwinds can disappear;
- her own euphonium may sound enormous to her but become absorbed in the hall;
- and the ensemble’s external balance cannot be fully perceived from within.

This is also a relational metaphor.

Every person inside a group hears a different mix.

No one possesses the total sound.

Taki, the audience, the judges, and each musician experience different versions of the same performance.

## Mizore’s solo

Mizore now acknowledges nervousness, a significant change from the emotionally sealed girl of Volume 2.

Her solo remains controlled and expressive. She thinks of Nozomi’s praise and intends to earn further recognition.

Her motivation is still relationally concentrated, but she is no longer isolated. She fist-bumps Kumiko using a ritual learned from Yuuko.

That tiny gesture demonstrates the broader social world now surrounding her.

## Reina’s solo

Reina’s playing remains:

- direct;
- bright;
- self-confident;
- and capable of filling the hall.

Her private fear does not weaken the sound. It gives the performance greater stakes.

## Kumiko’s development in sound

A difficult low-brass phrase that Asuka once played effortlessly has become one of Kumiko’s strengths.

This is an important concrete marker of growth. She is not merely more emotionally invested. Her body and technique have changed through repetition.

## Wanting the music not to end

Near the conclusion, Kumiko feels grief that the piece is ending.

She wants to keep playing.

This is the exact opposite of the detached girl who began Volume 1.

Her problem is no longer whether she cares.

It is how to live after caring so completely.

## A truthful performance

Taki tells the students afterward that the performance represented their present ability.

There is no suggestion that they catastrophically failed.

The bronze medal is not punishment for an unworthy performance.

It is the judgment produced when a genuinely good emerging band enters a field of mature national institutions.

# 17. Bronze: the first defeat Kumiko fully owns

The bronze result is thematically superior to an uncomplicated gold victory.

## Full effort does not guarantee the desired ranking

Kitauji:

- reaches the dream stage;
- performs without collapse;
- expresses its accumulated work;
- and still receives bronze.

This is the first time Kumiko experiences failure after fully owning the goal.

In Volume 1’s opening, Reina cries and asks whether Kumiko feels frustrated. Kumiko does not understand.

At nationals, Kumiko repeats:

> 悔しい。  
> 悔しい。

The feeling is overwhelming and new.

The opening question has finally been answered—not through intellectual agreement, but lived experience.

## The result does not invalidate the performance

The band’s work is not retroactively meaningless.

Haruka states that they performed to their level and that the other schools were better.

This is neither self-abasement nor excuse.

It preserves two truths:

1. Kitauji’s performance was worthy of pride.
2. Bronze accurately indicates how much farther the band must develop.

## Seira’s “Bravo”

After Kitauji performs, it receives strong applause.

After Seira performs, the hall erupts with “Bravo.”

Kumiko hears the difference.

National gold is not merely a slightly better score. It represents a communicative authority Kitauji has not yet achieved consistently.

## Envy remains

Kumiko can congratulate the gold schools and envy them simultaneously.

The novel continues refusing purified emotion. Good sportsmanship does not require the disappearance of disappointment.

## The future enters the result

For the third-years, bronze is final.

For the younger members, it becomes an instruction.

Haruka hopes they might win next year.

Asuka refuses the softness of “might”:

> Return and take gold.  
> This is the final vice-president’s order.

The medal closes one generation’s story and opens another’s obligation.

# 18. Shindō’s C and his personal message

The resolution of Asuka’s father plot is exceptionally well constructed.

Shindō gives Kitauji a **C** evaluation, consistent with its bronze result.

He does not distort public judgment because his daughter is playing.

Then, separately, he sends a message to the euphonium player:

> 「よくここまで続けてきたね。美しい音色だったよ」  
> You did well to continue this far. It was a beautiful tone.

The two responses serve different functions.

## Institutional judgment

As a competition judge, he evaluates the ensemble according to professional standards.

Kitauji is not gold level.

## Personal recognition

As the person who sent Asuka the instrument, he recognizes:

- her persistence;
- her sound;
- and the fact that the connection survived.

Asuka does not receive special treatment.

She receives something more appropriate to what she truly wanted:

> Proof that he heard her.

## “You continued”

The message praises continuation before beauty.

This matters because continuation has defined Asuka’s life.

She continued:

- under her mother’s restrictions;
- through academic demands;
- without ordinary contact with her father;
- while concealing desire;
- and after nearly being forced out immediately before nationals.

The father hears not only one performance but the years required to produce its tone.

## The result she actually needed

Asuka wanted national qualification partly so Shindō could hear her.

She receives bronze but fulfills the personal objective.

This separates:

- competitive success;
- from relational completion.

The medal cannot measure whether the performance reached the person for whom it mattered.

# 19. Music, inheritance, and the difference between repetition and continuation

Volume 3 is structured around inherited music.

## Shindō to Asuka

The father sends:

- his instrument;
- his old composition;
- and an invitation to share a musical lineage.

## Mamiko to Kumiko

Mamiko’s trombone performance inspires Kumiko to begin playing.

Even after Mamiko quits, the effect continues.

## Taki’s wife to Taki

Her unrealized dream becomes part of Taki’s reason for teaching.

## Asuka to Natsuki

Her annotated score prepares a successor.

## Asuka to Kumiko

The notebook transfers the private composition and the responsibility to let others hear it.

## Kumiko to future juniors

The epilogue explicitly imagines Kumiko playing the piece for a discouraged younger student.

Inheritance in this novel does not mean copying the predecessor.

Kumiko does not become Asuka.  
Taki does not become his wife.  
Natsuki does not become a substitute Asuka.  
Asuka does not become Shindō.

Each person receives something unfinished and must give it a new life.

> **Tradition is not repetition of the same person.  
> It is the survival of meaning through different people.**

# 20. The chapter-title progression: from force to resonance

The three verbs form a meaningful progression.

## 唸れ — growl, roar, sound under pressure

The trumpet chapter is full of strained declaration:

- parental shouting;
- Taki’s refusal;
- Haruka’s speech;
- Reina’s ambition;
- and a band struggling to find its own force.

The sound is powerful but conflicted.

## 轟け — thunder, reverberate with impact

The trombone chapter carries consequences across time:

- Mamiko’s abandoned music;
- Kumiko’s childhood;
- future paths;
- Seira’s overwhelming performance;
- and the choices now demanding acknowledgment.

The past does not remain quiet. It returns with force.

## 響け — resonate

The euphonium chapter values something subtler than raw loudness.

To resonate is not merely to be heard at high volume. It is to create response in:

- another person;
- another instrument;
- another generation;
- or another life.

Asuka’s tone reaches Shindō.  
Kumiko’s plea reaches Asuka.  
Mamiko hears Kumiko.  
The notebook reaches a future senior.

The trilogy moves toward resonance as its mature model of power.

# 21. Character voices and speaking styles

Volume 3 uses dialogue style as a major part of its characterization.

## Kumiko

Kumiko normally speaks cautious standard Japanese, often softened by uncertainty.

During the Asuka confrontation, that register breaks open.

Her speech becomes:

- direct;
- repetitive;
- emotionally unqualified;
- full of first-person desire;
- and willing to sound childish.

The change is the linguistic form of her growth. She stops translating emotion into socially acceptable ambiguity.

## Asuka

Asuka remains the cast’s most sophisticated register-switcher.

She can sound:

- theatrical;
- comic;
- flirtatious;
- managerial;
- analytical;
- coldly forensic;
- or unexpectedly young.

During arguments, she uses rhetorical questions to dismantle assumptions. She controls the conversation by defining what counts as evidence.

When discussing her family, she adopts distancing vocabulary:

- debt;
- use;
- efficiency;
- punishment;
- best outcome.

When she returns and says “ただいま,” or admits “うちも,” the intellectual scaffolding disappears.

Those simple lines sound more vulnerable than a long confession would.

## Haruka

Haruka’s voice begins with hesitation and self-qualification.

As she grows into leadership, her sentences become more declarative without losing emotional openness.

She does not imitate Asuka’s wit. Her authority comes from saying what she actually fears.

## Natsuki

Natsuki’s casual Kansai speech usually coats emotion in irony.

Her command that Asuka stop apologizing is sharp and unambiguous. “おかえりなさい” then shifts into ordinary domestic warmth.

## Kaori

Kaori speaks gently and often avoids burdening others. Much of her emotional force appears through action rather than syntax:

- bringing food;
- tying a shoe;
- waiting;
- embracing.

Her restrained speech makes the physical actions louder.

## Mamiko

Mamiko’s standard Japanese is blunt, defensive, and initially dismissive.

During the kitchen conversation, her sentences become extended and self-analytical. She names:

- envy;
- fear;
- complicity;
- and resentment

with an adult precision Kumiko has rarely heard from her.

## Reina

Reina’s **アタシ**, concise declarations, and emotional Kansai remain strongly identifiable.

Her speech becomes more fragmented when confronting grief. At Daikichiyama, she admits weakness without abandoning her essential directness.

Her confession to Taki is linguistically clear even though he misreads its category.

## Taki

Taki uses controlled, polite standard Japanese.

His refusal to accept the withdrawal form is calm and absolute. He does not need to match the mother’s volume to oppose her.

When speaking about his wife, he remains restrained, which intensifies the grief.

## Asuka’s mother

Her language is repetitive, escalating, and possessive.

Disagreement rapidly becomes:

- accusation;
- self-victimization;
- and demand for emotional reassurance.

The rhythm itself demonstrates how little room Asuka has to form an independent reply.

Because OCR damage is concentrated around punctuation and particles, any future close dialect study should check the corresponding scans.

# 22. Relationships and yuri-coded intensity

Volume 3 continues to sustain several intense relationships among girls without flattening them into one category.

## Kumiko and Reina

Their relationship includes:

- embracing at Daikichiyama;
- handholding;
- hair styling;
- sleeping against one another;
- bodily and aesthetic fascination;
- emotional exclusivity;
- and private truths Reina does not readily share elsewhere.

It remains strongly available to yuri interpretation.

Reina’s explicit romantic desire for Taki remains equally real. The novel permits emotional and erotic ambiguity rather than treating one attachment as proof that another is insignificant.

## Asuka and Kaori

Kaori’s devotion is one of the strongest unreciprocated or asymmetrically reciprocated attachments in the volume.

The shoelace scene, embrace, constant worry, and Asuka’s sensual joking about Kaori’s neck all contribute yuri-coded charge.

Yet Asuka guards the relationship. Kaori receives affection but limited access.

## Kumiko and Asuka

Their bond is not framed straightforwardly as romance, but it is intensely intimate.

Kumiko:

- is chosen as Asuka’s confidante;
- hears the private composition;
- crosses Asuka’s emotional boundary;
- becomes the recipient of the notebook;
- and fears saying goodbye.

Asuka repeatedly touches Kumiko’s head, hides her blushing face, and entrusts her with continuation.

The relationship is simultaneously:

- senior–junior;
- instrumental lineage;
- personal recognition;
- and deep emotional attachment.

Its power comes partly from resisting easy classification.

# 23. Visual paratext

## Original cover

The cover abandons the broader ensemble composition of the earlier volumes and centers Kumiko and Asuka.

Kumiko stands in the foreground, looking upward and away. Asuka towers behind her, holding the silver euphonium close to her body.

The composition communicates:

- Kumiko’s movement toward Asuka;
- Asuka’s apparent scale and authority;
- the emotional distance between them;
- and their shared relationship to the instrument.

Asuka appears larger, but her expression is subdued rather than triumphant. The euphonium is partly concealed behind text and bodies, matching the novel’s gradual revelation of what the instrument means privately.

The bright blue background conflicts productively with the volume’s family violence, grief, anxiety, and defeat. As before, the series packages emotionally severe material through youthful visual brightness.

## Character-reference pages

The reference pages are valuable for confirming:

- school years;
- instruments;
- ensemble roles;
- Kumiko and Reina’s competition history;
- Shuuichi’s trombone;
- Haruka’s baritone saxophone;
- Asuka’s vice-presidency and low-brass leadership;
- Natsuki’s B-ensemble result;
- and the instructors’ specialties.

The pages visually preserve the ensemble as a network even though the cover focuses on one dyad.

# 24. Adaptation baseline

A later anime comparison should pay special attention to the following.

## 1. The severity of Asuka’s home life

The staff-room scene must preserve:

- the mother’s claim of ownership;
- the slap;
- the immediate guilt collapse;
- and Asuka comforting the person who struck her.

Softening this into ordinary parental disagreement would substantially weaken Asuka’s psychology.

## 2. Haruka’s institutional diagnosis

“Asuka was not special; we made her special” is one of the volume’s governing ideas.

It explains both:

- Asuka’s isolation;
- and the band’s fragility.

## 3. Kumiko’s failed rational arguments

Kumiko does not win by logically proving Asuka wrong.

Asuka defeats every institutional argument.

Kumiko succeeds only when she makes the conversation personal.

That transition is essential.

## 4. Asuka’s accusation against Kumiko

The adaptation should retain Asuka’s insight that Kumiko repeatedly approaches emotional boundaries without crossing them.

This is the necessary pressure behind Kumiko’s breakthrough.

## 5. Mamiko as a structural mirror

Mamiko’s plot is not an unrelated family subplot.

Her confession about:

- performing the good daughter;
- using obedience to avoid responsibility;
- envying Kumiko;
- and choosing a risky future

directly enables Kumiko to confront Asuka.

## 6. Natsuki’s preparation

The annotated score and serious substitute training are crucial.

Without them, her “welcome home” risks appearing effortless. Its value depends upon the real opportunity she surrenders.

## 7. Kaori’s physical devotion

The shoelace, food, waiting, and embrace reveal a relationship that dialogue alone does not articulate.

## 8. Taki’s wife and Italian White

The flower motif connects:

- enduring grief;
- proposal;
- Shuuichi’s gift;
- and the question of continuing to think about someone across absence.

## 9. The difference between Kitauji and Seira

An audiovisual adaptation can make the distinction audible.

Kitauji should sound genuinely accomplished—not secretly poor—while Seira should demonstrate a further level of stability and communicative authority.

## 10. National bronze

The bronze result must feel both:

- devastating;
- and compatible with pride in the performance.

The climax depends upon separating artistic truth from institutional ranking.

## 11. Shindō’s two judgments

His C score and personal praise must remain distinct.

He does not award professional favoritism. He does recognize his daughter’s sound.

## 12. The notebook

The epilogue’s transfer is the narrative origin of the series title and the clearest symbol of Kumiko becoming a senior.

# 27. Provisional questions for later volumes

## Kumiko as a senior

The notebook explicitly gives her a future responsibility.

Can she become the kind of senior who:

- hears a junior’s hidden desire;
- supports without controlling;
- and avoids reproducing the mythology of the invulnerable exceptional person?

## Life after Asuka

Kumiko can no longer rely upon Asuka’s euphonium beside her.

Will she:

- imitate Asuka;
- become overwhelmed by comparison;
- or develop a distinctly Kumiko-like form of section leadership?

## Institutional continuity

Kitauji must now replace:

- Haruka;
- Asuka;
- Kaori;
- and other third-years

while carrying national expectations.

Can the band preserve seriousness after the personalities who produced the transformation have left?

## The cost of success

Asuka predicts that every achievement will become the next minimum.

Will Kitauji’s ambition become:

- healthy tradition;
- coercive expectation;
- or both?

## Taki’s dream after bronze

The wife’s national-gold dream remains unfulfilled.

Does Taki treat the next generation as another chance, and can he do so without turning students into instruments of private grief?

## Reina and distance

Reina is already considering an overseas conservatory.

How will Kumiko respond to a relationship whose future contains geographic separation and unequal artistic trajectories?

## Shuuichi and explicit romance

The epilogue confirms movement beyond friendship, but the central story has shown how poorly both communicate romantic desire.

Can the relationship become more direct, or will familiarity remain another form of evasion?

## Mamiko’s adult path

Mamiko has chosen hairstyling, but the novel has carefully refused to guarantee success.

How will she live with a decision that can no longer be blamed upon her parents?

## Asuka after graduation

Asuka gives away the notebook and says she no longer needs it.

This does not prove she has abandoned the euphonium. It does suggest that the instrument’s paternal message has completed one phase.

What place will music occupy once it no longer needs to function as secret contact, resistance, or proof?

# Final assessment

Volume 3 is the strongest and most complete of the opening trilogy.

Volume 1 establishes the cast and gives Kumiko a clean awakening into desire.

Volume 2 complicates merit, friendship, and competition by revealing that correct choices can still wound and that emotional attachment is rarely symmetrical.

Volume 3 brings those concerns together inside the question of self-authorship:

> Who owns a life?  
> What does another person’s sacrifice entitle them to decide?  
> When does responsibility become self-erasure?  
> Can inherited desire become authentic?  
> Is a person valuable because she is needed, or because someone wants her there?  
> What remains when a fully committed performance still loses?

Asuka is the ideal character through whom to ask these questions because she appears to possess everything Kumiko lacks:

- confidence;
- intelligence;
- musical authority;
- social command;
- and mature perspective.

The novel reveals that this maturity is partly an adaptation to coercion.

She appears independent because dependency has been dangerous.  
She appears selfless because desire produces guilt.  
She appears neutral because hidden investment feels safer than open hope.  
She appears invulnerable because other people need her to remain functional.

Kumiko does not save Asuka by becoming wiser than her.

She reaches her by becoming less sophisticated.

She says:

> I do not care what the optimal solution is.  
> I want you.  
> I want your sound.  
> I do not want you to choose regret and call it adulthood.

That speech is the culmination of Kumiko’s first-year arc. The girl who began by avoiding strong desire now permits desire to make her impolite, irrational, intrusive, and vulnerable.

The national bronze then prevents that growth from becoming a simplistic triumph.

Kitauji plays well.  
Asuka returns.  
Shindō hears her.  
Mamiko attends.  
Reina gives everything.  
Kumiko wants the music never to end.

They still lose.

For the first time, Kumiko understands Reina’s original tears from inside:

> 悔しい。

That frustration is not evidence that the year failed.

It is proof that the year mattered.

Shindō’s response provides the volume’s most elegant final distinction. Professionally, he gives Kitauji a C. Personally, he tells the euphonium player that her tone was beautiful and that she did well to continue.

The competition measures the ensemble’s present national standing.

The message recognizes a life.

Finally, Asuka gives Kumiko the old notebook. The composition passes from absent father to daughter, from senior to junior, and potentially from Kumiko to students not yet known.

The title is no longer merely a command to make an instrument audible.

It becomes an ethic:

> **Let what was given to you resonate beyond you.  
> Do not preserve it by remaining the same.  
> Carry it into another relationship, another year, another player.**

The first movement of *Sound! Euphonium* therefore ends not with national gold, but with a warmer and more durable achievement:

> A girl discovers what she wants.  
> She learns that wanting creates pain.  
> She finally speaks before certainty can protect her.  
> And she inherits a sound that now belongs to the future.
