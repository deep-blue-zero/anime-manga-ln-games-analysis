# What kind of volume this is

Volume 10 is simultaneously:

- the conclusion of Kumiko’s third year;
- the resolution of the Mayu–Kumiko conflict;
- the culmination of Kitauji’s national-gold project;
- the completion of Kumiko’s movement from observer to leader;
- the answer to her unresolved future;
- and the closing of the principal narrative circle begun in Volume 1.

Its most important achievement is not that Kitauji wins national gold.

That result matters enormously, but the novel has spent nine volumes proving that:

- gold is not an absolute measure of human worth;
- technical judgment cannot contain the total meaning of music;
- hard work does not create guaranteed entitlement;
- and a disappointing result does not retroactively make the performance false.

Volume 10 therefore earns the right to celebrate victory without converting victory into its complete moral argument.

The deeper climax occurs before the final audition, when Kumiko stands before all 103 members and tells them:

- not to surrender their places for someone else’s convenience;
- not to conceal withdrawal beneath the language of kindness;
- not to allow another person to take ownership of their regret;
- and not to treat effort as a performance staged to satisfy surrounding judgment.

Her central formulation is:

> 「努力は、周りを納得させるためにするんじゃない。自分が納得するためにするものだって、私は思ってます」  
>  
> Effort is not something you make in order to satisfy the people around you. It is something you make so that you can satisfy yourself.

That sentence completes Kumiko’s original arc.

Volume 1 began with a girl who did not want strongly enough to admit disappointment.

Volume 10 gives us a president who tells an entire institution that disappointment belongs to the person willing to risk it.

# Executive thesis

Volume 9 asked whether Kumiko could defend merit when merit threatened her own place.

Volume 10 asks a harder question:

> **Can Kumiko lead people whose reasons for participating remain fundamentally different from hers?**

Mayu does not secretly want the euphonium soli as intensely as Kumiko does.

She is not:

- concealing a Reina-like competitive drive;
- waiting for permission to become ambitious;
- or suppressing a true desire beneath excessive politeness.

Her repeatedly stated position is largely genuine.

Mayu values concert band because it gives her:

- friends;
- shared time;
- photographs;
- memories;
- and a temporary social world worth inhabiting.

For her, competition is secondary.

She practices seriously because everyone else practices seriously.

She performs well because she possesses skill and respects the people beside her.

She does not need the result to validate her identity.

Mayu’s central statement is:

> The time itself has value.

This places her far outside Kitauji’s inherited emotional grammar.

For Kumiko, music has become inseparable from:

- frustration;
- ambition;
- self-definition;
- Reina;
- Asuka;
- leadership;
- and the desire to become someone capable of standing proudly inside public judgment.

Kumiko initially assumes that Mayu’s willingness to withdraw must conceal:

- fear;
- self-protection;
- or unrealized desire.

She applies the interpretive tools that worked upon:

- Asuka;
- Kanade;
- Hazuki;
- Nozomi;
- and herself.

They fail.

Mayu is not waiting for Kumiko to discover the ambitious “real Mayu.”

Mayu’s support for Kumiko is itself real.

Her devastating question is:

> 「久美子ちゃんの応援をしたいっていう私の本音は、無視されちゃうの？」  
>  
> Is my sincere wish to support you going to be ignored?

Kumiko cannot answer because Mayu has exposed the hidden coercion inside Kumiko’s theory of honest desire.

Kumiko claims:

> Everyone should act according to what she truly wants.

But what Kumiko actually means is:

> Everyone should want enough to compete fully.

Volume 10 does not solve this contradiction by declaring Kumiko wrong and Mayu right.

A collective ensemble cannot function if every individual withdraws whenever participation becomes socially inconvenient.

Nor can a fair audition remain fair if talented players:

- intentionally fail;
- withdraw preemptively;
- or ask others to determine whether their presence is emotionally acceptable.

The novel’s mature synthesis is:

> **People do not need identical motives.  
> They do need to act honestly inside the shared structure they have chosen to inhabit.**

Mayu may play for memories.

Reina may play for professional excellence.

Kumiko may play because music has become the language through which she understands herself.

Hazuki may play because participation is joyful.

Motomu may play because music preserves his sister and gives him a place where he is not his grandfather’s appendage.

Sally may play while feeling responsible for everyone around her.

All of them can still produce one ensemble.

Kumiko’s leadership breakthrough is not making these motives uniform.

It is naming a common action:

> Give everything now.  
> Let the result come afterward.  
> Keep your own regret.  
> Do not surrender it to someone else.

Volume 10’s central thesis can therefore be stated as:

> **An ensemble does not require everyone to want the same thing.  
> It requires each person’s distinct desire—or lack of desire—to enter the shared breath honestly enough that one sound can be made.**

# 1. Completing the final movement

Volumes 9 and 10 form one continuous argument.

## Volume 9: legitimacy becomes uncertain

Part One establishes:

- Mayu as the talented outsider;
- Kumiko’s fear of replacement;
- repeated auditions;
- Sally’s critique of equal severity;
- Mirei’s challenge to inherited faith in Taki;
- the band’s social preference for Kumiko;
- and Kumiko’s inability to trust her first soli victory completely.

The Kyoto result allows the conflict to remain temporarily suspended.

## Volume 10: legitimacy must be reconstructed

Part Two intensifies every instability.

- Mayu receives the Kansai soli.
- Kanade loses her A-ensemble place.
- Kumiko’s supporters become more vocal.
- Mayu becomes increasingly isolated.
- the band divides into emotional camps;
- Reina treats doubt as betrayal;
- Shuuichi treats excessive severity as institutional harm;
- Taki’s changing ensemble choices appear opaque;
- and Kumiko loses confidence in her own ability to mediate.

The novel then reconstructs legitimacy through:

- direct speech;
- acknowledged imperfection;
- personal ownership of desire;
- acceptance of differentiated motives;
- and a final audition in which all participants are asked to perform fully.

The final result is accepted not because every student receives what she wants, but because the process is no longer being protected through concealed sacrifice.

# 2. The musical chapter architecture

The three chapter titles form a precise emotional score.

## 「駆け出すオブリガート」 — Rushing Obbligato

An **obbligato** is an independent accompanying line whose presence is necessary to the musical whole.

It is not the principal melody.

It is not disposable background.

The chapter disperses attention among people whose lines exist beside Kumiko’s:

- Mayu;
- Motomu;
- Sally;
- the B ensemble;
- Hazuki;
- Tsubame;
- Mizore;
- Nozomi;
- Yuuko;
- Natsuki;
- Kumiko’s family;
- and the students beginning to choose post-school futures.

Each line starts moving.

Some characters run toward:

- music school;
- teaching;
- childcare;
- performance;
- university;
- or adult independence.

Others run away from:

- family identity;
- fixed self-image;
- competition;
- or the fear of being left.

The title announces that the final movement will not belong to one melody alone.

## 「悩めるオスティナート」 — Troubled Ostinato

An **ostinato** is a persistent repeated figure.

The chapter is governed by emotional repetition:

- Mayu repeatedly offers to withdraw.
- Kumiko repeatedly refuses without understanding why Mayu asks.
- Kanade repeatedly warns Kumiko.
- students repeatedly debate whether Taki is right;
- Reina repeatedly returns to absolute trust;
- Kumiko repeatedly conceals her own fear;
- auditions repeat;
- and the same unresolved questions return in new forms.

The institutional atmosphere becomes trapped in a loop.

Each attempt to protect harmony produces more tension.

## 「つながるメロディー」 — Connecting Melody

The final chapter does not eliminate the independent lines.

It connects them.

Connection occurs through:

- Motomu naming Kitauji as his chosen home;
- Asuka and Kaori living together beyond graduation;
- Shuuichi waiting for Kumiko;
- Kumiko writing honestly in the executive notebook;
- Kumiko and Reina stating their love directly;
- Kumiko speaking publicly to all 103 members;
- Mayu entering the group photograph;
- Asuka’s secret piece becoming a section inheritance;
- Kumiko and Reina performing the soli;
- and the full band transforming one year into a collective musical biography.

The melody connects because the separate voices remain audible.

# 3. Prologue: Motomu before the grave

The volume begins not with competition, but with death.

Motomu stands before his family grave.

The newest inscription belongs to his sister:

> 月永満

She died three years earlier.

## Music as assumed inheritance

Motomu grew up in a family where music was treated as natural.

- both parents teach music;
- his grandfather is a famous conductor;
- piano instruction begins early;
- scores and recordings fill the home;
- and his older sister loves music.

In such an environment, liking music is not merely personal.

It is a family expectation.

## The grave as fixed identity

The grave records:

- name;
- date;
- age;
- and lineage.

Motomu’s entire third-year-adjacent arc concerns resistance to identities fixed by family history.

Others see:

> Tsukinaga Genichirō’s grandson.

Motomu wants to be:

> himself.

Yet he plays the contrabass partly because his sister played it.

He rejects one inheritance while carrying another.

## The prologue’s function

Beginning at the grave establishes the volume’s mature theory of continuation.

The dead do not continue by controlling the living.

They continue through what the living choose to carry.

This principle will later govern:

- Motomu and his sister;
- Taki and his wife;
- Asuka and Shindō;
- Kumiko and Asuka;
- Kumiko’s graduating class and the cherry tree;
- and adult Kumiko’s return to Kitauji.

# 4. The B ensemble and the right to have a complete experience

The first major event is the B-ensemble competition.

Forty-eight students perform Lehár’s *The Merry Widow* Overture under Miechie’s direction.

They receive gold.

## More than consolation

The B ensemble is not framed as a holding area for those who failed the real audition.

Its students receive:

- rehearsal;
- an actual conductor;
- a public stage;
- a result;
- and the experience of completing a performance together.

## Yayoi’s response

Yayoi says that performing feels extraordinary.

Her response matters more than the medal.

She has moved from:

- beginner;
- to participating musician;
- to someone who has experienced the pleasure of public ensemble sound.

## Miechie’s tears

Miechie explains that an instructor knows how hard students have worked.

Watching them finish smiling is emotionally overwhelming.

The scene establishes a standard against which the later A-ensemble conflict should be judged:

> A good institutional result should not require the unsuccessful members to experience themselves as discarded material.

## The complete Kitauji

The final national performance will contain fifty-five players.

The institutional achievement belongs to 103.

Volume 10 repeatedly resists identifying Kitauji only with the bodies on the competition stage.

# 5. Mayu’s actual philosophy of band

The novel’s most important revelation is that Mayu has been telling the truth.

She says:

- she enjoys being with friends;
- she enjoys creating memories;
- she enjoys playing with others;
- and the result does not determine whether the experience was worthwhile.

Her commitment is to **time**, not rank.

## “Memory-making”

Mayu calls band a form of memory-making.

This can initially sound dismissive beside Kitauji’s national ambition.

It is not shallow.

Repeated transfers have taught Mayu that shared environments end.

She therefore values:

- festivals;
- trips;
- photographs;
- ordinary rehearsal;
- and the period during which people happen to be together.

A medal is one object inside that period.

It is not the period’s justification.

## Seriousness without result-attachment

Mayu still:

- practices;
- performs accurately;
- cooperates with the ensemble;
- and accepts difficult roles.

Her skill is genuine.

She does not believe skill obliges her to hunger for victory in Reina’s sense.

This is one of the series’ most important distinctions:

> **A person can be serious about the activity without treating competitive success as the activity’s final meaning.**

---

# 6. Mayu’s interpretation of Liz

At the pool, Mayu comments upon *Liz and the Blue Bird*.

Tsubame dislikes the separation.

Mayu proposes that Liz was greedy.

Liz possessed:

- animals;
- a home;
- and other possible sources of connection.

Yet she concentrated too much meaning upon the blue bird.

If Liz had wanted less, the departure would have hurt less.

## A survival philosophy

Mayu’s reading emerges from repeated relocation.

The safest way to survive separation is:

- enjoy what is present;
- avoid turning it into the whole world;
- and retain enough emotional mobility to leave.

## The cost

The philosophy protects Mayu from Mizore’s level of collapse.

It also prevents her from making the full claim:

> This place is mine.  
> I want to remain.  
> I want to be remembered inside it.

Mayu does not build cages.

She also resists building homes.

## Kumiko’s difference

Kumiko increasingly believes that the possibility of pain is not a reason to want less.

Her final-year philosophy is almost the opposite:

> Want enough that the result can hurt.  
> Preserve the hurt as evidence that the experience belonged to you.

The Mayu–Kumiko conflict is therefore not merely musical rivalry.

It is a conflict between two survival ethics.

---

# 7. The photographer absent from the photograph

Mayu constantly takes pictures.

She records:

- the pool;
- the school trip;
- culture festival;
- competitions;
- friendships;
- and ordinary group life.

She dislikes appearing in photographs herself.

## Preserving everyone else

Mayu is excellent at making other people’s belonging visible.

She captures:

- physical closeness;
- shared laughter;
- awkward expressions;
- and temporary social arrangements.

## Refusing fixed selfhood

A photograph says:

> I was here.

Mayu is uncomfortable with that evidence.

The text does not reduce the discomfort to one simple diagnosis, but its relationship to her transfer history is clear.

She has repeatedly become:

- a temporary classmate;
- a temporary band member;
- a temporary local friend;
- and the person who later leaves the frame.

Remaining behind the camera makes departure easier.

## The arc of inclusion

The volume gradually moves Mayu into photographs.

- Kaho photographs Kumiko, Kanade, Mayu, and herself.
- Tsubame photographs Mayu.
- Kumiko asks Mayu to join.
- the graduating students allow their images to become physical evidence of shared time.

Mayu does not need to become competitive in order to belong.

She does need to permit the group to remember that she belonged.

---

# 8. “Mama” as portable social role

Mayu’s old nickname is “Mama.”

The name emerges because she naturally:

- carries useful objects;
- repairs things;
- anticipates needs;
- creates comfort;
- and makes herself nonthreatening.

## Usefulness as entry method

Mayu can enter almost any group by becoming helpful.

The role requires little reciprocal demand.

She can care for others without asking them to reorganize around her.

## The danger

Maternal usefulness may become another version of Asuka’s necessity.

A person can become indispensable as a function while remaining emotionally unclaimed.

Mayu does not consciously manipulate through care.

Her care still protects her from the vulnerability of asking:

> Do you want me here even when I am not making things easier?

---

# 9. Kumiko and Mayu as distorted mirrors

Kumiko is frustrated by Mayu because they share several traits.

Both can:

- read social atmosphere;
- adjust language;
- conceal discomfort;
- anticipate other people’s reactions;
- and occupy a mediating role.

Their differences make the similarities painful.

## Kumiko

Kumiko eventually learned to:

- want;
- speak;
- interfere;
- and accept that strong desire may be ugly.

## Mayu

Mayu has built a life around remaining adaptable.

She sees no moral value in creating unnecessary emotional damage merely to prove strength of will.

## Kumiko’s fear

Mayu threatens Kumiko not only because she can take the soli.

Mayu resembles a version of Kumiko who never met Reina, never cried over competition, and never learned to treat desire as worth the danger.

Kumiko wants to awaken Mayu partly because Mayu’s philosophy challenges the meaning Kumiko has assigned to her own development.

# 10. The Kansai audition: Mayu receives the soli

At summer camp, Taki announces the new A ensemble.

The major changes are:

- Kumiko and Mayu remain;
- Kanade falls out of A;
- all four major tuba players enter;
- and Mayu receives the euphonium part of the third-movement soli with Reina.

## Kumiko’s public response

Kumiko insists that the seating arrangement reflect the result.

Mayu should occupy first chair.

Mayu attempts to leave Kumiko’s old seat unchanged.

Kumiko refuses.

## The warm seat

The chair still carries Kumiko’s body heat.

Mayu inherits not an abstract place but a position Kumiko occupied moments earlier.

The physical detail makes replacement almost unbearably literal.

## No hidden injustice

The novel does not reveal that Mayu cheated, manipulated, or received favoritism.

Her playing is excellent.

Taki’s judgment is musically defensible.

That is why the result hurts.

---

# 11. Hearing Mayu and Reina

Taki repeatedly rehearses the new soli.

Kumiko must sit nearby and listen to Mayu perform the passage she wanted to share with Reina.

## The duet is good

The novel does not protect Kumiko by making the new pairing visibly inferior.

Mayu and Reina produce:

- balance;
- technical control;
- dialogue;
- and a convincing musical relationship.

## Replacement at the level of function

Kumiko must confront the fact that another euphonium can answer Reina’s trumpet.

The shared part was never proof that only Kumiko could perform the notes.

## Non-replacement at the level of person

The rest of the volume separates:

- musical function;
- from relational significance.

Mayu can occupy the soli.

She cannot retroactively become:

- the girl on Daikichiyama;
- the person Reina first selected;
- the friend invited into Reina’s home;
- or the history carried between Kumiko and Reina.

Kaori will later give Kumiko the language required to understand this.

---

# 12. Kanade’s removal from A

Kanade loses her A-ensemble place.

The result confirms that repeated auditions create real precarity.

## Kanade’s acceptance

She does not intentionally fail.

She does not accuse Mayu of stealing the role illegitimately.

She accepts that Mayu is better.

This demonstrates the permanence of her Volume 6 development.

## Her anger remains

Kanade’s acceptance of musical judgment does not require emotional neutrality.

She remains:

- angry for Kumiko;
- suspicious of Mayu;
- and frustrated by the social consequences of Mayu’s self-effacement.

## The absence beside Kumiko

At the Kansai venue, Kumiko notices that Kanade is not there.

The empty relational space matters independently of the instrumentation.

One audition decision changes not only sound, but daily life.

---

# 13. Mayu as the jellyfish

Kanade describes Mayu as a jellyfish.

The metaphor is exact.

A jellyfish can be:

- beautiful;
- translucent;
- apparently passive;
- and painful to touch.

## Unintentional injury

Mayu does not intend to hurt Kumiko.

Her repeated offers to withdraw still sting.

Each offer implies:

- Kumiko cannot defeat her honestly;
- the institution may prefer social peace over full ability;
- and Mayu can casually surrender what Kumiko desperately wants.

## Drifting

Mayu appears to move wherever the environment carries her.

Kanade does not trust that passivity.

She suspects that a person who never claims direction may still control everyone who tries to interpret her.

## The limitation of the metaphor

Mayu is not empty.

The later confrontation proves that she has a coherent philosophy.

The problem is that her values are so different from Kumiko’s that her genuine statements sound like evasions.

---

# 14. Kanade’s self-recognition

Kanade’s hostility toward Mayu is partly hostility toward her former self.

Kanade once said:

- she would fail intentionally;
- Natsuki should receive the place;
- and social peace mattered more than honest judgment.

Mayu now proposes something similar with far less visible anguish.

## The apparent invalidation

Mayu’s behavior seems to Kanade to invalidate the intervention through which Kumiko and Natsuki rescued her.

If withdrawal can be a sincere and reasonable choice, what did Kanade’s earlier pain mean?

## Kanade’s mature uncertainty

Kanade eventually admits that she does not know whether she wants Kumiko to receive the soli because:

- Kumiko is musically better;
- or because she loves Kumiko.

This is a major development.

The girl who once claimed pure analytical objectivity recognizes that affection affects judgment.

## Subjectivity does not erase evaluation

Kanade continues to judge Mayu’s playing accurately.

Her acknowledgment of partiality does not require giving up discernment.

It makes the discernment more honest.

---

# 15. The first Kumiko–Reina rupture

Kumiko and Reina disagree over Taki’s selection and the band’s growing doubt.

## Reina’s position

Reina argues that:

- Taki’s judgment must be trusted;
- doubt in the conductor will destroy ensemble unity;
- Mayu received the soli because she played better in that audition;
- and insufficient results reflect insufficient execution by the players.

## Kumiko’s position

Kumiko argues that:

- judgments occur in context;
- effort has limits imposed by circumstances;
- not every person can be held to Reina’s personal standard;
- and questioning authority is not identical to betrayal.

## Kaori and Reina as precedent

Kumiko reminds Reina that even the famous solo conflict depended upon who happened to audition.

Had another player entered, the supposedly clear merit hierarchy might have changed.

Merit is real.

Its expression remains contextual.

## The breaking line

Reina tells Kumiko:

> 「だったら、部長失格やな」  
> Then you are unfit to be president.

The sentence wounds Kumiko because it attacks the identity she has spent the year trying to inhabit.

## “See you tomorrow”

They do not end the relationship.

They part with an expectation of tomorrow.

That detail distinguishes conflict from abandonment.

---

# 16. Conflict as proof of a survivable relationship

Kanade tells Kumiko that a relationship in which disagreement is forbidden is distorted.

A person does not oppose a god.

The statement applies to:

- Kumiko and Reina;
- students and Taki;
- children and family authority;
- and the band’s relation to its inherited goal.

## Earlier Kumiko and Reina

They once believed they did not need the “big love hug.”

Their understanding appeared immediate and wordless.

## Volume 10’s correction

Wordless understanding has limits.

Two people can be:

- mutually special;
- musically intimate;
- and emotionally devoted

while disagreeing over central principles.

The mature relationship is not the one without collision.

It is the one capable of returning after collision.

---

# 17. Taki under question

Kumiko asks Taki whether expanding the low brass and reducing euphoniums reflects the music he truly wants.

She suspects that he is optimizing for contest success rather than creating freely.

## Taki’s answer

Taki rejects a simple opposition between:

- authentic music;
- and contest-specialized music.

Every ensemble constructs what it believes is good.

He acknowledges that he possesses particular ability to:

- aim sound toward competitive criteria;
- develop students quickly;
- and produce results.

He also genuinely enjoys hearing their improvement.

## Kumiko’s displaced fear

Kumiko realizes that some of her institutional questioning has been carrying personal anxiety.

She fears criticism of her own soli.

She uses wider band concerns partly to avoid naming that fear.

## The candy

Taki gives Kumiko a candy and asks her to keep it secret.

The gesture humanizes him.

He is not only:

- conductor;
- evaluator;
- or embodiment of Kitauji’s standard.

He is an awkward young teacher trying to comfort an overwhelmed student with the small object available to him.

---

# 18. Taki is not a god

Asuka later states the principle directly.

Taki is:

- talented;
- hardworking;
- effective;
- young;
- imperfect;
- and sometimes poor at human relations.

During Asuka’s time, she filled several gaps in his management.

## Why he still deserves trust

Taki’s legitimacy does not come from infallibility.

It comes from continued labor.

He:

- arrives early;
- studies rival ensembles;
- redesigns rehearsal;
- listens;
- reconsiders;
- invites external instruction;
- and accepts responsibility for decisions affecting students’ lives.

## Informed trust

Kumiko’s final position is neither Reina’s original faith nor Mirei’s pure skepticism.

It is:

> Taki can be wrong.  
> Taki is still worthy of being followed because he works seriously toward a purpose the members chose.

This is more durable than charismatic worship.

---

# 19. The conductor as human learner

Kumiko and Reina later find Taki studying Ryūsei’s previous national performance.

He praises Genichirō’s work and says it reminds him of his own immaturity.

This image is decisive.

## The teacher still listens

Taki’s expertise does not end his education.

The conductor who transformed Kitauji remains:

- a student of other conductors;
- a colleague;
- and a person attempting to improve.

## Hashimoto’s challenge

Hashimoto has asked whether result is all that remains.

He warns that pushing children toward competitive outcome can become adult ego.

Taki carries the criticism.

## The ideal teacher

Taki says that when he became a teacher, he decided to approximate the teacher he would have wanted as a child.

His model is aspirational rather than complete.

That distinction makes Kumiko’s later choice meaningful.

She does not want to become a perfect authority.

She wants to become an adult who keeps trying to deserve the trust placed in her.

---

# 20. Sally’s support and the birth of a faction

Sally tells Kumiko that she wants her to perform the soli at nationals.

Other students express similar support.

Their affection strengthens Kumiko.

It also isolates Mayu.

## Personal loyalty becoming institutional force

Each statement is individually kind.

Collectively, the statements create a social atmosphere:

> Kumiko is the person Kitauji wants.

Mayu may be an accepted member.

She is not the emotional candidate of the majority.

## Kumiko’s incomplete response

Kumiko knows she should tell the students to support Mayu as well.

She cannot bring herself to interrupt their enthusiasm.

She enjoys being chosen.

## The honest flaw

The novel does not demand that Kumiko become selflessly indifferent.

It requires her eventually to admit that she wants the part.

Her moral failure lies less in wanting support than in pretending no opposing camp exists.

Kanade sees the change clearly:

> Kumiko has become better at lying since becoming president.

---

# 21. The Kansai performance

Kitauji performs extremely well.

Mayu and Reina’s soli succeeds.

Kumiko participates from another chair.

The band receives gold and advances to nationals alongside:

- Meisei Technical;
- and Ryūsei Academy.

## “It connected”

Kumiko’s first dominant emotion is relief.

The seniors’ promise has been carried forward.

The second-year failure did not become the end of the road.

## Success does not solve the internal conflict

Kansai qualification proves that:

- Mayu’s selection did not destroy the music;
- Reina’s rigor produced results;
- and Taki’s ensemble design worked.

It does not prove that:

- the process was emotionally harmless;
- doubt was illegitimate;
- or the final national lineup should remain unchanged.

The repeated-audition system guarantees another judgment.

---

# 22. Motomu: “I am a Kitauji person”

At the culture festival, Higuchi reveals Motomu’s family history.

Motomu later explains the truth directly.

## The famous surname

Motomu hates being treated as:

> Genichirō’s grandson.

Adults approach him through:

- his grandfather’s prestige;
- possible influence;
- and imagined access.

What appears to others as enviable lineage becomes a denial of individual identity.

## Benevolent pressure

Genichirō cares for Motomu.

That care does not automatically help.

Motomu states one of the volume’s central propositions:

> Good intentions do not necessarily save the recipient. They can corner someone.

This applies far beyond his family.

It describes:

- Mayu’s withdrawal;
- Kumiko’s pressure upon Mayu;
- Suzume’s protection of Tsubame;
- Reina’s severity;
- Shuuichi’s comfort;
- and the entire adult project of directing students.

## Why Kitauji

Motomu knew Taki’s family history.

He recognized someone who also lived beneath the name of a famous musical relative and carried the death of a loved person.

He believed Taki would not treat him specially.

## His sister

Motomu chose contrabass because his sister played it.

She jokingly invited him to call her “Master.”

Midori resembles her.

Motomu’s attachment to Midori therefore contains:

- admiration;
- grief;
- repetition;
- and the hope of preserving a lost relational form.

## The identity claim

Motomu says:

> 「僕は、北宇治の人間です」  
> I am a Kitauji person.

This is his strongest declaration in the series.

Kitauji is not merely the school he chose to spite his grandfather.

It is the place where he believes he can exist as himself.

---

# 23. Higuchi and intrusive friendship

Higuchi sees himself as Motomu’s closest friend.

Motomu sees him as:

- thoughtless;
- intrusive;
- and prone to converting private pain into social intervention.

Both interpretations contain truth.

## Higuchi’s motive

He visits Kitauji because he wants:

- Motomu and Genichirō to reconcile;
- Kitauji not to misunderstand Motomu;
- and his friend’s choices to be recognized as genuine.

## The violation

He reveals:

- Motomu’s sister’s death;
- family conflict;
- and private history

without permission.

Concern does not grant authority over another person’s story.

## Kumiko’s role

Kumiko arranges a direct meeting.

She acknowledges afterward that this may have been another act of overreach.

Her leadership repeatedly places her in the ambiguous position of creating conversations other people have avoided.

The series does not guarantee that intervention is always correct.

It does insist that some relationships cannot change while all parties communicate only through intermediaries.

---

# 24. Midori and Motomu

Motomu tells Midori his history privately.

She cries.

She does not turn the revelation into permanent pity.

## Midori as chosen master

Motomu tests whether Midori can receive the truth without changing the entire relationship.

She responds by remaining recognizably herself:

- affectionate;
- enthusiastic;
- musically serious;
- and unwilling to let grief erase the future.

## Graduation fear

When Midori’s post-school future becomes certain, Motomu cries at the thought that she will leave.

She plays Elgar’s *Salut d’Amour* with him—the piece through which their relationship first gained musical form.

## Midori’s correction

Motomu wants time to stop.

Midori tells him that remaining in place will lead nowhere.

He must enjoy what comes next.

This is the healthier answer to Mayu’s desire to avoid painful attachment and Mizore’s old desire to keep one moment forever:

> preserve the memory;  
> continue moving.

---

# 25. Alumni as possible futures

Kumiko attends Mizore’s university concert.

The scene displays multiple post-Kitauji lives.

## Mizore

Mizore performs inside an orchestral environment and receives a prominent oboe passage.

Her sound now exists beyond:

- Kitauji;
- competition;
- and Nozomi’s immediate validation.

## Nozomi

Nozomi continues playing in an orchestra club.

She expresses pride in Mizore alongside a residual undertone of comparison.

When offered a bird-shaped accessory, she declines:

> She is fine as she is now.

The blue-bird identity no longer needs to govern her appearance or future.

## Mizore still runs to Nozomi

Mizore seeks Nozomi after the performance.

The attachment remains.

The difference is that Mizore also:

- thanks everyone;
- has university peers;
- and possesses a professional trajectory.

Nozomi is central without remaining total.

## Yuuko and Natsuki

They play together in a university band:

- Natsuki on bass;
- Yuuko as guitarist and vocalist.

Their music has changed form.

Their argumentative intimacy remains.

## The lesson for Kumiko

Graduation does not produce one correct relationship to music.

Music may become:

- profession;
- university activity;
- another instrument;
- hobby;
- teaching;
- or memory.

Kitauji is an origin, not a prison.

---

# 26. Future choice and the fear of an ordinary life

Kumiko struggles to choose a university path.

She is surrounded by people who appear more certain:

- Reina chooses professional music abroad.
- Midori chooses fashion.
- Hazuki chooses childcare education.
- Mizore pursues high-level performance.
- Mamiko chooses hairdressing.
- Shuuichi has a local university plan.

## Kumiko’s anxiety

She believes a worthy future should arrive as vocation.

Nothing calls her with Reina’s certainty.

## Her mother’s answer

Most adults do not possess one transcendent calling.

They choose:

- stable work;
- tolerable conditions;
- and lives they can sustain.

## Mamiko’s insight

Kumiko’s high-school life has been unusually fulfilling.

The uncertainty afterward is partly the terror of believing that the best part of life may already be ending.

## Hazuki’s vocational intuition

Hazuki suggests that Kumiko might be suited to teaching.

The suggestion initially appears casual.

It recognizes something the protagonist cannot yet name:

> Kumiko has spent years learning how to hear people.

---

# 27. The troubled executive trio

Before the final audition, Team Oumae begins to fracture.

## Reina and Shuuichi

Reina accuses Shuuichi of:

- offering pleasant reassurance;
- avoiding difficult correction;
- and leaving the unpleasant leadership work to her.

Shuuichi accuses Reina of:

- intimidating students;
- overreacting to criticism of Taki;
- and treating technical weakness as loss of the right to speak.

## Two incomplete forms of care

Shuuichi risks care without demand.

Reina risks demand without sufficient accommodation.

The band needs both.

## Kumiko’s failed mediation

Kumiko initially tries to stop the argument.

Reina accuses her of smoothing over conflict rather than resolving it.

Kumiko realizes that she has frequently:

- borrowed the others’ positions;
- prevented full disagreement;
- and called the resulting calm successful leadership.

## Team Oumae’s real test

Distributed leadership does not mean the members naturally harmonize.

It means the institution must survive the moment their governing principles openly collide.

---

# 28. Reina’s fear politics

Reina’s monitoring becomes increasingly severe.

She reprimands students for criticizing Taki and declares that underprepared players should not bring weakness into ensemble rehearsal.

## The truth inside the severity

Reina understands that:

- national gold demands extraordinary standards;
- regret after the result will be irreversible;
- and tolerance of preventable mistakes can become collective failure.

## The institutional damage

Students begin:

- watching what they say;
- resenting correction;
- and treating ordinary frustration as dangerous speech.

Shuuichi calls this fear-based rule.

## The problem of technical correctness

Reina insists that she attacks mistakes, not personalities.

The recipient may still experience the correction through:

- shame;
- hierarchy;
- and public exposure.

The intention–effect gap cannot be resolved by declaring the statement technically impersonal.

---

# 29. Kumiko visits Asuka

Unable to solve the leadership crisis, Kumiko uses the one-time “magic ticket” Asuka gave her.

She travels to Asuka’s address.

Kaori answers the door.

## The magic has changed

Kumiko once treated Asuka as someone almost capable of revealing the correct answer to any emotional mechanism.

The adult Asuka remains:

- perceptive;
- theatrical;
- and capable of immediate diagnosis.

She cannot solve Kumiko’s presidency for her.

## Why the visit matters

The journey allows Kumiko to be a junior again briefly.

She can:

- be received;
- ask;
- listen;
- and occupy a room where someone else is the senior.

The relief also makes her realize that this position is no longer available inside Kitauji.

She must return as the person others seek.

---

# 30. Asuka and Kaori living together

Kaori and Asuka share an apartment.

Their daily interaction is relaxed and domestic.

Kaori:

- makes tea;
- manages groceries;
- knows Asuka’s favorite biscuits;
- teases her;
- and speaks as someone accustomed to her rhythms.

Asuka:

- accepts the arrangement;
- behaves childishly around Kaori;
- and permits forms of care she once kept at greater distance.

## The cohabitation request

Kaori admits that she pushed for the arrangement.

Kumiko correctly observes that Asuka would have refused if she did not want it.

## Source-level relational significance

This is among the strongest post-graduation Asuka–Kaori material in the novels.

Their bond contains:

- chosen cohabitation;
- domestic familiarity;
- mutual teasing;
- care labor;
- emotional priority;
- and continuity beyond the institution that first brought them together.

The text still does not formally label them as romantic partners.

It supplies an unmistakably intimate adult dyad.

---

# 31. Kaori’s theory of specialness

Kaori says that Haruka was right to reject the band’s treatment of Asuka as uniquely superhuman.

She nevertheless believes Asuka is special.

The apparent contradiction resolves through relationship.

> Everyone is special to someone.

Kaori says:

> Asuka is special to me, even if I am not special to Asuka in the same way.

## Role versus person

A role can be transferred.

A person’s relational history cannot.

- Mayu can receive the soli.
- Mayu cannot become Kumiko.
- Kumiko can later recover the soli.
- That does not make Mayu’s existence unnecessary.
- Reina may perform with several musicians.
- Her relationship with Kumiko remains its own history.

## The answer to replacement anxiety

Kumiko’s central fear has been:

> If Mayu can perform my role, perhaps my place never belonged to me.

Kaori supplies the distinction:

> Functional replaceability does not erase personal irreplaceability.

This principle appeared earlier in the series.

Volume 10 finally allows Kumiko to internalize it.

---

# 32. Asuka’s advice

Asuka asks the blunt question:

> If Mayu wants to withdraw and everyone would be happier, why not let her?

The question exposes Kumiko’s ego.

## Kumiko’s answer

She wants:

- honest competition;
- full participation;
- no villain;
- no coerced sacrifice;
- and a graduation everyone can remember without regret.

She wants more than procedural peace.

She wants the institution’s moral story to remain beautiful.

## Asuka’s analysis of results

Competitive success creates helpful atmosphere.

Results make:

- parents;
- school administration;
- students;
- and the surrounding community

more willing to cooperate.

This is one reason Taki is effective.

He understands how to make success reproduce institutional support.

## No perfect leader

Asuka reminds Kumiko that:

- Taki is imperfect;
- all adults are imperfect;
- and the absence of perfection does not make responsibility meaningless.

The question is not whether the leader lacks flaws.

It is whether the leader works honestly to compensate for them.

---

# 33. The limits of borrowed reassurance

Asuka tells Kumiko she can do it.

For a moment, Kumiko feels light.

On the way home, the reassurance fades.

## Why the “magic” fails

Kumiko is no longer the junior whose problem can be resolved by trusting a superior’s interpretation.

She is president.

The final decision cannot be outsourced to:

- Asuka;
- Kaori;
- Taki;
- Reina;
- or Shuuichi.

## Growth as loneliness

Becoming capable does not eliminate the wish to be carried.

It means recognizing that some choices remain yours even after everyone supports you.

The scene refuses the fantasy that mentorship permanently removes uncertainty.

---

# 34. Shuuichi waiting

Kumiko returns home to find Shuuichi waiting outside her building.

He has been concerned since the executive argument.

## “You do not need to work harder”

Shuuichi tells Kumiko that she is already working.

His role as vice-president is not to become:

- Asuka;
- Natsuki;
- or another spectacular senior.

He can remain with her when the burden becomes difficult.

## Why the kindness no longer has to weaken her

Kumiko ended their romance partly because she feared comfort would make leadership harder.

Shuuichi demonstrates another possibility:

> Support can make responsibility sustainable.

## Kumiko’s realization

She still loves him.

The feeling does not disappear because she chose institutional distance.

This prepares the final reconciliation after national gold.

---

# 35. The executive notebook as confession

Kumiko writes what she has not been able to say aloud.

She admits:

- she doubted Taki;
- she feared Mayu;
- she worried that her relation with Reina would be taken;
- she confused her presidential self with her performing self;
- and she benefited from the band’s support of her.

She also writes her emerging conclusion:

> Mayu performing the soli did not make Kumiko cease to be Kumiko.

## Written language as bridge

The notebook was created as an administrative tool.

It becomes:

- emotional confession;
- conflict repair;
- institutional record;
- and proof that leaders do not need to preserve an image of certainty before one another.

## Inheritance from the anthology letters

Like Kaori and Yuuko, Kumiko uses writing when immediate speech feels impossible.

The written form does not replace later conversation.

It makes conversation possible.

---

# 36. Kumiko and Reina’s “big love hug”

Kumiko waits for Reina at the station and opens her arms.

Reina is horrified by the public embarrassment.

She eventually runs into the embrace.

## Why the gesture matters

Kumiko and Reina previously believed they did not need such rituals.

They assumed their bond surpassed explicit explanation.

Volume 10 corrects that confidence.

## Reciprocal statements

Kumiko tells Reina that she loves:

- her effort;
- strength;
- determination;
- and the way she walks her chosen path.

Reina says she loves:

- Kumiko’s kindness;
- and even the occasionally unpleasant or sharp parts of her personality.

## Love without idealization

Reina does not love a purified Kumiko.

Kumiko does not love a perfect Reina.

They name the difficult traits as part of the person.

## The apology

Reina admits that Kumiko was correct and apologizes for calling her unfit as president.

She does not stop admiring Taki.

She allows admiration and criticism to coexist.

This is significant growth for a character whose earlier model of specialness depended heavily upon absolute hierarchy.

---

# 37. Trust after disagreement

Kumiko explains her mature position:

- Taki is not perfect.
- Students possess minds, hands, and feet of their own.
- They can attempt to understand the destination he sees.
- Their disagreement does not require abandoning him.
- His imperfection is part of why active participation matters.

Reina answers:

> Kumiko is also right.

## From faith to relationship

Blind faith treats doubt as contamination.

Mature trust allows:

- questioning;
- disappointment;
- and repair.

The Kumiko–Reina reconciliation therefore mirrors Kitauji’s needed relationship with Taki.

A relationship becomes stronger when obedience is no longer the condition of belonging.

---

# 38. Kumiko chooses teaching

Kumiko asks Taki what kind of adult he wants to become.

He asks her the same question.

She answers before fully deliberating:

> 「私は、先生みたいな人になりたいです」  
> I want to become someone like you. I want to become a teacher.

## Why teaching fits Kumiko

She is not the series’ greatest instrumental virtuoso.

Her defining abilities are:

- hearing emotional dissonance;
- noticing social structure;
- translating between people;
- remembering how beginners feel;
- understanding the pressure of auditions;
- and making others articulate what they have hidden.

## Not becoming Taki exactly

Kumiko does not want:

- his personality;
- his authority;
- or his precise musical career.

She wants to become the kind of adult whose arrival changes what young people believe is possible.

## Music without conservatory

Teaching gives Kumiko a future connected to music without requiring professional performance.

This resolves her fear that rejecting music college means music was insufficiently important.

Music becomes her method of relationship rather than her sole occupation as performer.

---

# 39. Kumiko’s speech to all 103 members

This is the volume’s central leadership scene.

Kumiko stands in the only empty position before the full band.

## “I like this Kitauji”

She names what she values:

- fairness;
- opportunity;
- shared work;
- and the ability of first-years and third-years to stand on the same field.

## “Do not give up”

She repeats the language she once used toward Asuka:

> 「諦めないでくださいよ！」

The line has changed scale.

What was once an intimate plea to one senior becomes public institutional philosophy.

## The right to regret

Kumiko says some members will:

- lose;
- miss the ensemble;
- or lose a featured part.

That frustration belongs to them.

No one has the right to remove it by making the decision in advance.

## Effort as self-authorization

Effort should not be performed to make observers approve.

Its purpose is to allow the person to say:

> I did what I could.

## Transparent ego

Kumiko acknowledges that asking everyone for full effort is her own wish.

She does not disguise the request as:

- universal truth;
- neutral necessity;
- or the impersonal voice of the band.

This is the ethical difference between her speech and coercive institutional rhetoric.

She says:

> This is what I want from you.

That directness allows others to answer honestly.

---

# 40. The unresolved tension inside the speech

Kumiko’s speech is powerful.

It is not ethically perfect.

## Mayu’s motive remains different

Mayu would genuinely prefer to support Kumiko and preserve social peace.

Kumiko asks her to compete fully despite that preference.

## Collective obligation

Once Mayu remains in the competitive ensemble, her withdrawal affects:

- sound;
- auditions;
- Taki’s judgment;
- and the agency of every player around her.

Kumiko’s request therefore possesses legitimate institutional force.

## The novel’s mature ambiguity

The speech does not establish one universal law that everyone must maximize ambition.

It establishes a local principle for this shared system:

> While you participate in the audition, do not falsify the performance.

Mayu remains free to:

- leave;
- redefine music;
- or treat competition as secondary.

She is not free to perform a false result while asking the institution to judge honestly.

---

# 41. The group photograph

After the speech, Kaho asks to photograph Kumiko, Kanade, and Mayu.

Mayu initially tries to decline.

Kumiko draws her into the frame.

## What the image records

The photograph contains four euphonium generations or positions:

- Kumiko, the graduating president;
- Mayu, the transfer;
- Kanade, the second-year inheritor;
- Kaho, the beginner.

## From rivalry to lineage

The euphonium section stops appearing as a limited number of chairs.

It becomes a continuity.

The girls may:

- compete;
- replace;
- teach;
- and graduate

without invalidating one another’s presence.

## Mayu’s visible place

Mayu accepts appearing in the photograph.

The change is quiet.

It is one of the clearest signs that Kitauji has become more than another temporary environment.

---

# 42. The final audition

The final A ensemble includes both Kumiko and Mayu.

Kanade remains outside the competition group.

The euphonium soli is awarded to Kumiko.

## No objective final ranking

Mayu’s Kansai selection was not revealed as mistaken.

Kumiko’s national selection does not prove she was always superior.

The auditions capture:

- different performances;
- at different moments;
- under different interpretive conditions.

## Kumiko’s preparation

She has practiced obsessively and finally spoken her desire without disguising it.

She enters knowing that losing would not erase her identity.

That psychological freedom likely changes the performer she can become, although the novel does not reduce Taki’s precise judgment to one explicit factor.

## Mayu’s response

Mayu congratulates Kumiko.

Her response appears sincere.

The conflict does not end through humiliation or exposure.

The two girls have participated fully and accepted the result.

---

# 43. Passing on 「響け！ユーフォニアム」

Kumiko finally explains Asuka’s piece to Mayu.

Kanade already knows the title.

Kumiko invites both to play it.

Mayu suggests making it the euphonium section’s theme.

## From secret inheritance to shared tradition

The piece began as:

- Shindō’s composition;
- Asuka’s private connection to her father;
- and Kumiko’s inherited emotional object.

Kumiko previously guarded it when Mayu asked.

Now she says:

> It is important. Treat it carefully.

She does not keep it private.

## The mature act of inheritance

Passing on the piece does not make Kumiko less special.

It demonstrates that specialness does not require scarcity.

The melody can belong to:

- Asuka;
- Kumiko;
- Kanade;
- Mayu;
- Kaho;
- and future euphonium players

without becoming emotionally identical for each.

## The title’s final meaning

The imperative **響け / sound, resonate** becomes generational.

Do not preserve the melody by locking it away.

Preserve it by allowing another body to breathe through it.

---

# 44. Hazuki’s completion

Hazuki reaches the A ensemble in her final year and performs at nationals.

Her arc is among the series’ quietest long-term achievements.

## She began at zero

Hazuki entered without instrumental experience.

She struggled to:

- produce sound;
- read music;
- manage the instrument;
- and understand the culture around her.

## Years outside A

She watched:

- Kumiko;
- Midori;
- Reina;
- and other friends

enter higher-level performance spaces before her.

She did not turn that delay into hostility.

## Her final place

Hazuki’s selection is earned.

It is also emotionally satisfying because technical judgment, long effort, friendship, and seniority finally align.

## Her enduring philosophy

Even after reaching A, Hazuki does not become Reina.

She remains someone capable of saying:

> Playing together is fun.

The final ensemble needs that voice.

---

# 45. The four-tuba solution

Hazuki, Mirei, Satsuki, and Suzume all enter the later A ensembles.

The configuration resolves the earlier apparent zero-sum conflict.

## Satsuki teaches Suzume

Satsuki possesses broader technique.

Suzume possesses exceptional natural volume and tone.

Rather than withholding knowledge from the person who once displaced her, Satsuki helps Suzume develop.

## Mirei as technical senior

Mirei uses the competence she once guarded defensively to improve the entire section.

## Hazuki as relational center

Hazuki maintains warmth across the group.

## Ensemble merit

No one tuba supplies everything.

The section succeeds through differentiated strengths.

This is the institutional answer to a large portion of the audition conflict:

> The best ensemble is not necessarily built from fifty-five identical all-purpose winners.

---

# 46. The graduation cherry tree

Five days before nationals, Kumiko’s class gathers around a newly planted cherry tree.

Miechie explains that it is their graduation memorial.

The tree is:

- thin;
- vulnerable;
- and not yet integrated into the landscape.

## Future growth

The students are told that when they have entered adult life, the tree may have become large enough to bloom fully.

## Institutional memory

The tree will remain at Kitauji after:

- Kumiko;
- Reina;
- Shuuichi;
- Hazuki;
- Midori;
- and the others

have left.

It embodies a central series principle:

> People leave.  
> What they cultivated may remain alive.

## The epilogue return

Adult Kumiko later sees the same tree as part of the school landscape.

The visual proof of time completes the metaphor.

---

# 47. Midori’s buried treasure

Hazuki fears that nothing in adult life will equal the intensity of Kitauji.

Midori offers another model.

The present is like burying treasure throughout the future.

One day:

- a familiar piece will play on television;
- a child ensemble will appear in public;
- an instrument will sound;
- or a forgotten memory will return.

The adult will recover part of the present.

## Memory without stasis

The goal is not to keep high school unchanged.

It is to allow high school to remain discoverable inside later life.

## The answer to Mayu and Motomu

Mayu wants memories because environments end.

Motomu wants time to stop because Midori will leave.

Midori’s model accommodates both truths:

- the moment ends;
- the value remains available.

---

# 48. Daikichiyama one final time

Two days before nationals, Reina invites Kumiko home.

They carry instruments up Daikichiyama.

The act deliberately returns to the place where their relationship first became extraordinary.

## Deliberate irrationality

Climbing a mountain with heavy instruments before nationals is absurd.

That is why it matters.

No institutional requirement produces the moment.

They choose it.

## Reina’s future

Reina reveals that she will attend a music school in the United States.

She wants:

- advanced study;
- greater musicianship;
- and eventually to become someone capable of confessing properly to Taki as an adult.

Her love for Taki remains explicit.

## Kumiko’s grief

Kumiko has expected physical separation.

The confirmed destination turns anticipation into reality.

## The third movement

They play the autumn movement’s soli together.

The music already concerns:

- destiny;
- ending;
- and the beauty of a moment known to be temporary.

The scene is not rehearsal alone.

It is preservation.

---

# 49. Kumiko and Reina after classification

The novel ends without forcing Kumiko and Reina into one simple category.

Their relationship contains:

- friendship;
- artistic partnership;
- mutual love language;
- physical affection;
- possessiveness;
- aesthetic fascination;
- and the expectation of life-changing separation.

Kumiko’s romance with Shuuichi is explicit.

Reina’s romantic desire for Taki is explicit.

Neither fact empties the Kumiko–Reina bond.

## Their mature intimacy

The relationship evolves from:

> We understand without speaking.

To:

> We disagree, speak, apologize, embrace, and choose one another again.

That is a far stronger relational structure.

---

# 50. The national performance as series autobiography

The national performance is the literary culmination of the ten-volume mainline.

Kumiko hears *Poem of a Year* as the story of her entire Kitauji life.

## Spring

Spring recalls:

- entering Kitauji;
- the poor school performance;
- Asuka’s silver euphonium;
- and Reina’s first fearless trumpet declarations.

Kumiko entered partly through drift.

The season becomes the beginning of everything.

## Summer

Summer carries:

- ambition;
- technical brilliance;
- Mizore and Nozomi;
- the violence of separation;
- and the belief that intensity may last forever.

The music becomes bright enough to contain arrogance.

## Autumn

Autumn introduces:

- Midori’s solitary contrabass;
- impermanence;
- Kumiko’s discovered weakness;
- and the euphonium–trumpet dialogue.

Kumiko answers Reina.

The soli contains their complete relationship:

- admiration;
- conflict;
- equality;
- and chosen response.

## Winter

Winter becomes:

- adulthood;
- graduation;
- unknown future;
- and the fear that shared life will vanish.

Earlier musical themes return in transformed forms.

The end contains the beginning.

## Spring again

The final return of the first movement’s material does not restore the original spring.

It creates another spring carrying:

- grief;
- memory;
- and accumulated sound.

This is the novel’s complete theory of growth.

One does not return unchanged.

---

# 51. Kumiko’s national soli

Kumiko performs the passage she feared losing.

Its significance is not that she proves Mayu objectively inferior.

It is that she can now play without requiring the passage to prove she is irreplaceable.

## Answering Reina

The euphonium does not imitate the trumpet.

Its:

- warmth;
- rounded tone;
- and legato line

answer Reina’s brilliance through another kind of voice.

## The instrumental resolution

Kumiko becomes equal to Reina by making the euphonium fully itself.

This completes the principle Asuka taught in Volume 5.

Equality is not sameness.

## The emotional content

Kumiko pours into the melody:

- loneliness;
- gratitude;
- conflict;
- memory;
- and the knowledge that the relationship will change.

The soli is not a promise that they will remain physically together.

It is proof that they were capable of answering one another completely in this moment.

---

# 52. National gold

Kitauji receives:

> 「ゴールド金賞」

The result fulfills the explicit goal carried across three generations.

## Why the victory does not cheapen the earlier argument

The series has already shown:

- South Middle silver;
- Kitauji national bronze;
- non-advancing gold;
- B-ensemble gold;
- audience-favorite performances that lose formal selection;
- and great players who do not receive the role they want.

It has thoroughly separated value from one medal.

National gold can therefore function as:

- earned culmination;
- collective reward;
- and fulfillment of a long promise

without becoming proof that the victorious characters were morally superior.

## Taki’s tears

Taki cries before the students.

The distant, controlled advisor becomes visibly overwhelmed.

The dream belonged to:

- his wife;
- himself;
- the students;
- and the institution they created together.

## Shared ownership

The gold belongs to more than the fifty-five performers.

Kumiko carries the photograph from the non-performing members in her pocket.

# 64. Final assessment

Volume 10 succeeds because it does not resolve its central conflict by exposing Mayu as deceptive.

Mayu has been honest.

That is what makes her difficult.

Kumiko has spent the series learning to distrust statements such as:

- “I do not mind.”
- “I will withdraw.”
- “Someone else should do it.”
- “It is for the band.”
- “I do not really want anything.”

In earlier cases, those statements often concealed:

- fear;
- defensive self-erasure;
- resentment;
- or unclaimed desire.

Mayu’s statements do not function in exactly the same way.

She genuinely values:

- presence;
- friendship;
- photographs;
- and shared time

more than a competitive role.

Kumiko cannot make Mayu legible by uncovering another Reina beneath the surface.

She must acknowledge that the ensemble contains people whose relation to music will never resemble her own.

This is the final movement’s decisive expansion.

The early series often treated strong desire as morally and artistically awakening.

Volume 10 retains that belief while adding a limit:

> A person who wants differently is not necessarily asleep.

Mayu’s way of living contains costs.

She remains too ready to disappear.

She underestimates the pain caused by offering to surrender what another person desperately wants.

She protects herself through temporary belonging and remains behind the camera.

But her delight in shared sound is genuine.

She does not need to be remade in Kumiko’s image.

Kumiko’s great leadership act is therefore not discovering Mayu’s true ambition.

It is making room for Mayu’s actual truth while asking her to participate honestly in a structure larger than either girl.

The final speech matters because Kumiko stops pretending the request is neutral.

She tells the members:

> I want this from you.

She acknowledges:

- loss;
- ego;
- regret;
- and the possibility that full effort will hurt.

She does not promise that everyone will be happy.

She promises that no one’s pain will be invalidated by a decision made for her in advance.

That is the mature form of the speech she gave Asuka.

With Asuka, Kumiko said:

> I want you to play.

With the whole band, she says:

> I want you to decide through your own complete action.

The movement from one person to 103 defines her presidency.

Her relationship with Taki develops in parallel.

Kumiko begins by wanting to know whether Taki is right.

She ends by understanding that no adult can provide that guarantee.

Taki is worthy of trust not because he is perfect, but because:

- he studies;
- doubts;
- works;
- listens;
- and accepts the moral weight of choosing among students.

This is the adult Kumiko decides to become.

She does not become a famous professional euphonium player.

She becomes the person who stands before another room of uncertain first-years and opens her arms.

That ending is more thematically exact than any virtuoso career could have been.

The euphonium has always been the connective voice:

- not necessarily the most visible;
- not interchangeable with the trumpet;
- rarely carrying the complete melody alone;
- but capable of linking harmony, bass, and upper line.

Kumiko’s adult role is euphonium-like.

She carries:

- people;
- scores;
- logistics;
- memory;
- and the quiet knowledge required to let others sound.

The national gold remains deeply satisfying.

It fulfills:

- Taki’s wife’s dream;
- Yuuko and Natsuki’s failed attempt;
- Asuka and Kaori’s original generation;
- the promise made after second-year defeat;
- and the explicit wish of the final-year band.

But the medal is not the endpoint.

Immediately afterward:

- Kumiko confesses to Shuuichi;
- takes Reina’s hand;
- preserves the photograph;
- carries the hairpin;
- and eventually returns to teach.

The victory opens paths rather than freezing the characters at their peak.

The full series can finally be summarized through the meaning of **響け**.

At first, the word appears to be an imperative directed toward the instrument:

> Sound.

Then it becomes a demand directed toward the self:

> Admit what you want.

Then toward another person:

> Let me hear what you feel.

Then toward the ensemble:

> Enter the shared sound without disappearing.

Finally, it becomes a generational command:

> Carry what you received into a future that does not belong to the people who first gave it to you.

Asuka receives a melody from her father.

Kumiko receives it from Asuka.

Mayu and Kanade receive it from Kumiko.

Adult Kumiko welcomes students who may one day carry it beyond her.

The national performance’s four seasons give the mainline its final form.

Spring begins.  
Summer believes it will last forever.  
Autumn learns that every shared moment contains separation.  
Winter destroys the familiar landscape.  
Then spring returns.

Not the same spring.

A spring containing all the others.

Kumiko’s first entrance into Kitauji was partly accidental.

Her return is a choice.

She stands before the next generation with:

- the full score;
- the handwritten melody;
- the practical manual;
- the flower from Shuuichi;
- the memory of Reina’s trumpet;
- and the knowledge that no two students will want music in exactly the same way.

Then she says:

> Welcome.

That is the true national-gold result.

Not merely a medal.

An institution capable of beginning again.
