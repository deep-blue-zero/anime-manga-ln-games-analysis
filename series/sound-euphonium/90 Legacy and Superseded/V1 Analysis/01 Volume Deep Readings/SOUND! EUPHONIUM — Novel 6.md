# What kind of volume this is

Volume 6 is the genuine beginning of Kumiko’s second school year.

It is also explicitly only the **first half** of the “Second Movement.” Its task is therefore not to resolve the year’s major conflicts, but to introduce the new ensemble, establish its fault lines, and carry Kitauji from spring recruitment through the first competitive auditions.

The opening trilogy asked whether an undistinguished band could become serious.

Volume 6 begins with a very different institution.

Kitauji is now:

- a national-level school;
- publicly recognized;
- attractive to ambitious middle-school musicians;
- larger than before;
- and already united around national gold.

Forty-three first-years join. The club expands to eighty-nine members. At the annual vote, every member raises a hand for the national-gold objective.

This looks like institutional success.

It also creates the central danger of the second movement:

> **The new members inherit Kitauji’s ambition before they inherit an understanding of what that ambition costs.**

The first-year Kumiko entered a band that had to debate whether it even wanted nationals.

The new first-years enter a band where national gold appears to be the obvious answer.

They have joined not the weak Kitauji that discovered seriousness, but the prestigious Kitauji created by someone else’s struggle.

Volume 6 asks whether the culture can reproduce its moral development—or whether it will retain only the pressure, ranking, and slogan.

# Executive thesis

Volume 6 is about **the difference between a fair system and a system in which people feel safe enough to trust fairness**.

Kitauji now possesses the meritocratic audition structure the first trilogy fought to establish.

In principle:

- skill determines selection;
- first-years may defeat seniors;
- past status does not guarantee present placement;
- and the strongest available ensemble should represent the school.

But the arrival of Kanade Hisaishi exposes a deeper problem.

A technically fair audition cannot prevent:

- resentment;
- pity;
- social comparison;
- loyalty toward seniors;
- suspicion of talented newcomers;
- or the belief that visible effort is morally worthier than efficient ability.

Kanade has learned that musical merit and social legitimacy are different things.

She believes she can win the audition and still lose the social world around it.

Her solution is to intentionally play badly.

She tries to surrender before she can be selected and hated.

This produces the novel’s central paradox:

> **Kanade corrupts the merit system because she does not trust the social community to accept its result.**

Her apparent sacrifice for Natsuki is not pure selflessness. Nor is it simple cynicism.

It is preemptive self-protection:

> If Kanade loses deliberately, she controls the humiliation.  
> If Natsuki receives the place, nobody can accuse Kanade of taking it.  
> If Kanade presents herself as selfish and calculating, nobody can discover how badly she wants to be recognized as someone who has also worked.

The emotional climax occurs when Kumiko finally tells her:

> 「奏ちゃんは、頑張ってるよ」  
> Kanade, you are trying hard.

That sentence answers the question Kanade has carried from middle school:

> 「頑張るってなんですか」  
> What does it mean to try?

Volume 6’s deepest argument is therefore:

> **Fairness requires more than judging ability correctly.  
> It also requires a community capable of recognizing effort without converting effort into entitlement, and capable of rewarding talent without turning the talented person into an enemy.**

Kitauji has built the first half of that culture.

Kumiko, Natsuki, and the others must now build the second.

# 1. The meaning of the “Second Movement”

The title operates on several levels.

## Kumiko’s second year

The first movement followed Kumiko’s entrance into Kitauji and the original ensemble’s rise to nationals.

The second movement begins after:

- the founding third-years have graduated;
- new leaders have taken over;
- Kumiko has become a senior;
- and younger students now look toward her for answers.

## The ensemble’s second generation

Kitauji must attempt to reproduce excellence without:

- Haruka’s emotional leadership;
- Asuka’s musical authority;
- Kaori’s stabilizing warmth;
- or the exact social circumstances that made the first transformation possible.

The second movement is therefore not repetition of the first.

It is a test of whether the original achievement has become institutional culture or remained a one-generation miracle.

## A musical second movement

Second movements often alter:

- tempo;
- mood;
- perspective;
- or emotional register

while preserving thematic connections to what came before.

Volume 6 does exactly this.

Earlier themes return:

- merit;
- seniority;
- hidden effort;
- unequal attachment;
- fear of replacement;
- and the desire to be personally wanted.

But they return through new characters and reversed roles.

Kumiko is no longer the uncertain newcomer.

She is the senior standing before a frightened first-year who asks whether the band can change her.

# 2. Prologue: “Liz and the Blue Bird” before anyone can escape its meaning

The prologue returns to Mizore and Nozomi.

Nozomi runs ahead up the stairs. Mizore follows more slowly and asks her to wait. Nozomi does not hear the first request, but eventually stops and waits near the music room.

The entire relationship is present in that physical arrangement:

> Nozomi moves forward naturally.  
> Mizore follows.  
> Nozomi sometimes waits.  
> Mizore experiences every interval between them as possible abandonment.

They enter the music room to practice Kitauji’s new free-choice piece:

> **Liz and the Blue Bird**

Nozomi remarks that the story resembles them.

Mizore agrees aloud.

Internally, she wishes the performance would never arrive.

## The countdown as threat

For Nozomi, the approaching performance means:

- exciting music;
- shared achievement;
- and the chance to play an important flute line.

For Mizore, every day crossed off the board is evidence that the shared present will end.

The competition is not merely a goal.

It is a deadline on proximity.

## Apparent reconciliation versus structural repair

Volume 2 repaired the factual misunderstanding between them.

Nozomi did care for Mizore.  
She did not invite Mizore to quit because she respected her commitment.  
Mizore was not forgotten in the absolute sense she feared.

Volume 6 reveals that this did not resolve the relationship’s asymmetry.

Mizore still:

- builds her future around Nozomi;
- fears being left;
- wants exclusive time;
- and cannot imagine music as meaningful without her.

Nozomi still:

- cares;
- includes;
- and invites

without understanding the scale at which Mizore experiences each gesture.

The prologue therefore establishes one of Volume 6’s central warnings:

> **A relationship can survive a crisis without changing the structure that produced it.**

# 3. 「不穏なダ・カーポ」  
## Beginning again under conditions that are not the same

**Da capo** instructs the performer to return to the beginning.

The new school year looks like a return:

- new classes;
- recruitment;
- instrument assignments;
- a goal vote;
- Sunrise Festival preparation;
- and first-years entering unfamiliar sections.

But the repetition is unsettling because Kitauji is no longer beginning from zero.

## The first-year cycle returns

The newcomers face familiar problems:

- Why join?
- Which instrument should I play?
- Do I belong here?
- Can a beginner matter?
- Can a first-year defeat a senior?
- What happens if I am better than someone older?
- What if I am worse than my peers?

These resemble the questions of Volumes 1 and 4.

The answers now come from Kumiko’s generation.

## The seniors are different

Kumiko, Hazuki, and Midori enter their second year with very different musical identities:

- Kumiko has become an A-ensemble player and junior instructor.
- Midori is an elite player beginning to transmit knowledge.
- Hazuki remains outside the A ensemble but has developed resilience and practical empathy.

They are no longer equally inexperienced friends discovering the club together.

Seniority separates them according to the different paths the first year produced.

## Kitauji’s inherited prestige

The newcomers have seen:

- the national result;
- public performances;
- or the increasingly strong reputation of the band.

Several have chosen Kitauji specifically because it is now serious.

The institution therefore attracts the very ambition it once struggled to create.

That is both reward and destabilization.

---

# 4. The unanimous national-gold vote

Every member votes for national gold.

This is one of the clearest signs of Kitauji’s transformation.

In Volume 1, the goal vote exposed:

- apathy;
- peer pressure;
- conflicting expectations;
- and the gap between a slogan and genuine consent.

In Volume 6, there is no visible hesitation.

## Genuine aspiration

The unanimity is not necessarily false.

The returning members have experienced nationals.

The newcomers intentionally joined a strong band.

Yuuko has inherited the goal sincerely.

The ensemble possesses evidence that extraordinary improvement is possible.

## The danger of inherited certainty

Yet unanimity can suppress the very discussion that once made the goal meaningful.

The new students did not all live through:

- Taki’s first confrontation with the band;
- the seniority conflict;
- Kaori and Reina’s solo dispute;
- Nozomi’s exclusion;
- Asuka’s crisis;
- or national bronze.

They inherit the conclusion:

> We aim for national gold.

They do not automatically inherit the ethical and emotional reasoning that should govern the attempt.

Volume 6 therefore begins asking:

> **Can a value remain authentic when it becomes institutional common sense?**

# 5. Kumiko’s first encounter with Yume: a senior meets her former self

Kumiko encounters Yume Kohinata in the instrument room before learning her name.

Yume has played trumpet in middle school but is unsure whether to continue. Other students have entered Kitauji specifically for band. Yume’s motive is weaker:

- familiarity;
- elimination of other options;
- and uncertainty about trying something new.

She asks Kumiko why she continued.

Kumiko recognizes her own first-year self.

## Motivation can change

Kumiko tells her that she also began without a strong reason.

She now practices because she genuinely wants nationals.

Her answer establishes an important principle for the new movement:

> A weak initial motive does not make later commitment false.

People can grow into their reasons.

## A senior cannot promise transformation

Yume asks:

> Can joining the band change me?

Kumiko answers:

> It depends upon you.

The reply sounds less comforting than a conventional inspirational promise. Yume appreciates it precisely because it is not false reassurance.

This is Kumiko’s first important act as a senior.

She does not claim that:

- Kitauji automatically improves people;
- music cures uncertainty;
- or entering a successful institution guarantees personal growth.

The band can supply:

- opportunity;
- relationships;
- discipline;
- and pressure.

The student must still act.

## Kumiko’s poor lying

Yume immediately identifies that Kumiko is bad at lying.

This becomes significant later.

Kumiko is surrounded by students who survive through performance:

- Kanade’s ideal-junior mask;
- Mirei’s coldness;
- Tomoe’s comic brightness;
- Nozomi’s easy simplifications;
- and even Reina’s composed certainty.

Kumiko’s value as a senior comes partly from the fact that her emotional leakage makes her difficult to experience as a strategist.

People tell her things because she appears insufficiently defended to weaponize them.

---

# 6. Kumiko’s new position: the Oumae Consultation Office

Across the volume, students begin bringing Kumiko personal problems.

She becomes informally known as an advice figure—the **Oumae Consultation Office**.

This is a natural continuation of her earlier role.

She has always been skilled at:

- observing contradictions;
- hearing different sides;
- asking one question too many;
- and standing near emotionally complicated people.

What changes is that others now seek this quality deliberately.

## From accidental witness to institutional listener

Earlier, Kumiko became involved because she:

- overheard;
- grew curious;
- followed another person;
- or was pulled into a conflict by Asuka or a friend.

Now junior instruction makes attention a responsibility.

Yume asks about change.  
Ririka asks how to approach Mizore.  
Kanade brings her anger about Natsuki.  
Mirei asks what she should do.  
Tomoe hides a health decision Kumiko believes she should have shared.

Kumiko is becoming a person through whom the band processes emotional information.

## Consultation is not command

The volume repeatedly warns Kumiko that noticing a problem does not mean possessing one correct method for solving it.

Tomoe’s meticulous beginner notebooks make Kumiko feel inadequate.

Tomoe tells her:

- the notebook is her own form of satisfaction;
- the small beginner group makes it possible;
- another instructor should not be required to imitate her;
- and Kumiko’s methods are not invalid simply because they are different.

This is an essential lesson in mentorship:

> **Care is not proven by reproducing another person’s labor exactly.**

Kumiko’s strength lies less in daily documentation than in the moment when someone needs:

- a listener;
- a reframing;
- or permission to speak.

---

# 7. Tomoe Kabe: contribution without performance

Tomoe’s arc broadens the novel’s definition of musical belonging.

She develops temporomandibular-joint problems and receives medical instructions to stop playing trumpet. Taki offers the possibility of changing to percussion.

Tomoe declines.

She becomes the band’s manager.

## The expected tragedy does not occur

Kumiko expects Tomoe to be devastated.

Tomoe is surprised by how little grief she feels.

She realizes that what she loves most is:

- the band;
- the people;
- daily participation;
- and helping the group function.

The trumpet was one route into that world, not necessarily the center of her identity.

## Kumiko’s discomfort

Kumiko privately wonders whether Tomoe has escaped the audition.

Tomoe admits that leaving the player category relieves her from being pitied as a third-year B member.

She dislikes the assumption that:

> A senior excluded from A must be tragic.

This exposes how thoroughly Kumiko has internalized competitive seriousness.

Because music has become central to Kumiko’s identity, she initially struggles to accept that someone else can value the same institution for different reasons.

## Selected truth

Tomoe’s cheerful public announcement is not false.

It is incomplete.

She feels:

- physical limitation;
- relief;
- possible regret;
- dislike of pity;
- awareness of her technical ceiling;
- and confidence that she can contribute more effectively elsewhere.

She chooses one coherent truth:

> She wants to support the band as manager.

The series does not demand that every complex feeling be reduced to one pure motive.

## Managerial excellence

Tomoe becomes extremely effective:

- scheduling;
- paperwork;
- movement between sections;
- audition logistics;
- and reducing Yuuko’s administrative burden.

Her shift proves another recurring principle:

> Leaving the visible performance is not the same as leaving the ensemble.

---

# 8. Tomoe’s beginner notebooks: knowledge as protection

Tomoe keeps detailed records on every novice:

- when a first sound appears;
- fingerings forgotten;
- reachable range;
- rhythmic problems;
- social worries;
- and personal development.

The notebooks arise from her own first-year experience.

During the old Kitauji conflict, she lacked:

- ability;
- knowledge;
- confidence;
- and the status to ask what was happening.

She watched others suffer without understanding why.

## Ignorance as powerlessness

Tomoe’s insight is not merely that beginners need technical instruction.

They need enough knowledge to believe their perceptions are legitimate.

A novice may notice that something is wrong but think:

> I am too inexperienced to speak.

Tomoe wants to prevent that form of silence.

## A different response to institutional trauma

Many older students remain shaped by the previous crisis:

- Nozomi quit and returned.
- Natsuki regrets passivity.
- Yuuko converts loyalty into leadership.
- Mizore fears abandonment.
- Tomoe creates a system of novice care.

Each turns the same institutional wound into a different practice.

Tomoe’s notebooks are memory transformed into infrastructure.

# 9. The new low-brass generation

The cover foregrounds the new low-brass first-years because their relationships form the volume’s main social laboratory.

## Kanade Hisaishi — euphonium

Kanade is:

- polished;
- perceptive;
- technically advanced;
- verbally strategic;
- and highly conscious of hierarchy.

She initially performs the ideal junior:

- respectful speech;
- admiration for Kumiko;
- admiration for Asuka;
- cheerful willingness;
- and fascination with the euphonium’s obscurity.

Her politeness is real as a social skill.

It is not the whole person.

## Satsuki Suzuki — tuba

Satsuki is:

- cheerful;
- socially immediate;
- enthusiastic;
- and relatively inexperienced.

She resembles Hazuki in her ability to create warmth before possessing strong technique.

She forms connections easily and treats Mirei with assumed familiarity.

That assumption is both affectionate and intrusive.

## Mirei Suzuki — tuba

Mirei is:

- tall;
- competent;
- serious;
- socially guarded;
- and resentful of being reduced to physical suitability for tuba.

She originally wanted trumpet.

She remained with tuba partly because it provided a reliable A-ensemble path in middle school.

Her relationship with the instrument therefore begins through a mixture of:

- imposed role;
- strategic choice;
- pride;
- and accumulated skill.

## Motomu Tsukinaga — contrabass

Motomu comes from Ryusei’s internal system and rejects discussion of his surname or family.

He is withdrawn from most people but intensely devoted to Midori.

His musical motivation is concentrated around one admired person, making him another possible variation on:

- Mizore and Nozomi;
- Kumiko and Reina;
- or Kanade and Kumiko.

## The low-brass section as miniature Kitauji

The section now contains:

- beginners;
- elite experience;
- insecure competence;
- masked ambition;
- senior regret;
- and several generations of inherited teaching.

It is no longer merely the friendly comic household of the first novels.

It has become a compressed version of the entire institution.

# 10. Satsuki and Mirei: affection that feels like condescension

Satsuki and Mirei knew one another before high school, but they understand the relationship differently.

Satsuki behaves as though they are naturally close.

She uses the nickname **Micchan**.

Mirei rejects it.

## Mirei’s resentment

Mirei sees Satsuki receiving affection despite weaker playing.

Satsuki is:

- easy to approach;
- cheerful;
- readily supported by seniors;
- and visibly trying.

Mirei is:

- technically superior;
- less socially inviting;
- and treated as though her competence means she needs less encouragement.

She interprets this as another unfair hierarchy:

> The likable person receives emotional reward.  
> The skilled person is merely expected to function.

## Hazuki’s correction

During Sunrise Festival preparation, Hazuki touches or adjusts Mirei’s instrument and attempts to help.

Mirei reacts sharply.

Her anger is not only about the physical correction. She resents being instructed by a weaker player.

This reveals a dangerous equation:

> Musical superiority should determine who may speak.

Volume 6 repeatedly contests that premise without denying that technical competence matters.

Hazuki may be weaker than Mirei and still possess:

- practical experience;
- performance knowledge;
- social insight;
- and legitimate senior responsibility.

## Kanade’s diagnosis

Kanade tells Mirei the truth Mirei wants acknowledged:

- Satsuki receives affection more easily.
- Mirei is better.
- Mirei is jealous.
- People have failed to communicate that they recognize her ability.

Kanade then says she prefers Mirei.

This creates immediate relief through exclusivity.

Mirei has finally been selected by someone.

## Kumiko’s alternative

Kumiko does not deny Kanade’s diagnosis.

She tells Mirei that the section does recognize her ability.

The barrier is that Mirei is difficult to approach.

Kumiko suggests accepting “Micchan” not as surrender of identity, but as a visible opening through which Satsuki can reconnect.

The repeated nickname eventually makes Mirei laugh.

She decides to apologize.

## Change versus capitulation

Kanade becomes angry because she interprets this result as:

> The socially acceptable girl is required to change herself for others.

Kumiko interprets it differently:

> Mirei wanted connection and asked what she could do. The nickname gives her a self-chosen way to signal that desire.

The distinction is crucial.

Changing for relationship is not automatically self-betrayal.

It becomes betrayal when the change is coerced or denies what the person actually wants.

# 11. Mirei’s instrument choice and strategic belonging

Mirei’s history complicates the series’ usual language of loving one’s instrument.

She wanted trumpet.

Her height led adults toward tuba.

She stayed with tuba because:

- she was already successful;
- she repeatedly entered A ensemble;
- and switching instruments threatened that status.

This is not a pure artistic calling.

It is still a real musical history.

## Identity can begin through assignment

Several characters enter instruments through forces other than spontaneous love:

- Kumiko follows her sister and is repeatedly assigned euphonium.
- Hazuki is directed toward tuba because of physique and breath.
- Natsuki is redirected after not receiving her preferred instrument.
- Mirei accepts tuba because it provides a competitive route.

The series does not demand a myth of instant destiny.

Attachment can emerge from repeated labor.

## Recognition remains central

Mirei’s pride in tuba is entangled with being selected.

Her fear is not only that she might lose A status.

It is that losing would reveal that the identity she accepted was never truly hers.

Her selection for A at the end provides immediate validation, but does not fully resolve the deeper question:

> Does Mirei love tuba, or does she love the proof of competence it has given her?

# 12. Motomu and Midori: admiration, naming, and fear of taking another person’s place

Motomu’s relationship with Midori begins with intense admiration.

Around most people he is:

- abrupt;
- withdrawn;
- defensive about his name;
- and reluctant to accept easy familiarity.

Around Midori, he becomes attentive and eager.

## The surname wound

Kanade needles him by using the surname he dislikes.

Midori mediates through her own experience with the name “Sapphire.”

She understands that a name can carry:

- parental intention;
- social embarrassment;
- imposed identity;
- and assumptions the named person did not choose.

Her intervention is effective because it arises from analogy rather than abstract moral correction.

## Selective musical desire

Motomu practices intensely when Midori is present.

His investment appears less connected to Kitauji as an institution than to standing beside her.

This is not necessarily false motivation.

The novels have repeatedly shown that another person can be the doorway into music.

The danger lies in whether the music can survive if that person leaves.

## Fear of displacing Natsuki

Motomu worries that his presence and the contrabass part may reduce the space available for a third-year euphonium player such as Natsuki.

His guilt assumes that A-ensemble positions form one undifferentiated pool.

Midori corrects this.

The contrabass contributes something another instrument cannot simply substitute.

## Resonance rather than volume

Midori explains that the contrabass’s primary value is not obvious loudness but the quality it adds to the ensemble’s resonance.

Its presence changes the whole sound even when the audience cannot isolate it.

This extends the tuba philosophy of Volume 4:

> Importance is not identical to visibility.

It also becomes a lesson for Motomu himself.

He is not justified by replacing another person.

He contributes through a form specific to him.

---

# 13. Yume and Reina: talent afraid of visibility

Yume is one of the strongest first-year trumpet players.

She is selected for A ensemble.

Yet she lacks confidence and avoids featured attention.

This creates a mentoring problem for Reina.

## Reina’s worldview

Reina understands:

- ambition;
- competition;
- practice;
- and the desire to be heard.

Her trumpet ethic is declarative.

A capable player should want the solo.

## Yume’s fear

Yume’s problem is not inability.

It is the prospect of exposure.

A solo does not merely display the instrument. It makes the player individually accountable before everyone.

Reina has difficulty comprehending why someone with ability would recoil from this opportunity.

## A limit in Reina’s mentorship

Reina can identify:

- technical weakness;
- poor preparation;
- and insufficient ambition.

She is less naturally equipped to help someone whose greatest obstacle is the emotional cost of being visible.

This reverses the first-year structure.

Reina once needed no senior to awaken her desire.

Now she must guide a junior whose temperament does not resemble hers.

The second movement requires the old protagonists to confront forms of difficulty they never personally experienced.

# 14. Ririka and Mizore: ordinary conversation as achievement

Ririka Kenzaki is cheerful, bold, flirtatious, and socially persistent.

She asks Kumiko how to approach Mizore.

Eventually she succeeds in having ordinary conversations with her about:

- weather;
- food;
- and soda-flavored gummies.

This sounds trivial.

For Mizore, it is important.

## The value of mundane relationship

Mizore’s major attachments have been organized around:

- rescue;
- abandonment;
- panic;
- elite musical skill;
- and fear of separation.

Ririka offers something much less dramatic:

> ordinary junior–senior conversation.

A relationship does not have to begin through catastrophe to be real.

## Ririka’s style

Ririka’s performative affection is openly playful.

She hugs Kanade, declares love, jokes about kissing, and calls Kanade her number one.

The boundary between joke and sincerity remains deliberately loose.

Unlike Mizore, Ririka does not treat every ranking of affection as permanent existential structure.

Her ease demonstrates another way intense language can function:

- not as binding contract;
- but as social play that still carries genuine fondness.

## Kanade’s safest peer

Kanade is more relaxed around Ririka than around most others.

Ririka recognizes the masks and responds without demanding that Kanade confess which expression is “real.”

Their friendship suggests that Kanade is not incapable of trust.

She trusts most easily where performance itself is accepted as part of the relationship.

# 15. Kanade Hisaishi: the ideal junior as defensive theater

Kanade is the central new character of Volume 6.

She initially appears almost perfectly designed for Kumiko:

- a euphonium junior;
- technically strong;
- polite;
- admiring;
- intelligent;
- and interested in the instrument’s unusual identity.

The perfection is suspicious because it is deliberate.

## Social surveillance

Kanade constantly watches:

- who is talented;
- who is popular;
- who is liked;
- who is protected;
- and whose effort receives public recognition.

She does not experience the band as a neutral social field.

She experiences it as a network of future judgments.

## Multiple masks

Kanade can perform:

- devoted junior;
- elegant young lady;
- amused cynic;
- ruthless meritocrat;
- affectionate friend;
- and wounded child.

The novel refuses to declare only one of these the true Kanade.

Her problem is not that every performance is false.

It is that she uses performance to prevent any reaction from reaching the vulnerable center.

## Why Kumiko attracts her

Kumiko possesses several qualities Kanade wants from a senior:

- musical credibility;
- proximity to Asuka;
- relative social neutrality;
- poor ability to conceal feeling;
- and a tendency to listen rather than immediately rank.

Kanade flatters her, tests her, and increasingly seeks exclusive reassurance from her.

She wants Kumiko to be the senior whose approval proves that Kitauji is different.

She is also constantly preparing for the possibility that Kumiko will disappoint her.

# 16. Kanade’s middle-school wound

Kanade’s ideology comes from a previous audition injustice.

Another euphonium player practiced constantly but inefficiently.

Kanade practiced less visibly and played better.

The senior was selected because people felt:

> She tried so hard.

Kanade learned several lessons:

1. Technical merit does not necessarily determine selection.
2. Visible suffering is socially legible in a way efficient ability is not.
3. Being naturally or efficiently capable can make effort invisible.
4. Other people may interpret ease as moral deficiency.
5. A likable, visibly hardworking person can defeat a more capable player through sympathy.

This is why Kanade reacts so strongly to Natsuki.

Natsuki appears to embody the same dangerous combination:

- seniority;
- visible effort;
- popularity;
- kindness;
- and uncertain technical superiority.

## “What is trying?”

Kanade’s question—

> 「頑張るってなんですか」

—is not rhetorical in the shallow sense.

She wants to know what society counts.

Does trying mean:

- time spent?
- suffering displayed?
- improvement achieved?
- technical result?
- refusing to quit?
- being witnessed by others?
- or being the kind of person people want to reward?

Kanade believes the term is manipulated after the fact to justify affection.

The person people like is called hardworking.

The person they dislike is called naturally gifted, calculating, or insufficiently sincere.

---

# 17. Kanade and Natsuki: why goodness becomes intolerable

Kanade tries to maintain distance from Natsuki.

She consistently calls her **Nakagawa-senpai**, avoiding the more intimate first-name register used elsewhere.

She insists that Natsuki is irrelevant to her.

Her vehemence demonstrates the opposite.

## Natsuki asks Kanade for help

Natsuki struggles with difficult passages and asks Kanade to teach her.

Kanade is outraged.

In Kanade’s model of hierarchy, a senior should:

- protect pride;
- conceal weakness;
- and resent a talented junior.

Natsuki instead openly acknowledges Kanade’s superiority in that passage.

This makes her difficult to hate.

## Why Kanade needs Natsuki to be bad

Kanade has constructed a defensive story:

> The older, popular, visibly hardworking player will receive sympathy and take the place I deserve.

If Natsuki is:

- humble;
- generous;
- self-aware;
- and sincerely interested in Kanade’s ability,

then Kanade loses the moral simplicity required to protect herself.

She begins trying to dislike Natsuki because liking her would make the audition emotionally dangerous.

## The impossible senior

Natsuki’s goodness becomes threatening not because Kanade believes it is fake, but because she increasingly knows it is real.

A cruel senior can be defeated without guilt.

A kind senior turns victory into possible injury.

Kanade’s solution is to avoid victory.

---

# 18. The intentional audition failure

Kanade enters the audition visibly distressed.

She plays passages she has never missed incorrectly.

Natsuki immediately recognizes that the failure is deliberate.

This is the climax of **「嘘つきアッチェレランド」**.

## The false performance

An audition is supposed to reveal actual ability under common conditions.

Kanade uses performance to conceal actual ability.

Her wrong notes are lies.

## Not simply a gift to Natsuki

Kanade insists she did not do it for Natsuki.

This is partly true.

Her motive is self-protection:

- she does not want to be resented;
- she does not want to become the first-year who displaced a beloved third-year;
- she does not trust the band to distinguish merit from moral worth;
- and she would rather surrender voluntarily than discover that she is socially unwanted.

The intentional loss allows her to say:

> I chose this.

That is psychologically safer than winning and waiting for punishment.

## Control through self-sabotage

Kanade’s act resembles several earlier characters who surrender before uncertainty can judge them:

- Kumiko once avoided wanting victory.
- Mamiko obeyed her parents so failure would not belong entirely to her.
- Asuka prepared to withdraw before her mother or the competition could deny her.
- Hazuki considered abandoning her crush before confessing.
- Tomoe feels relief at leaving the audition’s field of judgment.

Self-sabotage converts unpredictable rejection into controlled loss.

Volume 6 identifies this as a recurring human temptation.

---

# 19. Natsuki’s refusal

Natsuki interrupts the audition and physically removes Kanade.

She says that if Kanade throws the second attempt, she will withdraw from A consideration entirely.

The method is aggressive, but the underlying principle is exact.

## A gifted place is not a victory

Natsuki refuses to accept an A position created through Kanade’s false failure.

She says, in effect:

> My effort cannot be honored by invalidating yours.

This is the strongest answer to Kanade’s fear that everyone wants Natsuki selected regardless of ability.

Natsuki herself does not want that.

## Kindness without entitlement

Kanade believes good, hardworking people become socially dangerous because others insist upon rewarding them.

Natsuki demonstrates a different kind of goodness:

- she wants recognition;
- she wants A;
- she has practiced intensely;
- and she still refuses an illegitimate advantage.

Her kindness does not demand Kanade’s sacrifice.

## Seniority as responsibility

Natsuki’s intervention is also an act of senior authority.

She does not protect Kanade from competition.

She forces her to participate honestly.

A weaker version of care would allow Kanade to escape and then comfort her.

Natsuki insists that Kanade respect both of them enough to play fully.

---

# 20. Kanade’s confession: “Nobody wants me”

Under pressure, Kanade finally explains the fear beneath her ideology.

She says:

- everyone wants Natsuki in A;
- Natsuki is popular;
- people recognize her practice;
- a third-year losing to a first-year will appear tragic;
- and even silent observers will wonder why Kanade occupies the place.

Her deepest sentence is not about technical ability.

It is:

> **Nobody wants me.**

## Merit cannot answer belonging

Kanade may objectively be the stronger player.

That does not reassure her.

An audition can say:

> You played better.

It cannot by itself say:

> We are glad that you are here.

This returns to the series’ repeated distinction between:

- being needed;
- being selected;
- and being personally wanted.

## The defensive selfish persona

Kanade claims that she is acting only for herself.

Yet she suffers because Natsuki is hurt.

She avoids social enemies because relationship matters intensely to her.

She performs selfishness because genuine need feels humiliating.

Kumiko identifies this correctly:

> Kanade is not as cold as she wants to appear.

Her “selfishness” is partly an aesthetic of control.

---

# 21. Kumiko’s mistake

Before the audition, Kumiko tells Kanade that she wants her to like Natsuki because Natsuki is a good person.

This is well-intentioned and inadequate.

It asks Kanade to solve the visible relationship before Kumiko has fully heard the fear beneath it.

Kanade does not primarily need proof that Natsuki deserves affection.

She needs reassurance that her own effort and presence are visible.

## Reconciliation is not always the first need

Kumiko’s habitual desire is:

> Everyone should get along.

This has often been productive.

It can also pressure a wounded person to become socially convenient before her injury has been acknowledged.

Kumiko realizes this during the staircase confrontation.

The correct first statement was not:

> Please like Natsuki.

It was:

> I see that you are trying too.

This is a major development in Kumiko’s mentorship.

She learns that relational harmony cannot substitute for recognition.

---

# 22. “This is Kitauji”

Kumiko tells Kanade:

> 「ここは、奏ちゃんのいた中学校じゃないんだよ。いま奏ちゃんがいるのは、北宇治」

This is not merely geographical reassurance.

It is an institutional claim:

> The past does not have to determine how this community interprets you.

## The claim is aspirational

Kitauji is not historically innocent.

It has experienced:

- seniority favoritism;
- ostracism;
- pity;
- pressure;
- and socially distorted musical decisions.

Kumiko knows this.

Her statement means not:

> Kitauji has never done what you fear.

It means:

> We have fought to become different, and we will not silently allow the old pattern to repeat here.

Institutional culture is not a permanent property.

It is something members must reenact through decisions.

## Merit plus recognition

Kumiko tells Kanade:

- everyone wants Natsuki to succeed;
- nobody wants Kanade to fail;
- the members hear her every day;
- and no one who has witnessed her work can honestly claim she has not tried.

This allows two truths to coexist:

1. Natsuki is loved.
2. Kanade is not therefore unwanted.

Kanade’s zero-sum emotional logic begins to loosen.

---

# 23. “Kanade, you are trying”

The sentence makes Kanade cry because it recognizes the exact labor she believes nobody can see.

Kanade has worked at:

- euphonium;
- social survival;
- reading rooms;
- protecting herself;
- and ensuring that nobody can dismiss her as naïve.

Much of that labor has become counterproductive.

It remains labor.

## Recognition before correction

Kumiko does not say Kanade’s action was acceptable.

She says the person beneath it is visible.

Only after that recognition can Kanade:

- apologize;
- accept the second audition;
- and play honestly.

This is a central pedagogical principle of the novel:

> A person is more capable of changing when acknowledgment does not depend upon first becoming easy to approve.

## Physical collapse

Kanade clings to Kumiko and cries like a much younger child.

The polished junior persona disappears.

The moment confirms that her sophistication is partly accelerated self-protection.

She has learned to read others before learning to trust them.

---

# 24. Kumiko inherits Asuka—and becomes different from her

During the restaurant conversation, Kumiko consciously uses techniques learned from Asuka:

- noticing contradiction;
- predicting the answer behind the answer;
- pressing where another person is defended;
- and refusing the socially convenient surface.

Kanade identifies the trait accurately:

> Kumiko looks harmless, then walks directly into other people’s hearts.

## The Asuka inheritance

Kumiko has inherited:

- perceptiveness;
- willingness to cross boundaries;
- and the belief that hidden motives matter.

This fulfills part of the notebook’s symbolic transfer.

## The decisive difference

Asuka often translated people into:

- functions;
- useful pressures;
- risks;
- and manageable outcomes.

Kumiko increasingly uses perception to offer recognition.

Asuka might correctly diagnose Kanade’s strategy.

Kumiko tells Kanade that she has been seen.

The distinction can be formulated as:

> **Asuka enters another person’s interior in order to understand the mechanism.  
> Kumiko enters in order to remain with the person she finds there.**

Kumiko is not always successful or gentle.

She is developing an ethical use for the observational ability that once enabled detachment.

---

# 25. The audition result: three euphoniums

Natsuki, Kumiko, and Kanade are all selected for A ensemble.

The result is important on several levels.

## Kanade’s assumed scarcity was false

Kanade imagined a fixed zero-sum structure:

> Either Natsuki or Kanade must be sacrificed.

Taki selects all three.

The euphonium allocation was not as rigid as Kanade assumed.

Her self-sabotage would have accomplished nothing except removing her own ability from the ensemble.

## Flexible merit

The audition is not a single ranked ladder in which only one universally best person survives.

Taki is constructing an ensemble.

He can adjust:

- balance;
- part allocation;
- and instrumentation

according to the actual abilities available.

This is merit understood contextually rather than as abstract human ranking.

## Natsuki earns recognition

Natsuki’s selection is not charity.

She makes mistakes during the audition but has improved enough to be chosen within the available ensemble.

Kanade’s presence does not invalidate Natsuki’s growth.

## Kanade is relieved

Kanade’s whispered “thank goodness” is unambiguously sincere.

She wanted the place.

The relief exposes the emotional cost of pretending otherwise.

---

# 26. The tuba result: merit still wounds

Takuya, Riko, and Mirei are selected.

Hazuki and Satsuki are not.

This prevents the three-euphonium resolution from becoming a sentimental claim that everyone can receive the desired place.

## Mirei’s guilt

Mirei’s body folds inward when her name is called.

Her success places her directly beside two friends who have lost.

The moment realizes the fear Kanade carried.

## Hazuki’s response

Hazuki congratulates Mirei and touches her back.

Satsuki also reaches toward her.

Their disappointment does not become an accusation.

This is the social culture Kanade did not believe could exist:

> The selected junior does not have to become the enemy of the unselected friend.

## Pain remains

Hazuki and Satsuki’s kindness does not make their exclusion painless.

The volume does not grant them compensatory places.

Meritocratic selection still creates loss.

The ethical achievement lies not in eliminating the loss, but in refusing to convert it into moral condemnation of the person selected.

---

# 27. Motomu’s selection and instrumental specificity

Midori and Motomu are both selected as contrabass players.

This confirms Midori’s earlier argument.

Motomu’s position does not simply consume an arbitrary place that could have belonged to Natsuki or another senior.

The ensemble wants the color he contributes.

The result reinforces a core principle:

> An audition selects a configuration of sounds, not a moral ranking of people.

The audience may experience the combined resonance without identifying Motomu’s individual contribution.

His invisibility does not make the contribution unreal.

---

# 28. Yume’s selection

Yume is selected among the five trumpets.

Her result demonstrates that ability can be recognized before confidence has fully developed.

This creates future pressure.

Being chosen may:

- strengthen her belief;
- intensify fear;
- or force Reina to develop a more patient model of mentorship.

The result is an opportunity, not the completion of Yume’s transformation.

---

# 29. Leadership after Asuka: “Asuka’s curse”

Nozomi describes Kitauji’s organizational difficulty as something like **Asuka’s curse**.

The phrase does not blame Asuka personally.

It identifies an institutional problem:

> A system designed around an unusually capable person becomes fragile after that person leaves.

## Yuuko’s overextension

Yuuko attempts to carry:

- leadership;
- schedules;
- summer planning;
- member coordination;
- and the inherited national-gold objective.

She compares what she should manage with what Asuka once managed.

## Natsuki’s correction

Natsuki reminds her:

- Asuka’s capacity was unusual;
- Asuka was vice-president rather than president;
- and trying to duplicate her workload is not proof of leadership.

The solution is distribution.

Tomoe’s move into management immediately reduces the burden.

## Institutional maturation

Kitauji is gradually replacing the heroic-leader model with:

- president;
- vice-president;
- managers;
- section leaders;
- instructors;
- and informal listeners such as Kumiko.

This is healthier than dependence upon one dazzling center.

Whether the culture can maintain coherence without recreating another Asuka remains unresolved.

---

# 30. Yuuko and Natsuki as complementary leaders

Their arguments continue, but Volume 6 demonstrates why the pairing works.

## Yuuko

Yuuko supplies:

- urgency;
- conviction;
- emotional visibility;
- and relentless commitment to the national goal.

## Natsuki

Natsuki supplies:

- skepticism;
- workload awareness;
- social observation;
- and willingness to challenge emotionally appealing but impractical choices.

## The audition confrontation

Natsuki’s treatment of Kanade is also leadership.

She protects:

- the audition’s legitimacy;
- Kanade’s opportunity;
- and her own right to earn rather than receive a place.

Her intervention is emotionally explosive, but ethically tied to the institution’s stated values.

## The danger

Their balance can still fail.

Yuuko may overwork.  
Natsuki may become overprotective.  
Their constant correction may exhaust both.

The second movement has not yet shown whether this system can survive the greater pressure of actual competition.

---

# 31. Mizore and Nozomi: the blue bird has not yet flown

The free-choice piece formalizes the relationship’s unresolved structure.

## The fairy tale

Liz lives alone.

A blue-haired girl enters her home and becomes her family.

Liz discovers that the girl is the blue bird she once fed.

She loves the girl and wants her to remain, but recognizes that keeping her means confining her.

Liz opens the cage.

The blue bird flies away.

## Kumiko’s interpretation

Kumiko immediately sees Mizore as Liz and Nozomi as the blue bird.

Mizore wants continued proximity.

Nozomi possesses a more outward-moving social nature.

The relationship’s emotional weight remains asymmetrical.

## Nozomi’s interpretation

Nozomi says the story resembles them and appears excited to perform it.

She does not show Mizore’s fear.

It is unclear whether she:

- assigns the roles differently;
- treats the resemblance lightly;
- or simply believes their earlier reconciliation has made the parallel safe.

The discrepancy matters more than finding one objectively correct assignment.

The two girls are playing the same story while hearing different meanings.

## The third movement

The third movement, “A Decision Born of Love,” centers upon the oboe and flute dialogue.

The music forces their instruments to perform an intimacy and separation their speech has not resolved.

The free-choice piece is therefore not merely symbolically appropriate.

It is structurally dangerous.

---

# 32. Nozomi’s easy pluralism and Mizore’s exclusivity

Nozomi invites Mizore to the Agata Festival.

Mizore is delighted until Nozomi suggests inviting:

- Natsuki;
- Yuuko;
- and anyone else Mizore likes.

Mizore’s face tightens.

Nozomi does not notice.

## Different meanings of inclusion

For Nozomi, inviting more people means:

- greater fun;
- social generosity;
- and helping Mizore broaden her world.

For Mizore, adding others dilutes the exclusive time she believed she had been offered.

Neither interpretation is inherently malicious.

The problem is that one person continues to experience the relationship as singular while the other experiences it as expandable.

## Nozomi’s disappointment

When Mizore says there is nobody else she wants to invite, Nozomi appears slightly saddened.

She may recognize, dimly, that Mizore’s world remains too narrow.

Yet she does not understand that her own presence is the reason the world has been organized that way.

---

# 33. The epilogue: talent invites flight

Niiyama calls Mizore to discuss her future.

She says Mizore has musical possibility and offers:

- conservatory information;
- oboe-teacher connections;
- and theoretical support.

Mizore is bewildered.

She does not understand why anyone would invest in her.

## Talent without self-authored ambition

Mizore believes she lacks:

- talent;
- passion;
- and an independent reason to continue music.

This is striking because others regard her as one of the band’s most exceptional players.

Technical potential and personal vocation are not identical.

## Nozomi’s name as the only future criterion

Mizore sees no reason to attend music university if Nozomi is not there.

Her future remains relationally derivative.

She chose Kitauji because Nozomi chose it.

She considers music university because Nozomi might choose it.

## Nozomi’s reaction to Niiyama

When Nozomi hears that Niiyama gave Mizore the brochure, her movement briefly stops.

She then says she may apply to the same institution.

The novel does not explicitly explain the reaction.

Several feelings may coexist:

- genuine interest;
- revived ambition;
- fear of being left behind by Mizore’s talent;
- jealousy of Niiyama’s attention;
- or desire to preserve their shared path.

The ambiguity is important.

Nozomi is usually treated as the moving blue bird.

For a moment, Mizore’s talent may be the force capable of carrying her somewhere Nozomi cannot automatically follow.

## Mizore’s final reason

Mizore answers:

> 「希美が受けるなら、私も」

If Nozomi applies, she will too.

The narration confirms that this is genuinely her only reason.

The volume therefore ends by refusing premature growth.

Mizore has expanded socially and artistically.

She has not yet separated her future from Nozomi’s movement.

---

# 34. Kumiko and Reina: fear of losing the excuse to remain together

The Agata Festival returns Kumiko and Reina to the setting where their private bond first became explicit.

Kumiko attends the festival with Shuuichi, then brings Reina a citrus sweet and climbs toward Daikichiyama.

Reina is already there, playing trumpet.

## Relational plurality

Reina tells Kumiko to attend with Shuuichi because they are dating.

She does not frame him as an enemy.

Kumiko still feels guilty for not choosing Reina first.

The emotional structure remains plural rather than mutually exclusive.

## Reina’s future

Reina intends to:

- become professional;
- study abroad;
- learn from many musicians;
- and move beyond the local world.

This is the life she has always wanted.

It also frightens her.

She says:

> 「一緒にいる口実が、いつかなくなるんじゃないかって」

She fears that their excuse for being together will someday disappear.

## A healthier version of Mizore’s fear

Reina’s fear resembles Mizore’s:

> What remains when the shared institution ends?

The difference is that Reina says it directly.

Kumiko answers it.

They do not resolve the future, but the fear becomes mutual knowledge rather than private panic.

This contrast is one of the volume’s most important relational lessons:

> Dependence becomes especially dangerous when the person cannot name it before separation arrives.

## Kumiko’s exclusivity

Kumiko admits that she once treasured being the only person at Kitauji who called Reina by her given name.

The admission reveals possessiveness.

She wanted Reina to integrate into the ensemble.

She also wanted to remain specially chosen.

The novel continues to reject purified friendship. Intimacy includes:

- generosity;
- jealousy;
- pride;
- and the desire for private distinction.

## The private concert

Reina plays the oboe solo from the third movement of *Liz and the Blue Bird* on trumpet for Kumiko alone.

Her interpretation changes the emotional color.

Instead of fragile grief, the melody becomes:

- strong;
- forward-facing;
- and determined to release the loved person toward happiness.

Kumiko hears a Liz capable of letting the blue bird go.

The scene establishes Reina’s philosophy of attachment:

> Love may fear separation, but it should not require the loved person to remain small.

Whether she can live this principle when actual separation arrives remains open.

---

# 35. Kumiko and Shuuichi: romance as ordinary trust

Kumiko and Shuuichi are now openly dating in private, though the relationship remains discreet within the band.

Their festival time together contains:

- food;
- familiar teasing;
- concern for safety;
- and the expectation that Kumiko will later visit Reina.

## No demand for exclusivity

Shuuichi does not attempt to prevent Kumiko from going.

He knows Reina occupies a significant part of Kumiko’s emotional world.

His unease around Reina remains comic and genuine, but he does not ask Kumiko to choose between them.

## Kumiko’s awkwardness

Kumiko still finds overtly boyfriend-like behavior embarrassing.

Their long familiarity makes newly romantic gestures feel strangely artificial.

The relationship is not a transformation into a conventional romantic script.

It is an old bond learning a new register.

## Grounded continuity

Shuuichi represents a relational structure different from Reina’s:

- less aesthetically heightened;
- less verbally possessive;
- more ordinary;
- and rooted in shared daily geography.

The novel continues treating both relationships as real without requiring them to perform the same emotional work.

---

# 36. Audition merit as contextual rather than absolute

Volume 6 refines the series’ view of meritocracy.

## The old error

The pre-Taki band allowed seniority and popularity to override ability.

## Kanade’s feared error

A technically fair audition may still produce social punishment for the talented newcomer who wins.

## Motomu’s error

He imagines every selected player as consuming one generic place another person could have received.

## The actual model

Taki selects an ensemble according to:

- instrumentation;
- balance;
- part requirements;
- current ability;
- and the total configuration of available players.

Three euphoniums can be chosen.

Two contrabasses can be useful.

A first-year tuba player can be selected while a second-year is not.

Merit is real but role-dependent.

## Human worth remains outside the result

Hazuki’s exclusion does not make her a lesser person.

Mirei’s inclusion does not prove superior moral character.

Natsuki’s selection does not mean Kanade had to disappear.

The audition judges musical function.

The community must prevent that judgment from becoming a total hierarchy of human value.

---

# 37. Effort, efficiency, and legibility

Kanade’s middle-school experience forces the series to ask what counts as effort.

## Visible practice

A player who spends more hours struggling may appear more deserving.

## Efficient development

A player who learns quickly or practices strategically may appear not to care, even when she does.

## Emotional labor

Kanade expends enormous energy reading social danger.

That labor does not improve tone directly, but it shapes every musical choice she makes.

## Senior persistence

Natsuki has worked for years, improved gradually, and remains technically vulnerable beside Kanade.

Her effort is real.

## The novel’s answer

The volume refuses a single universal conversion formula.

Effort does not create automatic entitlement to a place.

Ability does not eliminate the need to respect others’ labor.

Visible struggle should not be romanticized into superiority.

Ease should not be interpreted as absence of commitment.

A healthy ensemble must recognize several forms of work without allowing recognition to predetermine the audition.

---

# 38. The chapter-title progression

The musical titles form a coherent emotional score.

## 「不穏なダ・カーポ」 — Unsettling Da Capo

The year begins again.

But returning to the beginning brings old structures with it:

- uncertain newcomers;
- inherited ambition;
- unresolved Nozomi–Mizore dependency;
- and the possibility of seniority conflict repeating under new names.

The repetition is unsettling because the characters know what can go wrong.

## 「孤独にマルカート」 — Lonely Marcato

**Marcato** asks for notes to be distinctly marked.

The new first-years emerge as sharply separated emotional voices:

- Mirei’s guarded competence;
- Satsuki’s social warmth;
- Motomu’s selective devotion;
- Yume’s fear of visibility;
- Ririka’s performative affection;
- and Kanade’s defensive intelligence.

Each is audible as an individual.

The challenge is to prevent clear articulation from becoming isolation.

## 「嘘つきアッチェレランド」 — Lying Accelerando

**Accelerando** increases speed.

As auditions approach, pressure accelerates and hidden motives become harder to contain.

The lies include:

- Kanade’s ideal-junior performance;
- Kanade’s intentional wrong notes;
- her claim that she does not care about Natsuki;
- Tomoe’s simplified public cheerfulness;
- Nozomi’s treatment of Mizore’s declaration as a joke;
- and the various ways characters select one truth from a more complicated emotional whole.

The chapter does not condemn all performance.

It distinguishes between:

- a selected truth that allows life to continue;
- and a false performance designed to prevent genuine judgment.

Kanade’s wrong notes belong to the latter category.

# 39. Character voices and speaking styles

Volume 6 introduces several highly distinctive new voices.

## Kumiko

Kumiko’s outward speech remains softer and less certain than her internal judgments.

As a senior, she increasingly asks direct questions and occasionally adopts Asuka-like pressure.

Her greatest linguistic development is the ability to move from diagnosis to affirmation:

> Kanade is trying.

## Kanade

Kanade’s default speech is polished and formally respectful.

She uses elaborate politeness to create distance and plausible innocence.

Her register changes according to purpose:

- ideal junior before seniors;
- dry sarcasm with Ririka;
- cold analysis with Mirei;
- theatrical selfishness with Kumiko;
- and childlike fragmentation during collapse.

Her politeness is not evidence of submission.

It is one of her sharpest instruments.

## Satsuki

Satsuki speaks with cheerful immediacy.

She assumes familiarity quickly and treats warmth as the default.

The same quality that makes her easy to like can make her insensitive to another person’s need for distance.

## Mirei

Mirei’s speech is restrained, serious, and defensive.

She rejects nicknames and social cushioning because both feel like attempts to avoid acknowledging the real hierarchy of ability.

When she finally laughs at “Micchan,” the shift is audible as a relaxation of controlled identity.

## Motomu

Motomu speaks abruptly and defensively around most people.

With Midori, his language becomes deferential and intensely attentive.

The change makes the concentration of his attachment visible.

## Ririka

Ririka uses playful intimacy:

- exaggerated affection;
- physical contact;
- teasing;
- and deliberately blurred joke–truth boundaries.

She creates social motion where Mizore and Kanade tend toward stasis.

## Yume

Yume’s speech is hesitant, self-correcting, and apologetic.

She often sounds as though she is asking permission to occupy the conversation.

Her difficulty is not lack of thought but lack of confidence that the thought deserves space.

## Natsuki

Natsuki’s casual Kansai speech remains relaxed until an ethical boundary is crossed.

During the audition confrontation, it becomes:

- forceful;
- profane;
- direct;
- and stripped of ironic distance.

Her anger is clearest when someone attempts to erase her agency through supposed kindness.

## Tomoe

Tomoe’s comic looseness hides careful social observation.

She can shift rapidly from joking to unusually clear reflection about:

- beginners;
- status;
- pity;
- and the social meaning of A and B membership.

## Nozomi

Nozomi’s light, expansive register continues to contrast with Mizore’s compressed speech.

She can unintentionally invalidate seriousness by interpreting an uncomfortable statement as a joke.

## Mizore

Mizore remains literal and minimal.

Her most consequential statements often sound simple because she does not decorate them:

> If Nozomi applies, she will apply.

The simplicity does not indicate lightness. It exposes the totality of the dependence.

# 40. Relational intensity and yuri-coded material

Volume 6 continues the novels’ dense field of emotionally intense female relationships.

## Kumiko and Reina

Their festival sequence contains:

- negotiated priority;
- possessiveness over forms of address;
- anxiety about future separation;
- physical closeness;
- a private musical performance;
- and the sense that each has selected the other.

This remains strongly yuri-coded even as Kumiko’s relationship with Shuuichi continues.

## Mizore and Nozomi

Their relationship is the central emotional referent of *Liz and the Blue Bird*.

It contains:

- singular attachment;
- fear of release;
- jealousy of plural social life;
- and the desire to build an entire future around continued proximity.

Its intensity is unquestionable. Its romantic category remains formally unstated.

## Kanade and Kumiko

Kanade seeks Kumiko’s exclusive recognition and finally clings to her while crying.

The relationship is primarily structured as junior–senior attachment, but Kanade’s performative admiration and Kumiko’s physical attentiveness create a deliberately charged intimacy.

## Kanade and Ririka

Ririka openly calls them:

- in love;
- best friends;
- and each other’s number one.

Much is joking, but the jokes function because genuine preference exists underneath them.

## Kanade and Natsuki

Their audition confrontation is physically and emotionally intense:

- wall pinning;
- close faces;
- anger;
- sacrifice;
- recognition;
- and the demand for honest competition.

The relationship is not conventionally romantic, but it is highly charged and likely to become one of the second movement’s central antagonistic intimacies.

# 41. Visual paratext

## Original cover

The original cover centers three new low-brass first-years:

- Kanade at left with euphonium;
- Satsuki in the middle holding a metronome;
- Mirei at right with tuba.

Kumiko is absent.

That omission is significant.

The cover announces that the new movement will not simply repeat Kumiko’s first-year story with Kumiko still occupying every visual center.

She is now the senior around whom other protagonists emerge.

## Kanade

Kanade smiles calmly and holds the euphonium with apparent ease.

The image preserves her polished ideal-junior surface.

Her placement at the edge and the instrument’s large bell nevertheless give her strong visual authority.

## Satsuki

Satsuki occupies the center without holding her tuba.

Instead, she holds the metronome.

This suits her social function.

She is not the strongest player, but she helps establish a shared tempo of friendliness around which the other girls react.

## Mirei

Mirei sits rigidly with the tuba across her body.

Her expression is reserved and slightly troubled.

The instrument appears almost defensive—a large physical object interposed between her and the viewer.

## One tuba spanning the composition

The tuba’s long tubing extends behind the central figure and visually connects the three girls.

The instrument becomes a line binding:

- competence;
- beginner enthusiasm;
- resentment;
- and the new low-brass generation.

## Promotional wrapper

The wrapper declares:

> **New chapter begins.**

It describes Kumiko becoming a second-year student and confronting highly individualistic juniors.

The lower anime advertisement, however, uses imagery from the Asuka-centered animated film.

The original book looks forward toward Kanade, Satsuki, and Mirei.

The adaptation branding still sells the emotional climax of the preceding generation.

This is an apt paratextual image of the series’ transitional moment.

# 42. Adaptation baseline

The following elements are essential for any later anime comparison.

## 1. The unanimous goal vote

The contrast with Volume 1 must remain clear.

Kitauji now inherits national ambition as institutional common sense.

## 2. Yume’s first conversation with Kumiko

This establishes:

- Kumiko as senior;
- motivation as something that can change;
- and transformation as something no institution can promise automatically.

## 3. Tomoe’s notebooks

They explain why she becomes novice-instruction leader and connect her care to the old Kitauji conflict.

## 4. Tomoe’s mixed reaction to leaving performance

Her relief, social motivation, dislike of pity, and managerial self-knowledge should not be simplified into either noble sacrifice or uncomplicated medical tragedy.

## 5. Mirei and Satsuki’s conflict

The story requires the full structure:

- technical inequality;
- popularity;
- unwanted nicknames;
- instrument assignment;
- and Mirei’s desire for recognition.

## 6. Kanade’s intervention with Mirei

Kanade’s social intelligence must appear genuinely valuable before its defensive dangers are exposed.

## 7. Kumiko’s disagreement with Kanade

The difference between coerced conformity and self-chosen relational change is central.

## 8. Motomu’s question about contrabass

Midori’s answer regarding resonance rather than volume is one of the volume’s key musical metaphors.

## 9. Yume and Reina

Reina’s difficulty mentoring a timid but gifted junior develops a limitation not visible in her own first-year story.

## 10. Ririka’s ordinary conversation with Mizore

This small success demonstrates real social expansion.

## 11. “Asuka’s curse”

The institutional cost of building a system around exceptional capability must be preserved.

## 12. Kumiko and Reina at the festival

The future-separation conversation, Kumiko’s possessiveness, and Reina’s private performance of the third movement are major source material.

## 13. Kanade’s restaurant scene

Her anger at Natsuki asking for help is the first clear exposure of her defensive logic.

## 14. The intentional audition failure

The wrong notes must sound recognizably inconsistent with Kanade’s established ability.

## 15. Natsuki’s interruption

Her refusal of a gifted place is the ethical counterargument to Kanade’s worldview.

## 16. Kanade’s full confession

Her fear is not simply that she will hurt Natsuki. She fears being socially hated despite deserving the musical place.

## 17. Kumiko’s correction of herself

She realizes that asking Kanade to like Natsuki was premature.

The needed statement was acknowledgment of Kanade’s effort.

## 18. All three euphoniums selected

The result reveals the false zero-sum assumption underlying Kanade’s sacrifice.

## 19. The tuba result

Hazuki and Satsuki’s response to Mirei must remain, because it proves that disappointment need not become hostility toward the selected player.

## 20. The epilogue

Niiyama’s offer, Nozomi’s reaction, and Mizore’s decision to follow her into music university establish the unresolved conflict for Part Two.

# Final assessment

Volume 6 successfully begins a second major phase without merely replaying Kumiko’s first year.

Its greatest achievement is that the new first-years are not simple replacements for the original cast.

There are echoes:

- Yume resembles the uncertain Kumiko who entered without conviction.
- Satsuki resembles Hazuki’s social warmth and technical beginnerhood.
- Kanade recalls Asuka’s perceptiveness and control.
- Mirei carries Reina-like pride without Reina’s security.
- Motomu concentrates musical meaning around an admired girl, as Mizore once did.

But each echo changes the argument.

The second movement begins **da capo**, from the beginning, but no one returns to the same measure unchanged.

Kumiko now stands on the other side of the senior–junior relationship.

She discovers that seniority does not mean possessing answers.

It means becoming responsible for the effects of one’s attempted answers.

Her first instinct is often reasonable:

- encourage Yume;
- reconcile Mirei and Satsuki;
- make Kanade appreciate Natsuki;
- imitate Tomoe’s care;
- preserve harmony.

Volume 6 teaches her that good intentions can still miss the actual need.

Mirei needs her ability recognized before social adjustment becomes possible.

Kanade needs her effort acknowledged before she can stop treating Natsuki as a threat.

Tomoe needs her chosen relationship to the band accepted without being measured against Kumiko’s musical passion.

Mizore needs more than the factual assurance that Nozomi cares.

Reina needs acknowledgment that her ambition and fear of separation coexist.

The volume’s central confrontation with Kanade brings the series’ meritocratic argument to its most sophisticated point so far.

Kanade is not opposed to merit.

She believes in merit so intensely that she no longer trusts other people to honor it.

Her middle school taught her that:

- visible struggle can be rewarded over ability;
- affection can disguise itself as fairness;
- and the better player can still be treated as morally undeserving.

Kitauji’s auditions appear to solve that problem.

Kanade understands that an audition result does not govern the hallway afterward.

She can earn the place and still become the girl who took a beloved senior’s final summer.

Her intentional wrong notes are therefore a lie told against anticipated social judgment.

She will choose the loss before others choose hatred.

Natsuki’s response matters because it dismantles the caricature Kanade needs.

Natsuki wants A.

She has worked for it.

She is popular and kind.

She also refuses to accept a place purchased by Kanade’s disappearance.

Her goodness is not a demand that talent yield to sentiment.

It is respect for the other player’s agency.

Kumiko then gives Kanade what the audition cannot:

> You have been heard.  
> Your work exists.  
> You are not merely the talented first-year standing in a senior’s way.

The immediate result—three euphoniums selected—is almost elegantly anticlimactic.

The scarcity Kanade treated as inevitable was partly imagined.

The ensemble had room for:

- Natsuki’s hard-earned growth;
- Kumiko’s established upper part;
- and Kanade’s technical strength.

But the tuba announcement prevents this from becoming a comforting universal solution.

Hazuki and Satsuki are not selected.

Mirei is.

The difference is social rather than procedural.

Hazuki and Satsuki do not convert disappointment into the claim that Mirei should be ashamed.

They touch her, congratulate her, and let the pain remain pain.

That is the Kitauji Kumiko promised Kanade:

> not a world without losing,  
> but a world in which another person’s victory does not have to become proof that she is unwanted.

The Nozomi–Mizore frame prevents the audition plot from standing alone.

*Liz and the Blue Bird* asks whether love can release what it most wants to keep.

Mizore is still not ready.

She wants the performance never to arrive. She chooses music university only because Nozomi may attend. Their reconciliation has preserved the relationship without resolving its unequal structure.

Kumiko and Reina provide a possible counter-model.

They also fear future separation.

Reina may leave for professional study. Kumiko fears being left behind. Both value the exclusivity of being chosen.

But they speak.

Reina says she is afraid their excuse for being together will disappear.

Kumiko admits her possessiveness.

They sit together, exchange festival sweets, and transform the oboe’s painful third movement into a private trumpet performance oriented toward courageous release.

The contrast suggests the emotional task awaiting Mizore and Nozomi:

> Separation becomes most destructive when it cannot be named until one person is already gone.

The first half of the second movement ends before Kitauji reaches a public competition stage.

Its climax is the creation of the ensemble that will go there.

Eighty-nine people enter the audition period.

Fifty-five are selected.

Some are relieved.  
Some cry.  
Some feel guilty.  
Some support the people who defeated them.  
One former player stands at the edge taking notes as manager.  
Three euphoniums discover that they did not need to erase one another.

The volume’s place in the larger series can therefore be summarized as:

> **The first movement established a fairer system.  
> The second movement begins by asking whether the people inside that system have learned how to live fairly with one another.**

That is a harder problem than writing the audition rules.

It requires the band to hear not only who plays best, but also the lonely, marked voices that fear what being heard will cost.
