# What kind of volume this is

Volume 9 begins Kumiko’s third and final year.

That alone would make it structurally important, but its more consequential change is institutional.

Kumiko is no longer merely:

- an observer;
- a mediator;
- a senior;
- an A-ensemble player;
- or the student who intervenes when someone else’s leadership fails.

She is now president.

She must help determine:

- the culture of the band;
- its rehearsal philosophy;
- how dissent is received;
- how beginners are treated;
- how auditions are understood;
- and how the members continue believing in a system whose decisions may hurt them.

She must do all of this while also competing inside that system.

That final condition is essential.

Kumiko is not an external administrator defending neutral rules. She personally wants:

- an A-ensemble place;
- the euphonium soli;
- another performance beside Reina;
- national gold;
- and recognition as someone worthy of leading Kitauji’s final year.

Then Mayu Kuroe arrives.

Mayu is:

- a third-year transfer student;
- a national-gold-level euphonium player;
- socially polished;
- musically formidable;
- apparently self-effacing;
- and unfamiliar with Kitauji’s accumulated relationships.

She is exactly the kind of musician Kitauji’s meritocratic philosophy says it should welcome.

She is also exactly the person capable of taking Kumiko’s place.

Volume 9 therefore asks:

> **Can Kumiko defend a fair system when fairness may deprive her of the thing she most wants?**

The answer remains unresolved at the end of Part One.

That incompletion is deliberate.

The first Kyoto audition gives Kumiko:

- A-ensemble membership;
- the soli;
- and public support.

Instead of reassuring her, it contaminates victory with a new suspicion:

> Was she selected because her playing was best—or because selecting the president preserved institutional harmony?

Volume 9 is thus not primarily a story about whether Kumiko defeats Mayu.

It is a story about whether Kumiko can continue trusting judgment once she understands that judgment is:

- contextual;
- socially consequential;
- partly opaque;
- and impossible to separate completely from the relationships surrounding it.

# Executive thesis

Volume 9 is about **legitimacy**.

Not only technical merit.

Not only leadership.

Legitimacy is the social condition under which people accept that a decision has the right to govern them.

The novel repeatedly asks:

- Why should 103 students accept national gold as their shared objective?
- Why should beginners accept Reina’s severity?
- Why should Sally believe that suffering is necessary?
- Why should Mayu believe that winning a place will not make her an intruder?
- Why should Mirei trust Taki’s selection of Suzume over Satsuki?
- Why should Kumiko trust that her own soli was awarded for musical reasons?
- Why should younger students revere Taki as absolutely as the generation that witnessed his revolution?
- Why should Shuuichi accept that Kumiko’s presidency requires their romantic separation?
- Why should Kumiko continue following a future she has not chosen for herself?

The first trilogy established a cleaner proposition:

> Merit should replace seniority.

Volume 9 reveals that “merit” is not a single transparent quantity.

An ensemble is not built by ranking every player along one universal scale.

Taki considers:

- tone;
- volume;
- role;
- instrumentation;
- balance;
- repertoire;
- growth;
- and the sound of the whole group.

That allows a novice such as Suzume to defeat the technically more experienced Satsuki.

The decision is musically reasoned.

It is not immediately legible to the people affected by it.

This leads to the volume’s central institutional problem:

> **A system can be rational without appearing fair.  
> A decision can be correct without producing trust.**

Kumiko’s presidential task is not to eliminate this contradiction.

She must keep the band functioning inside it.

That is why the volume gives her one of her darkest internal lines:

> 「反乱の芽は早めに摘み取らねばならない。部をまとめるというのは、そういうことだ」  
> The seeds of rebellion must be plucked early. That is part of holding a band together.

The sentence is startling because it comes from Kumiko—the listener, mediator, and former skeptic of official authority.

She has not become a tyrant.

She does recognize that leadership changes the meaning of empathy.

Listening to dissent is no longer only a moral act.

It is also a way of:

- managing dissent;
- preserving legitimacy;
- preventing factionalization;
- and keeping the institution governable.

Volume 9’s deepest argument can therefore be formulated as:

> **Leadership is not moral innocence elevated into office.  
> It is the moment care, persuasion, authority, partiality, and institutional survival become impossible to separate cleanly.**

# 1. “The Final Movement of Determination”

The title announces several simultaneous endings.

## Kumiko’s final school year

This is her last:

- spring recruitment;
- Sunrise Festival;
- Agata Festival as a Kitauji student;
- audition cycle;
- Kyoto competition;
- summer;
- and opportunity to perform beside the people who formed her.

## The final movement of Kitauji’s founding generation

The students who entered under Taki are now third-years.

Their class has experienced the entire institutional transformation:

- weak band;
- national ambition;
- merit conflict;
- national bronze;
- failure to return;
- and the current attempt at gold.

They are the last generation that remembers pre-prestige Kitauji from inside.

## The final movement of borrowed goals

Kumiko has become extraordinarily capable at pursuing goals once someone else establishes them.

She can work toward:

- nationals;
- A ensemble;
- a soli;
- leadership;
- and improvement.

She remains unable to answer what she wants after graduation.

The “determination” in the title is therefore not only competitive determination.

It concerns the choice of a life.

## A movement is not a conclusion

A final movement does not simply stop.

It gathers earlier themes, transforms them, and brings them into relation.

Volume 9 returns to nearly every major question from the series:

- merit versus seniority;
- insider versus outsider;
- effort versus talent;
- enjoyment versus discipline;
- personal desire versus collective need;
- charismatic authority versus institutional trust;
- and whether special relationships can survive the end of their shared environment.

The title promises resolution.

Part One primarily reveals how much remains unresolved.

# 2. Prologue: the cherry tree and the story larger than Kumiko

The prologue is narrated from Taki’s perspective.

This immediately changes the scale.

He looks from the staff room toward a cherry tree planted seven years earlier by former students. It has taken root and grown into part of the school landscape.

Taki reflects that this is his third year at Kitauji.

The first-years he met upon arrival are now seniors.

The band’s current privileges—administrative cooperation, rehearsal support, institutional prestige—have become ordinary.

What earlier students fought to establish is now the environment into which new students enter.

## The cherry tree as institutional memory

The tree was planted by students who are no longer present.

Its continued growth does not require their continued physical presence.

This repeats the series’ mature model of inheritance:

- Shindō gives Asuka the euphonium.
- Asuka gives Kumiko the notebook and song.
- Yuuko gives Kumiko the ensemble-contest reform and presidency.
- Graduates give Kitauji a culture their successors experience as normal.

The tree is living memory rather than monument.

## Success becoming nature

The younger students do not experience Kitauji’s status as a miracle.

They experience it as the school they intentionally joined.

This will later explain Mirei’s criticism.

Kumiko’s generation sees Taki as the person who transformed everything.

Mirei’s generation sees him as the current conductor of an already strong band.

The same man occupies different historical meanings depending upon when the student entered.

## 「音を楽しめ！」

The recruitment poster carries the slogan:

> 「音を楽しめ！」  
> Enjoy sound!

The phrase is almost ironic in light of the conflict that follows.

What does enjoyment mean inside a band where:

- beginners are scolded;
- every place is provisional;
- national gold governs the calendar;
- and 103 people compete for fifty-five seats?

The novel does not treat enjoyment and rigor as opposites.

It does insist that “enjoy music” is not self-explanatory.

Reina, Sally, Shuuichi, Mizore, Hazuki, and Taki would all define it differently.

## The final prologue line

> 「これは、北宇治高校吹奏楽部の物語だ」  
> This is the story of the Kitauji High School Concert Band.

The line shifts ownership away from any one protagonist.

Kumiko remains the main viewpoint.

The institution now contains more lives than even her highly observant narration can fully know.

That is the challenge of her presidency.

# 3. The executive notebook

The **Kitauji Executive Notebook** becomes Volume 9’s primary formal innovation.

Yuuko recommended that Kumiko, Shuuichi, and Reina maintain an exchange notebook so that the three leaders could communicate.

The opening entry establishes their intended division:

- president and vice-president handle human relations;
- drum major concentrates upon musical instruction.

The arrangement is a direct response to earlier institutional failures.

## Haruka and Asuka

Haruka possessed formal authority.

Asuka possessed practical, musical, and social authority.

The split created uncertainty over who truly led.

## Yuuko and Natsuki

Yuuko carried enormous emotional and administrative weight.

Natsuki moderated her but could not fully distribute the work.

## Team Oumae

The third-year structure attempts explicit differentiation:

- Kumiko hears people.
- Shuuichi stabilizes practical life.
- Reina drives the sound.

The notebook is the paper architecture of distributed leadership.

## Communication does not eliminate secrecy

The notebook contains:

- disagreements;
- jokes;
- rehearsal policy;
- anxiety;
- and commentary.

Yet the most consequential matters remain outside it.

Kumiko does not tell Reina that she has begun doubting Taki.

Shuuichi does not write how much the breakup hurts him.

Reina often speaks or acts before consulting the other two.

Kumiko uses private conversations to manage Sally and Mirei.

The notebook therefore represents an aspiration toward transparent leadership rather than its completion.

It is the official score.

The emotional performance remains full of tacit passages.

# 4. Team Oumae in practice

Volume 8 demonstrated that the three leaders could design a voting system together.

Volume 9 tests them under sustained institutional pressure.

## Reina: the principle of excellence

Reina believes:

- national gold is possible;
- every member must act toward it;
- beginners should be treated as full participants;
- Taki’s musical judgment is trustworthy;
- and competition should remain intense.

Her strengths are:

- decisiveness;
- technical hearing;
- authority;
- and the capacity to transform anxiety into action.

Her danger is that she often experiences fear or weakness as insufficient resolve.

## Shuuichi: the principle of continuity

Shuuichi wants:

- the band to remain humane;
- members to enjoy performance;
- avoidable conflict reduced;
- and practical life managed sensibly.

He is sensitive to:

- food;
- schedules;
- ordinary strain;
- and the social cost of relentless competition.

His danger is that kindness can become avoidance.

Reina correctly observes that his reluctance to confront people may transfer emotional labor to Kumiko.

## Kumiko: the principle of relation

Kumiko tries to understand:

- what people need;
- what they are not saying;
- and how one decision will reverberate through the social field.

Her strength is synthesis.

Her danger is that she may manage people without fully admitting that she is doing so.

## Their competition-day speeches

Before the Kyoto performance, the three give sharply characteristic addresses.

Reina says:

> All the pain and severity were for this day. Hold nothing back.

Shuuichi says:

> Music should be enjoyable. Leave the stage with a good memory.

Kumiko says:

> Show the supporters what this collective work has produced.

No one speech is sufficient.

Together they define Kitauji’s current identity:

- excellence;
- enjoyment;
- responsibility.

# 5. The unanimous national-gold vote

All 103 members raise their hands for national gold.

The moment appears to complete Kitauji’s transformation.

It also disturbs Kumiko.

## The Volume 1 echo

In Kumiko’s first year, the goal vote exposed the distance between:

- what students publicly endorsed;
- and what they were willing to do.

The old band could say “nationals” without possessing the culture necessary to make the word meaningful.

## The Volume 9 difference

The new members knowingly joined:

- a strong band;
- with a demanding conductor;
- after national and regional success.

Their aspiration is more credible.

The unanimity is not simply fraudulent conformity.

## Kumiko’s new position

Kumiko nevertheless recognizes the atmosphere pressing everyone toward the same answer.

She once stood among students being asked to consent.

She now stands at the front hoping for unanimity.

This is one of the clearest demonstrations of how office changes moral perception.

The leader sees why consensus is useful:

- it legitimizes sacrifice;
- simplifies communication;
- and allows discipline to be framed as the fulfillment of collective choice.

Kumiko does not stop noticing the pressure.

She accepts that she benefits from it.

## Inherited ambition

The vote raises a question that will govern the entire final movement:

> Is national gold each member’s chosen desire—or the institution’s desire inherited before the individual can examine it?

The novel does not invalidate the goal.

It refuses to treat a raised hand as the end of the ethical inquiry.

# 6. Kitauji after success: the problem of scale

Thirty-one first-years join.

The band reaches 103 members.

Success has solved Kitauji’s old recruitment problem and created new difficulties.

## Instrument scarcity

There are now more students than the existing equipment and rehearsal structures were designed to support.

## Audition scarcity

Only fifty-five people can enter the A ensemble.

An increasingly talented membership makes exclusion more severe.

## Administrative scarcity

More members mean:

- more interpersonal conflicts;
- more beginners;
- more families;
- more scheduling complications;
- and more people who need to be heard.

Kumiko’s old method—personally noticing nearly everything—cannot scale indefinitely.

## Prestige as pressure

The newcomers do not believe they have joined an ordinary school club.

They have joined a nationally recognized band.

That prestige supplies:

- motivation;
- expectations;
- fear of inadequacy;
- and the belief that weak performance is a form of obstruction.

The institution has become attractive enough to recruit the people who will intensify its competitive culture.

# 7. The new first-years

The first-year cohort is not represented by one simple newcomer archetype.

It contains several incompatible relationships to Kitauji.

## Kaho Hariya — the vulnerable beginner

Kaho comes from the manga club and begins euphonium without prior experience.

She:

- cries privately during difficult training;
- struggles under Reina’s standards;
- remains in the band;
- and accepts B-ensemble placement without believing her entire effort was invalidated.

Kaho represents the person for whom Kitauji’s threshold is genuinely overwhelming.

Her continued presence is partly made possible by Sally’s emotional labor.

## Yayoi Kamiishi — the energetic beginner

Yayoi enters tuba through the chaotic allocation process.

Her yellow bandana and directness make her visually and socially legible, but Volume 9 leaves much of her musical development for later.

She helps establish how many first-years begin without a refined instrumental destiny.

## Suzume Kamaya — instinct, volume, and social strategy

Suzume is:

- Tsubame’s younger sister;
- a former basketball player;
- physically energetic;
- comically intrusive;
- socially shrewd;
- and a novice tuba player with an exceptional natural sound.

She is both one of the volume’s funniest characters and one of its most ethically complicated.

## Sally / Sari Yoshii — the empathetic experienced musician

Sally is an experienced clarinetist who recruits her friends into Kitauji.

She possesses enough skill to enter A ensemble.

Her central problem is not her own playing.

It is her inability to stop internalizing everyone else’s pain.

Together, these first-years prevent “the newcomer experience” from becoming one story.

Kitauji must respond differently to:

- technical weakness;
- emotional fragility;
- instinctive talent;
- social manipulation;
- and excessive empathy.

# 8. Sally versus Reina: two theories of equal treatment

Sally calls Reina:

> 「正しさの塊」  
> a mass of correctness.

The phrase is not a simple insult.

Sally knows Reina is largely right.

That is why Reina frightens her.

## Reina’s theory

Reina treats beginners as genuine members.

She does not patronize them by pretending that:

- rhythm does not matter;
- poor tone does not affect others;
- or being new suspends collective responsibility.

From Reina’s perspective, demanding less because someone is inexperienced can be another form of exclusion.

The beginner is part of the sound now.

## Sally’s theory

Sally asks why all 103 members need to be treated as immediate candidates for perfection when only fifty-five will enter A.

She argues that:

- some students need time to discover enjoyment;
- not every beginner must be made competition-ready immediately;
- and demanding that someone stop being a burden before she has experienced pleasure can destroy the desire required for growth.

Her sharpest formulation is:

> Moving from zero to positive can be enjoyable.  
> Being scolded into moving from negative to zero is not.

## Equality versus equity

Reina offers equal seriousness.

Sally asks for unequal support according to need.

Neither position can simply replace the other.

Without Reina’s standard:

- beginners may be excluded from real responsibility;
- improvement may stagnate;
- and national ambition becomes hollow.

Without Sally’s care:

- beginners may quit before growth becomes possible;
- emotional suffering becomes invisible;
- and “equal treatment” merely privileges the already prepared.

## Kumiko’s synthesis

Kumiko does not refute Sally’s argument.

She recognizes what Sally personally needs.

Sally has:

- recruited her friends;
- persuaded several students not to quit;
- absorbed their pain;
- and received little acknowledgment for that labor.

Kumiko tells her:

> All 103 members are still here because of you.

The statement reaches Sally immediately.

Kumiko internally recognizes that she has found the phrase that will work.

That awareness complicates the kindness.

She is comforting Sally.

She is also retaining a valuable member.

# 9. Recognition as leadership—and management

Kumiko’s conversation with Sally demonstrates how far she has moved toward Asuka.

## The old Kumiko

Earlier Kumiko might have:

- listened;
- sympathized;
- and remained uncertain what to do.

## The new Kumiko

She:

- diagnoses Sally’s need;
- selects the effective language;
- invokes her authority as president;
- and restores Sally’s commitment to the institution.

The result is benevolent.

The method is strategic.

## “It landed”

Kumiko’s internal certainty that the statement has struck the correct emotional point is important.

She is no longer innocent of persuasion.

She understands that:

- people possess emotional levers;
- authority changes how words are received;
- and recognition can redirect behavior.

## The ethical distinction from Asuka

Asuka often understood people as functions inside a system.

Kumiko still sees the person’s suffering.

But the line between:

- helping Sally;
- and managing Sally

cannot be cleanly drawn.

The presidency forces the two actions to overlap.

# 10. Suzume’s benevolent manipulation

The apparent first-year boycott was largely engineered by Suzume.

She understood that Sally:

- was overwhelmed;
- would reject reassurance from peers;
- possessed unusual sensitivity to authority;
- and needed recognition from a senior.

Suzume therefore created conditions under which Kumiko would intervene.

## Social intelligence without moral solemnity

Suzume reads people extremely well.

Unlike Kumiko or Kanade, she does not experience this ability as dark or burdensome.

She enjoys human irregularity.

She says she likes people whose:

- positives;
- negatives;
- and uneven edges

make them interesting.

## Manipulation in the service of care

Suzume does not lie for personal advancement.

She manipulates authority to get Sally the acknowledgment friends cannot supply.

This raises the same question as Kumiko’s intervention:

> Does good intention make strategic emotional influence ethically simple?

The novel does not condemn Suzume.

It does show that the band’s emotional life is increasingly governed through people who understand how others can be moved.

## Sally and Suzume

Sally feels everyone’s pain.

Suzume sees everyone’s shape.

Sally can become paralyzed by empathy.

Suzume can act because she does not require moral purity before intervention.

Their friendship is one of the first-year class’s most promising balances.

---

# 11. Suzume and Tsubame: protection versus agency

Suzume adores her older sister.

Her devotion is sincere and frequently comic.

It also becomes possessive.

## The color-guard misunderstanding

Tsubame chooses to participate in color guard for Sunrise Festival.

Suzume assumes:

- Tsubame has been pushed aside;
- someone is exploiting her timidity;
- or she has sacrificed what she truly wants.

In reality, Tsubame has chosen the role because this is her final year and she wants to attempt something she previously feared.

## Speaking for the loved person

Suzume’s protective instinct risks converting Tsubame into someone whose choices require external interpretation.

She assumes that love grants her superior knowledge of Tsubame’s true desire.

The structure resembles several earlier relationships:

- Nozomi assuming she understood Mizore;
- Asuka’s mother claiming ownership over Asuka’s future;
- Reina and others deciding what Kumiko should do;
- Kumiko attempting to determine what is best for Kanade.

The scale is lighter.

The ethical pattern is the same.

## Tsubame’s public arrival

Tsubame later earns the marimba solo.

Her achievement completes the path begun in Volume 8:

- Junna recognizes hidden ability.
- Kumiko helps her synchronize through breath.
- Tsubame becomes an exposed musical voice.

The supposedly fragile older sister possesses an artistic identity Suzume cannot protect into existence.

She must be allowed to choose it.

# 12. Mayu Kuroe: the outsider who expects to disappear

Mayu is the central destabilizing figure of Volume 9.

She arrives as a third-year transfer from Seira Girls’ High School, a national-gold powerhouse.

Her family’s work has caused repeated relocations across Japan.

She has become skilled at:

- entering new groups;
- speaking unobtrusive standard Japanese;
- appearing pleasant;
- reading local expectations;
- and leaving before roots become permanent.

Her adaptation is so effective that it becomes difficult to locate the person adapting.

## Musical legitimacy

Mayu is not merely competent.

She is an excellent euphonium player formed inside a stronger institutional tradition.

Her sound is:

- polished;
- broad;
- efficient;
- and immediately threatening to Kumiko’s position.

The threat is not manufactured through arrogance.

Mayu appears to possess almost no visible desire to defeat anyone.

That makes her more difficult for Kumiko to understand.

## Social illegitimacy

Mayu repeatedly worries that:

- a long-standing Kitauji member will be displaced;
- the established students should receive priority;
- and her own success may damage the group’s emotional coherence.

She offers to withdraw from auditions.

The band rejects the offer.

Kumiko is actively offended by the implication that Kitauji would prefer false competition.

## Mayu’s fear is not irrational

Before Kumiko’s audition, she overhears first-years discussing the euphonium soli.

They say:

- Mayu may play better;
- Taki might select her because he does not read the social atmosphere;
- but the band would prefer Kumiko;
- because Kumiko is president;
- has endured the band’s history;
- and is “one of theirs.”

The conversation validates Mayu’s concern.

She can win musically and lose socially.

This is precisely the danger Kanade feared in Volume 6.

# 13. Mayu and Kanade: two responses to socially dangerous talent

Kanade and Mayu are natural antagonists because they understand the same problem differently.

## Kanade’s old solution

Kanade once intentionally failed.

She believed defeating Natsuki would make her:

- resented;
- morally illegitimate;
- and unwanted.

After Volume 6, she has rejected that solution.

She now believes the player must perform honestly and allow the institution to judge.

## Mayu’s proposed solution

Mayu repeatedly offers to withdraw before the audition can produce conflict.

She frames the offer as consideration for the people already present.

Kanade recognizes the danger.

## Why Kanade’s politeness sharpens

Kanade insists upon calling her:

> Kuroe-senpai.

She does not rush toward the intimacy of “Mayu.”

Her honorific distance functions as surveillance.

Kanade tests:

- why Mayu chose Kitauji;
- what she wants;
- and whether her gentleness contains an unspoken strategy.

## The irony

Mayu may not be consciously manipulative.

Her self-erasure can still manipulate the emotional field.

By repeatedly saying she will withdraw, she compels the others to reassure her:

- that she belongs;
- that they want her best;
- and that they will not resent her.

Kanade is especially sensitive to this form of preemptive martyrdom.

She has already learned that one person’s false sacrifice deprives everyone else of agency.

## “Wonderful personality”

After Mayu says she is glad Kanade was selected because established Kitauji members should be rewarded, Kanade calls her personality “wonderful” with unmistakable irony.

The hostility is not jealousy alone.

Kanade resents a moral framework in which Mayu can appear generous by treating her own full participation as a possible harm.

---

# 14. Mayu’s repeated movement

Mayu explains her many previous homes almost cheerfully.

A second school trip becomes:

> lucky.

Another forced transition becomes:

> an opportunity.

This does not prove she is emotionally untouched.

It shows the survival narrative she has learned to use.

## Belonging as temporary

Mayu’s statement that quitting can create a win–win arrangement follows from this history.

If someone leaves:

- she escapes pain;
- the remaining people stop worrying;
- and the institution continues.

After all:

> it is only a club.

For Kitauji’s established members, the statement is nearly sacrilegious.

For Mayu, it is a philosophy of survival.

No single group should demand that a person suffer merely to prove that the relationship mattered.

## The strength and danger of her view

Mayu’s position protects autonomy.

It also makes sustained commitment difficult.

A person who always preserves the exit may never learn whether others would make room for her full presence.

Mayu has become excellent at joining groups without demanding that any group become home.

# 15. The photographer who refuses the photograph

Mayu takes hundreds of pictures during:

- the Agata Festival;
- the school trip;
- and ordinary communal life.

Her photographs preserve:

- laughter;
- awkwardness;
- friendships;
- and the band’s daily texture.

She is extremely good at recording other people’s belonging.

She is almost absent from the images.

When asked why, she says that seeing herself in photographs makes her feel something like revulsion or dread.

## The communal archivist

Mayu creates proof that other people were together.

She does not place herself inside that proof.

This is one of the volume’s most precise images of her psychology.

## A provisional interpretation

Volume 9 does not yet fully explain the aversion.

The available evidence supports several possibilities:

- discomfort with fixed self-image;
- expectation that she will later disappear from the group;
- preference for observer or caretaker roles;
- or alienation produced by repeated changes of location and social identity.

The important point is not to diagnose her prematurely.

It is to recognize the structure:

> Mayu is comfortable preserving everyone else’s place in memory while resisting evidence of her own.

## The “Mama” persona

Her old nickname “Mama” comes from a classmate accidentally calling her mother.

The nickname persists because Mayu naturally:

- carries supplies;
- sews;
- anticipates needs;
- mediates;
- and creates a nonthreatening environment.

Maternal usefulness gives her a role anywhere she goes.

It may also protect her from having to ask what she personally wants.

---

# 16. Mayu as an Asuka echo

Mayu’s silver euphonium immediately evokes Asuka.

Her:

- long dark hair;
- exceptional playing;
- social composure;
- and emotional opacity

strengthen the association.

Kumiko repeatedly experiences involuntary flashes of Asuka while watching Mayu.

The resemblance is important precisely because the two are not identical.

## Asuka

Asuka deliberately controlled information.

She understood the social machinery around her and used performance strategically.

Her neutrality concealed intense private desire.

## Mayu

Mayu’s self-effacement appears more habitual.

She has spent years learning that:

- environments change;
- relationships end;
- and the least disruptive self is easiest to transport.

She does not necessarily possess Asuka’s appetite for control.

She shares the defensive safety of not asking too much.

## Kumiko’s reaction

Kumiko once wanted desperately to enter Asuka’s private world.

She now resists Mayu’s attempts to enter hers.

This reversal is one of the volume’s most revealing character developments.

---

# 17. The secret song

Kumiko practices:

> 「響け！ユーフォニアム」

the piece Asuka inherited from Shindō and passed to her.

Mayu hears it and asks what it is.

Kumiko refuses to explain fully.

She says only that she learned it from a senior.

## Inheritance becomes possession

Earlier, the piece represented transmission:

- Shindō to Asuka;
- Asuka to Kumiko;
- possibly Kumiko to the future.

In this scene, Kumiko treats the song as protected territory.

She does not want Mayu inside it.

## Why the refusal matters

Mayu has done nothing wrong.

Her admiration is sincere.

Kumiko’s discomfort comes from what Mayu represents:

- a new excellent euphonium player;
- an Asuka-like image;
- and the possibility that Kumiko’s apparently singular inheritance is not proof of permanent uniqueness.

## Kumiko’s incomplete generosity

Kumiko can defend Mayu’s right to audition.

She cannot yet offer her every form of belonging.

This distinction matters.

Institutional inclusion is not emotional intimacy.

Kumiko is entitled to private boundaries.

She is not yet honest with herself about why this boundary feels so urgent.

---

# 18. “That Girl’s Euphonium”

The first chapter title most obviously refers to Mayu.

She arrives first as:

> that girl;

an unfamiliar euphonium sound in Kumiko’s established world.

But the wording also carries a wider resonance.

Volume 9 contains several euphonium identities:

- Kumiko, president and inherited center;
- Mayu, the transfer and possible replacement;
- Kanade, the perceptive junior who no longer wants false sacrifice;
- Kaho, the beginner for whom the instrument is still an uncertain future;
- and Asuka, absent but audible through memory and composition.

The euphonium is no longer simply Kumiko’s narrative instrument.

It has become a contested lineage.

The title asks:

> Which girl owns the euphonium story now?

The final movement will need an answer broader than one soloist.

---

# 19. Kumiko’s attraction to talent

Ririka tells Kumiko that she likes talented people.

The observation unsettles her because it resembles Asuka.

Hazuki responds that this tendency is natural and may help make Kumiko a good president.

## The flattering interpretation

A leader should:

- recognize capability;
- value strong contribution;
- and create opportunities for excellence.

## The dangerous interpretation

Talent attracts Kumiko aesthetically and emotionally.

She is drawn to:

- Reina;
- Asuka;
- Mizore;
- Sally;
- and now Mayu.

This may lead her to:

- notice talented suffering more quickly;
- interpret musical value as personal fascination;
- or unconsciously prioritize people whose ability enriches Kitauji.

## Kumiko’s discomfort

The gap between:

- how others describe her;
- and how she understands herself

becomes physically unpleasant.

She wants to believe she helps people because they are people.

She is forced to consider that institutional usefulness affects whom she sees.

This is not proof that her care is false.

It is evidence that care is rarely free from preference.

---

# 20. The free-choice piece: one year as musical biography

Kitauji selects:

> **「一年の詩〜吹奏楽のための」**  
> *Poem of a Year—for Concert Band*

The piece contains four seasonal movements:

1. **Spring — new breath**
2. **Summer — song of glory**
3. **Autumn — hour of destiny**
4. **Winter — ending, and beginning again**

The work is exceptionally appropriate for Kumiko’s final year.

## Spring

The new students enter.

Mayu transfers.

The institution begins again.

## Summer

Kitauji pursues competition glory.

The band’s public strength reaches its most visible form.

## Autumn

The euphonium–trumpet soli belongs to a movement of approaching change.

Kumiko and Reina must play intimacy inside music already shadowed by separation.

## Winter

The final movement gathers earlier themes and transforms ending into another beginning.

This is the series’ larger inheritance philosophy.

Graduation will not erase the sound.

## Personal time inside public music

The piece was inspired by the composer’s year with his father.

Private memory becomes concert repertoire.

This mirrors the series itself:

- personal grief;
- private love;
- family history;
- and hidden relationships

are translated into public sound.

---

# 21. Selecting repertoire: democracy and artistic authority

The executives consider several possible required and free pieces.

All three independently favor *Poem of a Year*.

The surrounding debate concerns who should decide.

## Shuuichi’s instinct

He considers broad member participation.

A collective choice might create:

- ownership;
- consent;
- and shared investment.

## Reina’s objection

Most members do not possess equal expertise in:

- orchestration;
- instrumentation;
- balance;
- or competitive strategy.

A large vote may flatten the decision into preference.

Responsibility also becomes diffuse.

## Kumiko’s synthesis

The executives advise Taki.

Taki makes the final decision.

This arrangement preserves:

- student input;
- expert authority;
- and identifiable responsibility.

It is a miniature version of the volume’s political philosophy.

Democracy is valuable.

Expertise remains real.

The difficulty lies in constructing a relation between them that produces trust.

---

# 22. Multiple auditions: opportunity made into precarity

At Mayu’s suggestion, Kitauji adopts repeated auditions before:

- Kyoto;
- Kansai;
- and nationals.

A-ensemble membership and featured parts may change each time.

## The argument for repeated auditions

The system:

- rewards later improvement;
- prevents early selection from becoming permanent entitlement;
- keeps the strongest current ensemble onstage;
- and gives B members continued reason to practice the competition pieces.

This corrects the injustice of one moment deciding an entire summer.

## The emotional cost

No place is secure.

A student can:

- succeed;
- perform;
- and still lose the next stage.

The system converts the entire season into continuous evaluation.

## “Comrades and rivals”

Reina tells the members that all 103 are:

> comrades and rivals.

The phrase captures the contradiction.

The group’s collective success depends upon members being willing to replace one another.

Belonging is permanent in principle.

Musical function is continuously provisional.

## The Mayu problem intensified

A single audition might allow Kumiko to defeat Mayu and restore stability.

Repeated auditions guarantee that the question will return.

Volume 9’s first result is therefore not resolution.

It is the first movement of a recurring threat.

---

# 23. The first audition: Kumiko’s apparent victory

Kumiko, Mayu, and Kanade all enter A ensemble.

Kumiko receives the euphonium part of the third-movement soli.

On the surface, this is an ideal result.

- The established president succeeds.
- The excellent transfer joins the ensemble.
- The talented junior retains her place.
- No one must be erased.

The emotional reality is less stable.

## The toilet conversation

Before her audition, Kumiko overhears first-years saying they hope she receives the soli because:

- she is the president;
- she has struggled with Kitauji;
- the band will remain more unified;
- and Mayu remains a transfer student.

They do not accuse Mayu of wrongdoing.

They still prefer one of their own.

## Kumiko’s performance

Kumiko plays while imagining Reina’s sound from their Agata Festival practice.

Her musical desire is authentic.

She wants that shared passage.

## Taki’s shortened audition

Taki does not require the additional random passage from Kumiko.

He says it is unnecessary.

The unexplained choice creates anxiety.

Was he already certain of her ability?

Did he need less evidence because he knows her?

Did he treat her differently?

The novel does not supply an answer.

## The contaminated result

Kumiko receives what she wants.

She cannot enjoy it without qualification.

Mayu congratulates her with no visible bitterness.

That generosity does not reassure her.

It leaves Kumiko alone with the question of legitimacy.

---

# 24. Mayu’s full participation

Mayu enters A ensemble and immediately practices the soli for the next audition.

Kanade asks why she would practice it now when Kumiko currently holds the part.

Mayu answers simply:

> There will be another audition.

This is not aggression.

It is the logical consequence of the rules.

## Mayu’s contradiction

She repeatedly offers to withdraw.

When told to participate, she practices fully.

Both behaviors are sincere.

She does not necessarily want to take Kumiko’s place.

She does want to perform correctly within the system in which she has been placed.

## Why Kanade becomes frustrated

Mayu asks others to tell her if anything is wrong and says she will correct everything.

The statement sounds accommodating.

It also makes her almost impossible to confront.

She presents no hostile desire against which another person can define herself.

Kanade wants Mayu to claim what she wants so that their competition can become honest.

---

# 25. Hazuki’s A-ensemble selection

Hazuki’s name is called first among the tubas.

The moment pays off years of work.

She entered as a complete beginner.

She spent:

- her first year in B;
- her second year in B;
- and much of her identity supporting friends who advanced ahead of her.

Now she enters A in her final year.

## Delayed merit

Hazuki’s selection demonstrates that:

- improvement may take years;
- B status is not permanent identity;
- and repeated participation can produce competitive ability.

## Emotional recognition

Her former B-ensemble peers surround and congratulate her.

Her success belongs partly to a cohort of students who remember being outside together.

## A humane meritocracy

Hazuki does not receive the place because she is a senior.

Her selection still satisfies a powerful emotional desire for long effort to be recognized.

This is the version of meritocratic success people most easily experience as just:

- ability;
- effort;
- seniority;
- and affection

all point in the same direction.

The tuba result immediately complicates that harmony.

---

# 26. Suzume over Satsuki

Taki selects:

- Hazuki;
- Mirei;
- and first-year beginner Suzume.

Satsuki is placed in B.

This is the volume’s most important audition decision.

## The apparent ranking

Satsuki has:

- more experience;
- wider technique;
- greater established competence;
- and several years of tuba playing.

Suzume is:

- a beginner;
- technically limited;
- and still developing her usable range.

By a simple individual ranking, Satsuki appears better.

## Taki’s actual reasoning

Suzume can produce:

- an unusually beautiful large sound;
- with enough volume to stabilize Kitauji’s low foundation.

The selected repertoire does not require her weak high register in exposed form.

Taki needs a particular ensemble configuration.

He is not ranking abstract tuba players.

He is constructing this band for these pieces.

## Merit as role fit

The decision refines the series’ theory of merit.

Merit is not:

> Who is the better person?

Nor even always:

> Who is the more complete instrumentalist?

It can mean:

> Whose present capacities best serve the required whole?

## The emotional danger

The reasoning is not publicly visible.

Without explanation, the decision can look like:

- favoritism;
- potential over proven work;
- novelty over loyalty;
- or arbitrary authority.

A rational system without transparent reasoning depends heavily upon trust.

---

# 27. Mirei’s dissent

Mirei privately asks Kumiko whether she accepts the audition result.

She argues that:

- Satsuki is technically better;
- Kitauji claims to be meritocratic;
- and choosing Suzume appears inconsistent with that principle.

Her criticism is careful.

She speaks privately because she does not want to harm:

- Satsuki;
- Suzume;
- or the institution.

## Productive dissent

Mirei is not trying to start a faction.

She wants the system to be coherent.

Her willingness to speak is evidence that she takes Kitauji’s stated values seriously.

## The generational argument

Mirei explains that Kumiko’s generation treats Taki as almost sacred because it witnessed him transform Kitauji.

The younger students did not.

They joined an already successful school.

For them:

- Taki is a highly capable conductor;
- not the near-mythic founder of a new world.

They cannot borrow the older generation’s gratitude as proof of infallibility.

This is one of the series’ strongest observations about institutional succession.

## Revolution becoming orthodoxy

Taki’s reforms once challenged:

- seniority;
- complacency;
- and opaque tradition.

Three years later, trust in Taki risks becoming another unquestioned tradition.

The institution must learn to preserve his standards without converting his person into doctrine.

---

# 28. Kumiko and the “seeds of rebellion”

Kumiko reassures Mirei that Taki possesses a deeper musical view and that the decision will probably make sense by competition time.

Mirei accepts her answer provisionally.

Kumiko then thinks that the seeds of rebellion must be plucked early.

## Why the line matters

The thought reveals that Kumiko now sees dissent through two frames:

1. **A human frame**  
   Mirei deserves to be heard.

2. **An institutional frame**  
   Unresolved doubt can spread and divide the band.

Both are true.

## Kumiko does not merely suppress her

She:

- lets Mirei speak;
- praises her independent judgment;
- invites future concerns;
- and goes to Taki for an explanation.

Her action is more democratic than the internal phrase suggests.

## The emerging manager

Kumiko understands that private recognition can neutralize public conflict.

This is the same mechanism used with Sally.

The listener has become a political actor.

Volume 9 does not condemn this development.

It asks whether Kumiko can remain ethically alert to it.

---

# 29. Taki’s explanation

Taki responds to Kumiko’s question through an analogy with the ensemble contest.

Kumiko voted for the clarinet quartet not because each clarinetist was individually superior to every other performer, but because the group best understood:

- its roles;
- balance;
- and complete musical form.

Taki applies the same principle to full ensemble selection.

## The conductor as arranger of people

Taki hears:

- not only individual correctness;
- but the future collective sound.

His audition is therefore partly predictive and compositional.

## “I cannot take you there”

Kumiko asks whether Taki will take them to nationals.

He corrects her:

> They will reach nationals through their own power.  
> He cannot make music without players.

The correction resists the founder mythology Mirei identified.

Taki does not understand himself as the heroic cause of Kitauji’s success.

The students increasingly do.

## Kumiko’s unresolved doubt

Taki’s explanation of the tuba decision is coherent.

Kumiko still wonders whether she is simply doing what he tells her.

More painfully, she cannot ask:

> Why did you select me for the soli?

The question would reveal that she does not fully trust her own victory.

---

# 30. Trust after charisma

Volume 9 distinguishes three stages of institutional authority.

## Founding charisma

Kumiko’s generation follows Taki because it experienced direct transformation.

His legitimacy was proven through visible results.

## Inherited prestige

The younger students enter after those results.

They know the history as reputation.

They do not possess the same emotional debt.

## Mature institution

For Kitauji to survive Taki eventually, it must develop:

- transparent principles;
- distributed judgment;
- and members capable of asking questions without being treated as traitors.

Mirei’s dissent is therefore not merely a problem.

It may be evidence of institutional maturation.

Kumiko’s challenge is to distinguish:

- corrosive factionalism;
- from necessary scrutiny.

---

# 31. Kumiko as participant and president

Kumiko’s position is almost impossible.

As president, she must say:

> Mayu belongs.  
> Taki’s judgment should be trusted.  
> The best player should receive the part.

As a performer, she wants:

> the part for herself.

As Reina’s special partner, she wants:

> their shared soli.

As an established Kitauji member, she feels:

> Mayu’s excellence is an intrusion into a history Mayu did not live.

As a fair-minded person, she knows:

> that history should not become musical entitlement.

The volume does not ask Kumiko to eliminate these feelings.

It asks whether she can act ethically while possessing them.

## Public generosity and private resistance

Kumiko helps Kanade correct a rhythm even though Kanade is technically a competitor.

She tells Mayu to audition honestly.

She welcomes Mayu as a Kitauji member.

She privately:

- lies to avoid an Agata Festival outing with Mayu;
- withholds Asuka’s song;
- listens anxiously to Mayu’s sound;
- and measures herself against her.

The contradiction is not hypocrisy in a simple sense.

It is the cost of trying to uphold a value one’s feelings do not make easy.

---

# 32. The Agata Festival: public festival, private election

Most of the band attends the festival together.

Kumiko and Reina deliberately do something different.

They meet at Reina’s home to practice the third-movement soli.

## The Volume 1 echo

Kumiko explains their choice:

> 「みんなと違うことがしたかったから」  
> Because I wanted to do something different from everyone else.

Reina whispers:

> 「アタシも」  
> Me too.

The exchange deliberately recalls their first-year intimacy.

The earlier bond has not faded into ordinary friendship.

They continue choosing difference together.

## Entering Reina’s home

Kumiko is:

- the first friend Reina has invited home;
- known to Reina’s mother through constant stories;
- and welcomed into a private family environment.

Reina’s mother says Reina talks about Kumiko constantly.

This domesticizes a relationship previously associated with:

- mountains;
- festivals;
- night;
- and exceptional moments.

Kumiko now enters the home from which Reina’s musical identity emerged.

## The instrument room

Reina’s father owns multiple brass instruments.

Kumiko borrows a euphonium.

Their duet is made possible by Reina’s family world.

Private relationship, family, and music temporarily become one space.

---

# 33. “Are we special?”

After practicing, Kumiko asks:

> 「いまの私たちって、特別かな」  
> Are we special now?

Reina answers:

> 「そりゃ、特別でしょ。こうやって一緒にいるんだから」  
> Of course we are. We are here together.

This is an important evolution of the series’ specialness language.

## Reina’s earlier specialness

Reina once wanted to become special through exceptional musical achievement.

Specialness was:

- distinction;
- superiority;
- and refusal of ordinary life.

## Asuka’s imposed specialness

The band made Asuka special by concentrating dependence upon her.

Specialness became isolation.

## Kumiko and Reina’s current specialness

Specialness is enacted through mutual choice.

They are special because:

- each could be elsewhere;
- each chose this encounter;
- and they share something not generalized to the group.

It is relational rather than hierarchical.

---

# 34. Future separation

Reina admits that Kumiko attending music college would provide a clear reason for them to remain connected.

Kumiko says she will not choose music college.

Reina then admits fear.

She cannot guarantee that:

- future Reina;
- and future Kumiko

will continue wanting the same relationship.

## Mutuality cannot be controlled

Reina is comfortable controlling:

- her practice;
- preparation;
- and musical trajectory.

Friendship requires another person’s continued consent.

She cannot achieve it alone.

That helplessness frightens her.

## Kumiko’s response

Kumiko says her happiness that Reina cares exceeds her anxiety.

She experiences Reina’s vulnerability as proof of being chosen.

This does not solve the future.

It changes the fear from private speculation into shared knowledge.

## Parallel with Mizore and Nozomi

Mizore’s dependency became dangerous because she could not articulate separation until it dominated her entire future.

Kumiko and Reina speak earlier.

Their relationship remains intense and possessive.

It has more room for negotiation.

---

# 35. Yuri-coded intimacy and relational plurality

The Agata sequence is among the source novels’ strongest Kumiko–Reina passages.

It contains:

- deliberate withdrawal from the group;
- whispered mutuality;
- domestic invitation;
- maternal recognition;
- musical duet;
- jealousy over Mayu;
- explicit discussion of specialness;
- and fear of losing one another after graduation.

The relationship remains strongly yuri-readable.

Kumiko’s romantic attachment to Shuuichi is also still present.

The volume again refuses the false choice that only one bond can be emotionally real.

## Reina’s jealousy

Reina denies being jealous that Kumiko practiced with Mayu.

She then insists that Kumiko practice the same passage with her.

Her behavior conveys more than the denial.

## Kumiko’s bodily perception

Kumiko watches:

- Reina’s hair;
- neck;
- eyes;
- expression;
- and physical proximity

with the familiar heightened attention that characterizes their scenes.

## No final category

The text does not formally define the relationship as romantic.

It gives it:

- exclusivity;
- mutual selection;
- domestic significance;
- artistic reciprocity;
- and separation anxiety

that exceeds generic casual friendship.

---

# 36. Shuuichi and Kumiko: romance held inside institutional distance

Kumiko and Shuuichi ceased being lovers at the end of Volume 7.

They now serve as president and vice-president.

Their emotional relationship has not disappeared.

## The stubborn title

Shuuichi repeatedly calls her:

> President.

Reina interprets this as deliberate distance or wounded pride.

The title converts former intimacy into office.

## Kumiko’s physical response

When Shuuichi touches her shoulder before a speech, Kumiko becomes flustered and resents herself for reacting.

Desire remains inside the professional structure she created to contain it.

## Shuuichi’s kindness

He is:

- attentive;
- practical;
- protective;
- and reluctant to cause pain.

Reina argues that his kindness can become another burden on Kumiko when it prevents him from confronting people or assuming harder leadership work.

## The epilogue’s hidden pain

Shuuichi thinks that the person who wants to cry may be him.

The breakup has not become emotionally easy merely because he accepted Kumiko’s decision.

His perspective prevents the relationship from appearing like an administrative inconvenience Kumiko resolved cleanly.

---

# 37. The epilogue: Reina and Shuuichi on the bridge

The epilogue shifts away from Kumiko’s viewpoint.

Shuuichi encounters Reina late at night on Uji Bridge.

Both are returning from forms of preparation:

- Shuuichi from academic study;
- Reina from musical study.

They occupy the two futures Kumiko has not reconciled.

## Reina’s challenge

Reina tells Shuuichi:

- he accepted the vice-presidency;
- he should stop retreating at crucial moments;
- he must act rather than merely call himself kind;
- and he should not make Kumiko cry.

## Shuuichi’s counterstatement

He tells Reina not to burden Kumiko either.

The line begins as retaliation.

It carries truth.

Reina’s demands can also place weight upon Kumiko.

## Two claimants, no possession

Reina and Shuuichi both care deeply about Kumiko.

Neither fully understands the form of the other’s relationship with her.

They do not become conventional romantic rivals.

They function as two people who recognize that Kumiko’s emotional life is partly entrusted to both of them.

## The forked road

The final image is:

> They begin walking along separate roads.

The symbolism is direct but not final.

Their paths divide.

All three remain connected through:

- leadership;
- music;
- history;
- and the promise of a future none can yet predict.

---

# 38. Future anxiety as the nonmusical audition

Kumiko’s academic uncertainty mirrors the musical audition structure.

An audition asks:

> What role are you currently capable of occupying?

A career choice asks:

> What kind of future self are you willing to attempt?

Kumiko is more comfortable with the first question.

## Borrowed goals

She admits that she likes pursuing goals someone else has established.

She can work extremely hard once the destination is visible.

She struggles to author the destination.

## Music college

Reina and Azusa treat music college as imaginable.

Kumiko rejects it.

She loves euphonium and ensemble.

She does not possess:

- the vocational determination;
- financial certainty;
- or exceptional confidence

required to make professional music her life.

The novel does not frame this as artistic betrayal.

Music can be life-defining without becoming employment.

## Miechie’s advice

Miechie says:

- adult lives rarely follow perfect plans;
- work may become meaningful after it begins;
- avoidance of what one hates can be a valid planning principle;
- and uncertainty must not become an excuse for inaction.

This is a practical alternative to Reina’s vocation-centered model.

## The next step rather than the final identity

Kumiko does not need to discover one perfect calling.

She needs to choose a direction that preserves movement.

The advice echoes the volume’s treatment of auditions:

> A current choice is consequential without becoming the final definition of the person.

---

# 39. Kumiko’s father and family repair

Kumiko’s father asks about her future.

The conversation is awkward and unusually gentle.

## After Mamiko

The family conflict surrounding Mamiko has changed him.

He now says that:

- he may oppose an unstable choice;
- but he will not force a decision;
- and Kumiko can consult Mamiko because they are family.

The word **family** surprises Kumiko.

## Authority without possession

The father has not become permissive.

He distinguishes:

- disagreement;
- from coercion.

This is the mature authority the novel repeatedly seeks elsewhere.

A parent, teacher, or leader can:

- warn;
- advise;
- and retain standards

without claiming ownership of another person’s future.

## The goldfish

The conversation occurs beside goldfish transferred from another household that could no longer keep them.

The novel does not explicitly assign symbolic meaning to them.

They nevertheless fit the volume’s repeated concern with:

- relocation;
- receiving what another environment cannot hold;
- and making a new place for displaced life.

That resonance is especially suggestive beside Mayu’s arrival.

---

# 40. Motomu’s secret and the contamination of trust

A Ryusei student urges Motomu to reconnect with Genichirō Tsukinaga.

Motomu rejects the appeal.

He confirms that the famous conductor is his grandfather but refuses further discussion.

## Respecting the boundary

Kumiko protects Motomu from further pressure.

Midori has previously accepted that:

- Motomu does not define the man as family;
- and the matter should not be forced open.

This is care through restraint.

## The suspicion

Kumiko later sees Taki speaking with Genichirō.

A brief thought appears:

> Could Motomu’s A-ensemble selection have involved favoritism?

She immediately feels ashamed.

## Information changes perception

No evidence of favoritism exists.

The secret connection is enough to alter how Kumiko interprets an earlier judgment.

This parallels the tuba audition and her own soli.

Trust is vulnerable because:

- selection is opaque;
- relationships exist;
- and observers cannot directly hear every reason inside the judge’s mind.

## Privacy versus institutional transparency

Motomu has a right to privacy.

The institution has an interest in decisions appearing impartial.

Volume 9 does not resolve the conflict by demanding total disclosure.

It shows why legitimate privacy can still produce political suspicion.

---

# 41. Midori and Motomu at the competition

Motomu becomes physically ill with anxiety before Kyoto.

He fears that a failure to advance will end his competitive performances beside Midori.

Midori distracts him by asking about ordinary future plans.

## Returning catastrophe to time

Motomu experiences the performance as possible finality.

Midori reminds him that:

- the day will end;
- tomorrow will arrive;
- holidays will come;
- and ordinary life continues beyond the stage.

This is a quiet philosophical counterpoint to competition.

The performance matters intensely.

It is not the totality of existence.

## Midori’s inadvertent intimacy

She calls him something like a younger brother.

Motomu’s feelings appear more complicated.

The scene preserves their asymmetry without turning it into immediate crisis.

Midori cares deeply.

She does not necessarily recognize the form in which Motomu cares for her.

---

# 42. The Kyoto performance

Kitauji draws the first performance slot.

The students initially treat it as a disadvantage.

Reina reframes it.

## First ears

The judges and audience will hear Kitauji before becoming fatigued or establishing other comparisons.

Kitauji can define the day’s initial standard.

## Freedom from rivals

The students do not have to hear:

- Ryusei;
- Rikka;
- or another powerhouse

before taking the stage.

The usual comparative anxiety is reduced.

## The first step

The performance begins the final competitive season.

The novel stops detailed narration at the downbeat:

> Kumiko’s final high-school competition begins.

The result is later delivered through the executive notebook:

- gold;
- representative status;
- advancement with Ryusei and Rikka.

## Why the performance is not the volume’s emotional climax

The central struggle of Part One is not whether Kitauji can survive Kyoto.

It is whether the institution can build a credible ensemble and carry its unresolved tensions into the next audition.

Kyoto is the first successful checkpoint.

The harder judgment remains ahead.

---

# 43. The executive notebook after Kyoto

Kumiko’s entry becomes unusually exuberant.

She writes:

- that the day felt endless;
- that she was terrified despite expecting advancement;
- and that she wants national gold with everyone.

Shuuichi allows the group to enjoy the result.

Reina immediately warns against relaxing.

The three voices again form the leadership chord:

- emotional acknowledgment;
- ordinary celebration;
- relentless forward pressure.

The notebook converts private feeling into an institutional record.

Someone in a later generation could read the page and encounter not only the result, but the leaders’ differing emotional tempos.

---

# 44. The chapter titles as a psychological score

## 「あの子のユーフォニアム」 — That Girl’s Euphonium

Mayu first appears as an external sound.

She is:

- “that girl”;
- the outsider;
- the silver instrument;
- the possible replacement.

The title also signals that Kumiko no longer possesses exclusive narrative ownership over the euphonium.

## 「秘密とフェスティバル」 — Secrets and Festivals

The public events are:

- Sunrise Festival;
- Agata Festival;
- and Kitauji’s visible unified performance culture.

The secrets include:

- Sally’s distress;
- Suzume’s manipulation;
- Motomu’s family;
- Mayu’s philosophy of exit;
- Kumiko’s jealousy;
- Reina’s fear of separation;
- and the private Agata evening chosen over the public festival.

Spectacle and concealment coexist.

## 「戸惑いオーディション」 — The Hesitant Audition

The hesitation belongs to nearly everyone:

- Mayu hesitates to participate.
- Kumiko hesitates to trust her own selection.
- Mirei hesitates to challenge Taki.
- Satsuki’s friends hesitate over the tuba result.
- Taki’s criteria prove more complex than expected.
- Kumiko hesitates to let Mayu enter private musical territory.
- the institution hesitates between expert judgment and social acceptance.

The audition produces names.

It does not produce certainty.

# 45. Voice and speaking style

Volume 9’s institutional complexity is carried through sharply differentiated voices.

## Kumiko

Kumiko’s public speech remains:

- slightly rambling;
- warm;
- self-conscious;
- and less rhetorically polished than Reina’s.

Her internal language is increasingly:

- strategic;
- suspicious;
- and managerial.

She can now recognize the emotional sentence that will change someone’s behavior.

## Mayu

Mayu speaks controlled standard Japanese with:

- soft questions;
- gentle self-minimization;
- and frequent concern about imposing.

Her register is geographically unrooted, fitting someone accustomed to relocation.

She rarely gives others a hard statement against which to push.

## Kanade

Kanade’s formal politeness functions as a blade.

She uses:

- honorific distance;
- irony;
- exact questions;
- and apparently sweet phrasing

to expose contradictions.

Her refusal to call Mayu by her given name is socially meaningful.

## Sally

Sally begins with careful politeness.

Under pressure, her speech becomes:

- repetitive;
- explanatory;
- and morally urgent.

She needs the listener to understand the complete causal chain because she fears being judged as irrational or disloyal.

## Suzume

Suzume’s lively Kansai register contains:

- jokes;
- exaggeration;
- direct affection;
- and tactical social intelligence.

Her comic surface allows her to manipulate situations without sounding ominous.

## Mirei

Mirei speaks precisely and formally.

She separates:

- personal affection;
- musical judgment;
- and institutional principle.

Her dissent sounds dangerous because it is so carefully reasoned.

## Reina

Reina’s speech remains:

- declarative;
- compact;
- confident;
- and low in hedging.

Her public rhetoric is powerful because she speaks as though the future is already answerable through action.

Her private speech with Kumiko becomes far more vulnerable.

## Shuuichi

Shuuichi’s casual Kansai speech is gentle and deflective.

He prefers:

- jokes;
- ordinary practical advice;
- and indirect care.

When Reina attacks his avoidance, his limited replies reveal how little language he possesses for his own hurt.

## Miechie

Miechie speaks in blunt practical propositions.

Her guidance lacks romantic vocation language.

She legitimizes:

- stability;
- experimentation;
- and reducing misery

as serious adult reasoning.

## Taki

Taki speaks procedurally and often answers questions with questions.

He avoids declaring personal infallibility.

The institution’s reverence for him exceeds his own rhetoric.

# 46. The visual architecture

## Original cover

The cover creates a euphonium triangle.

### Kumiko

Kumiko occupies the dominant right foreground.

She holds a gold euphonium and looks away from the viewer with a serious, slightly unsettled expression.

She is visually established as the protagonist and current institutional center.

### Mayu

Mayu stands at left holding her silver euphonium.

She smiles gently toward the viewer.

The silver instrument distinguishes her immediately from Kumiko while evoking Asuka’s visual lineage.

Her apparent openness contrasts with Kumiko’s guarded forward gaze.

### Kanade

Kanade appears between and slightly behind them.

Her eyes angle toward the others.

She is the observer inside the triangle:

- junior;
- competitor;
- and the person most suspicious of Mayu’s self-effacement.

### Reina and Shuuichi

The two appear much smaller near the lower center.

They face one another with instruments in hand.

Their placement anticipates the epilogue:

- two leadership partners;
- two people connected through Kumiko;
- standing within the same institution but oriented toward different paths.

## Gold and silver

The instrument colors are not simply decorative.

Gold belongs to:

- Kumiko;
- Kitauji continuity;
- and the sound readers have followed.

Silver belongs to:

- Mayu;
- transfer;
- Seira;
- and the recurring Asuka echo.

Neither color is morally privileged.

The contrast makes musical rivalry immediately visible.

## Promotional wrapper

The wrapper declares:

> Kumiko’s third spring.  
> The beginning of her final year.

Its lower section advertises the animated second-year material, meaning the novel’s forward movement is commercially attached to an adaptation still processing the previous generation.

The franchise is simultaneously:

- remembering Kitauji’s second movement;
- and beginning its last.

## Internal promotional illustration

The illustration places Mayu behind and around Kumiko, almost like a second euphonium presence entering her silhouette.

Its text emphasizes:

- the final high-school competition;
- repeated auditions;
- destabilized emotions;
- and the students proceeding toward separate paths.

Mayu is visually framed not as a conventional villain but as a presence that makes Kumiko’s previously stable identity newly uncertain.

# 47. Adaptation baseline

The following elements are essential for any later anime comparison.

## 1. Taki’s prologue

The cherry tree, institutional memory, and final declaration that this is Kitauji’s story establish the volume’s scale.

## 2. The executive notebook

Its entries and marginal comments reveal the leadership structure more efficiently than ordinary dialogue alone.

## 3. Kumiko’s discomfort during the unanimous vote

The scene should preserve that she both recognizes and benefits from the pressure toward consensus.

## 4. Mayu’s transfer history

Her repeated relocations are indispensable to understanding:

- adaptability;
- emotional distance;
- and willingness to leave.

## 5. Mayu’s silver euphonium and Seira background

She must sound legitimately capable of defeating Kumiko.

## 6. Mayu’s repeated offers to withdraw

These are not generic kindness. They are the central expression of her outsider psychology.

## 7. “It is only a club”

Mayu’s exit philosophy must remain intelligible rather than being framed simply as coldness.

## 8. Sally’s complete argument

The distinction between:

- zero to positive;
- and negative to zero

is one of the volume’s central ethical formulations.

## 9. Suzume’s orchestration of Kumiko’s intervention

The revelation changes the scene from spontaneous rescue into strategically produced recognition.

## 10. Tsubame choosing color guard

Suzume’s protection must be shown as capable of denying Tsubame’s agency.

## 11. The repertoire-selection debate

Shuuichi, Reina, Kumiko, and Taki articulate different models of artistic authority.

## 12. The seasonal structure of *Poem of a Year*

The euphonium–trumpet soli belongs to a larger composition about time, family, ending, and renewal.

## 13. The repeated-audition policy

This is the structural engine of the final movement.

## 14. Motomu’s Ryusei encounter

Kumiko’s later suspicion of favoritism depends upon the family secret.

## 15. The Agata Festival alternative

Kumiko and Reina deliberately choose privacy over the public festival.

## 16. Reina’s home

Her mother’s familiarity with Kumiko and the fact that Kumiko is the first friend invited home are major relational evidence.

## 17. The future-separation conversation

Reina’s fear is not merely that Kumiko will choose another person. It is that mutual desire cannot be guaranteed through time.

## 18. Mayu’s photographs

Her pleasure in photographing everyone and aversion to appearing in photographs are essential characterization.

## 19. Kumiko refusing to explain Asuka’s piece

The scene reveals a private possessiveness beneath her public fairness.

## 20. The toilet conversation

Kumiko must hear that the band socially prefers her before auditioning.

Without it, her later doubt loses its institutional foundation.

## 21. Hazuki’s selection

Her first A-ensemble placement is a major multi-volume payoff.

## 22. Suzume over Satsuki

The result must remain surprising and difficult.

## 23. Mirei’s challenge

Her generational distinction between Taki’s founding cohort and later students is one of the novel’s strongest institutional insights.

## 24. Taki’s ensemble-balance explanation

The selection is contextual, not arbitrary.

## 25. Kumiko’s unasked question

She cannot ask Taki why he chose her for the soli.

## 26. Mayu practicing the soli afterward

The repeated-audition structure prevents Kumiko’s victory from becoming closure.

## 27. The first performance slot

Reina’s reframing of disadvantage into opportunity illustrates her leadership.

## 28. The three pre-performance speeches

Each executive supplies a different necessary value.

## 29. Kyoto gold and advancement

The result should feel like the first checkpoint, not a climax.

## 30. The Shuuichi–Reina epilogue

The late-night bridge conversation, “do not make Kumiko cry,” Shuuichi’s hidden pain, and the final forked road are essential setup for Part Two.

# 50. Provisional questions for Part Two

## Mayu’s actual desire

Does Mayu want the euphonium soli?

Can she state that desire without:

- apologizing;
- offering withdrawal;
- or converting ambition into consideration for others?

## Kumiko after losing

The first audition lets Kumiko preserve every role.

What happens if a later audition selects Mayu?

Can Kumiko:

- congratulate her;
- defend the result publicly;
- and continue leading without using institutional harmony as an argument for reversal?

## The legitimacy of Taki’s authority

Mirei has spoken the generational doubt aloud.

Will another opaque selection produce broader dissent?

Can Taki’s reasons be made understandable without turning artistic judgment into a public trial?

## Repeated auditions

Will the system stimulate growth—or create continuous insecurity that damages ensemble trust?

What happens when a player begins hearing every rehearsal as evidence in a future case against her?

## Kanade and Mayu

Both understand the social danger of merit.

Kanade has chosen honest competition.

Mayu still protects herself through possible disappearance.

Can either recognize the other without escalation?

## Sally and Reina

Can the band create a pedagogical culture in which:

- standards remain high;
- beginners remain full members;
- and emotional support is not left to one overempathetic student?

## Satsuki and Suzume

Can Satsuki accept ensemble-specific selection without believing her years of work have been devalued?

Can Suzume inhabit A ensemble without turning success into another instrument of social performance?

## Kumiko’s future

Will Kumiko choose a university, field, or adult identity through her own desire?

Or will she once again accept the path that appears least disruptive?

## Kumiko and Reina

Can their specialness survive:

- different educational paths;
- separate musical futures;
- and the disappearance of Kitauji as their shared daily excuse?

## Kumiko and Shuuichi

Was the breakup a mature pause or preemptive austerity?

Can the two work through leadership without allowing hurt to hide behind official titles?

## Motomu and Ryusei

What is the complete history of Motomu, his grandfather, and his deceased sister?

Will the rising rival school force private family history into Kitauji’s competitive narrative?

# Final assessment

Volume 9 is a quieter novel than the Asuka crisis or *Liz and the Blue Bird*, but it may be the series’ most institutionally mature volume so far.

Its central insight is that a successful institution does not escape moral ambiguity.

Success creates new forms of it.

The weak Kitauji had obvious problems:

- apathy;
- seniority;
- low standards;
- and conflict between serious and unserious members.

The strong Kitauji possesses harder problems:

- prestige;
- oversubscription;
- continuous competition;
- expert opacity;
- inherited orthodoxy;
- social preference for insiders;
- and a goal so universally endorsed that disagreement becomes difficult to articulate.

Mayu arrives as the perfect test.

She is not:

- cruel;
- arrogant;
- lazy;
- or visibly hungry for Kumiko’s place.

She is good.

She is also better than Kumiko can comfortably ignore.

Her presence forces Kumiko to distinguish principles she previously experienced as naturally aligned:

> Welcome talented newcomers.  
> Reward the best player.  
> Protect institutional cohesion.  
> Preserve long-standing relationships.  
> Pursue national gold.  
> Be fair.  
> Want the soli for yourself.

These cannot all be satisfied without tension.

Mayu understands that before Kumiko does.

Her repeated offer to withdraw is wrong because it denies:

- Taki’s judgment;
- the other players’ agency;
- and her own honest participation.

It is also socially intelligent.

The first-years’ bathroom conversation confirms that Kitauji prefers Kumiko for reasons beyond sound.

The band knows Kumiko.

It has lived beside her.

It wants its president to be rewarded.

Mayu can become musically indispensable without immediately becoming emotionally native.

Volume 9 refuses to pretend that institutional inclusion instantly creates belonging.

Kumiko responds admirably in public.

She insists Mayu is a Kitauji member.

She rejects thrown auditions.

She wants the strongest ensemble.

Privately, she guards:

- Reina;
- Asuka’s piece;
- the euphonium lineage;
- and the sense that her three years should mean something no transfer can reproduce.

This does not make her fraudulent.

It makes her a person attempting to act by values more generous than her immediate feelings.

The audition’s first result appears to reward her.

Kumiko receives the soli.

Instead of concluding the conflict, the result makes fairness harder to trust.

Taki did not require the same full audition structure from her.

The band wanted her to win.

She cannot ask whether those facts mattered.

Her victory contains the very doubt Mayu anticipated.

The tuba selection makes the institutional argument explicit.

Satsuki is the more complete technician.

Suzume supplies the sound Taki needs.

The “best player” is not a person abstracted from repertoire and ensemble.

She is a role inside a designed whole.

Taki’s choice is rational.

Mirei’s objection is also rational because the reasoning remained invisible.

The novel’s key political insight follows:

> **People do not trust a system merely because its private logic is correct.  
> They trust it when they understand enough of its values to accept decisions that hurt them.**

Kumiko is now responsible for that trust.

Her instinct is humane.

Her methods are increasingly strategic.

She recognizes Sally’s hidden labor and gives her the needed words.

She listens to Mirei and seeks an explanation.

She also understands that both interventions keep potentially disruptive members inside the institutional structure.

The phrase about plucking the seeds of rebellion is therefore not a sudden corruption.

It is Kumiko realizing what leadership has made her.

She can no longer help a person without also affecting the band.

She can no longer express a preference without giving it institutional weight.

She can no longer receive a soli without wondering what her office contributed to the result.

She can no longer ask for personal intimacy without considering how the relationship will appear to others.

The Agata Festival sequence offers temporary relief from this role.

Kumiko and Reina choose to be different together.

They play privately.

Kumiko enters Reina’s home and family world.

They define specialness not as superiority, but as the fact of mutual choice.

Yet even there, the future intrudes.

Reina wants music college to preserve an excuse for connection.

Kumiko will not choose music as a profession.

Neither can promise what her future self will desire.

Their relationship is special precisely because it cannot be guaranteed by office, school, or rule.

Shuuichi’s epilogue supplies the complementary pain.

Kumiko tried to clear emotional space for leadership by ending their romance.

Leadership places Shuuichi beside her anyway.

He calls her “President.”

He performs kindness.

He hides that he may be the one who wants to cry.

Reina orders him not to make Kumiko cry, and he warns her in return.

They then take separate roads.

The image condenses the final movement’s central problem:

> People can walk together for years and still arrive at a junction where no shared institution can choose the path for them.

Volume 9’s place in the larger series can therefore be summarized as:

> **Volume 1 taught Kumiko to desire.  
> Volume 3 taught her to speak desire without proving it rational.  
> Volumes 6–7 taught her to recognize the desires and wounds of juniors.  
> Volume 9 asks whether she can govern a community in which her own desire is only one legitimate claim among many.**

The Kyoto result is successful.

Kitauji receives gold and advances.

The true audition has barely begun.

Mayu continues practicing the soli.

Mirei continues watching the system.

Reina continues trusting Taki.

Shuuichi continues hiding behind office.

Kumiko continues hearing Asuka’s song as something she cannot yet share.

The band has chosen national gold.

Its president has not yet chosen herself.

That is the unresolved determination of the first half of the final movement.
