# 『NANA』Volume 20 Deep Reading
## Other people's priorities, impossible sacrifice, and the terror of being left behind

> **Spoiler boundary:** this document uses the manga only through Volume 20. Volume 21 has not been consulted for any interpretive claim in this artifact.

## 1. Central thesis and volume role

Volume 20 is the point at which Nana Osaki becomes unusually explicit about a contradiction the series has been building for years: she understands, intellectually, that the people she loves possess lives whose centers are not identical with her, but she cannot emotionally tolerate what that plurality means.

The decisive formulation comes late in the volume:

> 「レンには あたしより大切なものがある」  
> 「ハチにも あたしより大切な人がいる」  
> 「自分だって 譲れない夢があるのに」  
> 「どうして あたしはそれが許せないんだよ」

Ren has something more important than Nana. Hachi has someone more important than Nana. Nana herself has a dream she will not surrender. She can see the symmetry. She can state it. What she cannot yet do is live inside it.

That is why Volume 20 is less a volume of revelation than a volume of **self-recognition without release**.

Nana finally names the extremity of what she wants from Ren:

> 「全てを犠牲にして あたしを愛して欲しい」  
> 「あたしが死んだら 一緒に死んで欲しい」

She immediately recognizes the danger in that desire. Her longing, when it rises, feels capable of taking everything from him. The manga therefore does not merely romanticize possessiveness. It lets Nana understand that her ideal of love contains annihilating potential.

The most powerful structural move is that the reader has just been given a second, private confession that Nana herself does not hear. Nana's estranged mother, Misuzu Uehara, tells the reporter that she did **not** abandon Nana for Nana's sake. She says:

> 「あたしがナナの為に… 自分を犠牲に出来ひんかっただけや」

She could not sacrifice herself for Nana. She chose life with a man who hated children instead.

The lexical echo is devastating:

- Nana's mother says she **could not sacrifice herself** for Nana.
- Nana later says she wants Ren to **sacrifice everything** for her.

The text does not make this a therapeutic revelation because Nana does not know it. Instead, it creates dramatic irony. The reader can see one possible historical root of Nana's totalizing demand while Nana remains trapped inside the wound without access to the explanation.

This same problem is refracted through nearly every major relationship in the volume.

- Miu says she refuses to become Yasu's girlfriend because she does not want to demand that another person be hers alone.
- Nana says she wants Ren to sacrifice everything.
- Ren says he no longer wants to force his feelings on Nana.
- Hachi imagines that being desperately wanted could make almost any injury forgivable.
- Reira tells Takumi that his claim to have given her what she wanted mistakes a stated relational fantasy for consent to what actually occurred.
- Reira then discovers that the only weapon she has against the system is to stop singing.
- Ren considers leaving Trapnest because his addiction has made him dangerous to the band, then decides he still wants to protect it.
- Hachi cancels a birthday visit to Nana because Takumi is physically unwell and she cannot bring herself to leave him.

Again and again the volume asks:

> **Can love survive the fact that the beloved has other loyalties, other obligations, other people, and a self that cannot be wholly surrendered?**

The answer at Volume 20's boundary is not reassuring. Several characters can articulate healthier principles. None has fully solved the underlying problem.

The volume closes by returning to the oldest and most dangerous version of Nana and Ren's promise:

> 「死ぬ時は道連れだって言ったじゃない」

followed by the isolated final line:

> 「置き去りになんてされたくなかった」

The closing does not establish what will happen next. It does establish the emotional law beneath the cliffhanger: Nana's deepest terror remains not simply loneliness, but **being left behind by the person whose love was supposed to prove that abandonment could finally be defeated**.

### Structural observation

The volume begins with a future-facing sequence in which names survive people and circumstances, then ends with a present-tense road sequence in which an old death promise returns. That architecture creates a temporal trap: the reader sees that relationships and names persist into a future, but is denied the causal bridge that explains how the present reaches it.

The correct epistemic stance at this point is therefore:

- the future has changed;
- absence and memory are real;
- particular people remain bound by names and stories;
- but the cause of those absences is still unresolved within Volume 20.

## 3. Narrative architecture and causal close read

### 3.1 Chapter 74 — the future remembers through names

Volume 20 does not open by returning cleanly to the present. It opens by making the reader live inside the consequences first.

The chapter title page places us in London. The future household contains Hachi, Takumi, and a boy named Ren. The name immediately matters because the volume's frontmatter has already turned naming into an act of attachment:

> 「レンが与えてくれたこの愛しい名前を」

and then:

> 「声を潜めて名前を呼んでみる」

A name is not merely identification in this volume. It is a way to keep someone available to speech after ordinary access has been interrupted.

The future boy Ren is not presented as a substitute who erases Honjo Ren. The opposite occurs. His name activates memory. Reira recalls something Ren once said about continuing to value a person even if that person's life does not follow one's wishes, even if that person becomes connected to someone else. Holding the hand of a child with Ren's name lets her feel gentleness arise naturally.

This establishes one of Volume 20's deepest oppositions before the present story resumes:

> **Can attachment remain care when it stops controlling outcome?**

Future Reira remembers Ren through precisely that possibility. Present Nana will later confess that this is almost exactly what she cannot do.

The chapter then returns to the present and creates another naming sequence around Hachi's unborn child. Ren proposes the name 「皐」, explaining that he had decided the child should have a flower name. The choice also preserves Hachi's desired nickname, 「サっちゃん」.

This seemingly domestic scene carries several currents at once.

First, Ren participates in the imagined family future even though he is not its socially recognized father. His joke that the child might be his is unserious at the level of literal paternity, but serious at the level of relational adjacency. He is close enough to Hachi's household to help name the child.

Second, the flower motif links names to living continuity. The frontmatter's "beautiful flower's name" is followed by a real act of naming a future child after a flower/seasonal term.

Third, the chapter has already shown us a later child named Ren. The reader therefore experiences names as vessels that outlive the conditions in which they were originally given.

The chapter then moves through the fractured adult network around BLAST and Trapnest. Shin learns that fans are still waiting for BLAST's return. This is important because the band's crisis has become a crisis of social identity: Shin had begun to imagine that the public relation itself might have disappeared. The reassurance that fans are waiting gives "return" an institutional as well as personal meaning.

Ren, meanwhile, tells Takumi:

> 「おれトラネス抜けようと思うんだけど」

This is not yet a settled resignation. It is a statement produced by guilt, addiction, and a belief that he has become a liability. But it changes the problem. Ren is no longer simply being dragged between Nana and Trapnest. He is beginning to ask whether his continued presence harms the collective he wants to protect.

The chapter's Nana/Hachi material turns March 5 into a double anniversary. Hachi reminds Nana that the date is not only Nana's birthday but also the anniversary of their meeting. Hachi wants to celebrate it together.

Then the manga cuts to future Nana's direct address:

> 「ねえ ハチ」

and the extraordinary one-way-ticket passage:

> 「今年の誕生日こそは」  
> 「がんばった自分にプレゼントを買いに行くよ」  
> 「東京までの片道キップ」  
> 「そう思いながら もう何年経ったんだっけ」

The page does not tell us enough to reconstruct the whole future. It does tell us that years have passed in which Nana has imagined, but not completed, a one-way return to Tokyo.

That future is then juxtaposed with present Nana imagining something very small: being in Osaka on her birthday and eating okonomiyaki with Hachi.

The contrast is one of Yazawa's most effective temporal techniques:

- present happiness is modest and immediate;
- future memory makes even that modest wish feel already lost.

### 3.2 Chapter 75 — addiction converts intimacy into management

Chapter 75 establishes a precise date: **2002-03-04, 00:02**. The proximity to Nana's birthday makes the whole chapter feel like a countdown.

The major movement is Ren's addiction becoming impossible to treat as background strain.

Takumi already knows. More importantly, he has a plan that reveals the ethical structure of Trapnest as an institution under pressure. He tells Ren to endure until a break can be created, and explicitly tells him to use the drugs he has left so he can keep functioning until then:

> 「その間部屋に隠って薬抜け」  
> 「それまでしょーがねえから 成田にもらった残りでやり過ごせよ」  
> 「切れたら仕事にならねえんだろ？」

Ren responds by asking why Takumi does not simply fire him. Takumi refuses that logic. If Ren cares that much about the band, he says, then he should make the effort to recover rather than quit.

This is one of the volume's most ethically mixed scenes.

Takumi is not indifferent to Ren. He does want him alive and rehabilitated. He does not regard him as disposable. But his care is filtered through production continuity. The immediate solution is not "stop now"; it is "remain functional until the schedule allows withdrawal."

The band leader, friend, and manager cannot be cleanly separated.

That ambiguity is central to *NANA*'s mature treatment of professional adulthood. Care inside an institution can be genuine and still be deformed by the institution's needs.

Ren admits:

> 「でももう 自力で抜く自信ねえし…」

This is crucial. His crisis is not simply moral weakness or insufficient will. He states that he no longer trusts himself to stop unaided.

Takumi tells him that the despair may itself be a side effect and insists that because hospitalization is difficult, he will have to do it himself. Ren's answer is not a strategic plan. It is Nana:

> 「ナナに会いてぇ……」

The desire for Nana is therefore doing two things at once.

It is real love and longing.

It is also being recruited as an imagined mechanism of recovery. That makes the impending reunion emotionally necessary but potentially dangerous: Nana is being asked, at least in Ren's mind, to occupy a therapeutic function no partner can safely guarantee.

The Reira/Takumi confrontation complicates another form of mistaken relational entitlement.

Reira tells Takumi she knows he is tired of the adult version of her and that the person he wants is the girl she used to be. She then demands to know why he acted as he did. Takumi answers by referring to her earlier statement that she wanted to become his mistress:

> 「そこまで分かってて おれの愛人になりたい っつったのは おまえだろ！」

Reira replies with the deliberately explosive:

> 「強姦罪で訴えてやるー!!」

The scene is tonally volatile, but its ethical content is not difficult to identify. Reira rejects Takumi's attempt to convert a prior relational desire into blanket consent for the actual encounter. Her next line is even clearer:

> 「あたしが望んでるのは そんなもんじゃないんだよ」

What she wants is the lost intimacy of "昔みたいに 仲良くしたい"—to be close as they once were.

The volume therefore puts two forms of overreach near each other:

- Takumi interprets what Reira wanted in one register as permission in another.
- Nana will later interpret love itself as something that should justify total sacrifice.

Both errors collapse another person's independent interiority into the lover's preferred story.

Reira's relation to singing also changes sharply. She insists to Takumi:

> 「あたしは歌うよ！」  
> 「あたしはタクミの為に 歌ってるわけじゃないんだから！」

This is an assertion of artistic autonomy. Her voice does not belong to him merely because he built the band around it.

Yet when Ren later asks her to sing while he suffers through withdrawal, she cannot.

That inability matters. Reira's voice is both her strongest public capacity and something she cannot always mobilize as private care. The fantasy that "the singer can save through song" breaks at the moment someone she loves actually asks for it.

Her dream that night returns to Ren's departure for Tokyo. The present crisis therefore activates an older separation wound. She is not just afraid he may die. She is reliving the experience of being left.

### 3.3 Chapter 76 — not repeating the old answer

Chapter 76 begins under the pressure of repetition.

The phrase:

> 「3年前と 同じ答えを 出したくは ない」

captures a series-wide question: can people return to the same emotional configuration and choose differently?

Ren's answer to Hachi suggests some genuine change. Hachi tells him that if Takumi had once longed to see her as desperately as Ren longs for Nana, she thinks she could have forgiven everything simply from knowing that.

For Hachi, being intensely wanted possesses enormous reparative power. This is consistent with her long history of equating desire, selection, and belonging.

Ren, however, says:

> 「でもおれはもう 自分の気持ちを強引に押しつけたく ねえんだよ」

This is one of Ren's clearest statements of relational learning. He still wants Nana. He is not renouncing desire. He is distinguishing desire from the right to force it upon her.

That distinction places him temporarily ahead of Nana's later self-description. Ren is trying to discover how love can remain intense without becoming coercive. Nana will admit that her own desire still wants total surrender.

The chapter then turns Reira's voice into an instrument of refusal.

Her message explains that she has realized, ironically, that because singing is the only weapon she has, the only fight currently available to her is **not to sing**:

> 「でも歌う事しか武器を持たない私に」  
> 「今出来る唯一の戦いは」  
> 「歌わない事だと皮肉にも気付きました」

She knows the media will reduce this to the whim of a spoiled princess. She asks that the pause be used to let Ren rest and, if he wants it, receive hospital treatment.

She concludes:

> 「私もしばらく羽を休めて」  
> 「自分が歌う意味を」  
> 「もう一度見つめ直します。」

This is not merely petulance and not merely self-sacrifice.

Functionally, it resembles a one-person labor stoppage. Reira discovers that because the institution depends on her voice, withholding that voice gives her leverage that ordinary pleading did not. At the same time, she uses the stoppage to recover authorship over the meaning of her art.

That is a major development in the series' treatment of celebrity labor:

> **The asset becomes powerful only by refusing to function as an asset.**

The emotional center of the chapter then shifts to Hachi and Nana.

Hachi has permission to travel to Osaka for Nana's birthday. But Takumi returns in a physically and emotionally depleted state, collapses, and will not explain what has happened. Hachi changes her plan.

She tells Nana she may not be able to come because Takumi is unwell.

Nana's spoken answer is controlled:

> 「いや いいよ別に それならしょうがねえじゃん」

Her narration is not:

> 「ハチはいつも あたしより タクミを選ぶんだから」  
> 「みんな あたしより 大切なものがあるんだよ」

This is a perfect example of why character belief must remain distinct from textual fact.

It is **not** objectively established that Hachi "always" chooses Takumi over Nana. Nana is speaking from hurt. Yet the statement is extraordinarily important because it reveals how Nana interprets relational plurality: if someone attends to another obligation when Nana needs them, the event becomes evidence that Nana is not first.

For Nana, love still tends to organize itself as ranking.

The chapter crosscuts this with Shin's promise to Reira that he will come for her once he can become "Junichiro" and with Nana's future address to Hachi:

> 「このままあんたに会えないまま」  
> 「年だけ取って行きたくねえよ」

Waiting and return are now everywhere.

The question is no longer simply "who loves whom?" It is:

> **Who is allowed to come back, who waits, who goes to retrieve whom, and what form must a person take before they believe they have earned return?**

The chapter closes on Nana's clearest cognitive recognition:

> 「レンには あたしより大切なものがある」  
> 「ハチにも あたしより大切な人がいる」  
> 「自分だって 譲れない夢があるのに」  
> 「どうして あたしはそれが許せないんだよ」

This is the hinge into Chapter 77.

### 3.4 Chapter 77 — the mother who would not sacrifice herself and the daughter who wants total sacrifice

The final chapter gives the reader information Nana does not possess.

The reporter Kurata confronts Misuzu Uehara, Nana's estranged mother, in the snow. He uses Misato's disappearance and information about the family as leverage. He tells her that he showed Misato photographic proof that her mother was alive; he presses Misuzu for the story of why Nana was abandoned.

The scene is ethically ugly because information itself has become coercive currency.

Kurata possesses:

- private photographs;
- knowledge of Misato's whereabouts;
- knowledge of Nana's family history;
- the power to publish;
- and the ability to exchange one piece of family safety for another piece of family trauma.

The scene makes celebrity journalism inseparable from the economy of intimacy. Nana's public value makes the family's private wounds professionally valuable.

Misuzu's confession strips away any comforting maternal mythology:

> 「ナナの為なんかやない…」  
> 「あたしがナナの為に…」  
> 「自分を犠牲に出来ひんかっただけや」  
> 「ナナより 子供嫌いの男との暮らしを選んだんや」

She does not claim that leaving Nana was an altruistic act. She says the opposite. She chose a life she wanted more than she chose Nana.

The text then returns to Nana, who does not know any of this.

After Hachi's canceled visit, Nana's mood collapses. What keeps her moving is the density of professional work:

> 「だけど奮い立つしかない過密スケジュールに支えられた」  
> 「あたしを救えるのは やっぱり自分の夢だけだ」

This is not proof that art is merely avoidance.

Nana's dream is genuinely hers. It is the most stable self-authored structure she possesses. The line matters because it preserves the legitimacy of her ambition even while the volume shows the emotional cost of using vocation as the only structure that never leaves.

The home question then becomes explicit:

> 「あたしには帰れる故郷がない」

The Uehara household, however flawed, still functions as a place from which Misato can be called home. Nana does not possess an equivalent natal refuge.

She can imagine returning to the band community through continued work. What she does not know is how to "return" to Ren.

This distinction is critical:

- **professional home** can be re-entered through performance and membership;
- **romantic home** requires mutuality and cannot be earned unilaterally;
- **natal home** is absent.

The volume then places Miu's philosophy directly before Nana's.

Miu says she does not want to be Yasu's girlfriend even though she cares about him, because:

> 「でも他人を独り占めするなんて しょせん出来ないのよ」

and:

> 「あたしはそんな無理を強いる存在にはわざわざなりたくないよ」

She fears that making an impossible demand would cause her to become the source of the relationship's destruction.

The very next movement gives us Nana:

> 「全てを犠牲にして あたしを愛して欲しい」  
> 「あたしが死んだら 一緒に死んで欲しい」  
> 「昂ると抑えのきかない あたしの望みは」  
> 「いつかレンの全てを 奪い尽くしそうで」  
> 「怖いんだよ」

This juxtaposition is too exact to treat as incidental.

Miu's principle is:

> I do not want to demand monopoly because the demand itself could destroy what I love.

Nana's admission is:

> I want total sacrifice and I am afraid that desire will consume the person I love.

The difference is not that Miu loves weakly and Nana loves strongly. The difference is how each understands the ethical boundary between attachment and ownership.

Yet Yazawa refuses to make Miu a simplistic "healthy relationship" mouthpiece. Immediately after refusing the girlfriend label, Miu and Yasu are shown sharing physical intimacy. Their bond does not fit a neat category. It may preserve freedom; it may also preserve avoidance. The point is that formal labels and actual intimacy do not map cleanly onto each other.

The final movement gives Ren agency again.

Hachi tells him that Nana does not know how bad his condition is and offers to tell her. Ren says he will tell Nana himself:

> 「いや 自分で話すよ 蹴り入れてもらって更生するから」

He asks for Nana's lodging information and begins moving toward Osaka.

He also says:

> 「おれはやっぱり トラネスを守りてえんだ……」

This is essential because Ren is not simply choosing Nana over Trapnest after all. He is trying to preserve multiple commitments:

- recover;
- tell Nana the truth;
- reach Nana;
- protect Trapnest;
- retrieve Reira;
- respond to the collapse around him.

That plural structure is precisely what Nana has admitted she struggles to permit.

The final pages bind Ren to Nana through sound before they are physically reunited. Ren hears BLACK STONES over the radio. The band becomes a bridge across distance. Then the manga returns to the warehouse district, to the old promise, to snow, to the language of death and abandonment.

The final sequence is not an objective prediction. It is emotional foreshadowing:

> 「ねえ レン」  
> 「レンはあたしに」  
> 「死ぬ時は道連れだって言ったじゃない」

and finally:

> 「置き去りになんてされたくなかった」

The volume stops before explaining what event will give those words their later force.

At the Volume 20 boundary, the responsible reading is:

- an old death pact has become narratively ominous;
- Nana's fear of abandonment is at maximum explicitness;
- Ren is moving toward return and recovery;
- future narration tells us that the present configuration will not remain intact;
- the exact causal outcome remains unresolved.

## 4. Nana Osaki — character-state update

Volume 20 is one of the most important Nana volumes because it moves her from enacted possessiveness toward **conscious possessiveness**.

### 4.1 She can finally describe the contradiction

Nana's key realization is not that she is jealous. She has known jealousy for a long time.

The new step is that she can articulate the unfairness of her own expectation.

She knows:

- Ren has priorities beyond her;
- Hachi has Takumi and a household beyond her;
- she herself has a dream she refuses to relinquish.

She does not deny this. Her question is why knowing it does not make her capable of accepting it.

This means her problem can no longer be reduced to ignorance.

She is not merely someone who "doesn't understand" that other people have lives. She understands and still hurts.

That is a more mature and more difficult characterization.

### 4.2 Her desire for sacrifice is explicitly linked to fear

Nana's confession that she wants Ren to sacrifice everything is not presented triumphantly.

She is afraid of herself.

> 「いつかレンの全てを 奪い尽くしそうで 怖いんだよ」

The important noun is not just love. It is **everything**.

Nana does not merely want fidelity. She wants to become the organizing absolute of Ren's life.

This helps clarify earlier contradictions in her behavior. She has often demanded independence for herself while responding intensely to signs that the people she loves possess independent centers.

Volume 20 makes the asymmetry visible to Nana herself.

### 4.3 The mother sequence explains without curing

Misuzu's confession gives the reader a psychologically powerful frame for Nana's totalizing attachment:

- the first person who should have prioritized Nana did not;
- the mother explicitly says she would not sacrifice her own desired life for Nana;
- adult Nana now longs for proof that someone will make the opposite choice.

But the method requires a crucial distinction:

**Nana does not hear this confession.**

Therefore it is not yet a conscious causal explanation available to her.

The manga creates dramatic irony, not therapeutic insight.

A strong inference is that the juxtaposition invites us to see Nana's demand for total sacrifice as historically intelligible. It does not make that demand ethically legitimate.

### 4.4 Dream as rescue is genuine, not merely defensive

Nana says:

> 「あたしを救えるのは やっぱり自分の夢だけだ」

This line could be flattened into "Nana uses work to avoid feelings." That is insufficient.

Her dream predates many of the current crises. It is one of the few things she has authored herself rather than inherited as abandonment, romance, or family role.

The dream is therefore both:

- a genuine source of identity;
- and a defense against relational collapse.

Those truths do not cancel each other.

### 4.5 Nana's home problem becomes categorical

Her statement that she has no hometown to return to is not simply geographic.

Volume 20 differentiates multiple kinds of return:

- Misato can be called back to a family home.
- Shin imagines BLAST's return through fans.
- Reira imagines return through being picked up.
- Ren drives toward Nana.
- Nana can return to band life by continuing to sing.
- Nana cannot identify how to return to Ren.
- future Nana imagines a one-way ticket back to Tokyo for years without taking it.

Nana therefore has many relational destinations but no uncomplicated home.

That is why "being left behind" carries more force for her than ordinary breakup anxiety. If a person becomes home because no stable home exists behind them, the beloved's autonomy can feel existentially threatening.

### 4.6 Nana/Hachi remains central even when absent

Hachi does not physically reach Nana for the birthday sequence, yet their bond dominates Nana's narration.

The date itself is dual: birthday and anniversary of their first meeting.

Future Nana addresses Hachi across years.

Present Nana imagines Osaka with Hachi.

Hachi's cancellation becomes evidence in Nana's mind that everyone has someone more important.

The dyad therefore continues to occupy structural centrality even when the plot's immediate crisis is Nana/Ren.

The two bonds are not interchangeable:

- Ren is erotic/romantic home and the person Nana imagines dying beside.
- Hachi is domestic/relational home and the person future Nana imagines returning to Tokyo for.

Volume 20 intensifies both forms of absence simultaneously.

---

## 5. Komatsu Nana / Hachi — character-state update

Hachi's Volume 20 role is less spectacular than Nana's, but she is one of the volume's major causal organizers. She keeps trying to connect people who cannot reliably connect themselves.

### 5.1 Hachi as mediator

She:

- plans Nana's birthday visit;
- understands Nana's longing for Ren;
- urges Ren to contact Nana;
- offers to disclose Ren's condition if he cannot;
- gives Ren access to Nana's location;
- monitors Takumi's physical state;
- remains attentive to Reira's whereabouts;
- participates in the naming of her unborn child.

Hachi is no longer simply someone to whom things happen. She actively coordinates an unstable adult network.

Her domestic intelligence has become social infrastructure.

### 5.2 Care becomes a real constraint on mobility

When Takumi returns depleted and collapses, Hachi stays.

This is not best read as a simple test of "who she loves more."

The material situation matters:

- Takumi is physically present and unwell;
- Hachi is his wife and cohabiting partner;
- she has immediate caregiving capacity;
- Nana's need is emotionally real but not physically acute in the same way.

Hachi makes a choice, but it is a choice inside a dense domestic role.

Nana interprets the result as another instance of Hachi choosing Takumi over her. Both realities can coexist:

- Hachi's decision is understandable care;
- Nana experiences it as abandonment.

### 5.3 Hachi still gives enormous moral weight to being desired

Hachi's conversation with Ren is especially revealing.

She imagines that if Takumi had once been away and had desperately wanted to see her the way Ren wants to see Nana, the knowledge alone might have allowed her to forgive everything.

This is not a universal moral proposition. It is Hachi's relational psychology.

For Hachi, being chosen and wanted carries extraordinary reparative force.

That helps explain why competence, provision, sexual desire, domestic attention, and explicit need have mattered so much in her attachment history. She is highly sensitive to signs that another person has made her central.

The danger is obvious: intense desire can be mistaken for ethical repair.

Ren's response supplies the counterpoint. He no longer wants to force his feelings on Nana.

### 5.4 Future Hachi is embedded in a transformed family structure

The London sequence establishes Hachi, Takumi, and a boy named Ren in a future domestic setting.

The volume does not explain every intervening event, but it does show that Hachi's life continues in an ordinary material register:

- shared room;
- parenting;
- food;
- teasing;
- routine.

This matters because *NANA* repeatedly treats ordinary domesticity as something that becomes precious precisely because the dramatic present is so unstable.

Future Hachi is not presented as having escaped complexity. Reira, Nana, Ren's memory, and the past all remain active around the household.

### 5.5 Hachi's future relation to Nana remains one of waiting

Nana's future address tells us that years pass in which she imagines buying a one-way ticket to Tokyo.

Hachi's own future-facing narration elsewhere in the volume continues to position Nana as someone whose return is still desired.

The bond therefore survives without ordinary co-presence.

Volume 20 strengthens the interpretation that for Hachi, "home" is not reducible to marriage. Her legally and materially recognized household is one home structure; Nana remains another relational destination whose absence is not absorbed by marriage.

## 6. Other materially affected characters

### 6.1 Ren Honjo — from romantic absolutism toward plural responsibility

Ren's Volume 20 movement is one of the most important in the book.

He is physically and psychologically in crisis, but he also makes several statements that indicate ethical development.

#### Addiction is now explicit self-knowledge

He admits he no longer has confidence that he can stop by himself.

This is a reduction of grandiosity. Ren cannot solve this through will alone.

#### He considers leaving Trapnest to protect it

His initial impulse is self-removal. If he is damaging the band, perhaps he should leave.

Takumi rejects that as the only option and reframes responsibility as recovery.

#### He does not want to force Nana anymore

This is a major relational shift:

> 「自分の気持ちを強引に押しつけたく ねえんだよ」

Ren is trying to separate love from entitlement.

#### He still wants several things at once

By the end he wants:

- Nana;
- recovery;
- Trapnest's survival;
- Reira's return;
- professional repair.

This plural desire is not indecision. It is adult complexity.

The tragedy is that Nana's attachment system experiences such plurality as evidence that she is not enough.

### 6.2 Reira Serizawa — voice becomes refusal

Reira's development is equally substantial.

#### She rejects Takumi's interpretation of her desire

"I wanted to be your mistress" does not mean "what happened is what I wanted." The distinction between role fantasy and actual consent is made explicit by Reira herself.

#### She reclaims her singing from Takumi

She tells him she does not sing for him.

That sentence matters because Takumi's managerial position and childhood history have made it easy for her voice to feel like something he organizes.

#### She cannot sing when Ren asks

Her public power fails as private rescue. This exposes the limit of artistic identity.

#### She weaponizes absence

Her realization that not singing is the only fight available to her turns celebrity dependence upside down. The institution depends on her more than she normally allows herself to recognize.

#### Future Reira and the child named Ren

The future frame does not show grief resolved. It shows grief mediated through continuity. Another Ren's hand allows Reira to practice the gentleness the older Ren once imagined.

The name is not replacement. It is an ethical prompt.

### 6.3 Takumi Ichinose — competence, care, control, and exhaustion

Takumi remains difficult precisely because the volume refuses to make his capacities unreal.

He:

- identifies Ren's addiction;
- tries to build a detox window;
- refuses to discard Ren;
- tries to keep Trapnest functioning;
- responds to Reira's collapse;
- returns home depleted enough that Hachi will not leave him.

These are forms of competence and care.

But his mode of care repeatedly becomes managerial control.

With Ren, he tells him to keep using enough drugs to stay functional until the schedule permits withdrawal.

With Reira, he treats her prior statement of desire as if it justified his chosen implementation.

Takumi's recurring danger is not that he never cares. It is that he is extremely good at translating care into decisions made on other people's behalf.

### 6.4 Miu Shinoda — the anti-monopoly countermodel

Miu has one of the volume's most concentrated thematic scenes.

She says she does not want to become Yasu's girlfriend because she does not believe another person can actually be monopolized, especially someone like Yasu whose care extends broadly.

She fears that if she accepted the formal role, she would begin demanding what cannot be given, make him miserable, and destroy the relationship.

Her solution is ethically self-aware but not necessarily complete. The following intimacy with Yasu demonstrates that emotional and sexual closeness continue without the formal label.

Miu therefore becomes an alternative relational experiment:

> Can one preserve intimacy by refusing the social role that would trigger possessive expectations?

The manga does not yet declare whether that strategy is sustainable.

### 6.5 Yasu Takagi — care that resists exclusive ownership

Yasu's broader disposition makes Miu's reasoning plausible. He is a person many others rely upon. A conventional couple structure could force a conflict between his diffuse protective role and a partner's reasonable desire for priority.

Volume 20 does not solve that contradiction. It simply makes Miu unusually conscious of it.

### 6.6 Misato Uehara and Misuzu Uehara — the family history the protagonist cannot hear

Misato's disappearance gives the reporter leverage against Misuzu.

The Uehara household matters not only as backstory but as a foil for Nana's statement that she has no hometown to return to. Misato can still be summoned back into a family structure, however fraught.

Misuzu's confession is one of the most important pieces of reader-only information in the series so far. It does not excuse her. She specifically rejects the idea that she abandoned Nana altruistically.

That honesty makes the history harsher and more useful analytically.

### 6.7 Nobu and Yuri

Their material is not the volume's primary axis, but it continues *NANA*'s examination of how profession, sexual history, jealousy, and self-worth contaminate ordinary couple reassurance.

Yuri's work and public/sexual identity cannot be left outside the relationship. Nobu's care is real, but neither partner occupies a socially simple position.

The subplot supports the volume's larger claim that adulthood does not allow relationships to remain sealed from work, money, reputation, and prior choices.

## 7. Relationship-state changes

### 7.1 Nana / Ren

**Previous pressure:** love, artistic identity, jealousy, separation, mutually intense attachment, old death-pact language.  
**Volume 20 change:** both parties become more conscious of the danger of force, but they develop asymmetrically.

Ren says he no longer wants to force his feelings.

Nana says she wants total sacrifice and fears she may consume him.

This is not a simple reversal in which Ren becomes healthy and Nana unhealthy. Ren remains addicted, emotionally dependent, and inclined to imagine Nana as part of his recovery. But he is articulating a boundary Nana has not yet internalized.

The relationship's central question becomes:

> Can they remain intensely attached without requiring one another to abandon the rest of life?

### 7.2 Nana / Hachi

The birthday cancellation materially strengthens Nana's fear that Hachi's recognized household has displaced her.

Hachi does not reject Nana. She is actively trying to see her and treats March 5 as their anniversary. Yet a real domestic emergency changes the plan.

The dyad therefore enters an adult phase in which love must compete with schedule, illness, marriage, travel, and responsibility.

Future Nana's one-way-ticket passage makes the loss of spontaneous access central. The question is no longer whether they love one another. It is whether love can remain a form of home when ordinary cohabitation and priority are gone.

### 7.3 Hachi / Takumi

Hachi chooses immediate care when Takumi collapses.

Takumi's vulnerability matters because it disrupts a purely one-directional provider model. The competent husband returns home looking as though he might cry and physically gives out.

Hachi becomes the caretaker.

This does not erase prior coercive or unequal dynamics. It shows why the relationship cannot be reduced to them.

Their marriage contains:

- material dependence;
- asymmetrical power;
- genuine care;
- sexuality;
- hurt;
- habit;
- responsibility;
- and ordinary domestic attachment.

### 7.4 Takumi / Reira

The relationship becomes more explicitly impossible.

Reira wants a prior kind of closeness that cannot simply be restored by sex or by the mistress role she once imagined.

Takumi answers relational ambiguity through action and decision; Reira experiences those actions as proof that he has misunderstood what she actually wanted.

Her refusal to sing then makes the personal conflict institutional.

### 7.5 Ren / Reira

Reira's care during withdrawal is intimate but not framed as romantic replacement.

She wants Ren alive. She trusts the depth of his attachment to Nana. She cannot provide the song he asks for, and his crisis reactivates the memory of his old departure.

Their bond is therefore one of the strongest examples of *NANA* refusing to classify care neatly by romance.

### 7.6 Miu / Yasu

Their relation becomes a formal counterexample to possession.

Miu refuses the girlfriend role while continuing intimacy.

The relation may be freer because it avoids exclusivity claims; it may also remain unstable because it avoids naming needs. Both readings remain viable.

### 7.7 Shin / Reira

Shin's message promises retrieval once he becomes capable of occupying a more legitimate adult identity:

> 「順一郎になれたら迎えに行きます。」

The formulation turns love into delayed return. He must become someone else—or someone more socially viable—before he feels entitled to arrive.

That makes their relationship another version of the volume's return motif.

## 8. Japanese voice, address, and translation-sensitive language

Volume 20 is unusually rich in lexical echoes. Several words do structural work far beyond their dictionary meaning.

### 8.1 「犠牲」 — sacrifice

The strongest lexical mirror in the volume is between Misuzu and Nana.

Misuzu:

> 「自分を犠牲に出来ひんかっただけや」

Nana:

> 「全てを犠牲にして あたしを愛して欲しい」

The first sentence describes failure to sacrifice the self for a child.

The second demands sacrifice of the beloved self as proof of love.

The repetition creates a transgenerational structure without requiring Nana to know the source.

This is a textbook example of why the Japanese wording matters. A paraphrase like "her mother chose herself" / "Nana wants Ren to choose her" would preserve plot but lose the exact mirrored concept.

### 8.2 「大切」 and the language of ranking

Nana repeatedly describes others as having something or someone 「あたしより大切」—more important than her.

The emotional logic is comparative.

The problem is not merely that Ren loves Trapnest or Hachi loves Takumi. It is that Nana converts plurality into hierarchy:

> If they value something else, where am I ranked?

That structure helps explain why ordinary adult obligations feel like abandonment.

### 8.3 「選ぶ」 — choosing

Nana's internal statement that Hachi always chooses Takumi over her turns care into selection.

Again, this is character belief rather than objective narration.

But the verb reveals Nana's model of intimacy: simultaneous obligations become mutually exclusive choices.

### 8.4 「譲れない夢」 — a dream one cannot yield

Nana's self-critique depends on the fact that she herself has an unyielding dream.

The word choice prevents the analysis from treating her as simply hypocritical. She knows why another person might have something they cannot surrender because she has exactly such a thing.

Her failure is emotional integration, not conceptual incomprehension.

### 8.5 「独り占め」 — monopolize / keep entirely to oneself

Miu's use of 「独り占め」 is almost a gloss on Nana's problem.

Miu does not say love is impossible. She says **monopoly** is impossible.

Placed immediately before Nana's confession, the word gives the reader a vocabulary Nana herself does not use for her desire.

### 8.6 「歌う」 / 「歌わない」 — singing and refusing to sing

Reira first states:

> I sing, but not for Takumi.

Then she decides:

> not singing is the only fight available to me.

The polarity matters.

Voice is not simply self-expression. It is labor, commodity, identity, gift, and leverage.

To sing can be autonomy.

To refuse to sing can also be autonomy.

Context decides which.

### 8.7 「戻る」「帰る」「迎えに行く」 — return, go home, go retrieve

These verbs form one of the volume's largest lexical systems.

- future Nana imagines a one-way ticket back to Tokyo;
- Shin promises to come get Reira;
- Ren promises to bring Reira back;
- Nana says she has no hometown to return to;
- Nana wonders how to return to Ren;
- Nana imagines going back with Ren to the warehouse district.

The volume's deepest spatial question is not "where do people live?"

It is:

> **Which relationships remain places one can still return to?**

### 8.8 「名前」 — name

The volume begins by calling a beloved name quietly in darkness and repeatedly returns to names as continuity:

- the name Ren gave Nana;
- the unborn child's proposed name, 皐;
- the future boy named Ren;
- Reira's memory being softened by another Ren.

Names hold relations after circumstances change.

### 8.9 「道連れ」 and 「置き去り」

These closing words should not be domesticated in translation.

「道連れ」 carries the sense of taking someone along, here in death.

「置き去り」 means being left behind, abandoned in place.

The emotional logic is brutally simple:

> If being left behind is intolerable, "going together" can acquire lethal romantic force.

The manga does not ask us to approve that logic. It asks us to understand how Nana gets there.

## 9. Narration, time, memory, and information distribution

### 9.1 The future frame does not explain the future

The London opening gives concrete information:

- Hachi and Takumi are in a future household;
- there is a boy named Ren;
- Reira's relationship to Ren's memory persists;
- the past remains emotionally active.

It does **not** tell us the causal sequence from March 2002 to that future.

This distinction must remain intact.

### 9.2 Nana's future address contaminates ordinary present scenes

The repeated 「ねえ ハチ」 passages make present events feel like relics while they are still happening.

The birthday plan is immediately followed by future Nana remembering years of not buying the ticket back.

The effect is not simply foreshadowing. It changes the emotional tense of the present.

A small plan to eat okonomiyaki feels tragic because the reader has already been told that future Nana will spend years imagining return.

### 9.3 Addressee shifts matter

The volume moves between:

- Nana addressing Hachi;
- Nana addressing Ren;
- Reira addressing Ren in memory;
- characters calling children's names;
- written messages that substitute for physical presence.

This makes direct address itself a motif of absence.

People speak most intensely to those who are not physically available.

### 9.4 Reader knowledge exceeds Nana's knowledge

The mother sequence is the clearest information-distribution device.

The reader learns why Misuzu says she left.

Nana does not.

That asymmetry prevents the analysis from treating the volume as a psychological resolution. It is instead a formal explanation withheld from the character who most needs it.

### 9.5 The ending is prospective, not retrospective proof

The final lines carry enormous ominous force, but within the Volume 20 boundary they remain a prospective signal.

They establish:

- Nana remembers the death pact;
- abandonment is the emotional frame;
- the current road/return movement is dangerous in tone.

They do not establish the precise outcome of the road sequence.

## 10. Visual and formal analysis

### 10.1 Snow links memory, danger, and suspended time

Snow appears across the volume's late movements around Misuzu, Nana, Ren, roads, and return.

The white flecks against dark backgrounds create an ambiguous field:

- weather;
- visual noise;
- memory particles;
- isolation.

The effect is to make the closing pages feel detached from ordinary chronological texture.

The road is real, but it already looks like memory.

### 10.2 Roads and cars create mobile isolation

Characters are repeatedly in transit while emotionally trying to return:

- Ren drives;
- Nana moves through professional travel;
- Hachi plans travel to Osaka;
- future Nana imagines a one-way ticket;
- the warehouse district becomes a destination of memory.

Mobility does not guarantee access.

The volume is full of movement between people who remain emotionally separated.

### 10.3 Phones and messages replace bodies

A large portion of the volume's intimacy is mediated through:

- calls;
- texts;
- emails;
- messages relayed through third parties.

This is not merely period technology.

Formally, it allows Yazawa to stage relationships as chains of partial knowledge. Hachi knows one thing, Ren another, Nana another, Reira another. Contact occurs without full disclosure.

### 10.4 The p.157 → p.158 transition is a deliberate ideological cut

Miu says she does not want to monopolize another person or force an impossible demand.

The next movement gives Nana's wish for total sacrifice.

The sequencing performs comparison more strongly than an expository narrator could.

### 10.5 The mother confession and Nana's sacrifice language form a delayed visual rhyme

The pages are not adjacent, but the repeated 「犠牲」 makes them function as a formal pair.

The mother in snow says she could not sacrifice herself for Nana.

Later Nana, in a different emotional space, wants Ren to sacrifice everything.

Because Nana does not know the confession, the visual/lexical echo belongs to the reader's level of interpretation.

### 10.6 The future boy Ren makes repetition visible through bodies

The future child's name places Honjo Ren inside another body without pretending they are the same person.

This is a sophisticated visual use of naming:

- the face is different;
- the age is different;
- the relation is different;
- the name carries memory.

Reira's handholding scene turns memorial continuity into bodily contact.

### 10.7 BLACK STONES on the radio reconnects before reunion

Near the ending, Ren hears BLACK STONES while physically moving through snow and distance.

Nana's artistic voice reaches him before Nana herself does.

This gives music a literal connective function without making it magical.

### 10.8 The final black field

The final line:

> 「置き去りになんてされたくなかった」

is isolated on darkness.

After a volume crowded with people, calls, travel, work, and competing obligations, the visual field finally strips everything away.

What remains is the abandonment sentence.

That formal reduction tells us what the volume believes the emotional core has been all along.

- a romantic home;
- a remembered origin place;

Nana's crisis is partly that these categories do not coincide for her.

## 12. Material and economic adulthood

Volume 20 is full of choices that cannot be understood if love is treated as the only variable.

### 12.1 Trapnest's schedule governs health

Ren cannot simply stop working without consequence.

Takumi's detox plan is built around available rest days. The institution's schedule shapes what medical action feels possible.

This does not justify the decision to let Ren keep using drugs temporarily. It explains why the decision emerges.

### 12.2 Reira's value is labor power

Reira discovers leverage because her voice is economically indispensable.

When she stops singing, the system must respond.

Her personal crisis therefore becomes a labor crisis.

### 12.3 Hachi's marriage is a material relation

Hachi's decision to stay with a sick Takumi is not merely romantic priority. It comes from cohabitation, marriage, bodily proximity, and everyday responsibility.

This is exactly the kind of adult materiality the series increasingly foregrounds.

### 12.4 Travel matters

Tokyo and Osaka are not abstract emotional spaces.

People need schedules, transport, hotel information, and time off.

Future Nana's one-way ticket is powerful because it converts a relational wish into the most material possible object: a ticket that must actually be bought.

### 12.5 Celebrity turns family history into marketable material

Kurata can exploit Nana's mother because Nana is public property in the eyes of the press.

Family trauma acquires economic/media value because it can be converted into a story.

## 13. Music, celebrity, industry, and fashion

### 13.1 Trapnest as both family and employer

Ren's crisis shows why the band cannot be analyzed as either pure friendship or pure workplace.

Takumi's response is simultaneously:

- managerial;
- fraternal;
- coercive;
- protective;
- pragmatic.

The categories overlap because the band members' livelihoods and intimate histories overlap.

### 13.2 Reira's refusal clarifies artistic ownership

Reira's statement that she does not sing for Takumi and later decision not to sing form a coherent progression.

She first separates the art from the man.

Then she separates the artist from the institution.

The question becomes:

> Who owns the conditions under which Reira's voice appears?

For a brief period, she insists that she does.

### 13.3 BLACK STONES remains Nana's self-authored continuity

Nana says her dream is what can save her.

The late radio sequence gives that claim formal force. Her voice/work travels beyond her body and reaches Ren.

Music is therefore not only career or fame. It is one of the few forms through which Nana can remain present across distance without demanding another person stop having a life.

### 13.4 Fandom as waiting

Shin's surprise that fans still await BLAST matters because fame is often invasive in *NANA*, but fandom is not uniformly predatory.

There is also a sustaining form of audience relation:

> people wait for artists to return.

That becomes a public analogue to the volume's private waiting relationships.

### 13.5 Media simplification

Reira anticipates that the media will describe her refusal to sing as a spoiled princess's whim.

Kurata wants a clean abandonment story from Misuzu.

The press repeatedly converts complicated relationships into legible narratives.

The volume's own form does the opposite: it proliferates incompatible motives and partial truths.

## 14. Gender, sexuality, and social scripts

### 14.1 Women's care is expected but differently inhabited

Hachi's response to Takumi's illness is immediate domestic care.

Reira tries to care for Ren through presence, then through institutional refusal.

Miu avoids a girlfriend role partly because she fears what expectations that role would awaken in herself.

Misuzu's confession is framed around failure to make the maternal sacrifice expected of her.

The volume therefore shows several women negotiating the social expectation that love should mean self-sacrifice.

Importantly, it does not endorse one universal answer.

### 14.2 Nana reproduces the sacrificial script in reverse

The most interesting gendered reversal is Nana's demand that Ren sacrifice everything for her.

Her mother refused maternal self-sacrifice.

Nana now imagines romantic love as the condition under which a man should make total sacrifice.

The desire is psychologically understandable and ethically dangerous.

### 14.3 Reira and the mistress role

Reira's earlier willingness to imagine herself as Takumi's mistress does not settle what she consented to in any specific encounter.

The volume itself insists on that distinction through her objection.

A social label—wife, mistress, girlfriend, lover—cannot substitute for ongoing consent.

### 14.4 Miu destabilizes the couple form

Miu's refusal to become Yasu's girlfriend while maintaining intimacy shows that formal relationship recognition and actual closeness are separable.

The text is not necessarily proposing this as superior. It is testing whether refusing a possessive script can protect a bond.

## 15. Ethics, consent, coercion, care, and power

### 15.1 Takumi / Reira — desire is not blanket consent

Takumi's defense is essentially:

> You said you wanted to be my mistress; I gave you what you wanted.

Reira's answer is:

> That is not what I wanted.

The ethical principle is straightforward:

- a general fantasy or relational wish does not authorize every sexual act;
- prior desire does not eliminate the need for situational consent;
- later attachment does not retroactively establish consent.

The scene's comic volume does not erase the content of Reira's objection.

### 15.2 Takumi / Ren — care becomes enabling under production pressure

Takumi wants Ren to recover.

He also knowingly tells him to keep using what remains until a workable detox window.

That is a morally compromised form of care.

The best reading is neither "Takumi does not care" nor "Takumi is simply pragmatic."

He cares inside a system whose requirements deform what care looks like.

### 15.3 Nana / Ren — self-aware possessiveness is still possessiveness

Nana's recognition that her desire is dangerous is ethically meaningful. Awareness is a precondition for change.

But awareness does not make the demand benign.

Wanting another person to sacrifice everything, or to die with you, erases that person's independent claim to life.

The manga is powerful here because Nana herself senses that problem.

### 15.4 Hachi / Takumi — care should not be flattened into either bondage or romance

Hachi chooses to remain with a sick spouse.

That act cannot be fairly described solely as Takumi's control or solely as proof of ideal marriage.

The relationship contains real power asymmetry and real care simultaneously.

### 15.5 Kurata — journalism as coercive information exchange

Kurata uses:

- Misato's vulnerability;
- Misuzu's fear;
- private photographs;
- the threat of exposure;
- and information about family safety

to extract a story.

The scene shows how professional media power can operate without physical force.

### 15.6 Reira's refusal to sing — agency with collateral consequences

Her decision protects Ren and reclaims self-authorship.

It also destabilizes staff, schedules, and the livelihoods attached to Trapnest.

The ethical force of the decision does not require pretending it is costless.

## 17. Counterreadings, limitations, and hypothesis stress-test

### Counterreading 1 — Nana's demand is simply romantic hyperbole

A weaker reading would treat "sacrifice everything" and "die with me" as melodramatic romantic language rather than a serious psychological structure.

**Why it remains possible:** *NANA* uses heightened language, especially around Nana/Ren. The statements occur in emotionally intensified narration.

**Why it is insufficient:** Nana immediately says she fears the desire may take everything from Ren. The text itself marks the wish as dangerous. Miu's preceding anti-monopoly statement and Misuzu's earlier sacrifice confession make the language structurally deliberate.

**Preferred status:** the lines are emotionally heightened, but they reveal a real attachment pattern rather than disposable metaphor.

### Counterreading 2 — Miu represents the healthy answer

Miu's refusal to monopolize Yasu can look like the volume's simple ethical solution.

**Counterevidence:** she avoids the girlfriend role rather than demonstrating a negotiated form of committed non-possessive partnership. The subsequent intimacy may indicate freedom, but it may also indicate avoidance of vulnerability and expectations.

**Preferred status:** Miu is a serious countermodel, not an authorial final answer.

### Counterreading 3 — Ren is now ethically mature

His refusal to force his feelings is significant.

**Counterevidence:** he is addicted, emotionally unstable, still imagines Nana as part of his recovery, and is trying to manage multiple crises through personal will and immediate action.

**Preferred status:** genuine relational growth inside continuing instability.

### Counterreading 4 — Takumi's treatment of Ren is simply pragmatic care

He creates a recovery plan and refuses to abandon Ren.

**Counterevidence:** he knowingly permits continued drug use so work can continue until a break. The institution's needs shape the care in ethically troubling ways.

**Preferred status:** care and enabling are simultaneously present.

### Counterreading 5 — Hachi choosing Takumi proves Nana is secondary

Nana believes this.

**Counterevidence:** Hachi treats March 5 as their anniversary, actively plans the visit, remains deeply attached to Nana, and cancels only after Takumi becomes physically unwell.

**Preferred status:** Nana's perception of ranking is psychologically true for her but not a complete objective map of Hachi's attachments.

### Counterreading 6 — Misuzu's confession explains Nana

The lexical echo strongly invites a transgenerational reading.

**Limitation:** Nana does not hear the confession. Many intervening experiences also shape her attachment. The mother's failure should not become a single-cause psychological formula.

**Preferred status:** strong explanatory context for the reader, not a total causal theory.

### Counterreading 7 — the final death-pact lines establish a specific coming event

The ending is intensely ominous.

**Limitation:** Volume 20 stops before the causal event is shown.

**Preferred status:** strong foreshadowing signal, exact outcome unresolved.

## 20. Cumulative delta and retrospective-correction status

This section records what Volume 20 changes in the series-so-far model without importing Volume 21.

### Strengthened

#### Nana's independence is both authorship and armor

The statement that only her dream can save her strongly confirms that artistic vocation is a real self-authored source of survival. At the same time, the timing shows how quickly Nana recruits work when attachment fails.

#### Nana's fear of abandonment remains central

The closing 「置き去り」 language makes the pattern explicit rather than merely inferred.

#### Nana/Hachi is structurally central beyond ordinary friendship logistics

The birthday/meeting anniversary, future one-way-ticket passage, and future direct address all strengthen the view that the relationship remains one of the series' principal forms of home even after cohabitation ends.

#### Material adulthood governs relational possibility

Hachi's canceled trip, Trapnest's schedule, Reira's labor stoppage, and travel logistics all deepen the series' insistence that emotion operates inside work, money, health, and domestic obligation.

### Complicated

#### Ren's possessiveness

Earlier readings of Ren as simply the counterpart to Nana's possessiveness require complication. In Volume 20 he explicitly says he does not want to force his feelings anymore. This does not erase prior behavior, but it indicates genuine development.

#### Reira's dependence on Takumi

She remains deeply attached to him, but explicitly denies that she sings for him and uses refusal to sing as leverage against his system. Her dependency now coexists with sharpened artistic agency.

#### Hachi/Takumi

The relationship's coercive history remains important, but Volume 20 adds a strong caregiving reversal: Takumi returns depleted and Hachi refuses to leave him. The marriage cannot be summarized as a static one-way dependency.

#### Miu/Yasu

Their relationship complicates the assumption that greater intimacy necessarily demands stronger formal claims. Yet the text has not established that their anti-label strategy solves jealousy or vulnerability.

### Revised

#### Nana's possessiveness is no longer only visible from outside

Volume 20 upgrades this from reader diagnosis to character self-knowledge. Nana herself understands that her desire may consume Ren.

#### The abandonment wound gains new reader-only evidence

Misuzu's confession materially changes the reader's understanding of the original maternal abandonment. It should be logged as a retrospective correction to any earlier hypothesis that Nana may have been left for protective/altruistic reasons.

Crucially, this is a **reader-level correction**, not a Nana-level correction, because Nana has not learned it.

### Still unresolved

- What exact future circumstances prevent Nana from buying the one-way ticket to Tokyo for years?
- What has happened to Honjo Ren in the future frame?
- What is the exact relationship between future boy Ren and the memory structure surrounding Honjo Ren?
- Can Nana and Ren actually convert mutual recognition into a non-annihilating relationship?
- Can Ren recover from addiction without making Nana responsible for recovery?
- How long will Reira's refusal to sing last, and what meaning of singing will she ultimately recover?
- Can Hachi's multiple homes coexist without one becoming a permanent wound for the other?
- Is Miu's refusal of girlfriend status sustainable intimacy or protective avoidance?
- What will become of Misato and Misuzu after the reporter's intervention?
- What event gives the final death-pact narration its future significance?

No later volume is used to answer these questions here.

## 21. Volume thesis and questions carried forward

Volume 20 is fundamentally about **the impossibility of making another person prove love by ceasing to be a person**.

Everyone is confronting a version of that problem.

Takumi cannot make Reira's desire mean whatever is operationally convenient for him.

Trapnest cannot treat Ren's body as an endlessly schedulable resource.

Reira discovers that even a singer may have to stop singing to reclaim herself.

Miu refuses a role she thinks would tempt her to demand monopoly.

Ren still loves Nana but begins to understand that love does not justify forcing himself upon her emotional world.

Hachi loves Nana and still cannot abandon a sick husband in front of her.

Nana understands all of this in principle. She can see that other people have priorities. She can see that she herself has a dream she will not surrender.

And still she wants the impossible proof:

> Sacrifice everything for me.  
> If I die, die with me.  
> Do not leave me behind.

The crucial difference from earlier volumes is that Nana now knows the wish is dangerous.

That self-knowledge does not cure her. It makes the tragedy more adult.

The child who was abandoned by a mother who says she could not sacrifice herself for Nana has become a young woman who longs for a lover willing to make total sacrifice. The manga lets the reader see the connection while withholding it from Nana herself.

At the same time, Nana's dream remains real. She cannot simply give it up in exchange for the absolute love she demands from others. This is why her contradiction cannot be solved by telling her to become less ambitious or less attached. The deeper task would be to accept a form of love in which both people remain partly unavailable to each other because both remain alive as separate selves.

Volume 20 ends before showing whether Nana and Ren can reach that form.

Instead it returns to the most dangerous alternative they ever imagined:

> if separation is unbearable, go together.

And beneath that fantasy lies the sentence that explains almost everything Nana has been doing:

> **「置き去りになんてされたくなかった」 — I didn't want to be left behind.**

### Questions carried into Volume 21

1. Will Ren's movement toward Nana become genuine repair, another repetition of mutual dependency, or something else?
2. Can Ren treat recovery as his own responsibility rather than proof of Nana's love?
3. What will Nana do with the self-knowledge that she wants impossible exclusivity?
4. Will Reira's withdrawal of labor create space for recovery or deepen institutional collapse?
5. Can Hachi remain a meaningful home to Nana while inhabiting a separate household with Takumi?
6. Will the series connect Nana's maternal abandonment to her conscious self-understanding, or keep the causal knowledge asymmetrical between reader and character?
7. What does "return" mean when natal home, romantic home, professional home, and chosen-family home no longer coincide?
8. What, exactly, is the Volume 20 ending foreshadowing? At this boundary, that must remain unanswered.
