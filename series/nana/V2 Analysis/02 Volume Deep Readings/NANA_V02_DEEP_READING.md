# 『NANA』Volume 2 Deep Dive
## Coincidence becomes infrastructure: Apartment 707, chosen home, artistic rebirth, and the first architecture of Nana and Hachi

> **Migration / Phase-2 provenance note:** This is the archival Markdown version of the original legacy analysis. The historical analytical body is preserved. During Phase 2, the governing Japanese EPUB was reopened and a deliberately limited set of load-bearing evidence IDs and verified spine-page locators was appended below. No printed page number has been inferred where it was not visibly present.

**Primary source:** 『NANA』Volume 2, original Japanese manga pages, read sequentially through the complete 197-page EPUB.

**Cumulative scope:** Volumes 1–2 only.

**Spoiler boundary:** Nothing from Volume 3 onward, *NANA 7.8*, the anime, or later retrospective knowledge is used to decide what these characters “really” mean.

**Terminology:** I continue calling 小松奈々 **Komatsu Nana** until Osaki Nana actually gives her the nickname **ハチ公 / Hachikō**. From that point, “Hachi” becomes analytically useful because Volume 2 itself establishes the nickname and its dog imagery.

---

# I. Position in the larger narrative

Volume 1 was a diptych.

It gave us two separate young women who had never met:

- Komatsu Nana, whose imagination tends to make romance provide narrative direction;
- Osaki Nana, whose independence and artistic identity protect her from being absorbed into someone else's life.

Both were negotiating the same broad problem:

> **How can I belong to someone without ceasing to belong to myself?**

Volume 2 answers neither question.

Instead, it does something more interesting:

> **It gives them somewhere to live together.**

The structural movement is enormous.

Volume 1 repeatedly made **Tokyo** the place men went:

- Asano goes to Tokyo.
- Shoji goes to Tokyo.
- Ren goes to Tokyo.

Those departures left women behind.

Volume 2 reverses the vector.

Komatsu Nana gets on the train herself.

Osaki Nana gets on the same train.

The women who spent Volume 1 responding to men leaving now become the people who move.

But Tokyo does not magically resolve the relationships that drew them toward it. Shoji is physically closer but still busy. Ren is physically somewhere in the same enormous city yet remains absent from Nana's life. Professional ambition, money, work schedules, privacy and emotional distance survive geographical relocation.

The volume therefore introduces a third possibility between:

> **staying behind**

and

> **following somebody.**

That possibility is:

> **building something else after you arrive.**

And the material form of that “something else” is Apartment **707**.

---

# II. The future enters before the friendship begins

Volume 2 opens by violating chronological innocence.

Before present-day Nana and Nana have become friends, future Komatsu Nana addresses Osaki Nana directly:

> 「ねえ ナナ  
> あたし達の出会いを覚えてる？」

“Hey, Nana. Do you remember how we met?”

She explains that she is the sort of person who believes strongly in fate and therefore still thinks their meeting must have been fate:

> 「これはやっぱり運命だと思う」

The line is affectionate, almost embarrassed:

> 「笑ってもいいよ」

“You can laugh if you want.”

The crucial formal choice is the image associated with this narration:

**Apartment 707 is empty.**

We have not even watched the women live there yet, and Yazawa has already shown the room as something from which life has receded.

This produces a stronger version of the temporal effect Volume 1 only began to establish.

The reader is being asked to experience the relationship simultaneously in two tenses:

**It is about to begin.**

and:

**Someone is already remembering it.**

That is *NANA*'s anticipatory nostalgia becoming a real narrative system rather than merely a melancholy tone.

Future Hachi does not say exactly what happened.

She does not tell us:

- where Nana is now;
- why she is speaking to her this way;
- what ended;
- who is alive or dead;
- what became impossible.

And we should not infer those answers.

What the volume establishes is narrower and more powerful:

> **The ordinary experiences we are about to watch will later become precious enough to be addressed across time.**

---

# III. The train: two migrations become one story

Komatsu Nana's movement to Tokyo begins exuberantly.

Shoji has passed his examination. She is finally able to make the move she has imagined. She leaves with luggage, excitement and the expectation that geographical proximity will materially transform her relationship.

Then the weather intervenes.

The train becomes trapped in a snowstorm.

And beside her sits a striking young woman carrying a guitar.

Osaki Nana.

The visual contrast is immediate.

Komatsu Nana looks at Osaki Nana almost as though she has encountered an inhabitant of another aesthetic world. Nana's dark clothing, makeup, punk styling and physical composure produce instant fascination.

Osaki Nana, meanwhile, encounters an extremely expressive young woman who begins talking.

And talking.

And talking.

The snow gives the encounter time.

The journey that should have been transitional space becomes their first shared environment.

## 1. The first important asymmetry

Komatsu Nana explains herself freely.

She talks about:

- Shoji;
- why she is moving;
- her life;
- her circumstances.

Osaki Nana reveals much less.

Future Hachi later recognizes this imbalance:

> 「あたしは自分の事ばかりしゃべって  
> ナナの話は少しも聞いてあげられなかったね」

She admits that she spent the journey talking about herself and did not really ask Nana about Nana.

But the retrospective narrator immediately complicates the self-criticism:

> 「もっともナナの事だから  
> 聞いてもはぐらかしていたとは思うけど」

Knowing Nana, she probably would have dodged the questions anyway.

This gives us the first relational asymmetry that will be worth tracking.

Komatsu Nana's defense against uncertainty is **expression**.

Osaki Nana's is **opacity**.

One fills silence.

The other controls what the silence contains.

---

# IV. Coincidence becomes “fate”

The key revelation is absurdly simple.

They are both named Nana.

Komatsu Nana marvels at the improbability:

> 「同い年の女が同じ電車で同じ時刻に上京する  
> こんな偶然なかなかないし」

Two women of the same age, moving to Tokyo on the same train at the same time.

Then:

> 「あたしもナナってゆーんだ」

“I'm Nana too.”

The coincidence is structurally perfect for Komatsu Nana because she is exactly the kind of person who turns coincidence into narrative significance.

But Volume 2 does something clever.

It does not ask us merely to laugh at her belief in fate.

The coincidence will become consequential precisely because the women **act on it later**.

This gives us the first refinement of the “fate” motif:

> Fate in *NANA* may begin as accident, but relationships acquire meaning because people build structures around accidents.

The train encounter by itself could have meant nothing.

Apartment 707 will turn it into history.

---

# V. Tokyo does not give Komatsu Nana the romance she imagined

Shoji meets Nana at the station.

He is genuinely happy to have her there.

Their relationship is real.

That matters because Volume 2 does **not** create Nana/Hachi's bond by retroactively making Shoji worthless.

Instead, it lets Komatsu Nana discover that wanting to live close to a boyfriend and knowing how to live *with* a boyfriend are different capacities.

At Shoji's small apartment she immediately begins:

- cooking;
- cleaning;
- organizing;
- imagining domestic routine.

Her internal fantasy accelerates rapidly:

> 「毎日こんな風に彼の身の周りのお世話をして暮らせたら  
> どんなに幸せかしら♡」

How wonderful it would be if she could spend every day taking care of him like this.

And then:

> 「あたしちょっと新妻みたい？」

“I feel a little like a newlywed wife.”

This is important evidence for Hachi's domestic imagination.

She does not merely desire:

> boyfriend.

She desires:

> **a recognizable household role through which affection becomes daily usefulness.**

She wants to feed.

Clean.

Care.

Wait.

Be needed.

Become part of another person's ordinary life.

That desire deserves to be treated seriously rather than dismissed as childish conservatism.

But Shoji's response exposes a problem inside it.

---

# VI. Shoji's surprisingly important boundary

Shoji tells her:

> 「おまえ そんな事しに東京来たんじゃねえだろ」

“You didn't come to Tokyo to do this.”

He reminds her of their agreement:

> 「一人立ち出来るような仕事見つけてしっかりやるって約束だろ？」

She was supposed to find work that would allow her to **一人立ち / hitoridachi**—stand independently.

Komatsu Nana is hurt:

> 「あたしは章司のためにやってあげたのに！」

“But I did it for you!”

Shoji replies that he can take care of his own things.

This exchange deserves more weight than it might initially receive.

Shoji is not demanding domestic service.

He is resisting it.

He does not say:

> If you love me, become useful around my apartment.

He says, effectively:

> **You came here to build your own life. Do that.**

That complicates any simple gender reading.

Komatsu Nana is the person trying to install herself into the conventional girlfriend/wife-caregiver role.

Shoji is the one insisting she establish economic independence.

## Care and self-worth

The emotional problem lies in Nana's interpretation.

She does not initially hear:

> Shoji does not need a maid.

She feels something closer to:

> Perhaps Shoji does not need *me*.

Her internal response becomes:

> 「ダメだな あたし…章司に嫌われちゃう」

“I’m no good… Shoji's going to hate me.”

This is an early sign of something that deserves longitudinal tracking:

> **Hachi has difficulty separating rejection of what she does from rejection of who she is.**

Domestic usefulness becomes emotionally attractive partly because it provides visible evidence that she belongs.

If someone eats the meal, needs the cleaning, waits for her, relies upon her—

then her place seems secure.

The weakness in this model is obvious:

> What happens when the other person is capable of taking care of himself?

---

# VII. The fantasy of cohabitation is easier than cohabitation itself

Komatsu Nana later admits something that sharply complicates her newlywed fantasy.

She tells Junko:

> 「彼氏と一緒に住むなんて やっぱりあたしには無理かも」

Maybe living with her boyfriend is actually too much for her.

The reason is not that she dislikes Shoji.

Their relationship began and almost immediately became long-distance.

She says:

> 「だからまだどっかぎこちないってゆーか  
> 2人きりだと緊張とかするし」

There is still awkwardness.

Being alone with him makes her nervous.

This is psychologically revealing.

Komatsu Nana is extremely comfortable imagining **the role** of girlfriend-wife.

She is less comfortable inhabiting the actual unstructured intimacy of two people alone together.

That distinction will matter.

She knows how romantic stories are supposed to look.

She is still learning what sustained intimacy feels like when nobody is performing the recognizable scene.

---

# VIII. Apartment 707: economics produces the encounter that fate could not

Komatsu Nana begins looking for an apartment.

She discovers an attractive old building and falls in love with Room **707**.

Then Osaki Nana appears.

The girl from the train.

Future Hachi later admits that even she—someone inclined toward dreams and fate—never imagined she would encounter again a woman about whom she knew virtually nothing except her name.

This is where coincidence stops being symbolic and becomes practical.

Both want the apartment.

Neither needs all of it.

And the rent is **¥70,000**.

Then comes one of Volume 2's least romantic and most consequential revelations:

> **「家賃が半分!!」**

Half the rent.

The emotional center of *NANA* begins with arithmetic.

That is exactly why it matters.

---

# IX. 707 is founded on both intimacy and privacy

The arrangement is not:

> two soulmates feel fate and immediately merge their lives.

It is negotiated.

Osaki Nana asks Komatsu Nana directly whether she can respect privacy:

> 「あんた ちゃんとプライバシー守れるタイプ？」

This is one of the most important early statements Nana makes about shared life.

Then she asks about putting **locks on the individual bedrooms**.

The apartment therefore begins with two simultaneous principles:

> **We will share a home.**

and:

> **You are not entitled to everything inside me.**

That is a remarkably useful foundational image for a series concerned with dependency and possession.

707 is not created through the abolition of boundaries.

It is created because boundaries make closeness possible.

This is already an alternative to the romantic fusion imagined by Komatsu Nana at Shoji's apartment.

With Shoji she imagines:

> everything together.

With Nana she agrees to:

> common living space + separate private rooms + separate locks.

And strangely, the latter immediately feels safer.

---

# X. The handshake

Once the agreement is made, Nana extends her hand.

> 「明日からよろしくね 奈々ちゃん」

“Starting tomorrow, let's get along, Nana-chan.”

Komatsu Nana shakes it.

And something disproportionate happens emotionally.

Future Hachi remembers:

> 「その時 何故か  
> あたしは少し泣きそうになったんだ」

“For some reason, I almost felt like crying.”

She cannot explain why.

Then:

> 「差し出されたナナの手は  
> 意外な程 温かくて  
> 胸まで熱くなったんだよ」

Nana's offered hand was unexpectedly warm, and the warmth seemed to reach Hachi's chest.

The most important analytical response is **not to explain this too quickly**.

The text explicitly preserves Komatsu Nana's inability to categorize the feeling.

It could contain:

- relief;
- attraction;
- recognition;
- excitement;
- gratitude;
- the beginning of friendship;
- the first sensation of chosen belonging;
- something romantically coded;
- several of these simultaneously.

The proper conclusion after Volume 2 is simply:

> **The formation of this household carries an emotional intensity for Hachi far exceeding mere rental convenience.**

That is established.

Its final category is not.

---

# XI. A home is built rather than discovered

The next stretch of Volume 2 is crucial because the manga spends time on things a less materially attentive romance might skip.

The women have to make 707 livable.

They need:

- furniture;
- household objects;
- food;
- heating;
- bathing;
- transportation;
- help moving;
- money;
- labor.

The apartment acquires personality because **their things begin accumulating inside it**.

707 does not become a symbolic “home” because the narrator tells us it is sacred.

It becomes one through activity.

## Their domestic competencies are complementary

Komatsu Nana gravitates toward:

- food;
- cooking;
- shopping;
- decorative domesticity;
- emotional atmosphere.

Osaki Nana is much more comfortable with:

- assembling;
- arranging;
- practical decisions;
- physical setup;
- handling logistics;
- setting boundaries.

Later, Hachi returns to discover that Nana has continued putting the apartment together and gotten the bath functioning.

Hachi reacts with enormous gratitude.

Nana basically tells her not to make such a fuss.

The relationship is therefore not simply:

> Hachi mothers Nana.

Nor:

> Nana protects helpless Hachi.

They begin producing different kinds of domestic labor.

That reciprocity will be important.

---

# XII. The strongest early comparison: Shoji's apartment versus 707

One of the most revealing retrospective lines in the volume is:

> 「ナナと暮らし始める事に  
> 不思議と不安はなかったんだよ」

Hachi says that, strangely, she felt **no anxiety** about beginning to live with Nana.

Again:

> 「それがどうしてかは  
> やっぱり上手く言えないけど」

She cannot quite say why.

Set this against the Shoji material.

With her boyfriend:

- she imagines perfect domestic life;
- but real cohabitation makes her nervous;
- being alone with him can feel awkward.

With this woman she has barely met:

- they establish boundaries;
- split the rent;
- shake hands;
- and Hachi feels unexpectedly safe.

This is one of Volume 2's most significant relational facts.

It does not prove that Nana/Hachi is romantic.

It does establish that **Hachi's lived experience of emotional safety does not align neatly with the relationship hierarchy she consciously believes should organize her life.**

That discrepancy deserves to remain open.

---

# XIII. Yasu: friendship as infrastructure

Yasu's arrival also broadens our understanding of Nana's social world.

He is visually imposing:

- bald head;
- sunglasses;
- suit;
- cigarette;
- adult professional presentation.

But his role in Nana's life is immediately practical.

He helps.

He transports.

He handles problems.

He possesses enough professional and legal-world competence that his mere presence makes the logistics around Nana easier.

This builds on Volume 1's contrast between Nobu's emotional loyalty and Yasu's structural care.

Yasu's care language is not:

> “I love you all so much.”

It is:

> **What needs doing?**

He represents something *NANA* seems increasingly interested in:

> affection translated into reliable infrastructure.

---

# XIV. Trapnest enters 707 as a commodity

One of Volume 2's strongest scenes begins almost comically.

Hachi is excited because a new **TRAPNEST** album is coming out.

She announces:

> 「あたし 大ファンなんだ！」

She is a huge fan.

For her, Trapnest exists as celebrity culture:

- CDs;
- posters;
- concerts;
- attractive members;
- fandom;
- personal preferences.

For Nana, Trapnest contains **Ren**.

The same image therefore exists in two radically different ontologies.

To Hachi:

> celebrity photograph.

To Nana:

> someone whose body she knows.

This is one of the first moments where the manga brings **public image and private personhood** into direct collision.

---

# XV. Hachi's fandom is wonderfully ordinary

Hachi tells Nana about the band.

She says that she likes the bassist, **Takumi**.

At this stage, this is simply a celebrity crush.

That distinction must remain intact.

She also admires the vocalist Reira enormously—her beauty and voice.

And then she discusses Ren.

Her judgment is striking precisely because it is so casual:

> 「確かに一番人気だけど  
> なんか無口で怖そうだから  
> あたしはちょっと苦手」

Yes, he is probably the most popular, but he seems quiet and scary, so she is not particularly fond of him.

Hachi is describing Nana's former lover according to a commercial persona.

And Nana has to listen.

There is something almost cruel in the innocence of it.

Nobody has done anything wrong.

That is the point.

Celebrity makes an intimate person available for interpretation by strangers.

---

# XVI. Nana hides knowledge inside fandom language

Nana responds by talking about Ren's appearance and Sid Vicious.

She notes the padlock and stylistic resemblance.

But she disguises extraordinarily intimate knowledge as aesthetic commentary.

This is classic Volume 2 Nana.

She does not lie by inventing another reality.

She allows the truth to appear in a form that does not disclose its emotional ownership.

This is another feature to add to her voice profile:

> **Nana often protects vulnerability not by silence alone, but by converting personal knowledge into impersonal wit.**

Hachi hears fashion commentary.

The reader sees history.

---

# XVII. Hachi's romantic interpretive machine misfires

Hachi observes Nana's relationship with Yasu and reaches an almost entirely wrong conclusion:

> Nana must secretly love Yasu.

Then she gives advice:

> 「意地ばっかり張ってると  
> 幸せが逃げちゃうよ？」

“If you keep being stubborn, happiness will run away from you.”

Nana responds dryly:

> 「ご忠告ありがとう」

“Thanks for the advice.”

The page then does something much smarter than simply making Hachi look silly.

After Hachi leaves, her advice remains conceptually present while Nana stares at **Ren**.

Hachi has diagnosed the wrong relationship.

But her general statement accidentally touches the real wound.

That gives us an excellent early model of Hachi's emotional intelligence:

> **She often perceives emotional shape more accurately than relational structure.**

She knows someone is refusing vulnerability.

She is simply wrong about who the vulnerability concerns.

---

# XVIII. Nana and Ren: love as absolute union

The resulting flashback gives us one of the most disturbing lines yet attached to their relationship.

Nana asks Ren:

> 「もし あたしが死んだら  
> 一緒に死んでくれる？」

“If I died, would you die with me?”

Ren responds:

> 「いいよ」

“Sure.”

This should not be reduced to “romantic goth dialogue.”

The scene establishes that their love has at least at some point been imagined through **absolute co-destruction**.

The fantasy is not:

> We will each retain our own lives while remaining committed.

It is:

> **If my existence ends, yours should end with it.**

That does not yet tell us everything about Nana/Ren.

It does tell us that their romantic imagination contains a desire for union so complete that separate survival can be framed as betrayal.

That is a very different model from the lockable bedrooms of 707.

---

# XIX. Nana's loneliness becomes textual fact

Volume 1 strongly suggested that Nana's independence had a defensive dimension.

Volume 2 moves one part of that from inference into direct evidence.

Nana remembers:

> 「あたし ずっと  
> 一人で 寂しかった」

“I was lonely, alone, for so long.”

Then:

> 「もう二度と  
> 独りぼっちは嫌だよ……」

“I never want to be completely alone again.”

This requires a major cumulative revision.

After Volume 1, we could responsibly say:

> Nana may fear abandonment.

After Volume 2, we can say much more strongly:

> **Osaki Nana explicitly experiences prolonged aloneness as painful and explicitly does not want to return to it.**

Her independence is still real.

Her artistic ambition is still real.

Her refusal to follow Ren is still real.

But we can no longer treat those things as evidence that she is psychologically comfortable with solitude.

Quite the opposite.

---

# XX. Independence versus aloneness

This may be the most important conceptual distinction Volume 2 adds to Nana's character.

Nana wants independence.

She does **not** want isolation.

Those are not the same thing.

In fact, her great problem may eventually prove to be how difficult she finds separating:

> **I can stand on my own**

from:

> **I can bear being left alone.**

She can do the first.

Volume 2 gives us evidence that she cannot easily do the second.

That makes her refusal to follow Ren more impressive and more painful simultaneously.

She is not choosing independence because attachment means little to her.

She is choosing it **despite attachment meaning an enormous amount**.

---

# XXI. Hachi chooses to go home to Nana

Later, Hachi is with Shoji.

Shoji is affectionate and delighted that they can finally see one another regularly.

He tells her it still almost feels like a dream.

This is exactly the romantic achievement Volume 1 built toward.

Yet Hachi refuses to stay with him that night.

Why?

Because Nana is alone in an almost empty apartment.

> 「あんな何もない部屋で 一人ぽっちじゃ  
> ナナが寂しいじゃん」

“If she's all alone in an empty room like that, Nana'll be lonely.”

This is fascinating because Shoji objects that Nana hardly seems like the type who would get lonely.

Hachi, without knowing Nana's private monologue, is actually right.

Nana **is** lonely.

The emotionally extravagant, supposedly naïve Hachi perceives something Shoji cannot see from Nana's presentation.

## 「惚れた」

The scene includes the teasing formulation:

> 「マジで惚れたか あの女に…」

Roughly:

> “Have you seriously fallen for that woman?”

**惚れる / horeru** is stronger and more romantically colored than neutral “like,” although it can certainly be used jokingly or figuratively.

We should neither ignore it nor canonize it.

The responsible reading is:

> **The manga itself has begun allowing romantic vocabulary to hover humorously around Hachi's attachment to Nana.**

That matters.

It is not proof that Hachi consciously understands herself as romantically attracted to Nana.

But a queer reading is no longer dependent entirely upon later readers imposing a framework from outside.

The text has begun generating the ambiguity.

---

# XXII. Future Hachi corrects present Hachi's gaze

Hachi returns to 707 and finds Nana asleep beside the stove.

Then future Hachi delivers one of the most important self-corrections so far:

> 「ナナは気ままな ノラ猫みたいで  
> 誇り高く 自由だけれど  
> 癒えない傷を負ってたよね」

Nana was like a capricious stray cat—proud and free, but carrying wounds that would not heal.

Then the crucial admission:

> 「能天気なあたしは  
> それさえ かっこいい事のように思ってた」

Carefree Hachi thought even those wounds seemed cool.

And:

> 「それが どれ程の痛みなのかも知らずに」

without understanding how much pain they actually represented.

This is enormously important for how *NANA* wants us to read its aesthetics.

Nana looks cool.

Her scars look cool.

Her punk clothing looks cool.

Her refusal to explain herself looks cool.

Her loneliness can look cool.

But future Hachi tells us directly:

> **Do not confuse the beauty of the image with the desirability of the suffering that produced it.**

That is a sophisticated warning from a manga so intensely invested in fashion and aesthetic charisma.

It does not renounce style.

It distinguishes style from pain.

---

# XXIII. The stray cat and the dog

This passage gives Osaki Nana an explicit animal metaphor:

> **ノラ猫 / nora-neko — stray cat**

Proud.

Free.

Hard to own.

Wounded.

Then, shortly afterward, Osaki Nana invents Komatsu Nana's enduring nickname.

She barks:

> 「ワン ワン」

and greets her:

> 「おはよー ハチ公」

“Morning, Hachikō.”

Then asks whether she wants to go for a walk.

Later another joke explicitly invokes:

> 「犬だけに」

“Because you're a dog.”

This is not subtle.

The animal pairing is being constructed inside the text itself.

## Cat and dog as provisional relational language

At Volume 2 only, we can responsibly say:

**Osaki Nana / stray cat**
- proud;
- self-directed;
- difficult to possess;
- outwardly independent;
- secretly lonely.

**Hachi / dog**
- social;
- eager;
- affectionate;
- food-oriented;
- companionship-seeking;
- emotionally transparent;
- quick to attach.

We should not turn this into amateur diagnosis.

But Yazawa has supplied a comic vocabulary for two very different modes of attachment.

And there is a poignant irony:

> The “stray cat” says she never wants to be alone again.

> The “dog” is constantly searching for somewhere and someone to belong to.

707 gives both animals a house.

Whether it becomes a home is the real question.

---

# XXIV. Why “Hachi” matters as a name

The nickname also solves a basic narrative problem.

Two protagonists are named Nana.

But the solution is not neutral.

Osaki Nana gets to remain **Nana**.

Komatsu Nana becomes **Hachi**.

That gives Osaki Nana a naming power inside their relationship.

“Hachi” is affectionate.

Teasing.

Slightly infantilizing.

Domestic.

And crucially, it is a name generated **inside 707** rather than inherited from Hachi's family or romance.

This is a small act of identity formation.

Komatsu Nana enters Tokyo under her family name and given name.

Inside the new home, she acquires another self.

---

# XXV. Work: Hachi begins constructing an adult Tokyo life

Hachi finds work at the vintage clothing store **SABRINA**.

This matters because it fulfills the promise Shoji reminded her of.

She is not merely living in Tokyo because her boyfriend is there.

She now has:

- her own job;
- her own rent obligation;
- her own room;
- her own housemate;
- her own routines.

That is meaningful autonomy.

It is also very Hachi that almost immediately she buys clothes before she has even received her pay.

> 「バイト料もらえるのまだ先なのに」

Her consumption habits have not suddenly become disciplined because she has gained employment.

Again, Yazawa's characterization is stronger because development does not mean personality replacement.

Hachi can become more independent while remaining impulsive.

---

# XXVI. “Cherishing” a boyfriend sounds easier than doing it

One of Hachi's coworkers tells her that a man who thinks about her as much as Shoji does is valuable and that she should treasure him.

Hachi thinks:

> Of course I know that.

But then asks herself:

> 「でも大事にってのは一体何をどうすればいいんだろう」

What exactly does “cherishing him” mean?

This is one of Volume 2's best questions.

Hachi knows the vocabulary of romance.

She knows:

- love;
- boyfriend;
- happiness;
- devotion;
- marriage fantasy.

But “cherishing” is a verb.

It requires behavior.

What does love ask you to **do** when:

- both people work;
- one studies;
- schedules conflict;
- domestic service is not wanted;
- attraction to other people does not magically disappear;
- proximity does not mean constant access?

Hachi does not yet know.

That is a far more interesting form of immaturity than merely calling her naïve.

---

# XXVII. 「章司がいないと生きてけない」

Hachi soon worries that noticing another man or thinking romantically at all may mean she is failing Shoji.

She responds with characteristic emotional maximalism:

> 「あたしは章司がいないと生きてけないもん！」

“I can't live without Shoji!”

This should be entered in the ledger as **character belief**, not objective truth.

The volume itself is already complicating it.

Just pages earlier:

- she declined to stay with Shoji;
- she returned home because Nana might be lonely;
- she feels remarkably secure in 707;
- she has begun constructing a job and social life of her own.

The line tells us a great deal about **how Hachi wants to understand her love**.

It does not establish how her life actually functions.

---

# XXVIII. Tokyo does not abolish distance

Hachi then has one of her first genuinely adult realizations about migration.

She notes that just because she has moved to Tokyo does not mean she can see Shoji whenever she wants.

Shoji will:

- attend university;
- work part-time.

Hachi also has:

- a job;
- obligations.

Then:

> 「やっぱり人生そう甘くはないな」

Life is not that easy after all.

And, more importantly:

> 「東京に出て来さえすれば  
> 欲しい物がなんでも手に入る気がしてたけど」

She had felt as though merely coming to Tokyo would put everything she wanted within reach.

But:

> 「この手につかんでるという実感があんましないや」

She does not actually feel as though she has what she wanted in her hand.

Then:

> 「なんでだろう」

“Why?”

That is one of the major developmental sentences of Volume 2.

Hachi has achieved the geographic goal.

The emotional result does not match the fantasy.

---

# XXIX. Desire has no terminal station

This revises the meaning of Tokyo.

In Volume 1, Tokyo was primarily the place that took people away.

In Volume 2, Tokyo becomes accessible.

But access does not equal possession.

Hachi discovers:

> **You can reach the city without reaching the life you imagined the city contained.**

This is a very adult conception of desire.

Milestones do not necessarily resolve the needs projected onto them.

Moving.

Dating.

Having sex.

Getting an apartment.

Getting a job.

Living near the person you love.

Each event can be real and valuable without delivering the total psychological completion imagined beforehand.

---

# XXX. The counterpoint: ordinary life with Nana actually feels good

Only a few pages after Hachi wonders why reaching Tokyo has not made her feel as though she possesses what she wanted, she takes a bath in 707 and thinks:

> 「ああ 幸せ♡」

“Ahh, happiness.”

Then:

> 「ナナといると 楽しい事ばっかりだ」

“Being with Nana, everything is fun.”

And:

> 「なんか すごく気持ちが上向きになれる」

Being with Nana seems to lift her emotionally.

She cannot fully explain why.

Their:

- tastes differ;
- personalities differ.

Yet:

> 「ウマが合うって こういう事を言うのかな」

“Is this what it means to just click with someone?”

This juxtaposition is too carefully placed to ignore.

Hachi's imagined happiness:

> Tokyo → Shoji → romantic completion.

Her experienced happiness:

> bath → shared apartment → Nana → ordinary day.

The manga is not saying Shoji is fake.

It is beginning to distinguish:

> **the happiness Hachi imagines she should want**

from:

> **the happiness her body and ordinary routines actually register.**

That distinction may become extremely important.

---

# XXXI. Nana becomes an artist in Hachi's eyes

Until this point Hachi knows Nana as:

- cool woman;
- housemate;
- train companion;
- someone with mysterious friends;
- someone carrying wounds she does not understand.

Then she notices the guitar.

She initially assumes Nana may want to become a guitarist.

Nana corrects her:

> 「あたしはギタリストじゃなくて  
> ボーカリストだから」

“I’m not a guitarist. I'm a vocalist.”

And:

> 「そのセリフは あたしの歌を聴いてから言ってよね」

“Say that after you've heard me sing.”

This is a major relational shift.

Hachi has admired Nana's **persona**.

Now she is about to encounter Nana's **vocation**.

Those are not the same thing.

---

# XXXII. Nobu arrives: BLAST enters the new home

Nobu's arrival literally imports Nana's earlier musical life into Apartment 707.

Nana greets him with characteristic aggression:

> 「ノブー！てめえ何しに来たー！」

The roughness is affectionate enough that Hachi can immediately see a different social history around Nana.

Nobu eats Hachi's cooking.

He brings belongings.

He has driven for hours.

And then he reveals something much more serious:

> 「家出して来やした」

He has run away from home.

Why?

Music.

---

# XXXIII. Nobu and the economics of artistic vocation

The story reveals that Nobu is the heir to a **旅館 / ryokan**, a traditional inn.

He has an economically secure path.

Nana gets furious precisely because she understands what that means.

She tells him:

> 「てめえは恵まれてんだよ！」

You're fortunate.

And:

> 「幸せな将来が保障されてんだよ！」

A happy/secure future is basically guaranteed for him.

Why would he throw it away?

This is important for Nana because it prevents us from turning her artistic ambition into simple romantic idealism.

She understands economic security.

She values it enough to be angry when someone discards it casually.

Nobu responds:

> 「そんなもん捨てても全然おしかねえや」

He does not care about losing it.

Because:

> 「おれはギターさえ弾けりゃ  
> それで幸せなんだよ」

“If I can play guitar, that's enough to make me happy.”

---

# XXXIV. Three emerging definitions of happiness

Volume 2 is quietly accumulating different versions of **幸せ / shiawase**.

For Hachi, happiness is initially imagined through:

> romantic domestic union.

Then experienced through:

> ordinary shared life with Nana.

For Nobu:

> guitar itself can be happiness.

For Nana:

we do not yet receive such a simple declarative formula.

But her life is already organized around the conflict between:

> Ren

and:

> music.

This makes “happiness” one of the most important terms to keep tracking.

Characters use the same word for radically different life architectures.

---

# XXXV. Yasu's response: art needs structure

Nana calls Yasu about Nobu.

Yasu does not respond as a sentimental dreamer.

He assesses reality.

Nobu is serious.

Nana wants a band more than a solo path.

Therefore:

> why not find new members in Tokyo?

He also endorses Nobu's new composition.

And more significantly, he tells Nana that if **she** sings it, he thinks they will be extraordinarily strong.

Yasu's role is again structural.

Nobu brings:

> desire.

Nana brings:

> voice.

Yasu brings:

> a viable next step.

The band is not resurrected through nostalgia alone.

Someone has to convert longing into organization.

---

# XXXVI. The dining table becomes a stage

The volume's ending is one of the strongest pieces of formal construction in the first two books.

Nobu plays his new song.

Hachi loves it.

Nana becomes visibly interested.

She says:

> 「歌ってみたい」

“I want to sing it.”

Then the apartment changes.

Not physically.

Perceptually.

Nana asks:

> 「今日の客は一人だけ？」

“Only one audience member tonight?”

Hachi is the audience.

Then:

> 「こんばんは  
> ブラストです  
> 東京ファーストライブにようこそ」

“Good evening. We're BLAST. Welcome to our first Tokyo live.”

This repeats the public BLAST language we saw in Volume 1.

But now the “venue” is home.

---

# XXXVII. Nana's performance voice

Then Nana says:

> 「忘れられない夜にしてあげる♡」

“I'll make it a night you won't forget.”

This is fascinating linguistically.

Everyday Nana frequently uses:

- contractions;
- blunt masculine-coded vocabulary;
- rough second-person forms;
- aggressive teasing.

But onstage she can deploy a deliberately seductive feminine register.

The **～てあげる♡** construction is almost playfully benefactive:

> I will do this *for you*.

So Volume 2 immediately complicates the idea that Nana simply “talks like a man.”

Her gender presentation is more authored than that.

She can choose from multiple registers.

Her roughness is one component of her femininity.

Her performance persona can deliberately intensify erotic/feminine address when she wants to control an audience.

That makes her voice significantly richer.

---

# XXXVIII. Hachi becomes captive to Nana's voice

Future Hachi tells us:

> 「その夜の事は  
> 本当に今でも忘れられない」

She still cannot forget that night.

The song does not even have finished lyrics.

Nana improvises nonsense English.

But Hachi describes the experience as though a spell is being cast.

Then comes the most important formulation:

> 「あたしは その声の虜になったんだよ」

“I became captive to that voice.”

**虜 / toriko** can mean captive, prisoner, thrall—someone completely fascinated or enchanted.

Again, this does not establish sexual desire.

But the emotional vocabulary is intense.

Hachi does not simply conclude:

> Nana is talented.

She experiences Nana's performance as **enchantment**.

---

# XXXIX. The final magic

Future Hachi completes the volume with an extraordinary domestic-performance transformation:

> 「食卓がステージに」

The dining table becomes a stage.

> 「携帯がマイクに」

The mobile phone becomes a microphone.

> 「三日月がスポットライトになる」

The crescent moon becomes a spotlight.

Then:

> 「あんな魔法を使えるのは  
> この世にナナしかいないって  
> あたしは今でも そう信じてる」

Even now, Hachi believes Nana is the only person in the world who can perform that kind of magic.

Notice what has happened formally.

At the beginning of the volume:

> 707 is empty and remembered.

At the end:

> 707 contains enough life that a table can become a stage.

The apartment has progressed from:

> **vacant space**

to:

> **shared dwelling**

to:

> **home**

to:

> **artistic venue.**

That is the central visual arc of Volume 2.

---

# XL. Osaki Nana: Volume 2 character update

Volume 2 changes our model of Nana substantially.

## Independence is no longer sufficient

Volume 1 gave us the compelling image of a young woman refusing to follow Ren to Tokyo merely as his girlfriend.

Volume 2 tells us what that independence costs.

She explicitly says:

> she has been lonely;

> she does not want to be alone again.

Her autonomy therefore cannot be understood as emotional detachment.

A better provisional formulation becomes:

> **Nana refuses dependency without having ceased to need attachment.**

That tension is much stronger.

## Her privacy is healthy and defensive at once

Nana insists on locks in 707.

There is no reason to pathologize that.

She barely knows Hachi.

Privacy is sensible.

But placed beside her broader emotional architecture, the locks become formally interesting.

She wants:

> someone in the house.

She also wants:

> control over access to her room.

This may become one of the series' most useful models of her relational problem:

> **closeness without invasion.**

Whether she can sustain that balance remains unresolved.

## Ren remains privately central

Ren has left her immediate life.

But Volume 2 demonstrates that he has not become psychologically peripheral.

He re-enters through:

- the Trapnest album;
- public photographs;
- the Sid Vicious comparison;
- the lotus imagery;
- memory;
- the death-pact conversation;
- Nana's silence.

The extraordinary irony is that fame makes Ren **more visible to everyone except Nana in the form she actually wants him**.

She can buy his CD.

See his face.

Read about him.

Hear strangers discuss him.

But those forms of access intensify absence rather than repair it.

## Her artistic self begins moving again

Volume 1 ended with Nana maintaining her musical identity after Ren's departure.

Volume 2 gives that identity new infrastructure.

Nobu arrives.

Yasu encourages.

A new song exists.

707 provides a temporary stage.

And Nana sings.

This matters because artistic rebirth does not occur by returning to Ren.

It occurs inside the life she has begun building separately.

---

# XLI. Hachi: Volume 2 character update

Volume 2 substantially complicates the easy reading of Hachi as “dependent.”

She absolutely possesses dependency-oriented language.

She says she cannot live without Shoji.

She dreams of caring for him every day.

She fears rejection.

She interprets male attention romantically.

But she also:

- moves only after gaining enough footing to do so;
- seeks her own apartment;
- accepts rent responsibility;
- finds work;
- develops a domestic life outside Shoji;
- builds new friendships.

So the more accurate model is:

> **Hachi wants attachment intensely, but she is also trying—sometimes clumsily—to create enough independent structure that attachment does not require total material dependence.**

That contradiction makes her much more interesting.

## Her actual gift may be emotional receptivity

Hachi's romantic interpretations can be comically wrong.

She thinks Nana loves Yasu.

She worries noticing another man means she is betraying Shoji.

She converts feelings rapidly into familiar scripts.

But she also correctly perceives things other people miss.

She senses Nana's loneliness.

She responds intensely to Nana's performance.

She makes ordinary spaces warm quickly.

She recognizes social atmosphere.

This suggests an important distinction:

> **Hachi is not always good at explaining emotions, but she may be unusually receptive to them.**

Interpretive accuracy and emotional sensitivity are not identical.

---

# XLII. Shoji: a more sympathetic and more difficult boyfriend than Hachi's fantasy requires

Volume 2 treats Shoji fairly.

He is:

- affectionate;
- sexually/romantically interested in Hachi;
- happy she moved;
- willing to support her;
- committed to his own study and work;
- unwilling to let her turn herself into his domestic servant.

His demand for her independence is arguably one of the healthiest boundaries in the volume.

But this creates a problem for Hachi because Shoji refuses to play the role her romantic imagination has prepared for him.

She expects being a good girlfriend to mean:

> caring for him materially.

He says:

> take care of yourself.

That forces her to confront a harder form of reciprocity.

---

# XLIII. Nobu: artistic vocation versus guaranteed future

Nobu becomes much more legible in Volume 2.

His defining statement is almost brutally simple:

> guitar is enough for happiness.

This makes him an important foil to Hachi.

Hachi wants happiness through relationship and home.

Nobu imagines happiness through artistic activity.

And Nana—who might appear closer to Nobu philosophically—is the one who objects most strongly to his abandonment of material security.

That complexity is excellent.

The artists themselves are not uniformly romantic about sacrifice.

---

# XLIV. Yasu: the adult organizer

Yasu increasingly looks like the person who translates feeling into feasible action.

He does not suppress the others' ambitions.

He makes them administratively survivable.

That is a distinct form of masculine care within the cast.

At this point his authority comes from:

- composure;
- practical knowledge;
- professional competence;
- emotional steadiness.

Not domination.

---

# XLV. Nana and Hachi: what has actually been established?

This deserves careful separation from fandom categories.

By the end of Volume 2, Nana/Hachi is no longer merely:

> two roommates.

Their bond has accumulated:

- extraordinary coincidence;
- direct future-address narration;
- emotional warmth;
- domestic partnership;
- practical interdependence;
- Hachi's unexpected sense of safety;
- Hachi prioritizing Nana's loneliness over spending the night with Shoji;
- joking use of **惚れた**;
- future Hachi describing herself as **虜** to Nana's voice;
- intense aesthetic admiration;
- the transformation of shared domestic space into private performance space.

That is a lot.

## Is the relationship romantic yet?

**Not textually established.**

Hachi remains actively attracted to Shoji.

She speaks of Shoji as her boyfriend and desired partner.

She shows no explicit sexual desire toward Nana.

Neither woman names their relationship romantically.

A large amount of what we see is entirely compatible with intense female friendship and chosen family.

But the inverse overcorrection would also be methodologically wrong.

It would now be inadequate to say:

> “There's nothing queer or romance-adjacent here; they're just friends.”

Volume 2 itself deliberately uses emotional and linguistic material overlapping with romance.

The most defensible formulation is:

> **By Volume 2, Nana/Hachi occupies an emotionally intensified and formally privileged territory that overlaps with romantic grammar without being categorically named as romance.**

Or more simply:

> **A queer reading has become textually reasonable, but not textually compulsory.**

That is exactly the kind of ambiguity the method should preserve.

---

# XLVI. The most important relational comparison

The contrast is not:

> Hachi loves Nana instead of Shoji.

Volume 2 does not establish that.

The contrast is more interesting:

### With Shoji

Hachi knows the category:

> boyfriend.

But she does not yet know how to inhabit it naturally.

She is anxious.

She performs domesticity.

She worries about whether she is loving correctly.

She fears rejection.

### With Nana

Hachi does not know the category beyond roommate/new friend.

But her behavior feels unusually easy.

She reports:

- no anxiety;
- emotional uplift;
- inexplicable warmth;
- fun;
- the sensation that they simply fit.

This yields a foundational *NANA* question:

> **What happens when the socially legible relationship is psychologically difficult, while the relationship without an obvious social script feels most like home?**

Volume 2 has not answered it.

It has unmistakably begun asking.

---

# XLVII. Japanese voice and language

## Komatsu Nana / Hachi

Volume 2 makes the split between **present Hachi** and **future narrating Hachi** much clearer.

### Present Hachi

Her speech remains:

- effusive;
- colloquial;
- highly feminine;
- full of exclamation;
- romantically hyperbolic;
- rapidly associative.

She produces statements like:

> 「章司がいないと生きてけないもん！」

Her feeling is enormous because the sentence needs feeling to be enormous.

### Future Hachi

The retrospective voice becomes much quieter.

It uses direct address:

> 「ねえ ナナ」

and recurring temporal language:

> 「あの頃」  
> “back then”

> 「今でも」  
> “even now”

> 「もう一度」  
> “once more”

> 「忘れられない」  
> “cannot forget”

The future narrator can also criticize her younger self.

That is crucial.

She knows she aestheticized Nana's pain.

She knows she talked too much on the train.

She knows there were things she did not understand.

This is not omniscience.

It is accumulated experience.

## Osaki Nana

Her everyday register remains sharply differentiated.

She uses:

- rough contractions;
- blunt imperatives;
- masculine-coded lexical forms;
- confrontational joking;
- very little deferential softness among intimates.

But Volume 2 introduces a crucial counter-register.

Onstage:

> 「忘れられない夜にしてあげる♡」

The heart and **～てあげる** produce deliberately seductive performance femininity.

This tells us that Nana's linguistic roughness is not an inability to perform femininity.

It is a choice within a wider repertoire.

## Nana's evasive register

She also repeatedly allows people to possess incomplete interpretations.

Hachi thinks she loves Yasu.

Nana does not rush to correct the emotional architecture by revealing Ren.

Hachi discusses Ren as celebrity.

Nana disguises knowledge as aesthetic commentary.

So one of Nana's developing linguistic strategies is:

> **truthful speech whose context is withheld.**

That is more sophisticated than simple secrecy.

## Nobu

Nobu sounds emotionally transparent by comparison.

His declaration that guitar is enough for happiness is direct and unarmored.

He does not construct complicated theories to justify desire.

He wants music.

He says so.

## Yasu

Yasu's speech is comparatively measured and solution-oriented.

His language repeatedly converts emotional emergency into:

> what is possible next?

His voice therefore carries authority without needing Nana's aggression or Nobu's exuberance.

---

# XLVIII. Names and address as relationship evidence

Volume 2 gives us several meaningful naming changes.

First:

> two strangers discover both are **Nana**.

Then Osaki Nana uses:

> 「奈々ちゃん」

when formally beginning their cohabitation.

Finally she invents:

> **ハチ公**

The movement is:

> shared given name → affectionate differentiation → private nickname.

That is relational development encoded linguistically.

And importantly, the nickname begins in domestic comedy.

“Hachi” is not initially a grand symbolic title.

Its symbolic potential grows out of an ordinary joke.

That is very characteristic of how *NANA* seems to generate motifs.

---

# XLIX. Visual/formal analysis: empty space → inhabited space → transformed space

Volume 2 has an unusually clear visual architecture.

## 1. Empty 707

The retrospective opening shows the apartment emptied of lived presence.

That immediately places absence around a relationship that has not yet begun.

## 2. The river beside the new home

Future Hachi remembers standing with Nana beside the river:

> watching light on the water;

> hearing the melody Nana used to hum.

She asks:

> let me hear it once more.

The exterior geography of the apartment is therefore memorialized almost immediately.

Home extends beyond walls.

The neighborhood itself enters memory.

## 3. The handshake

A practical rental agreement receives emotional visual emphasis through touch.

The body knows something before Hachi's narration can name it.

## 4. Furnishing

Objects accumulate.

Empty floor becomes shared life.

The visual density of 707 increases as relationship density increases.

## 5. The Trapnest display

Ren appears first as a mass-produced image.

The composition makes the discrepancy between:

> public idol

and:

> Nana's private remembered lover

visually unavoidable.

## 6. Nana alone

After Hachi's mistaken Yasu advice, Nana remains with Ren's image.

The page allows Hachi's wrong interpretation to hang over the correct emotional object.

That is visual irony rather than expository correction.

## 7. The final live

The apartment does not change physically.

Yazawa changes its **meaning**.

The dining table becomes stage.

The phone becomes microphone.

The moon becomes spotlight.

Domestic space and artistic space cease being opposites.

---

# L. 707 as the first true third protagonist

It may be premature to call the apartment a “character,” but it is already more than setting.

707 creates possibilities neither woman possessed separately.

For Hachi, it provides:

- independence from Shoji;
- companionship;
- domestic expression;
- social space.

For Nana, it provides:

- privacy;
- companionship;
- somewhere to receive Nobu;
- somewhere to sing;
- a Tokyo existence not defined through Ren.

And importantly:

> **neither woman owns the other person's place in it.**

They are co-tenants.

That material equality is significant.

Their intimacy begins horizontally.

---

# LI. Material and social adulthood

Volume 2 makes young adulthood extraordinarily concrete.

## Rent

707 exists because ¥70,000 divided by two becomes manageable.

Emotional history is enabled by economics.

## Locks

Privacy is physically installed.

The story refuses to equate closeness with unrestricted access.

## Employment

Hachi needs work because being in Tokyo requires money.

Her job is part of making romance materially possible without becoming Shoji's dependent.

## Work and study schedules

Shoji cannot be constantly available simply because he lives nearby.

Hachi learns that adult intimacy has to fit around obligations.

## Consumption

Hachi buys clothes before payday.

Economic independence is not the same thing as financial maturity.

## Nobu's inheritance

Artistic vocation becomes explicitly comparable against:

- property;
- family expectation;
- guaranteed future.

Music has opportunity cost.

## Band formation

A dream requires:

- people;
- equipment;
- songs;
- transport;
- rooms;
- social networks.

The manga repeatedly drags emotion back into infrastructure.

That is one of its great strengths.

---

# LII. Gender: domesticity is not the same thing as submission

Hachi's desire to cook for Shoji is genuine.

The manga does not ridicule her for wanting domestic life.

But it does challenge her assumption that domestic service is automatically what love requires.

Shoji's response is essentially:

> build your own life first.

At the same time, 707 allows Hachi to express domesticity without subordinating herself to a boyfriend.

She cooks because:

> cooking is something she does well and enjoys doing for people.

Nana contributes other forms of labor.

The household therefore offers an early alternative model:

> **domesticity without immediate patriarchal dependency.**

That does not make 707 utopian.

It simply makes Hachi's domestic desire more complicated than “traditional girl wants husband.”

---

# LIII. Music and gendered self-authorship

Nana's relationship to art also becomes more interesting through the women's home.

Her singing is not performed on a prestigious stage.

She performs:

- in her apartment;
- for another woman;
- using a phone as microphone;
- on a dining table.

Yet Hachi remembers it as magic.

This suggests something important about artistic legitimacy in *NANA*:

> **Scale and institutional recognition are not prerequisites for artistic truth.**

Ren has:

- record stores;
- posters;
- celebrity.

Nana has:

- one room;
- one song without lyrics;
- one listener.

The volume gives the smaller performance enormous subjective power.

---

# LIV. Ethical and psychological question: when does care become identity?

Hachi says she did the household work “for Shoji.”

But when Shoji refuses the service, she experiences personal rejection.

That suggests her care is not purely altruistic.

It also gives her:

- role;
- reassurance;
- usefulness.

This does not make the care insincere.

It means care can serve two people psychologically:

> the recipient receives help;

> the caregiver receives proof of belonging.

That distinction will be worth watching carefully.

---

# LV. Ethical and psychological question: is independence always healthy?

Nana's refusal to follow Ren was admirable in many respects.

Volume 2 now asks whether independence can also become armor.

The answer is not yet clear.

She does healthy things:

- maintains privacy;
- pursues work/art;
- refuses total dependence.

She also:

- hides vulnerability;
- lets misunderstandings stand;
- carries intense loneliness alone.

The question becomes:

> **At what point does refusing dependency become refusing to ask for care?**

Still unresolved.

---

# LVI. Ethical and psychological question: idealizing wounded people

Future Hachi explicitly condemns one of her younger self's habits.

She thought Nana's wounds were cool.

That deserves to become a methodological warning for us as readers too.

Nana is exquisitely designed.

Her pain contributes to her charisma.

The manga knows this.

And then it tells us:

> Charisma does not make pain good.

That is a sophisticated self-awareness for a work whose visual identity makes wounded beautiful people intensely attractive.

---

# LVII. Narration, time and memory

Volume 2 substantially strengthens the hypothesis that retrospective narration is one of *NANA*'s central formal mechanisms.

Future Hachi repeatedly addresses Nana.

The major forms include:

> **「ねえ ナナ」** — “Hey, Nana…”

> **「あの頃」** — “back then…”

> **「もう一度」** — “once more…”

> **「今でも」** — “even now…”

> **「忘れられない」** — “I can't forget…”

This creates a paradoxical reading experience.

When present Hachi thinks:

> this is fun,

future Hachi's voice quietly tells us:

> remember this.

When Nana sings at the dining table, the scene is funny and spontaneous.

Future Hachi tells us it became unforgettable.

Ordinariness is therefore being archived while it happens.

# LVIII. Anticipatory nostalgia is now demonstrable

After Volume 1 I described *NANA* as possessing anticipatory nostalgia.

Volume 2 gives enough formal evidence to strengthen that claim considerably.

The effect is generated through the combination of:

- retrospective direct address;
- empty 707 imagery;
- river and reflected light;
- past-tense emotional correction;
- requests to hear an old melody again;
- “even now” formulations;
- domestic events later declared unforgettable.

The sadness does not require a tragedy to occur on the current page.

It comes from a temporal fact:

> **The narrator already lives in a world where this present has become irrecoverable past.**

That is much more precise than merely saying the manga “feels nostalgic.”

Hachi remains romantically attached to Shoji.

Nana sings Nobu's new song in 707 for Hachi.

Future Hachi says she became **虜 / captivated, enthralled** by Nana's voice.

Future Hachi still believes Nana alone could turn the ordinary apartment into that kind of magical performance space.

## B — strongly established

707 is already becoming emotionally more than shared cheap housing.

Nana's independence coexists with a powerful need for attachment and fear of solitude.

Hachi feels a form of interpersonal ease with Nana that differs markedly from her awkwardness around cohabitation with Shoji.

Hachi habitually understands unfamiliar emotional structures through romantic templates and can therefore misidentify the relationship involved.

The Trapnest scenes establish a meaningful public/private split between celebrity representation and personal intimacy.

Nana's artistry is becoming central to Hachi's understanding of her, not merely her appearance or attitude.

## C — plausible inference

Nana's lotus imagery/tattoo is emotionally connected to Ren, although Hachi does not yet fully understand it.

Nana's pride and refusal to disclose Ren are partly mechanisms for containing vulnerability.

Nana/Hachi is developing into a relationship whose narrative importance exceeds ordinary roommate convenience.

707 will remain an important site of identity and memory; the retrospective narration already makes that very difficult to doubt, though its ultimate meaning is unknown.

## D — plausible inference

Hachi's fascination with Nana may already contain romantic or erotic potential.

Evidence supporting that inference includes:

- inexplicable emotional heat at their handshake;
- unusually effortless cohabitation;
- prioritization of Nana's loneliness over staying with Shoji;
- Shoji's **惚れた** joke;
- Hachi becoming **虜** of Nana's voice;
- the visual and narrative centrality granted to their pairing.

But no explicit sexual desire or self-recognized romantic attraction has been established.

Nana's insistence upon privacy may partly function as emotional self-protection as well as ordinary sensible boundary-setting.

The home Hachi is beginning to build with Nana may satisfy needs she had previously assumed could only be fulfilled through romance, but Volume 2 cannot yet tell us how conscious or durable that is.

# LXIV. Volume thesis

Volume 2 is fundamentally about **coincidence becoming infrastructure**.

Two women happen to sit beside one another on a delayed train.

That is coincidence.

They happen to share a name.

That is coincidence.

They happen to want the same apartment.

That is coincidence.

But then they:

- split the rent;
- establish privacy;
- install locks;
- move their belongings in;
- cook;
- assemble furniture;
- invite friends over;
- wait for one another;
- return to one another;
- and make music in the living room.

That is no longer coincidence.

That is a life being built.

Komatsu Nana reaches Tokyo believing that physical proximity to Shoji will finally let her grasp the happiness she has imagined. Instead she discovers that love must coexist with university, work, independence and the uncomfortable question of what “cherishing” another person actually requires.

Osaki Nana reaches Tokyo carrying a different contradiction. She refused to follow Ren because she needed a life that remained hers, but Volume 2 reveals that the freedom she fought to protect coexists with an almost unbearable fear of being alone.

Apartment 707 places those contradictions beside each other.

And crucially, the apartment is founded neither on romantic fusion nor heroic independence.

It is founded on:

> **half the rent and separate locks.**

That may be the volume's most elegant image of relational possibility.

You can share.

You can remain separate.

You can have a key to the home without having a key to every room.

Then, inside that bounded shared life, something extraordinary happens.

The dining table becomes a stage.

The phone becomes a microphone.

The moon becomes a spotlight.

Hachi becomes Nana's audience.

And what began as a housing arrangement becomes the setting of a memory future Hachi still calls magic.

So the central question carried forward from Volume 1 has changed.

Volume 1 asked:

> **How can I belong to someone without ceasing to belong to myself?**

Volume 2 offers the beginning of an answer:

> **Perhaps belonging is not something one person grants another. Perhaps it can be built—room by room, boundary by boundary, meal by meal, song by song—until two independent lives have created a place that neither could have made alone.**

Whether 707 can sustain that promise is entirely unresolved.

But for the first time in *NANA*, the possibility exists.

And that is why Volume 2's deepest event is not that Nana and Nana meet.

It is that, after meeting, **they come home to the same place.**
