# 『NANA』Volume 3 Deep Dive
## The relationship that feels like home, the romance that is supposed to become home, and adulthood as the collision between feeling and structure

During Phase 2, the governing Japanese EPUB was reopened and a deliberately limited set of load-bearing evidence IDs and verified spine-page locators was appended below. No printed page number has been inferred where it was not visibly present.

**Primary source:** 『NANA』Volume 3, original Japanese manga pages, read sequentially through Chapters 5–8.

**Cumulative scope:** Volumes 1–3 only.

**Spoiler boundary:** Nothing from Volume 4 onward, the anime, *NANA 7.8*, or later series knowledge is used to settle unresolved questions.

**Source note:** The supplied image EPUB contains a scan-site advertisement inserted between two sequential narrative pages. It is excluded as a non-manga artifact. The two-page **「淳子の部屋」** at the end is treated separately as metatextual bonus material rather than equal-weight narrative evidence.

# I. Executive thesis

Volume 2 built a home.

Volume 3 asks whether the relationships surrounding that home are actually capable of surviving adult life.

Its deepest contrast is no longer simply between the two Nanas. It is between two forms of belonging:

> **the relationship Hachi consciously believes should become her home**

and

> **the relationship that increasingly feels like home before she knows what to call it.**

The first is Shoji.

He is Hachi's boyfriend. She loves him. She moved to Tokyo partly because of him. She thinks about marriage, bridal clothes, money, a future house, and eventually living together.

But their relationship is increasingly forced to coexist with:

- university;
- work;
- conflicting schedules;
- money;
- emotional exhaustion;
- other friendships;
- sexual attraction to other people;
- and the fact that loving someone does not automatically tell you how to treat them.

The second relationship is Nana.

Hachi does not classify Nana as a romantic partner. Yet Volume 3 makes it impossible to dismiss the romantic register as merely something readers project onto their friendship. Future Hachi herself says her admiration for Nana was **「かなり恋に近いもの」**—something “quite close to romantic love.” She calls Nana **「とても幸福な初恋みたい」**—“like a very happy first love.” And she admits that she often thought:

> **「もしもナナが男だったら  
> 一世一代の恋が出来るのに」**

> If Nana were a man, I could have the love of a lifetime.

The important thing is the qualification.

Hachi does not simply say:

> I was in love with Nana.

She repeatedly constructs the feeling as:

> close to love;  
> like first love;  
> the love of a lifetime if Nana were male.

Volume 3 therefore gives us something more interesting than a hidden canonical romance or a platonic friendship onto which audiences can freely project romance.

It gives us a woman who recognizes the **emotional shape of romantic love** in another woman while simultaneously treating gender as the boundary that prevents her from placing that feeling completely inside the category of **恋 / koi**.

At the same time, the volume turns Apartment 707 into something even more important.

When Nana says:

> **「あたしには故郷などない」**  
> “I don't have a hometown.”

future Hachi answers across time:

> **「ナナは今でも自分の故郷が  
> どこにもないと思ってる？」**

> Nana, do you still think you have no hometown?

and reminds her that the table and chairs beside the window remain where they were.

So Volume 3's central movement can be summarized this way:

> **Hachi's official romance begins losing its capacity to feel like secure belonging at exactly the moment her supposedly non-romantic relationship with Nana becomes explicitly associated with first love, home, artistic awakening, and emotional survival.**

The manga does not yet resolve that contradiction.

It makes the contradiction impossible to ignore.

---

# II. Chapter 5 — creative desire acquires a body

The volume begins by puncturing the magical ending of Volume 2.

Nana and Nobu's impromptu “Tokyo first live” was emotionally transformative for Hachi.

It was also extremely loud.

The residents complain.

This is a small joke with substantial thematic value.

Volume 2 ended by transforming:

- dining table → stage;
- mobile phone → microphone;
- moon → spotlight.

Volume 3 immediately says:

> **Yes—but the stage is still inside an apartment building where other people are trying to sleep.**

That is almost a miniature version of everything Volume 3 will do.

Dreams do not disappear.

They acquire neighbors.

Bills.

Rules.

Schedules.

Consequences.

---

# III. The bed scene: Volume 3 deliberately introduces a sexual possibility only to leave it unresolved

After Hachi's fear of ghosts and the apartment disturbance, Hachi and Nana end up sleeping together.

The scene is intimate without being solemn.

Hachi says she is too **ドキドキ / dokidoki** to sleep.

Nana initially thinks she means fear.

Hachi says:

> **「違うよ  
> そーゆードキドキじゃなくて！」**

Not that kind of excitement.

Nana then makes the crude joke:

> **「期待させて悪いけど  
> あたしは女を犯す趣味はないから」**

Very roughly:

> Sorry to get your hopes up, but I don't have a thing for assaulting women.

**犯す / okasu** is deliberately harsh language. Nana is joking, but the joke only works because she has explicitly shifted their physical proximity into a sexual frame.

Hachi denies that this is the meaning of her excitement.

That denial matters.

But so does Nana having introduced the possibility at all.

The disciplined reading is therefore not:

> Hachi is secretly sexually aroused by Nana.

The text explicitly denies that reading of this particular **ドキドキ**.

But neither can we say:

> The manga never sexualizes their closeness.

It just did.

---

# IV. 「こんなドキドキ」 — Hachi discovers excitement that is not romance

The next page performs an elegant semantic transformation.

Hachi makes a homemade flyer:

> **ドラムとベース募集中!!**

BLAST is recruiting a drummer and bassist.

Future/present narration then says:

> **「こんなドキドキは  
> 生まれて初めてかもしれない」**

“This kind of excitement may be a first for me.”

The repetition of **ドキドキ** matters.

Hachi has spent much of her emotional life treating excitement as something produced by men:

- crush;
- flirtation;
- boyfriend;
- romantic anticipation.

Now the same embodied vocabulary attaches itself to:

> **helping another person create something.**

That is developmental.

Hachi is beginning to discover forms of desire that are neither purely romantic nor purely economic:

- participation;
- creative anticipation;
- fandom;
- communal purpose.

Her emotional life is becoming larger than romance even while she continues consciously defining herself through romance.

---

# V. Hachi becomes a producer before she knows she is doing it

Hachi is not a musician.

She cannot join BLAST by playing tambourine simply because she wants to.

But she can:

- make the recruitment flyer;
- find candidates;
- advertise;
- encourage;
- bring social energy;
- become an audience;
- turn the band's ambition into something socially contagious.

This is an important complication of her apparent frivolity.

Hachi's tendency to get overexcited is often genuinely dysfunctional.

It is also productive.

When Nobu explains that Nana's real goal is essentially:

> **to make a living by singing,**

Hachi immediately begins imagining:

> new members → performances → audience recognition → scouts → success.

The fantasy is naïve.

But it contains an important strength:

> **Hachi is extremely good at believing in another person's future before the infrastructure exists.**

Nana has ambition.

Nobu has music.

Yasu will bring stability.

Hachi brings **belief as social momentum**.

She is becoming Nana's first Tokyo fan in the fullest sense.

---

# VI. Shin: a person cannot be read from paper

The new bassist arrives through Hachi's flyer.

His résumé initially says he is eighteen.

Nana's reaction to paperwork is revealing:

> **「こんな紙切れ一枚で  
> 人間の何が分かるっつーんだよ」**

What can you know about a human being from one little piece of paper?

She wants to see:

> **やる気と実力**  
> motivation and ability.

That is a recognizably Nana criterion.

She evaluates through:

- body;
- performance;
- sound;
- commitment.

Not credentials.

And the volume almost immediately vindicates the skepticism.

The résumé itself is false.

Shin is not eighteen.

He is **fifteen**.

So the joke is structurally precise:

> the paper fails to tell Nana who Shin is both artistically and biographically.

---

# VII. Shin and Ren: the past enters the rebuilt BLAST from inside its newest member

Shin turns out to be talented enough that Nana and Nobu are genuinely startled.

Then Nana asks whom he admires.

His answer:

> **「本城 蓮！」**

Ren.

This is an excellent complication.

Shin is not recruited because Nana is consciously trying to manufacture a replacement Ren.

He arrives independently carrying Ren's influence inside his playing.

Ren has become famous enough that a fifteen-year-old bassist in Tokyo can model himself after him.

So the man Nana refused to follow has nevertheless become part of the musical vocabulary through which her own new band is rebuilt.

Nana responds:

> **「レンを越えてね シン」**

“Surpass Ren, Shin.”

This is not emotional closure.

It is artistic conversion.

Ren is no longer merely:

> lost lover.

He is also:

> benchmark.

The new BLAST therefore begins with a productive contradiction:

> **Nana is building an artistic future without Ren, but she has not built an artistic imagination in which Ren no longer matters.**

---

# VIII. The rebirth of BLAST is not nostalgia

That distinction matters.

The new lineup is not the old band magically restored.

Ren is absent.

Shin is new.

Tokyo is new.

Their circumstances are new.

Yasu has his own professional decisions to make.

The music is therefore not restoration.

It is continuation through alteration.

That is one of the first important versions of *NANA*'s growing question about memory:

> **How do you preserve something without merely reproducing its previous form?**

BLAST will still be BLAST.

But the meaning of being BLAST has changed.

---

# IX. Nana kisses Hachi—and Hachi categorizes the kiss through gender

After Shin joins, Nana acknowledges Hachi's role.

She kisses her.

> **「サンキュー ハチ公♡」**

Hachi immediately calls back to the bed joke:

> **「女は犯さないんじゃなかったの？」**

Didn't you say you weren't into assaulting women?

Nana dismisses it:

> **「なんだよ  
> キスぐらいで」**

“What? It's just a kiss.”

Then Hachi mentally apologizes to Shoji:

> she has kissed someone other than him;

> **but the other person is a woman**, so surely he can overlook it.

Again, the gender classification is doing work.

Hachi's logic is not:

> kissing Nana means nothing.

It is closer to:

> this would normally enter the category of infidelity, **except that Nana is female**.

That is substantially more interesting.

---

# X. Volume 3 changes the evidentiary status of the Nana/Hachi queer reading

Then the manga stops leaving the question entirely to inference.

Future Hachi tells us:

> **「今だから正直に打ち明けると  
> あたしのナナへの憧れは  
> かなり恋に近いものだったと思います」**

The key components are:

**憧れ / akogare** — admiration, longing, aspiration.

**恋に近い / koi ni chikai** — close to romantic love.

**だったと思います / datta to omoimasu** — “I think it was.”

The language is reflective and qualified.

Future Hachi is doing retrospective classification.

Younger Hachi could not name the feeling cleanly.

Older Hachi can get closer—but still does not erase ambiguity.

Then, over an image of the two women sleeping peacefully together, she says Nana gave her an exceptionally sweet dream in a reality where she could no longer simply depend on other people.

And:

> **「とても幸福な初恋みたいだったよ」**

“It was like a very happy first love.”

At this point, saying that Nana/Hachi contains no textual romantic coding is simply no longer defensible.

The text itself has supplied it.

But saying:

> Hachi explicitly identifies Nana as her lover

would also be false.

The qualification is part of the characterization.

---

# XI. Why the qualification matters

Hachi possesses a very powerful tendency to understand life through socially available categories.

She knows:

- boyfriend;
- girlfriend;
- cheating;
- bride;
- husband;
- marriage;
- domestic life;
- first love.

What she does **not** seem to possess is a comfortable ready-made category for:

> the woman whose presence resembles first love and with whom she feels safer than she does inside conventional romance.

That problem becomes explicit later in the volume.

So the question after Volume 3 is no longer:

> Is it reasonable for readers to notice romantic potential between Nana and Hachi?

Yes. The manga notices it.

The better question is:

> **What does Hachi do with an attachment whose emotional content resembles 恋 when her conceptual model of 恋 presumes a man?**

That question remains open.

---

# XII. Shoji and Hachi: the problem is not yet absence of love

Meanwhile, Hachi's official romantic relationship starts deteriorating.

One of the strengths of Volume 3 is that it does not initially make either party ridiculous enough to provide an easy explanation.

Shoji is busy.

He:

- attends school;
- works;
- saves;
- invests in equipment for his future.

Hachi is also increasingly busy.

She:

- works;
- manages her apartment;
- helps BLAST;
- develops a life in Tokyo independent of Shoji.

The relationship therefore encounters a problem Hachi's fantasies rarely accounted for:

> **Two adults can love one another and still repeatedly fail to be available at the same time.**

---

# XIII. The forgotten message and Hachi's capacity for self-correction

Hachi becomes angry that Shoji cannot accompany her.

Shoji asks why he should always have to reorganize his life around her schedule.

Then she realizes:

> she had asked him to call;

> he had responded;

> she herself had forgotten.

This is one of the places where Hachi deserves more credit than the “selfish ditz” reading gives her.

Once evidence confronts her, she is capable of recognizing:

> **I was being unfair.**

Her weakness is often not an inability to care.

It is that strong immediate emotion reorganizes her perspective so quickly that other people's constraints temporarily disappear from view.

She can correct afterward.

The cost is that relationships sometimes get damaged before the correction arrives.

---

# XIV. The cruelest coincidence in the volume

Hachi decides she should reconcile with Shoji.

Her imagined plan is completely Hachi:

> wait for him;

> apologize;

> go home together;

> have sex;

> be reconciled.

Then a beautiful young man answers the BLAST flyer.

Hachi changes plans.

At almost exactly the same narrative moment, Shoji is introduced to the new girl at his workplace:

> **川村幸子 / Kawamura Sachiko.**

This is the actual Sachiko.

The joke Shoji previously invented—an imaginary woman named Sachiko—suddenly acquires a body.

That is classic Yazawa irony.

It feels fated.

But the tragedy that follows will not actually be caused by fate.

It will be caused by decisions.

That distinction matters.

---

# XV. Money: 707's love has rules

Hachi's next crisis is brutally mundane:

> **「お金がない！」**

She has ¥2,328 left.

She has spent too much on:

- clothes;
- furnishings;
- pleasurable consumption.

Nana does not rescue her.

She reminds Hachi:

> **「使えばなくなるんだよ 金は」**

Money disappears when you spend it.

More importantly:

> **「人の金をあてにすんな！」**

Don't count on someone else's money.

And:

> living costs at 707 are supposed to be split evenly.

If Hachi cannot provide her share by the end of the month, Nana says she will have to leave.

This is superficially harsh.

Structurally it is extremely important.

---

# XVI. Chosen family does not mean unlimited rescue

Volume 2 founded 707 through:

> half the rent + separate locks.

Volume 3 preserves the same ethic.

Nana loves having Hachi around.

Hachi increasingly matters to her.

But Nana does not say:

> Since we care about each other, economic boundaries no longer matter.

707 remains sustainable because each person remains responsible for herself.

That gives the apartment a very different structure from fusion.

It is:

> **shared life without automatic entitlement.**

This matters enormously to a story increasingly concerned with dependence.

The home survives through boundaries.

---

# XVII. Hachi's fear is not only hunger—it is being abandoned

When Nana threatens the logical consequence of unpaid rent, Hachi's response is:

> **「お願い 見捨てないで！」**

“Please don't abandon me.”

That vocabulary tells us what money means psychologically.

The literal problem is:

> I cannot pay.

The emotional translation is:

> You will leave me.

Hachi repeatedly turns practical instability into relational insecurity.

That does not make the practical instability unreal.

It shows why ordinary adult failures hit her so hard.

---

# XVIII. Junko's rice: care without enabling

Hachi eventually turns to Junko and Kyosuke.

Kyosuke is willing to provide money.

Junko resists simply handing Hachi cash.

Instead she gives her rice.

That is a very revealing mode of friendship.

Junko provides:

> enough material support to prevent catastrophe;

without providing:

> enough unrestricted support to allow Hachi to continue behaving exactly as before.

This is one of Volume 3's recurring ethical patterns:

> **good care sometimes contains a boundary.**

Nana does it with rent.

Junko does it with food.

Later, Sachiko will attempt it through withdrawal.

The volume is increasingly interested in the difference between:

> helping someone

and:

> helping someone avoid the consequences that might force change.

---

# XIX. Hachi learns arithmetic

The budgeting sequence is deceptively important.

Hachi has to calculate:

- wages;
- phone expenses;
- rent;
- utilities;
- food.

Adulthood stops being an aesthetic.

It becomes numbers.

This is especially important because Hachi's self-image is so heavily built from images:

- cute bride;
- stylish Tokyo woman;
- nice apartment;
- fashionable clothes;
- successful relationship.

Volume 3 forces every image to acquire a price.

---

# XX. Hachi's mother and the bride fund

Then ¥300,000 appears in Hachi's account.

It comes from her mother.

The origin is simultaneously comic and thematically perfect:

> **花嫁資金 / hanayome shikin**  
> money saved for her future marriage.

Hachi has spent her whole life dreaming of becoming:

> **「かわいいお嫁さん」**  
> a cute bride.

She says this was essentially her only childhood conception of a future occupation/identity.

So her mother has materially saved toward exactly the feminine future Hachi imagined.

But now that capital is arriving at a strange transitional moment:

Hachi is not getting married.

She is trying to learn how to support herself.

The “bride fund” becomes a tool for autonomy.

That is a beautiful inversion.

---

# XXI. Hachi does not stop wanting marriage

Importantly, the manga does **not** resolve Hachi's growth by making her reject domestic aspiration.

She continues wanting:

- wedding clothes;
- marriage;
- a pleasant house;
- a garden;
- domestic comfort.

What changes is her understanding of the conditions.

She begins thinking:

> cohabitation and marriage are different;

> marriage costs money;

> Shoji is only beginning university;

> a one-room apartment is not the sweet home she imagines;

> therefore she needs savings.

This is much better characterization than:

> naïve girl learns career good, marriage dream bad.

Instead:

> **fantasy becomes planning.**

Hachi's domestic desire survives maturation.

It simply acquires economics.

---

# XXII. 「あたしには故郷などない」

Hachi asks Nana whether she ever gets homesick.

Nana answers:

> **「あたしには故郷などない」**

“I don't have a hometown.”

The sentence is striking partly because of how absolute it sounds.

Nana does not say:

> I don't miss home.

She denies that **home-as-origin** exists for her at all.

Given what Volume 1 established about her childhood, this is not teenage affectation.

It is identity.

---

# XXIII. Future Hachi answers Nana across time

And then the volume gives one of the most important retrospective passages yet:

> **「ナナは今でも自分の故郷が  
> どこにもないと思ってる？」**

Do you still think even now that you have no hometown?

Then:

> **「あの窓辺の食卓も椅子も  
> あの頃のまま  
> あの場所にあるよ」**

The table and chairs beside that window are still there, just as they were back then.

The accompanying image shows the shared table alive with people.

This is the strongest home statement in the manga so far.

Future Hachi is effectively arguing:

> **A hometown does not have to be the place you inherited.  
> It can be the place where your life became shared.**

707 is ceasing to be merely “the apartment.”

It is becoming Nana's answer to a question Nana does not yet believe has an answer.

---

# XXIV. The table matters more than the lease

Notice what future Hachi remembers.

Not:

- square footage;
- price;
- ownership;
- legal tenancy.

She remembers:

> **the table and chairs beside the window.**

Because home is being defined through repeated use:

- eating;
- talking;
- listening;
- teasing;
- band planning;
- friends gathering;
- returning.

The furniture becomes an archive of relationship.

This is exactly how *NANA* produces anticipatory nostalgia:

> it identifies an ordinary object in the present and tells us that someone in the future still knows where it is.

---

# XXV. Yasu returns: adulthood without abandoning art

Yasu's arrival gives Nana another part of her former life back.

But Yasu differs sharply from Nobu.

Nobu's artistic logic is:

> guitar is enough for happiness.

He is willing to throw away a guaranteed family future.

Yasu's logic is more integrative.

He can come to Tokyo.

He can play drums.

And:

> there are law offices in Tokyo too.

That makes Yasu one of the first characters who refuses the false binary:

> art **or** adult professional stability.

He attempts:

> art **and** professional adulthood.

That is extremely appropriate to his character.

---

# XXVI. Yasu as another relationship Hachi initially cannot read

Nana becomes visibly joyful when Yasu arrives.

Later she gets drunk, clings to him, says:

> **「愛してるわ～～♡」**

and behaves with unusual physical looseness.

Hachi immediately begins trying to classify them as a romantic couple.

Yet nothing in the volume confirms her interpretation.

This continues a pattern:

> **Hachi is highly sensitive to intimacy but tends to believe intimacy must be romantic if it crosses a certain threshold.**

Nana and Yasu are therefore important not merely in themselves.

They expose the limits of Hachi's relationship vocabulary.

---

# XXVII. Hachi's ideal husband becomes more sophisticated

Hachi observes Yasu and thinks that if she were marrying someone, a man like Yasu would be good:

- kind;
- attentive;
- dependable;
- not domineering.

That last trait matters.

Her ideal masculinity is becoming less about glamorous romantic excitement and more about:

> **reliability without control.**

This is a meaningful shift.

She still fantasizes.

But the qualities entering the fantasy are becoming more adult.

---

# XXVIII. Nana's realism about bad parents

Shin gives Volume 3 another version of the home problem.

Nana asks whether his parents worry about him.

Shin effectively says no.

Most adults, he notes, respond to that kind of statement with:

> of course your parents care about you.

Nana does not.

She says:

> **「だって 子供を捨てる親は  
> 現実にいるじゃない」**

Parents who abandon their children really exist.

This is an extraordinarily Nana response.

It is compassionate precisely because it refuses sentimental reassurance.

She does not impose the normative story:

> parents love children.

She recognizes:

> sometimes they do not behave as though they do.

For Shin, that means Nana accepts his reality instead of correcting it into something more comforting.

For Nana, the line is obviously resonant with her own history, even though she does not turn the conversation toward herself.

---

# XXIX. Shin: the dark underside of having nowhere stable to go

Near the end of the volume we learn that Shin has not returned home.

Nor is he simply staying permanently with Nobu.

He says he moves among women's homes.

Why?

Because he is fifteen, looks the way he does, cannot readily obtain normal work—and has discovered that living this way means he does not run out of money.

The scene is played partly as outrageous comedy.

But the underlying fact should not be trivialized.

A fifteen-year-old boy without reliable home or employment has found that:

> **his attractiveness and intimacy can function as economic resources.**

Volume 3 does not yet give us enough information to characterize every arrangement or determine exactly how exploitative each adult is.

But it clearly establishes **transactional survival through older women's desire** as part of Shin's life.

That places him at the darkest point of the volume's home spectrum.

Hachi receives money from family.

Nana constructs chosen home.

Shin turns interpersonal/sexual availability into temporary shelter and income.

“Home” is not equally available to everyone.

---

# XXX. Hachi gets a respectable job—and respectability is mostly fantasy

After SABRINA closes, Hachi finds work at a publisher.

At first this thrills her.

She thinks:

> working at a publishing company sounds cool;

> she will look like a **デキル女**, a capable professional woman.

Again she begins from image.

Then she experiences the job.

Her actual duties are:

- cleaning;
- serving tea;
- copying;
- fetching manuscripts;
- miscellaneous errands.

She is overworked.

She gets home late.

She is called in on weekends.

A senior coworker harshly scrutinizes her mistakes.

And her male superior behaves in ways Hachi explicitly experiences as **セクハラ / sexual harassment**.

The glamorous “publishing woman” fantasy becomes ordinary gendered labor.

---

# XXXI. Adulthood is bodily fatigue

Hachi rides home exhausted:

> **「もうクタクタだ！」**

This is a major step in the manga's treatment of adulthood.

Adulthood is not only:

- independence;
- sex;
- apartments;
- marriage.

It is:

> **being physically tired because somebody else purchased most of your day.**

That matters enormously for the romance plot.

Hachi and Shoji do not simply “grow apart.”

Their available emotional energy is being redistributed by institutions:

- university;
- wage labor;
- commuting;
- deadlines.

Love has to survive after work.

---

# XXXII. Hachi's independence is real—but not pure

Nana recognizes that Hachi is more serious about independence than she originally expected.

Hachi herself admits her immediate motive is mostly money.

And there is another important qualification.

She chooses the publisher partly because the advertised schedule includes weekends off.

Why does that matter?

Because she thinks:

> she will finally have more time with Shoji.

So even her move toward economic independence remains partly organized around romance.

That does not make the independence false.

It makes it Hachi's.

Her motives are mixed.

That is exactly how *NANA* treats most adult choices.

---

# XXXIII. Hachi and Shoji are experiencing different economic adulthood

Their argument about work is particularly revealing.

Hachi resents Shoji's suggestion that she simply quit if she hates the job.

From her perspective:

- she needs income;
- another job is not guaranteed;
- she has rent;
- she has bills.

Shoji, by contrast, is:

- a student;
- still receiving some family support;
- able to buy a computer connected to his professional goals.

Hachi tells him their:

> **危機感 / sense of crisis**

is different.

This is a subtle class/material tension inside the romance.

They may be similarly young.

They are not currently exposed to the same economic risk.

---

# XXXIV. 「かわいい女になりきれない」

After fighting with Shoji, Hachi thinks:

> **「章司の前だと  
> いまいちかわいい女になりきれない」**

She cannot quite fully become a “cute woman” in front of him.

The verb **なりきる** is important.

It means becoming fully, inhabiting the role completely.

Hachi's model of ideal girlfriendhood still includes a performance:

> soft;

> agreeable;

> cute;

> non-confrontational.

But actual intimacy makes her:

- angry;
- demanding;
- contradictory;
- argumentative.

This is one of the sharpest gender observations in the volume.

Hachi is not failing to become a woman.

She is failing to sustain **her image of the woman she thinks a boyfriend should want**.

---

# XXXV. Shoji and Sachiko: why the affair is more disturbing than a simple temptation plot

The manga takes extraordinary care not to write Sachiko as an evil seductress.

Before anything goes decisively wrong, Sachiko displays explicit boundary consciousness.

She tells Shoji she does not want a man with a girlfriend paying for her.

When another man with a hometown girlfriend expresses interest, she refuses him partly on those grounds.

When she realizes she likes Shoji, she insists:

> she will not interfere with Shoji and Hachi.

She understands the rule.

So does Shoji.

That is precisely why the story is more adult than:

> bad woman steals boyfriend.

---

# XXXVI. Shoji recognizes the danger before he crosses the line

Shoji knows almost immediately that Sachiko is his type.

His reaction is essentially:

> this is bad;

> I should avoid getting involved.

Later Junko confronts him.

She tells him that if Sachiko has feelings for him, he needs to establish a real boundary.

Her warning is excellent:

> **「フォローすればフォローするほど  
> どんどん傷つけて深みにハマるよ」**

The more you keep “following up”—comforting her, reassuring her—the more you will hurt her and the deeper this will become.

Junko understands the actual danger.

It is not only sexual attraction.

It is **care without boundaries**.

---

# XXXVII. “Healing” becomes dangerous

Shoji admits something important:

> **「確かにおれは幸子といると  
> 癒される気がするよ」**

He feels soothed—**癒される / iyasareru**—when he is with Sachiko.

That is the emotional mechanism.

Hachi is exhausting at this point.

She is:

- demanding;
- volatile;
- distracted;
- financially stressed;
- increasingly busy;
- difficult to coordinate with.

Sachiko feels easier.

That does not make Hachi responsible for Shoji's betrayal.

It explains why the temptation is powerful.

There is an important moral distinction:

> **relationship problems can explain why betrayal becomes attractive without making betrayal justified.**

Volume 3 is sophisticated enough to hold both propositions at once.

---

# XXXVIII. Junko's brutal but fair judgment

Junko essentially tells Shoji:

> you knew Hachi was exhausting when you chose to get involved with her;

> you do not get to act surprised now.

This is not an argument that someone must remain forever in an unhappy relationship.

It is an argument against quietly using a partner's known flaws as retroactive moral permission for dishonesty.

If Shoji no longer wants the relationship, he can end it.

What he cannot ethically do is:

> keep Hachi as girlfriend while using Sachiko as emotional refuge.

---

# XXXIX. Kyosuke's definition of fidelity

Shoji later asks Kyosuke whether he has ever cheated.

The most important answer is not a claim that Kyosuke never sees attractive people.

It is:

> **「淳子を失うのが怖いから」**

Because he is afraid of losing Junko.

This gives Volume 3 one of its clearest relationship ethics.

Commitment is not:

> never encountering another possible desire.

It is:

> **knowing which relationship you are unwilling to gamble away.**

That becomes devastating in Shoji's case because he increasingly cannot answer the same question cleanly.

---

# XL. Sachiko's request to remain friends is where the boundary collapses

When Shoji tries to pull away, Sachiko begs him:

> don't cut me off;

> even just friends is enough.

She genuinely seems to mean it.

Shoji genuinely wants to comfort her.

And then he embraces her.

This is precisely the sequence Junko predicted.

There does not need to be a malicious plan.

The boundary can fail through mutually comprehensible tenderness.

That is what makes the scene dangerous.

---

# XLI. By Chapter 8, “Sachiko” is no longer imaginary in any sense

Chapter 8 opens with one of Yazawa's strongest pieces of dramatic irony so far.

Hachi sends Shoji an upbeat late-night message:

> the new job is settled;

> she has lots to tell him;

> she wants to see him;

and jokingly asks whether his phone is unavailable because he is:

> **「幸子と浮気中？」**

“Cheating with Sachiko?”

Shoji reads this in Sachiko's apartment.

Sachiko sleeps in the bed behind him.

The exact sexual act is not narrated on this page, so there is no reason to manufacture detail the volume does not give us.

But the relationship has unquestionably crossed far beyond innocent friendship.

And Shoji himself immediately begins asking Kyosuke about:

> **浮気 / cheating.**

His moral vocabulary has caught up with his behavior.

---

# XLII. Shoji is not morally confused about whether this is wrong

This distinction is important.

Shoji does not appear to believe:

> I am entitled to this because Hachi is difficult.

He knows he is failing.

Later he hides in the restroom and thinks, in effect:

> I am pathetic.

He experiences serious self-disgust.

That does not absolve him.

In some ways it makes the failure more revealing.

He possesses moral knowledge.

He lacks the willingness to endure the pain required to act consistently with it.

---

# XLIII. Kyosuke's frighteningly accurate diagnosis: this is not “just cheating”

After observing Shoji, Kyosuke tells Junko:

> **「ありゃ浮気つーより  
> 本気じゃん？」**

That's less “cheating” than serious love, isn't it?

And:

> Shoji seems genuinely in love with Sachiko—enough to cross reason and morality.

This should **not** be interpreted as moral exoneration.

“True love” does not make deception ethical.

What the line does is remove another easy explanation.

Shoji is not merely pursuing novelty or sex.

He is becoming emotionally attached to another person.

That makes the decision more painful.

It does not make the decision less necessary.

---

# XLIV. The volume's romantic ethics: feeling is real, but feeling does not settle obligation

This is emerging as one of *NANA*'s strongest ideas.

Shoji's feelings for Sachiko may be real.

His affection and accumulated attachment toward Hachi are also real.

Sachiko's feelings are real.

Hachi's love is real.

None of those facts answers:

> **What should the people involved do?**

Emotion explains.

It does not automatically justify.

That is a much more mature conception of romance than:

> love proves moral rightness.

---

# XLV. Sachiko meets Hachi—and abstraction becomes personhood

The restaurant collision is one of the volume's finest scenes.

Hachi arrives with Nana and the BLAST circle.

Sachiko sees her.

Then Sachiko drops a tray and cuts her hand.

Hachi does not know who she is.

She sees only:

> another young woman working a difficult restaurant job who has hurt herself.

And she immediately helps.

She presses a handkerchief against the wound.

She tells Sachiko to get treatment.

When Nana compliments Hachi's kindness, Hachi explains why:

> she used to work in a family restaurant too;

> she cannot treat what happened as someone else's problem.

This is important.

Hachi's compassion comes through **recognition of shared labor**.

---

# XLVI. The rival becomes human

For Sachiko, this is devastating.

“Hachi” had previously been:

> Shoji's girlfriend.

An obstacle.

A relationship category.

Now she has:

- a face;
- warmth;
- vulnerability;
- kindness;
- working-class experience;
- a hand touching Sachiko's injured hand.

Recognition makes betrayal harder.

Sachiko subsequently tells Shoji:

> Hachi is cute;

> kind;

> a wonderful girlfriend.

And:

> **「傷つけないうちに  
> やめよう……」**

Let's stop before we hurt her.

This is morally significant.

Sachiko's conscience does not activate because she suddenly learns cheating is against the rules.

She already knew that.

It activates with greater force because the abstract person who would be harmed becomes **particular**.

That is an important *NANA* ethical principle already appearing:

> **Recognition of another person's interior reality can constrain desire more powerfully than an abstract rule.**

---

# XLVII. Sachiko is not absolved either

Her attempt to end things matters.

So does the fact that the relationship has already progressed this far.

Her self-blame—

> if I hadn't kept following you around—

may be sincere, but Shoji correctly rejects the idea that this is solely her doing.

There are two agents here.

Neither should absorb all responsibility so the other can escape it.

That is another place where the manga resists simple gendered blame.

---

# XLVIII. Junko becomes morally compromised by knowledge

Junko knows something Hachi does not.

And when Hachi says she feels Shoji has been avoiding her, Junko reassures her.

Immediately afterward, Junko internally protests:

> why am **I** having to cover for Shoji?

This is the beginning of another important problem:

> **What does a friend owe someone when revealing a painful truth may devastate her, but concealing it allows another person to continue deceiving her?**

Junko does not have a clean answer.

Nor does Volume 3.

What it establishes is that information creates responsibility.

Once Junko knows, neutrality becomes difficult.

---

# XLIX. Hachi still trusts Shoji

The irony becomes painful because Hachi continues insisting:

> Shoji would never cheat.

This is not presented as stupidity.

It is trust.

And trust is precisely what makes deception morally consequential.

A person cannot betray trust that was never extended.

---

# L. May 5: one date, two incompatible lives

BLAST receives its first Tokyo live opportunity.

The date is:

> **May 5.**

Hachi is ecstatic.

She calls Shoji and asks him to come.

But May 5 is also Sachiko's birthday.

Shoji has already offered to spend the evening celebrating with her.

The page construction is excellent.

Hachi asks over the phone whether Shoji can attend Nana's live.

Sachiko waits nearby holding the small Children's Day flag associated with her birthday.

Shoji says he cannot come because of work.

That is not entirely fabricated—he does have work.

But it conceals the important part of the truth.

One calendar date now contains two lives he is trying not to make collide.

It cannot remain sustainable.

---

# LI. The Children's Day flag

The little flag is almost absurdly innocent.

Sachiko likes it because her birthday is Children's Day.

But visually it becomes a marker of concealed obligation.

On one side of the call:

> Hachi's excitement about Nana's future.

On the other:

> Sachiko's private birthday significance.

Shoji is standing between them.

The object turns emotional conflict into something materially visible.

---

# LII. Hachi's job misery and Nana's smile

After exhausting work, Hachi returns home.

Nana greets her with the news about the live.

The visual transition is striking.

Hachi has just been:

- criticized;
- overworked;
- sexually harassed;
- physically exhausted.

Then she sees Nana.

The emotional register lifts almost instantly.

That does not mean Nana magically solves employment.

It means 707 functions as a **counter-space**.

Work treats Hachi as:

> low-level labor.

Nana's world treats her as:

> someone whose excitement matters.

This helps explain why Hachi's attachment to Nana is becoming so intense.

---

# LIII. 「じゃあ あたしが歌で稼いで建ててやるよ」

Hachi tells Nana about her dream:

> a detached house with a garden.

Nana answers:

> **「じゃあ あたしが  
> 歌で稼いで建ててやるよ」**

“Then I'll earn money singing and build it for you.”

Then, of course:

> Hachi's room will be the doghouse.

It is a joke.

But it is a very revealing joke.

Hachi's romantic-domestic future has conventionally been organized around:

> Shoji → marriage → house.

Nana casually inserts herself into the provider position.

She imagines her artistic success materially underwriting Hachi's domestic dream.

And future Hachi responds with the line:

> **「もしもナナが男だったら  
> 一世一代の恋が出来るのに」**

This is not accidental placement.

---

# LIV. “If Nana were a man”: gender becomes the missing permission

This is probably the single most important Nana/Hachi statement in the volume.

Hachi is not saying:

> Nana lacks the traits I would want in a lover.

Quite the opposite.

She is saying:

> **the relationship already contains enough of the emotional substance that, if Nana were male, it could become the love of her lifetime.**

The hypothetical obstacle is gender.

That does not automatically tell us Hachi's sexual orientation in modern identity terminology.

The manga has not established that.

But it does tell us something about **Hachi's conceptual apparatus**.

She can imagine Nana as:

- provider;
- beloved;
- first-love analogue;
- object of near-romantic admiration;
- life-defining partner.

But to promote that emotional structure into the culturally legible category of “great romance,” she imagines changing Nana into a man.

That is profoundly queer-coded.

---

# LV. But Hachi then tells us why she likes the relationship as it is

The continuation is just as important.

She says that if Nana were male:

> perhaps their relationship would no longer be nothing but fun.

Because:

> **「恋に痛みはつきものだから」**

Pain comes with love.

And:

> **「溺れてゆく程  
> 苦しいものだから」**

The more deeply you drown in it, the more painful it becomes.

The visual juxtaposition is devastating.

Hachi's narration about painful romantic drowning runs over Shoji and Sachiko's increasingly intimate entanglement.

So the page produces a three-part contrast:

### Nana/Hachi

> “like first love,” but still largely joyful.

### Hachi's idea of conventional romance

> possession, vulnerability, drowning, pain.

### Shoji/Sachiko

> the visual embodiment of exactly that painful romantic entanglement.

This is sophisticated.

---

# LVI. Nana is Hachi's “sweet dream” partly because Hachi does not demand that the relationship become romance

This may be the most important provisional relational inference from Volume 3.

Hachi experiences Nana as extraordinarily safe partly because she does not yet impose the same expectations upon Nana that she imposes upon a boyfriend.

With Shoji she asks:

- Are you available?
- Do you love me enough?
- Will you marry me?
- Am I a good girlfriend?
- Could you betray me?
- Why aren't we together?

With Nana, much of the relationship is:

- come home;
- eat;
- talk;
- tease;
- listen;
- help;
- sleep nearby;
- build something;
- enjoy being together.

So there is a paradox.

> **Hachi's bond with Nana may feel freer partly because she does not yet believe she has the right to demand romantic guarantees from Nana.**

That makes the relationship happier.

It may also mean Hachi has not yet confronted everything she wants from it.

That remains an inference, not a settled conclusion.

---

# LVII. The volume's other crucial comparison: Nana/Yasu

Hachi repeatedly interprets Nana/Yasu as romance because their intimacy exceeds her model of ordinary friendship.

But the relationship keeps refusing the expected script.

Yasu:

- helps;
- relocates;
- plays;
- absorbs Nana's drunken affection;
- remains calm;
- does not convert her vulnerability into sexual entitlement.

This creates an important alternative model of intimacy:

> **a man and woman can be profoundly attached without the text immediately making romance the explanatory key.**

That is precisely the category Hachi has trouble imagining.

And, ironically, the same difficulty may apply to her own relationship with Nana in the opposite gender configuration.

---

# LVIII. Volume 3's emerging relationship theory

We now have several different forms of attachment:

### Hachi → Shoji

Recognized romance, increasingly difficult in practice.

### Hachi → Nana

Unrecognized/qualified near-romantic attachment, extraordinarily easy in ordinary life.

### Nana → Yasu

Deep trust and physical/emotional ease that Hachi repeatedly misclassifies as romance.

### Shoji → Sachiko

Emotion becoming romantic despite an existing commitment.

### Shin → adult women

Intimacy functioning partly as economic survival.

### Nana → Ren

Absent romance still structuring artistic memory.

The series is very rapidly dismantling the assumption:

> **one form of intimacy = one kind of relationship.**

---

# LIX. The final cliffhanger: Shoji finally chooses conversation

After the restaurant collision and Sachiko's attempt to withdraw, Shoji asks Hachi:

> **「あとで家行っていい？  
> 話があるんだ」**

Can I come to your place later? I need to talk to you.

The volume ends before that conversation occurs.

Our spoiler discipline matters here.

We do **not** yet know from Volume 3 alone:

- the exact words Shoji will use;
- what Hachi will learn;
- how the relationship will formally change;
- what Nana will do.

But the final retrospective narration tells us the magnitude.

---

# LX. 「もしもナナが一緒にいてくれなかったら」

Future Hachi says:

> **「あの夜  
> もしもナナが一緒にいてくれなかったら  
> あたしは身投げして多摩川の底に沈んでたと思う」**

That night, if Nana had not been with me, I think I would have thrown myself into the Tama River and sunk to the bottom.

Then:

> **「本気で そう思う」**

“I truly think so.”

The text should be taken seriously while preserving the counterfactual nature of the statement.

We cannot know that she literally would have done so.

Future Hachi herself believes the danger was real.

More important narratively:

> **Nana's presence becomes associated not merely with happiness but with survival through emotional catastrophe.**

That is an enormous escalation in relational importance.

Volume 2:

> Nana makes ordinary life joyful.

Volume 3:

> future Hachi believes Nana kept unbearable pain survivable.

---

# LXI. A disturbing echo: Kyosuke's joke becomes future Hachi's serious statement

Earlier, Kyosuke jokes darkly about how frightening it would be to imagine Hachi suddenly abandoned—perhaps she would cut her wrists.

At the time it is black humor about Hachi's emotional dependency.

Then the final page has future Hachi seriously state that she believes she might have thrown herself into the river without Nana.

That echo is uncomfortable.

And deliberately so.

The manga is telling us that what can look comically excessive from the outside may correspond to subjectively catastrophic dependence inside the person.

It does not make suicide romantic.

It makes Hachi's vulnerability much harder to laugh off.

---

# LXII. Hachi: Volume 3 character update

Volume 3 is one of the most important early Hachi volumes because several apparent contradictions become clearly compatible.

## She is genuinely trying to become independent

Evidence:

- budgets;
- job hunting;
- rent;
- savings;
- trying to move into stable employment;
- thinking about financial security.

Nana herself recognizes that Hachi's independence talk is more serious than she had assumed.

## She still organizes independence around relationship

The new job is attractive partly because weekends would let her see Shoji.

Her savings are imagined partly through marriage and home.

Her future remains relational.

So the correct model is not:

> dependent Hachi becomes independent.

It is:

> **Hachi is attempting to build enough independent material capacity to sustain the kind of intimate life she wants.**

## Her emotional perception remains better than her classification

She still gets relational categories wrong.

She thinks Nana/Yasu must be romance.

She thinks Shoji cannot possibly cheat.

She does not recognize Sachiko as the actual threat standing in front of her.

But she recognizes:

- Nana's loneliness;
- Sachiko's injury;
- the emotional uplift Nana gives her;
- when someone needs care.

Again:

> **Hachi can misread what a relationship is while responding accurately to what a person feels.**

## Her kindness is not abstract

The Sachiko scene reveals something important about Hachi.

She helps because:

> I used to do this work too.

That is empathy grounded in shared experience.

The person Hachi helps happens to be the woman involved with her boyfriend.

She does not know that.

But the scene establishes the moral quality independent of reward.

## Her flaws remain real

Volume 3 does not sanctify her.

She:

- overspends;
- expects Shoji to adapt;
- forgets her own messages;
- abandons a reconciliation plan when the band becomes exciting;
- uses cruel gendered/age-based language about an older female coworker;
- struggles to communicate needs directly.

None of this morally licenses deception against her.

But it prevents the betrayal plot from depending upon an artificially perfect victim.

---

# LXIII. Nana: Volume 3 character update

Nana becomes simultaneously more domestic and more artistic.

## She is building a future rather than waiting for Ren

BLAST now has:

- Nobu;
- Shin;
- Yasu;
- Nana.

A Tokyo live is booked.

The band is becoming materially real.

This is probably the strongest evidence yet that Nana's refusal to follow Ren was not merely defensive pride.

She genuinely wants authorship over her own artistic life.

## Ren still occupies the structure

Shin idolizes him.

Nana asks Shin to surpass him.

The absent lover becomes a standard inside the future band.

Nana is moving forward.

She is not psychologically starting from zero.

## Her harshness can function as respect

When Hachi runs out of money, Nana refuses to convert affection into indefinite subsidization.

That looks hard.

But it treats Hachi as someone capable of adult responsibility.

Nana does not say:

> poor helpless Hachi.

She says:

> pay your share.

This is a form of relational equality.

## Her abandonment history informs her compassion

The Shin-parent conversation reveals Nana's distinctive form of empathy.

She does not comfort people with a socially preferred lie.

Her care is sometimes:

> **I believe the ugly version of your experience because I know ugly things actually happen.**

That is very different from Hachi's more optimistic mode of care.

---

# LXIV. Shoji: Volume 3 character update

Shoji becomes substantially more morally complicated than he was in Volume 2.

Volume 2's insistence that Hachi become financially independent remains a healthy boundary.

Nothing in Volume 3 invalidates that.

But Shoji now fails to maintain a boundary of his own.

The arc is:

> attraction recognized → avoidance attempted → emotional soothing accepted → friendship preserved despite known risk → physical comfort → secrecy → guilt → affair → inability to cleanly choose.

He is not unaware.

He repeatedly knows what he should do.

That makes his failure primarily one of:

> **action under emotional cost.**

## He does not simply stop caring about Hachi

Kyosuke notes this explicitly.

Shoji has not suddenly come to hate Hachi.

There is:

- history;
- affection;
- attachment;
- obligation.

That is part of the problem.

Human relationships do not conveniently disappear when a new feeling arrives.

## But attachment is not enough

Volume 3 thereby introduces a crucial distinction:

> **caring about someone is not the same thing as choosing behavior that protects them.**

Shoji still cares.

He is still betraying her.

Both can be true.

---

# LXV. Sachiko: Volume 3 character baseline

Sachiko deserves careful treatment from the beginning.

She is:

- attractive;
- gentle;
- socially easy;
- practically minded;
- emotionally sincere;
- conscious that Shoji has a girlfriend;
- initially resistant to crossing that boundary.

She is not introduced as a predator.

Her tragedy is that her stated ethics and her desire diverge.

Then meeting Hachi makes the moral reality harder to evade.

Her strongest line becomes:

> **「誰も幸せになんかなれないよ」**

Nobody can become happy like this.

And:

> **「彼女の事…大切にしてあげて」**

Take care of your girlfriend.

That gives Sachiko moral self-awareness before Volume 3 ends.

Whether awareness will translate into sustainable action remains unresolved.

---

# LXVI. Junko and Kyosuke: the mature couple as moral comparator

Junko and Kyosuke are becoming more than Hachi's commentary chorus.

Together, they provide a relationship in which:

- attraction to other people is imaginable;
- conflict exists;
- individuality remains;
- but commitment has become an active priority.

Kyosuke's:

> “I'm afraid of losing Junko”

is not romantic grandiosity.

It is a practical ranking of value.

Junko's:

> “cut the relationship off cleanly”

is similarly unsentimental.

They understand something Hachi and Shoji are still learning:

> **A relationship is partly made from the alternatives you decide not to pursue.**

---

# LXVII. Japanese voice: Hachi lives through nouns for roles

Volume 3 gives us a clearer picture of Hachi's linguistic imagination.

She repeatedly reaches for culturally legible identity labels:

- **彼氏** — boyfriend;
- **浮気** — cheating;
- **恋** — romantic love;
- **初恋** — first love;
- **お嫁さん / 花嫁** — bride;
- **正社員** — full-time employee;
- **デキル女** — capable/professional woman;
- **一戸建て** — detached house.

This is not accidental vocabulary.

Hachi is constantly asking:

> **What is the name of the life I am living?**

She often knows the label before she knows the experience.

That is one of the best ways to understand her.

---

# LXVIII. Present Hachi versus future Hachi is becoming a major voice distinction

Present Hachi speaks in:

- exclamation;
- exaggeration;
- hearts;
- comic self-dramatization;
- immediate emotional certainty.

Future Hachi speaks much more carefully.

Notice the qualifiers:

> **「かなり恋に近い」**  
> quite close to love.

> **「初恋みたい」**  
> like a first love.

> **「そう思う」**  
> I think so.

Future Hachi is not less emotional.

She is more epistemically precise.

She understands that some feelings resist definitive nouns.

That difference between present and future narration is becoming one of the manga's strongest formal techniques.

---

# LXIX. Nana's voice: bluntness and startling existential compression

Nana's ordinary language remains:

- rough;
- contracted;
- direct;
- teasing;
- frequently masculine-coded in fictional register.

Examples include the financial scolding and her sexual joke in bed.

But then she can suddenly produce a sentence like:

> **「あたしには故郷などない」**

The simplicity makes it hit harder.

There is no long confession.

No explanation.

Nana's deepest wounds often appear as extremely compressed statements surrounded by ordinary roughness.

That appears to be one of her characteristic defensive forms.

---

# LXX. Sachiko's voice: apology and self-removal

Sachiko's language repeatedly attempts to minimize the burden she places on others.

She says, in various forms:

- I won't interfere;
- even being friends is enough;
- I'm sorry;
- I'll stop;
- take care of her.

That creates an important contrast with Hachi.

Hachi's feelings tend to expand outward.

Sachiko's tend to turn inward and attempt self-erasure.

Neither strategy is automatically healthier.

They simply create very different interpersonal pressures.

---

# LXXI. Shin's speech: visual delinquency, verbal composure

Shin is visually provocative:

- piercings;
- punk styling;
- precocious sexuality.

Yet much of his speech toward the older BLAST members is comparatively controlled and polite.

That mismatch is worth tracking.

He can matter-of-factly explain extraordinarily adult circumstances while still being fifteen.

The disconnect between:

> appearance of worldly autonomy

and:

> actual age/material vulnerability

may prove essential to his character.

At Volume 3, we should not mistake precocity for safety.

---

# LXXII. Visual analysis: the bed

The overhead compositions of Nana and Hachi sharing the bed matter because they remove a great deal of visual distance.

The scene is not framed only as a gag.

Later, when future Hachi describes Nana as something close to love and like a happy first love, Yazawa returns us to the sleeping pair.

That means the physical image becomes retrospectively recoded.

What looked like casual domestic intimacy becomes part of Hachi's archive of near-romantic memory.

---

# LXXIII. Visual analysis: 707's table becomes historical evidence

The page answering Nana's “no hometown” declaration places the remembered table and chairs inside a large field of retrospective darkness.

It is almost an exhibit.

Future Hachi is saying:

> Look.

> This existed.

> We sat here.

> These people were here.

The image is not an abstract symbol of home.

It is **evidence that Nana belonged somewhere**.

# LXXIV. Visual analysis: the group around Hachi and Nana

When the rebuilt BLAST gathers around 707, Yazawa increasingly composes the apartment as a social organism.

Nana and Hachi occupy its emotional center.

Around them gather:

- Yasu;
- Nobu;
- Shin.

The line between:

> Hachi's home

and:

> Nana's band world

is disappearing.

707 is becoming the shared infrastructure connecting domestic and artistic life.

# LXXV. Visual analysis: Hachi's email and sleeping Sachiko

The Chapter 8 opening is perhaps the best page of informational asymmetry so far.

At the top:

> Hachi's cheerful message.

She jokingly asks whether Shoji is with Sachiko.

Below:

> Shoji's face.

Then:

> Sachiko sleeping behind him.

Hachi's joke is funny because she thinks it is impossible.

The reader knows it is almost literal.

The page makes betrayal painful not through a speech about dishonesty but by giving us simultaneous access to:

> **trust and the hidden fact that violates it.**

# LXXVI. Visual analysis: high heels and sneakers

The volume quietly places two young women against the practical problem of catching the final train.

Hachi wears fashionable heels and loses one while rushing.

Sachiko deliberately changes into sneakers because she knows she will need to run.

It would be too reductive to turn this into:

> Hachi = frivolous, Sachiko = practical.

Both characters are much more complicated.

But the visual contrast does reflect two distinct modes of femininity.

Hachi often prioritizes:

> aesthetic self-presentation.

Sachiko often appears more willing to adapt presentation to practical constraint.

Given that Shoji increasingly finds Sachiko “healing,” the contrast is worth tracking without turning it into moral hierarchy.

---

# LXXVII. Visual analysis: Hachi touches Sachiko's injured hand

Hands have already mattered in *NANA*.

The Volume 2 handshake between Nana and Hachi carried enormous emotional weight.

Here Hachi uses her own hands and handkerchief to stop Sachiko's bleeding.

Touch again becomes recognition.

But now the reader knows more than the touching character.

Hachi is caring for the woman whose relationship with Shoji will devastate her.

The body performs compassion before narrative knowledge can organize resentment.

That is one of the volume's strongest ethical images.

# LXXVIII. Visual analysis: the Tama River

The volume closes by returning to the river/night imagery associated with future Hachi's narration.

But the meaning has darkened.

Earlier, the river could support:

- light;
- reflection;
- nostalgia;
- remembered ordinary life.

Now it becomes the imagined bottom into which Hachi believes she might have thrown herself.

The same geography can hold:

> home-memory

and:

> imagined annihilation.

Nana's presence is the thing future Hachi places between those possibilities.

# LXXIX. Temporal structure: future Hachi becomes an interpreter, not merely a mourner

In Volumes 1–2, future Hachi primarily created anticipatory nostalgia.

Volume 3 adds another function:

> **she interprets her younger self.**

She tells us:

- what her admiration meant;
- how close it was to love;
- what Nana felt like;
- how she understood the possibility of romance across gender;
- how severe the coming emotional crisis felt in retrospect.

This does not make future Hachi omniscient.

It makes her more reflective.

She can name what younger Hachi could only experience.

# LXXX. Anticipatory nostalgia becomes anticipatory catastrophe

The final page is especially effective because it tells us the emotional result without supplying the event.

Shoji says:

> I need to talk to you.

Future Hachi says:

> without Nana, I believe I might have died.

The volume cuts.

So the reader knows:

> **something catastrophic is coming**

without yet knowing exactly how it occurs.

That is not ordinary foreshadowing.

It is retrospective emotional measurement.

# LXXXI. Motif ledger after Volume 3

## 707

**Volume 2:** shared apartment / negotiated home / private stage.

**Volume 3:** chosen hometown / community hub / emotional refuge.

The key new development is that future Hachi explicitly offers 707 as an answer to Nana's belief that she has no hometown.

## Money

Now signifies:

- adult competence;
- survival;
- relational equality;
- parental support;
- marriage aspiration;
- class difference;
- artistic possibility.

Hachi's growth is increasingly measurable through whether she can make money last.

## Work

No longer an abstract marker of independence.

It now means:

- bodily fatigue;
- sexual harassment;
- hierarchy;
- scheduling;
- time scarcity;
- self-respect;
- financial survival.

## Home

Volume 3 gives us multiple models:

**Inherited support:** Hachi's mother.

**Denied origin:** Nana.

**Chosen home:** 707.

**Precarious/transactional shelter:** Shin.

**Future imagined household:** Hachi's detached house with garden.

The concept has become one of the series' central analytical axes.

## Phones and messages

Communication technology repeatedly produces irony.

Hachi and Shoji are technically easier to contact than ever.

Yet emotional communication deteriorates.

The most devastating message is perfectly delivered.

The problem is that Hachi does not know what world Shoji is standing inside when he reads it.

The word expands.

It can mean:

- romantic excitement;
- sexual suggestion;

- fear.

Hachi's discovery that the body can “doki-doki” over building a band is a small but meaningful expansion of self.

## Food

Still functions as:

- labor solidarity.

Hachi understands Sachiko's restaurant accident partly because she has done the same work.

## Shoes

High heels versus sneakers begin linking:

- mobility.

Worth tracking, not yet a settled master motif.

## Hands

The Nana/Hachi handshake from Volume 2 now gains a counterpart in Hachi treating Sachiko's wound.

Hands repeatedly turn abstract relationships into bodily contact.

# LXXXII. Relationship-state ledger

## Hachi / Nana

**Previous state:** roommates → domestic companions → audience/artist intimacy.

**Volume 3 state:** domestic companionship becomes explicitly near-romantic in Hachi's retrospective language.

New evidence:

- shared bed;
- Nana's sexual joke;
- Nana kissing Hachi;
- Hachi technically categorizing the kiss through fidelity language;
- **「かなり恋に近い」**;
- **「初恋みたい」**;
- **「もしもナナが男だったら 一世一代の恋」**;
- Nana promising through music to build Hachi's dream home;
- future Hachi associating Nana with surviving catastrophic heartbreak.

At this point:

> **romantic/queer coding is directly textual.**

Canonical mutual romance is not established.

## Hachi / Shoji

**Previous state:** romantic reunion complicated by independence and scheduling.

**Volume 3 state:** trust remains on Hachi's side while Shoji becomes emotionally and physically entangled with Sachiko.

Important complications:

- Hachi is sometimes neglectful and demanding;
- Shoji is genuinely exhausted by aspects of the relationship;
- Shoji remains emotionally attached to Hachi;
- none of those facts eliminates his responsibility for deception.

The relationship is approaching a crisis but has not formally changed status by the last main-story page.

## Shoji / Sachiko

**State:** coworker friendship → attraction → attempted boundary → emotional refuge → physical intimacy/secrecy → Sachiko attempts withdrawal.

Important:

> neither begins as a cartoon villain.

Both recognize moral constraints.

Both cross them.

Sachiko's direct encounter with Hachi makes her attempt to stop.

Shoji appears increasingly unable to cleanly separate his desires.

## Nana / Yasu

**State:** deep practical/emotional trust reconstituted in Tokyo.

Yasu returns to BLAST and relocates without abandoning his professional options.

Hachi continues reading their intimacy romantically.

The text still does not confirm her interpretation.

## Nana / Ren

**State:** absent lover becomes artistic standard inside the new BLAST.

Shin's admiration places Ren inside the band's new generation.

Nana tells Shin to surpass him.

The relationship is no longer only private memory; it now shapes artistic competition.

## Nana / Nobu

**State:** collaborative musical partnership strengthened.

Nobu continues to function as the person who turns his own radical commitment to music into faith in Nana's artistic future.

## Nana / Shin

**State:** new band relationship with immediate artistic respect.

Nana accepts him on performance rather than paperwork.

Her response to his family situation also shows unusually unsentimental empathy.

## Hachi / Sachiko

**State:** one-sided unknown relationship.

Hachi does not know who Sachiko is.

Sachiko knows exactly who Hachi is.

Hachi treats her kindly.

That knowledge asymmetry is now morally central.

- BLAST actively recruits new members in Tokyo.
- Shin joins as bassist.
- Shin is fifteen despite initially representing himself as eighteen.
- Shin admires Ren as a bassist.
- Nana tells Shin to surpass Ren.

- Hachi's mother sends her ¥300,000 originally saved as bridal money.
- Hachi has long imagined becoming a **かわいいお嫁さん**.
- Hachi obtains a publishing-company job.
- The job is low-level miscellaneous labor and involves overwork and sexual harassment.
- Hachi remains invested in eventual marriage/domestic life.
- Nana says she has no hometown.
- Future Hachi points to 707's table and chairs as an answer to that belief.
- Nana kisses Hachi.
- Future Hachi says her admiration for Nana was **quite close to romantic love**.
- Future Hachi describes Nana as **like a very happy first love**.
- Hachi says she often thought she could have the love of a lifetime if Nana were male.
- Shoji becomes romantically involved with Sachiko while still Hachi's boyfriend.
- Sachiko knows Shoji has a girlfriend.
- Sachiko later meets Hachi without Hachi knowing who she is.
- Hachi helps Sachiko after Sachiko cuts her hand.
- Sachiko subsequently tries to end the relationship with Shoji before Hachi is hurt further.
- Shoji asks Hachi if he can come to 707 because he needs to talk.
- Future Hachi says she genuinely believes Nana's presence that night prevented her from throwing herself into the Tama River.

## B — strongly established

- 707 has become chosen home for both women in a sense much deeper than affordable housing.
- Nana/Hachi now occupies explicitly romance-adjacent textual territory rather than merely reader-imposed subtext.
- Shoji's connection to Sachiko is emotional rather than merely sexual opportunism.
- Hachi's movement toward material independence is genuine, although intertwined with her desire for romantic/domestic security.
- Nana's strictness about money functions partly as maintenance of equality and boundaries.
- Nana's own abandonment experience informs the realism with which she treats Shin's claims about his parents.
- Shin's living situation is materially precarious and tied to transactional intimacy with women.

## C — plausible inference

- Hachi's “if Nana were a man” formulation suggests that gender is one of the conceptual mechanisms through which she keeps her feelings for Nana outside the fully recognized category of romance.
- Nana/Hachi feels unusually safe partly because Hachi has not attached the same possessive/guarantee-seeking expectations to Nana that she attaches to male romantic relationships.
- Sachiko's guilt intensifies because Hachi changes from an abstract rival into a recognizable human subject.
- 707 may increasingly become the home against which later forms of domestic life are measured.

These are strong analytical possibilities, not yet settled facts.

## D — unresolved

- Exactly what Shoji will tell Hachi after Volume 3 ends.
- The immediate formal status of Hachi and Shoji's relationship after that conversation.
- How Nana will respond to the coming crisis.
- Whether Sachiko's attempt to end her involvement will hold.
- The exact nature of Shin's arrangements with the women who house/support him.
- Whether Hachi will ever classify her attachment to Nana differently.
- What Nana herself understands Hachi to be in romantic/erotic terms.

## E — counterfactual that cannot be verified

Future Hachi says she believes she would have thrown herself into the Tama River without Nana.

We should take the statement seriously as evidence of:

> retrospective emotional magnitude.

We cannot convert an unrealized counterfactual into an objective fact about what would literally have happened.

### Hachi believes Nana and Yasu are romantically involved.

Not established.

### Hachi believes Shoji is incapable of cheating.

The volume has already disproved that belief.

### Hachi repeatedly imagines marriage as the natural destination of her relationship with Shoji.

Shoji has not made an equivalent commitment in this volume.

### Sachiko initially believes she can remain “just friends” without worsening the situation.

The sequence demonstrates how difficult that boundary becomes.

### Shoji appears to believe he can preserve Hachi while also continuing emotionally close contact with Sachiko.

The structure of the volume increasingly shows those desires to be incompatible.

# LXXXV. What Volume 3 forces us to revise from Volume 2

## 1. Nana/Hachi can no longer be described merely as “queer-readable”

Volume 2 justified a queer reading.

Volume 3 goes further.

The narrator herself supplies:

- 恋;
- 初恋;
- a hypothetical lifetime romance;
- sexual joking;
- kissing.

The better formulation is now:

> **Nana/Hachi is explicitly romance-adjacent and queer-coded within Hachi's own retrospective consciousness, while still not canonically constituted as a mutual romantic couple.**

That is a materially stronger claim.

## 2. 707 is now explicitly home

Volume 2 suggested it.

Volume 3 almost states it.

Nana says she has no hometown.

Future Hachi points to 707.

That is the argument.

## 3. Hachi's independence is more serious than it initially looked

She is bad with money.

She fantasizes constantly.

But she:

- works;
- budgets;
- saves;
- seeks stable employment;
- worries about long-term housing.

The correct reading is not that she wants someone else to completely maintain her.

She wants a relational life and is slowly realizing that she must acquire the material competence to sustain it.

## 4. Shoji's earlier healthy boundary does not guarantee later ethical behavior

Volume 2 deserved to be fair to him.

He rightly resisted Hachi simply becoming his domestic servant.

Volume 3 now adds:

> he fails to establish equivalent boundaries with Sachiko.

Characters do not remain morally legible through a single good decision.

## 5. “Home” now has a dark counterexample in Shin

707 shows chosen home through reciprocal contribution.

Shin shows what can happen when a young person lacks stable home and must make his body/social desirability part of his survival strategy.

That makes the home theme materially more serious.

## 6. Future Hachi's narration is now interpretive

She no longer merely mourns remembered scenes.

She tells us how she now understands feelings she could not fully classify then.

This makes the temporal structure essential to character psychology, not just atmosphere.

# LXXXVI. The bonus 「淳子の部屋」

The two bonus pages at the end are explicitly marked:

> **おまけページ**

and stage the familiar metatextual **7F スナック 淳子の部屋** space.

Yasu and Shin participate in fourth-wall comedy about the bonus-page environment and serialization itself.

Following the source hierarchy, this material is useful evidence of Yazawa's self-parodic tonal register and the series' willingness to let characters temporarily become comic performers outside ordinary dramatic continuity.

It is **not** evidence for reconstructing serious mainline psychology or chronology.

That separation becomes increasingly important as the collected volumes accumulate more bonus material.

# LXXXVII. Volume thesis

Volume 3 is the volume where *NANA* begins to distinguish **love, home, and relationship status as three different things**.

Hachi possesses a boyfriend.

That does not guarantee that she feels at home with him.

Nana is not Hachi's recognized romantic partner.

That does not prevent Nana from feeling like:

- first love;
- emotional refuge;
- provider fantasy;
- artistic enchantment;
- chosen family;
- the person future Hachi believes kept her alive through catastrophe.

Nana says she has no hometown.

Future Hachi answers:

> the table is still there.

Shin lacks reliable home and converts intimacy into survival.

Hachi dreams of a future house and discovers that houses require wages and savings.

BLAST itself becomes another form of home: not the old band restored, but a new collective capable of carrying old memory forward.

Shoji and Sachiko show the reverse problem.

They have real feelings.

But real feelings do not automatically create an ethical relationship.

They conceal.

Avoid.

Comfort.

Cross boundaries.

Feel guilty.

Try to stop.

Fail to stop cleanly.

And thereby reveal one of the volume's strongest arguments:

> **Sincerity is not the same thing as responsibility.**

A person can genuinely love someone and still behave badly toward another person.

A person can care and still need to leave.

A person can mean to comfort and thereby deepen an entanglement.

A person can be difficult to love without deserving deception.

A relationship can feel intensely romantic without feeling like home.

And a relationship that is not socially categorized as romance can become the place where someone most completely belongs.

That gives us the strongest cumulative formulation so far:

> **Volume 1 asked whether a person can love without surrendering herself.**  
> **Volume 2 asked whether two independent people can build a shared home.**  
> **Volume 3 asks what happens when home and romance stop pointing toward the same person.**

Hachi still thinks the future she wants is:

> Shoji → marriage → house.

But the actual architecture of her life now looks increasingly different:

> Nana → 707 → BLAST → shared table → chosen community.

The manga has not declared one of these structures authentic and the other false.

Instead it makes them coexist.

That is what gives the volume its unusual emotional maturity.

And its final image makes the stakes unmistakable.

Shoji is coming to talk.

Hachi does not yet know what is coming.

Future Hachi does.

And when she remembers that night, the person she identifies as standing between herself and the bottom of the Tama River is not her boyfriend.

It is Nana.
