# 10 — Japanese Voice, Address, Relational Language, and Lexical System
## How relationships become audible before they become settled

### Governing thesis

*NANA* does not use Japanese merely to carry information that could be transferred without loss into any other language. The manga repeatedly places relational meaning inside first-person choice, address terms, nicknames, honorifics, commands, requests, apologies, sentence endings, ellipsis, lexical recurrence, and the contrast between what a character calls a bond and what the bond is materially doing. The most useful linguistic question is therefore not simply “what does this line mean?” but:

> **Why does this person speak this way to this person at this moment, and what kind of relationship does that way of speaking attempt to create, defend, deny, or revise?**

The mature series-level conclusion is:

> **In *NANA*, relational language does not merely describe relationships after they exist. Address, pronouns, sentence form, commands, apologies, omissions, and recurring lexical choices actively negotiate who may claim whom, decide for whom, remain beside whom, and return to whom.**

This document is not a concordance. Document 16 provides the retrieval index for representative terms and verified source routes. The task here is interpretive: to identify the character-specific and relationship-specific systems those terms participate in.

Three cautions govern the analysis.

First, fictional Japanese is stylized. A sentence-final form or first-person pronoun can contribute to characterization without functioning as a deterministic code for gender, class, sexuality, or personality. Second, no single lexical item settles the category of a relationship. `恋`, `好き`, `愛してる`, `片想い`, `恋人`, `性欲`, `家族`, and `信頼` overlap in human life without becoming interchangeable. Third, Chapters 81–84 survive here through fan translation. They remain usable for event, visual, relational, and thematic continuity, but not for claims about the original Japanese wording.

# 1. Shared `あたし`, different selves

Both Nana Komatsu/Hachi and Nana Osaki generally inhabit the feminine first-person `あたし`, but the shared pronoun should not obscure how different their voices are. Yazawa does not need to distinguish the two protagonists by assigning one a highly marked pronoun and the other a neutral one. Their difference emerges from what they do with the same grammatical resources.

Hachi's speech tends to turn feeling into narration. She explains herself, dramatizes herself, imagines how another person sees her, changes interpretive frames midstream, apologizes, and supplies an emotional story around events. Her retrospective voice intensifies this tendency: future Hachi does not merely remember events but repeatedly asks what they meant, what she failed to understand, and whether a different recognition might have changed what followed. The linguistic habit is continuous with her broader psychology. Hachi wants emotion to become socially intelligible. When she cannot secure certainty through the relationship itself, she often tries to secure it by narrating the relationship.

This is why her early description of Nana is unusually important. She does not state the category as a bare fact. She says:

> 「あたしのナナへの憧れは かなり恋に近いものだったと思います」

Her admiration for Nana was, she thinks, something quite close to `恋`. `[NANA_V03_E002]`

The sentence is both strong and careful. `かなり恋に近い` does not erase the romantic category; it explicitly brings it into the sentence. But `近い` and the retrospective `と思います` preserve interpretive distance. Hachi is classifying her own earlier feeling rather than issuing an external ontology of the relationship.

One page later she calls the experience:

> 「とても幸福な初恋みたいだったよ」

something like a very happy first love. `[NANA_V03_E003]`

Again the analogy marker matters. `みたい` does not weaken the emotional force into irrelevance; it tells us that Hachi reaches for the semantic field of first love because ordinary friendship language is insufficient while the conventional couple category is not simply available.

Nana's speech produces almost the inverse effect. Her language more often compresses vulnerability into assertion, rough humor, teasing, insult, command, or silence. She can be verbally direct while remaining emotionally defensive. The result is a crucial distinction:

> **Hachi often speaks because she wants emotion to become legible. Nana often speaks in ways that prevent emotion from becoming usable against her.**

Nana's roughness should therefore not be confused with emotional simplicity. Some of her most revealing moments occur when the usual armor fails, when a sentence becomes unexpectedly plain, when joking possessiveness becomes explicit self-critique, or when narration grants the reader access to language she would not use in ordinary conversation.

The shared `あたし` thus creates a productive formal equality between the protagonists. Neither is grammatically installed as the neutral default while the other becomes marked deviation. The series makes two strongly differentiated female subjectivities from overlapping linguistic material.

# 2. Hachi: language as relationship-making

Hachi's voice is unusually sensitive to social categories because she habitually asks what kind of story she is living. `恋`, `恋人`, `運命`, `幸せ`, `家庭`, `家族`, `片想い`, and `帰る` are not abstract terms for her; they are ways of organizing experience.

That tendency can make Hachi perceptive and self-deceiving at the same time. She can identify the emotional structure of a scene with striking precision while building an optimistic narrative around incomplete information. The manga's linguistic treatment of her therefore resists the idea that verbal elaboration equals self-knowledge.

Her Nana language provides the clearest example. In Volume 3, she imagines:

> 「もしもナナが男だったら 一世一代の恋が出来るのに」

If Nana were a man, she could have the love of a lifetime. `[NANA_V03_E012]`

The counterfactual reveals rather than cancels romantic structure. Hachi does not say the relationship is literally a conventional heterosexual romance. She imagines a change in Nana's sex because the imagined change would make a socially legible romantic script available for a bond already carrying extraordinary affective force.

Later she can say, with more pain than fantasy:

> 「なんだか やっぱり片想いだ」

It somehow still feels like unrequited love. `[NANA_V13_E151]`

`片想い` names asymmetry. It does not prove that Nana has no reciprocal attachment; indeed the later corpus demonstrates the opposite. What it reveals is Hachi's recurring way of measuring legitimacy: if she does not feel chosen, needed, or granted a recognizable place, she experiences the bond as one-sided even when the other person's attachment is intense.

This becomes explicit in Volume 7:

> 「独り占めしたかったんじゃない」
>
> 「必要とされたかっただけなの」

She did not want to monopolize Nana; she only wanted to be needed. `[NANA_V07_E012]`

This distinction becomes one of the series' most important lexical corrections. Hachi is capable of criticizing `独り占め` while remaining dependent on `必要とされる` as a proof of belonging. The late extant narrative forces that distinction open: if Nana no longer visibly needs rescuing, does Hachi still believe she has the right simply to be beside her?

Hachi's language of home undergoes a similar maturation. `家庭` is not a term imposed on her from outside. She says explicitly:

> 「あたしは自分の家庭を持ちたいの！」

She wants a household/family home of her own. `[NANA_V10_E073]`

But later `帰る` becomes more complicated than returning to the legally recognized household. She can want to return to 707 and simultaneously refuse that return because she knows she would use other people's kindness as emotional escape:

> 「出来るなら仕事を見つけて 707号室に帰りたい」 `[NANA_V13_E006]`
>
> 「今707号室に帰っても あたしはみんなの優しさに甘えちゃうだけだ」 `[NANA_V13_E138]`

The language distinguishes desire from responsible action. A place can be home without every return to it being healthy.

# 3. Nana: roughness, refusal, and the exposure of need

Nana's characteristic voice gains much of its force from the mismatch between outward hardness and inward dependency. She is often socially legible as the tougher protagonist because her speech can be blunt, masculine-coded in flavor, confrontational, and resistant to sentimental explanation. But the text repeatedly shows that linguistic hardness is a defensive competence rather than evidence of low relational need.

The early Ren/Yasu exchange provides a compact ethical-linguistic model. When Ren's departure creates the possibility that Nana should follow, Yasu says:

> 「ナナはレンの飼い猫じゃねぇぞ」
>
> 「それはナナが決める事だ」

Nana is not Ren's pet cat; it is Nana's decision. `[NANA_V01_E009]`

The line matters throughout the series because the pet/ownership field later becomes one of Nana's own recurrent metaphors. At the point where she can recognize the danger more explicitly, she says:

> 「レンもハチ公も…あたしの所有物なわけじゃない」

Ren and Hachi are not her possessions. `[NANA_V06_E020]`

`所有物` is unusually stark. The line shows self-knowledge, but *NANA* never treats correct vocabulary as instantaneous ethical transformation. Nana can know that people are not property while still feeling jealousy, testing attachment, fearing replacement, or imagining security through possession.

The nickname `ハチ`/`ハチ公` sits inside this contradiction. It begins as Nana's comic act of naming, an affectionate comparison that also lightly animalizes Hachi. The nickname then circulates through the 707 network until it becomes a social identity independent of Nana's sole use. The name therefore performs two movements at once:

1. Nana authors a form of intimacy.
2. The community demonstrates that intimacy cannot remain entirely owned by its originator.

That pattern recurs across the manga. A relationship begins with a private linguistic invention and becomes part of a social world that neither participant completely controls.

Nana's later vocabulary also shows movement away from unbreakable possession toward relation capable of surviving separateness. The word family `繋ぐ / 繋がる` becomes important here. In Volume 13 the text distinguishes connection from a magically unbreakable knot:

> 「繋ぐものなんだよ」 `[NANA_V13_E147]`

and later characters speak of wanting to connect more properly:

> 「もっとちゃんと繋がりたい」 `[NANA_V16_E049]`

The mature relational ideal is not absence of attachment. It is attachment whose continuity must be maintained rather than guaranteed through ownership.

# 4. Names, nicknames, honorifics, and relational jurisdiction

Japanese address terms make relationship boundaries audible even when the semantic content of a line is ordinary. *NANA* repeatedly exploits the difference among given names, surnames, nicknames, honorifics, professional identities, kinship labels, and status labels.

The most important examples include `ナナ`, `ハチ`/`ハチ公`, `レン`, `ノブ`, `ヤス`, `シン`, `レイラ`/`レイラさん`, `タクミ`, wife/husband terminology, band-member labels, and public-professional forms.

A useful rule is:

> **Address is evidence of a relationship's current grammar, not proof of its complete emotional content.**

Someone may use an intimate name while behaving coercively. Someone may retain an honorific while feeling profound attachment. Someone may be publicly reduced to a formal relational label that conceals the complexity of private life.

Hachi's transformation into “Takumi's wife” in public discourse is the clearest institutional version. The label has real legal and protective force. At Ren's funeral, that status helps Hachi intervene against press intrusion. But the same label can also compress her into metadata: wife, pregnant woman, Nana's former roommate or best friend. Public language can recognize a relationship and simultaneously reduce a person.

Shin's address to Reira is equally important. His ordinary `レイラさん` preserves an audible asymmetry. The relationship may be emotionally intimate and the manga may sometimes frame it romantically, but the honorific does not let the adult/minor and celebrity/status difference disappear into atmosphere. Shin's experience, sexual knowledge, and performed sophistication do not linguistically turn him into Reira's adult peer.

Nicknames also reveal how the series treats chosen family. `ヤス`, `ノブ`, and `シン` belong to a social world in which intimacy is often expressed through shortened or familiar forms rather than through explicit declarations. These forms create a texture of everyday belonging that is easy to flatten in translation because English may simply reproduce the same Romanized name in every context.

# 5. Takumi: when competence becomes grammar

Takumi is the character for whom syntax and conversational sequencing most clearly reveal power. His controlling tendencies do not appear only when he raises his voice, threatens, or uses physical force. They also appear in how efficiently he converts an uncertain situation into a settled plan.

His characteristic movement is:

> **possibility → practical plan → apparent necessity → presumed joint decision**

This is one reason Takumi can be genuinely reassuring. He is often the person in the room most capable of turning chaos into an executable future. Money, transportation, professional authority, housing, information, and confidence give him a large repertoire of workable declaratives.

But the same competence creates ethical risk. If one person can formulate reality faster than everyone else, a decision can be rhetorically closed before another person has finished deciding whether it is theirs.

The linguistic question is therefore not whether imperatives are inherently abusive. Japanese intimate speech routinely contains commands, fragments, dropped subjects, and direct forms that would sound harsher if translated literally. The relevant pattern is relational: who repeatedly gets to define the problem, state the available options, set the timetable, and speak as though agreement has already occurred?

This is the sense in which Takumi's speech can turn **care into jurisdiction through grammar**.

The corpus's ethical conclusions remain important here. Takumi's direct `愛してる` is meaningful:

> 「愛してるって言ったの」 `[NANA_V13_E165]`

Love-language does not become false merely because the relationship also contains coercion. But neither does `愛してる` retroactively legalize or morally cleanse non-consensual acts. The word tells us something about feeling and relational claim; it does not decide every ethical question around that feeling.

Similarly, the language of material provision can be genuinely caring while also narrowing exit. The manga repeatedly separates `愛情` from capacity. Nobu's painful recognition that:

> 「愛情だけじゃ子供は育てられない」

love alone cannot raise a child `[NANA_V08_E080]` is one of the clearest statements that affect and infrastructure are different categories.

# 6. Yasu: trust before title

Yasu's mature relational vocabulary offers one of the strongest alternatives to possession. His conduct is not ethically flawless; he can be paternalistic, violent, secretive, and overinvolved. But his best moments repeatedly return choice to the person concerned rather than converting care into permanent authority.

The key lexical term is `信頼`:

> 「信頼で結ばれる」 `[NANA_V17_E149]`

The phrase matters because it imagines a bond strong enough to hold without requiring a more totalizing formal category. Trust can connect persons while leaving them separate.

This is close to the mature `繋ぐ` system. The bond is not valuable because it cannot be broken; it is valuable because people continue to maintain it. That is a less spectacular model of intimacy than the death-pact, red-thread, possession, or rescue fantasies elsewhere in the manga, but it becomes increasingly important as those stronger guarantees fail.

Yasu's language also demonstrates why titles cannot substitute for relationship. Formal roles can be real and socially consequential, but the series repeatedly asks whether the person behind the role remains visible. Yasu's strongest care recognizes the person first and the title second.

# 7. Shin and Reira: sophistication does not erase marked asymmetry

The Shin/Reira relationship requires especially disciplined linguistic reading because romantic coding, sexual experience, age, money, celebrity, and vulnerability are all present at once.

Shin's ordinary first-person `僕` and his use of `レイラさん` help preserve his youth and relational position. These forms do not make him naive. He can be cynical, sexually experienced, observant, and socially skilled. But they prevent a common interpretive shortcut: treating his competence as proof that the age asymmetry has ceased to matter.

Reira's voice varies sharply by context. She can sound like a polished celebrity, a teasing intimate, a commanding professional, a frightened dependent, or someone regressing into childlike need. Those variations are not evidence that the character is incoherent. They map the different roles through which other people encounter her: star, singer, protected object, friend, desired woman, refuge, patient, and institutional asset.

The lexical system around singing is especially revealing. Reira explicitly names singing as:

> `生きがい` — a reason for living / life-purpose. `[NANA_V15_E059–E062; routed in Document 16]`

But late in the corpus she can refuse to sing as the only available form of struggle:

> 「今出来る唯一の戦いは 歌わない事」 `[NANA_V20_E015]`

The apparent contradiction is the point. If singing is genuinely hers, then she must retain the ability not to sing. Vocation without refusal becomes institutional ownership.

The same logic applies relationally. Genuine attachment does not erase structural asymmetry; structural asymmetry does not prove every feeling false.

# 8. Apology, gratitude, and incomplete relational repair

Two ordinary Japanese speech acts—`ごめん` and `ありがとう`—are especially easy to overtranslate as complete resolution.

An apology can perform several different functions:

- recognize that another person is hurt;
- admit fault;
- express shame;
- ask for a conflict to stop;
- acknowledge damage without explaining motive;
- or seek permission to remain in relation.

These functions overlap but are not identical. A character saying `ごめん` does not automatically establish that the underlying problem has been understood, repaired, or forgiven.

`ありがとう` is similarly multivalent. Gratitude can receive care without accepting every implication attached to the care. It can soften refusal. It can close a scene whose central question remains unanswered. It can acknowledge another person's generosity while leaving the relationship category unchanged.

The broader principle is:

> **Acknowledging another person's care is not the same as accepting the relationship claim attached to that care.**

This matters throughout *NANA* because characters frequently mistake emotional recognition for relational restoration. Someone can be moved, grateful, guilty, affectionate, or dependent and still not have given the answer another person wants.

# 9. Love vocabulary is a field, not a ladder

The corpus uses a family of terms that English often compresses into “love.” They should not be arranged as a simple hierarchy from weak to strong.

## `好き`

`好き` can indicate liking, attraction, love, preference, and attachment depending on context. Its ambiguity is not a defect to be solved automatically by translating it more strongly or weakly. Speaker, addressee, scene, and relationship history matter.

## `恋`

`恋` is explicitly relevant to Nana/Hachi. Hachi says her admiration was `かなり恋に近い`; she imagines a `一世一代の恋`; later the relationship is repeatedly framed through romantic grammar. These are strong textual facts. They do not by themselves establish a completed conventional couple.

## `初恋`

Hachi's `初恋みたい` and Nana's later first-love-like comparison demonstrate that first-love imagery can characterize an emotional structure without functioning as a legal category.

## `恋人`

The counterfactual:

> 「たとえば あたし達が恋人同士なら」 `[NANA_V07_E011]`

is important because it makes the category visible precisely through its conditional form. The manga knows the difference between what Nana and Hachi are and what a conventional `恋人同士` label would permit or demand.

## `性欲`

Nana's:

> 「でも性欲ではない」 `[NANA_V08_E013]`

should be preserved exactly at its evidentiary level. Nana distinguishes the feeling from sexual desire as she understands it at that point. The line cannot responsibly be generalized into “the bond is definitively nonsexual in every possible future.”

## `愛してる`

Direct love-language is strong evidence of attachment. It is not an ethical solvent. Takumi can say or mean love while still violating Hachi's autonomy elsewhere.

## `片想い`

Hachi uses unrequited-love language to name felt asymmetry. That statement is evidence of her experience, not proof that Nana lacks reciprocal love or attachment.

The mature Nana/Hachi formulation therefore remains linguistically disciplined:

> **Explicit romantic coding is established; final categorical form is not.**

# 10. Fate language does not create a single predestined couple

`運命` and `赤い糸` carry powerful cultural and romantic force in *NANA*. The error would be treating each occurrence as an omniscient declaration from the author.

Future Hachi says of meeting Nana:

> 「これはやっぱり運命だと思う 笑ってもいいよ」 `[NANA_V02_E001]`

The phrase is explicitly framed as Hachi's thought: `と思う`. She interprets contingency as fate.

Elsewhere characters use `運命` for Nana/Ren and for other bonds:

> 「ナナとレンは 結ばれなきゃ いけない運命なんだよ」 `[NANA_V12_E080]`

and the text describes another person as someone toward whom fate was felt more strongly than anyone met before:

> 「それまで出会った誰より 運命を感じる相手だった」 `[NANA_V12_E127]`

Likewise `赤い糸` appears as relationship imagery in more than one context. `[NANA_V12_E128; NANA_V15_E106]`

The correct conclusion is not that fate language is empty. It is that the manga shows characters using fate to convert contingency into felt necessity. Fate is part of the emotional vocabulary through which people explain why a bond seems uniquely meaningful. It is not a mechanical authorial stamp assigning one exclusive endgame.

# 11. Happiness, dream, and the failure of simple equivalence

`幸せ` and `夢` form one of the series' most important lexical contrasts.

Hachi remembers believing:

> 「夢がひとつ叶う毎に 幸せになって行ける気がしてた」 `[NANA_V06_E004]`

and then asks:

> 「夢が叶う事と 幸せになる事は どうして別ものなんだろう」 `[NANA_V06_E013]`

Why are having a dream come true and becoming happy different things?

The series spends the rest of its extant narrative refusing to collapse them again. Professional success can create money, access, artistic possibility, and social recognition without producing a livable private world. Marriage can create household legitimacy and stability without guaranteeing emotional safety. Romantic intensity can feel like destiny without becoming sustainable adulthood.

Later the question becomes:

> 「幸せのゴールなんてあるのかな」 `[NANA_V13_E017]`

Is there even a finish line for happiness?

And Reira's desire for `フツーに幸せ` `[NANA_V19_E131]` is especially revealing because extraordinary celebrity success has failed to make ordinary reciprocal life unnecessary.

`夢` likewise changes from aspirational script to something insufficient by itself:

> `夢だけじゃ生きられない` `[NANA_V19_E193]`

The mature lexical lesson is not anti-dream. It is that dreams must enter material, relational, and temporal structures capable of sustaining a person.

# 12. Home vocabulary: `家`, `家庭`, `家族`, `故郷`, `帰る`

English “home” can flatten several Japanese categories that *NANA* keeps distinct.

`家` can name a dwelling or household location. `家庭` names domestic household/family life. `家族` names family but does not specify whether the relation is biological, legal, chosen, or emotionally functional. `故郷` invokes hometown or place of origin. `帰る` is an action of return whose object can be spatial, relational, or psychological.

Nana's:

> 「あたしには故郷などない」 `[NANA_V03_E004]`

is therefore not equivalent to “I do not have a house.” It rejects the category of origin-home itself. Later `帰れる故郷がない` `[NANA_V20_E022]` gives the absence a mature relational weight: a place one can return to is not guaranteed by birthplace.

Hachi, by contrast, explicitly wants `家庭`, but she also comes to experience 707 as a return-space even when it is no longer her ordinary residence. The future corpus further multiplies home: legal household, natal family, professional base, preserved 707, ritual gathering place, and the person toward whom one remains oriented can coexist.

This is why a mature translation strategy should resist using “home” as though every occurrence referred to the same concept.

# 13. Time and future: from script to blank page

The Japanese future vocabulary becomes especially important after Ren's death.

Earlier, future language can be prospective and authorial: if the present goes wrong, perhaps a brighter scenario can still be drawn. By Volume 21 the metaphor changes:

> 「昨日までの設計図はもう使えない」 `[NANA_V21_E029]`

The blueprint used until yesterday can no longer be used.

Then:

> 「未来は全て白紙になった」 `[NANA_V21_E034]`

The future became entirely blank.

And:

> 「今もそこに何も描けずにいるの」 `[NANA_V21_E035]`

She still cannot draw anything there.

`白紙` is not a cheerful “fresh start.” Its force comes from the failure of an already-authored future. The old design has become unusable, and no replacement has yet appeared.

Nana's subjective time likewise stops:

> 「あたしの時間は止まった」 `[NANA_V21_E016]`

Chronological time continues. Subjective time does not. The distinction is central to Document 09's account of memory and future narration.

The late survival vocabulary is therefore deliberately modest. Rather than demanding a complete recovered future, Nana can arrive at the possibility of trying to live one more day. Survival no longer requires possessing a finished narrative of what life will become.

# 14. Work, professionalism, and the right to speak

The music-industry vocabulary demonstrates another movement from desire into institutional form.

Nana says:

> 「歌はあたしの生きる為の手段」 `[NANA_V08_E006]`

Singing is a means by which she lives. That formulation protects music from being reduced to either pure self-expression or commercial career. It is existential and practical at once.

Professionalization then adds `プロ`, commercial calculation, contracts, schedules, market demand, publicity, and hierarchy. None automatically negates authentic music. Instead, the problem becomes whether professional success increases or decreases the artist's ability to decide what she makes.

Nana eventually seeks enough success to gain:

> `発言権` — a meaningful say / decision-making leverage. `[NANA_V19_E100; routed in D08/D16]`

The word is politically revealing. She does not imagine that artistic purity exists outside power. She wants enough power inside the professional structure to speak.

Reira's refusal to sing completes the other side of the system. If the institution depends on her output, her ability to refuse remains the clearest proof that the voice is still hers.

# 15. Relational vocabulary matures from possession toward connection

Across the complete Japanese corpus, several lexical families form an interpretable sequence:

> `所有物` / `独り占め` / binding / pet-collar imagery
>
> → `必要とされる`
>
> → `依存`
>
> → `信頼`
>
> → `繋ぐ`
>
> → waiting, searching, returning, remaining beside

This is not a neat historical replacement in which early words disappear. Possessive desire remains psychologically real, and being needed remains attractive. The development is ethical rather than lexical erasure: later language gives the characters more ways to imagine attachment without total jurisdiction.

`依存` is especially important because the manga can use clinicalizing or problem-focused language where reliance has become severe enough to narrow agency:

- `精神的な依存症だって立派な中毒` `[NANA_V11_E121]`
- `もうおれに依存してちゃダメ` `[NANA_V14_E086]`
- `そいつにすがらないと 生きて行く事も出来ない` `[NANA_V19_E115]`

These lines should not be generalized into the claim that all dependence is pathology. The later relational ideal is not independence from everyone. It is connection in which a person can still decide, refuse, and survive plurality.

That is why `信頼で結ばれる` and `繋ぐ` matter so much. The series increasingly values bonds maintained by practice rather than guaranteed through ownership.

# 16. Translation boundary: Chapters 81–84

The continuation chapters are essential to the mature narrative synthesis, but their surviving working-language provenance places a hard limit on this document.

The supplied archive uses English fan translation for Chapters 81–83 and most of Chapter 84, with Spanish translation supplying the six missing printed pages in Chapter 84. This is enough to establish broad semantic content, panel order, scene structure, relationship behavior, and visual form. It is not enough to establish original Japanese:

- first-person choice;
- second-person choice;
- honorific presence or omission;
- sentence-final particles;
- politeness level;
- register shift;
- exact lexical recurrence;
- phonological pun;
- or the pragmatic force of a Japanese wording that survives only through translation.

Accordingly, phrases such as Hachi's Chapter 84 statement about “our future” and the final question of whether “Takumi's wife” is still needed beside Nana remain narrative and thematic evidence. They do not enter this document as reconstructed Japanese quotations.

This segregation is not a minor caveat. It is necessary to keep the source hierarchy honest.

# 17. Public voice, private voice, and register as role performance

The series also repeatedly distinguishes the voice a person uses as a social role from the voice available in private. This is clearest in the celebrity layer, but it extends well beyond celebrities. Public language makes a person legible to institutions; private language can reveal how much of that legibility the person actually inhabits.

Nana the singer, Reira the star, Takumi the producer, Hachi the wife, Yasu the responsible adult, and Shin the prematurely self-sufficient young man all possess speech patterns that become more stable when a role is being performed. The instability appears when the role stops containing the person. Reira can move from polished performer to dependent private speech; Hachi can move from the socially recognized category of wife into narration that asks whether the role corresponds to what her marriage emotionally is; Nana can move from stage command to silence or exposed need.

This is why public titles are never useless in *NANA*. They have material consequences. They determine who may enter a hospital, who can speak to press, who can claim a household, who can protect someone through status, and who can be marketed. But the title is not the whole person. The manga's relational language repeatedly places private address beside public role so that the gap remains audible.

The same principle applies to future narration. Future Hachi's address `ねえ ナナ` and future Nana's answering `ねえ ハチ` are not ordinary dialogue turns. They are apostrophic forms: speech directed toward an absent person across time and distance. The second person remains present in the grammar even when reciprocal hearing is not established. The form itself therefore preserves relation without proving communication.

That matters methodologically. An address can establish whom the narrator remains oriented toward. It cannot establish that the addressee received, accepted, or answered the speech.

---

# 18. Commands, requests, and the ethics of asking

Directness in Japanese cannot be mapped mechanically onto dominance. Intimates often use plain imperatives, fragments, omitted subjects, and command-like forms without the interaction being coercive. Conversely, highly polite or calm language can exercise enormous power. The ethical unit is therefore not grammatical form in isolation but the relationship between form, alternatives, material leverage, and the other person's ability to answer.

The series' mature movement from forcing toward asking is most visible when characters who previously treated love as entitlement begin to tolerate refusal. Ren's late recognition that he does not want to impose himself by force is lexically concentrated in:

> 「強引に押しつけたく ねえんだよ」 `[NANA_V20_E013]`

The line does not erase earlier coercive or possessive behavior. It matters because it names a change in the desired *form* of relation. He wants the other person's answer to remain part of the relationship rather than treating his own intensity as sufficient authority.

That distinction also clarifies why Yasu's best interventions feel different from Takumi's. Both men can be decisive. Both can possess more practical information than the person in crisis. The difference is often whether the speech returns the decision to the other person or closes the decision on the other's behalf.

The ethical endpoint of this linguistic system is therefore not universal softness. It is **answerability**. A relation remains between persons when an utterance leaves room for another subject to produce a meaningful yes, no, not yet, or different plan.

# 21. Frozen linguistic propositions

The following propositions are stable enough to route into the final corpus.

1. Hachi and Nana commonly share `あたし`; their voices are differentiated primarily by syntax, register, relational behavior, and narrative function rather than by radically different pronouns.
2. Hachi's voice tends to narrativize emotion and classify relationships.
3. Nana's voice more often protects vulnerability through compression, roughness, teasing, command, or silence.
4. Shared pronouns do not imply interchangeable femininities.
5. Nicknames function as relational acts and can outgrow the person who originated them.
6. `ハチ`/`ハチ公` is affectionate, comic, lightly animalizing, domestic, and socially portable.
7. Address terms provide evidence of relational grammar, not complete emotional ontology.
8. Shin's `僕` and `レイラさん` preserve youth/status asymmetry even inside romantic framing.
9. Takumi's conversational power often operates by turning possibility into apparent necessity and then into presumed agreement.
10. Practical competence can become jurisdiction through grammar without making every direct statement coercive.
11. An apology does not automatically establish full comprehension, repair, or forgiveness.
12. Gratitude does not automatically accept every relational claim attached to an act of care.
13. `恋`, `初恋`, `恋人`, `性欲`, `愛してる`, and `片想い` are distinct relational terms, not steps on a single ladder.
14. Nana/Hachi is explicitly placed in the lexical field of `恋`.
15. Nana's `性欲ではない` is a time-bounded self-description, not proof that sexuality is impossible in every reading or future.
16. Fate vocabulary is characterially and culturally meaningful but not an exclusive authorial endgame stamp.
17. `夢` and `幸せ` become explicitly non-equivalent.
18. `家`, `家庭`, `家族`, `故郷`, and `帰る` must not be collapsed into one English “home” category.
19. `所有物` and `独り占め` expose possession as a relational danger recognized inside the text.
20. `必要とされる` is a central Hachi metric of relational legitimacy that the late corpus increasingly questions.
21. `依存` is used where reliance has become severe or agency-narrowing; the manga does not condemn all interdependence.
22. `信頼` and `繋ぐ` supply a mature alternative to unbreakable possession.
23. Reira's `生きがい` and later refusal to sing are compatible: a vocation is most genuinely hers if refusal remains possible.
24. `発言権` reveals that Nana seeks artistic authority through professional leverage rather than through withdrawal from industry.
25. Post-Ren `白紙` means an unusable future design, not an uncomplicated fresh start.
26. Chapters 81–84 cannot support original-Japanese micro-linguistic claims in the present corpus.

# 22. Final synthesis

The Japanese language of *NANA* does not produce a hidden solution beneath the plot. It makes the plot's relational negotiations more precise.

The early and middle series repeatedly reaches for strong guarantees: fate, red thread, possession, romantic titles, household roles, being needed, professional identity, and the fantasy that the right name can make a bond permanent. Yet the narrative continually demonstrates that a label can be real without being sufficient. A spouse can be loved and unsafe. A friend can occupy the structural position of a central romance without the relationship becoming a conventional couple. A singer can love singing and need the right not to sing. A home can remain home even when returning to it would be avoidance. A person can be needed without being freely chosen, and can be freely chosen without being indispensable.

The mature lexical movement therefore does not abandon attachment. It changes the grammar through which attachment is imagined:

> **from possession toward connection; from automatic necessity toward decision; from title toward trust; from unbreakable binding toward a relation people continue to maintain.**

That movement is unfinished because the manga is unfinished. But it is already visible in the language.

The best compact formulation is:

> **In *NANA*, a relationship becomes ethically mature not when Japanese supplies the perfect noun for it, but when the people inside it can keep speaking as separate subjects—able to ask, refuse, decide, leave, return, and remain connected without one voice becoming the other's permanent script.**
