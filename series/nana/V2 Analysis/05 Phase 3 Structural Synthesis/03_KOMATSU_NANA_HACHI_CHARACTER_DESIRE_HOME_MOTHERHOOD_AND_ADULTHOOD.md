# 03 — Komatsu Nana / Hachi: Desire, Home, Motherhood, and Adulthood
## How Hachi learns to build a life without making another person's need the proof that she deserves one

### Scope and governing question

This document is the definitive Phase-3 character study of **小松奈々 / Komatsu Nana / Hachi** across the complete extant primary-fiction corpus: Japanese tankōbon Volumes 1–21 and the uncollected continuation through Chapter 84. It inherits the chronology and structural propositions stabilized in `01_SERIES_ARCHITECTURE_CHRONOLOGY_AND_VOLUME_PROGRESSION.md`, uses the Osaki Nana study only as a companion rather than a substitute for Hachi's independent perspective, and routes disputed claims through the frozen Phase-2 evidence layer.

*NANA 7.8* is deliberately excluded. Its profiles and editorial framing will enter only after the manga-derived synthesis is stable. The anime is not evidence here.

The governing question is:

> **How does Hachi turn desire for romance, home, and being needed into an increasingly self-authored form of adulthood?**

The shortest answer is that she does not mature by ceasing to want ordinary things.

Hachi still wants love. She still wants a home. She still wants marriage, family, food shared at a table, a partner who comes back, children who are safe, friends who remain reachable, clothing and domestic beauty, and a life whose important people visibly choose one another. The manga never requires her to reject those desires in order to become serious. Instead it repeatedly asks whether she can distinguish the **form** of a desired life from the ethical and material conditions that make that life worth inhabiting.

Early Hachi often uses romance as a narrative technology. A relationship gives ordinary life plot, identifies a future, and reassures her that she matters. Her vulnerability is therefore not simply sexual impulsiveness or naïveté. It is a tendency to let being chosen answer questions that the relationship itself cannot answer: Who am I? Where do I belong? Am I valuable? Will I be abandoned? Is this life going somewhere?

Across Shoji, Nana, Takumi, Nobu, pregnancy, marriage, work, motherhood, celebrity, grief, and the future narration, Hachi acquires a different set of competencies. She learns to save money, work, negotiate households, name sexual and emotional shame, choose pregnancy under uncertainty, distinguish biological from chosen parenthood, write letters when speech fails, construct rituals of return, use legal status strategically, perform skilled paid labor, recognize when protection becomes intrusion, and coordinate care among people who cannot reliably care for themselves or one another.

But her deepest vulnerability persists for much longer than her practical immaturity does:

> **Hachi wants to be needed because necessity feels like evidence that she cannot be replaced.**

Volume 7 makes that distinction explicit. She says she did not want Nana all to herself; she wanted Nana to need her. Later the same structure appears in marriage: she wants to be useful to Takumi, wants him to miss her when she is absent, fears Reira can occupy a place she cannot, and can imagine intense desire functioning as moral repair. In the continuation, after Ren's death, this structure finally begins to loosen. Hachi first returns home because she believes Takumi has nobody else; then admits that his refuge may not be her; then sees evidence that he copes through work and Reira rather than through dependence on Hachi. Love begins to separate from indispensability.

The mature character thesis is therefore:

> **Hachi begins as a young woman who uses romance to give ordinary life narrative form and who measures security through being chosen. She becomes an adult not by abandoning domestic desire, but by learning to author material forms of care—home, food, money, work, letters, parenthood, mediation, protection, and ritual—while gradually discovering that care does not make her omnipotent and being needed cannot be the sole proof of love or personal worth. Her development is a movement from wanting a relationship to tell her who she is toward building structures in which other people can live. The extant story stops just as she begins to understand that she may still have a place beside someone who does not need her to rescue them.**

That last problem is not resolved by Chapter 84.

---

# 1. Before Tokyo: romance as narrative technology

The easiest reductive reading of early Hachi is that she is “boy-crazy.” The label notices something real and then stops too soon.

Hachi does organize a disproportionate amount of identity around romance. She idealizes older men, confuses secrecy with intimacy, imagines dramatic love as a privileged route to adulthood, and experiences separation with an intensity that can exceed the actual durability of the relationship. Yet Volume 1 also shows that she is not simply unable to see reality. During the Asano relationship, she knows:

> **「この恋にしょせん未来なんかない」**

She understands that the affair has no durable future. What she wants is not merely the man. She wants the emotional density produced by the story around him.

Her own wording is revealing:

> **「映画みたいに ロマンチックで ドラマチックな恋」**

A movie-like, romantic, dramatic love.

This is the first important key to Hachi. She does not merely desire attachment; she desires **legibility**. Romance supplies a script in which ordinary gestures become evidence of destiny, suffering becomes proof of depth, and the future appears to have shape. That tendency will later attach not only to men but to Nana/Ren, Nana herself, marriage, motherhood, Cinderella imagery, red thread, and even Hachi's retrospective account of her own mistakes.

The same capacity is also one of her narrative gifts. Hachi sees symbolic and emotional connections other people miss. She can turn a meal, a room, a ring, a train, a song, or a domestic routine into a meaningful structure. Her problem is not that she narrativizes life. *NANA* itself does the same thing. The problem is that Hachi can mistake the story she needs for the structure other people have actually consented to inhabit.

That is why her development will require not disenchantment but **better narration**: stories that can hold ambiguity, other people's agency, material constraint, and endings that do not retroactively make everything before them false.

# 2. Tokyo: Hachi's migration is more self-authored than her stereotype

Hachi moves to Tokyo partly because Shoji is there. That fact should not be denied. It should also not swallow the entire action.

Volume 2 stabilizes a corrective that matters for the whole character arc. Hachi does not immediately run after Shoji. She remains behind, saves money, and says:

> **「泣く泣く地元残って上京資金貯めたんだ」**

She accumulated funds for the move. She intends to work. She wants a Tokyo life, not merely physical proximity to a boyfriend.

Her self-authorship is therefore present from the beginning in a less glamorous form than Nana Osaki's. Nana's autonomy is expressed through refusal, artistic vocation, and geographic independence. Hachi's appears through saving, practical migration, job seeking, household competence, and an effort to build ordinary continuity.

This difference is crucial. If independence is defined only as having a prestigious career, rejecting marriage, or needing nobody, Hachi will always look like the less developed woman. The manga repeatedly destabilizes that standard. Hachi can be materially dependent and still make meaningful choices; she can work without being career-driven; she can want a household without wanting to disappear inside another person's will.

Her early problem is not absence of agency.

It is that agency is often recruited into the service of a prewritten romantic destination.

# 3. 707: home as privacy, mutual aid, economics, and reciprocal presence

Apartment 707 is where Hachi's idea of home becomes more complex than “live with the man I love.”

Her earlier fantasy with Shoji is explicit:

> **「毎日 こんな風に彼の身の周りのお世話をして暮らせたら どんなに幸せかしら♡」**

She imagines happiness through caring service to a boyfriend. The fantasy is genuine and should not be mocked. But 707 introduces a different domestic form.

The apartment works because Nana and Hachi choose it independently, keep separate rooms, retain privacy, divide costs, and still become available to one another in emergencies. The home is intimate without requiring merger. It is economically practical and emotionally transformative at once.

For Hachi this matters more than the word “friendship” can capture. 707 becomes the place where she discovers that domesticity can be reciprocal rather than merely service performed for a romantic partner. She cooks, waits, worries, sleeps, talks, entertains, pays, quarrels, apologizes, and returns. Nana does the same in her own idiom.

The later future narration makes the home durable by memory. When Nana says she has no hometown, future Hachi effectively answers with the preserved table and chairs at 707. The apartment eventually becomes an annual ritual space where people wait for Nana. A temporary rental becomes a chosen origin point.

This gives Hachi one of her most important mature ideas:

> **Home is not only where a legally recognized family lives. It is a place built through repeated, reciprocal practices that make return possible.**

That is why later luxury cannot simply replace 707. Shirokane can provide security, space, money, and legal household status while Hachi still experiences **ホームシック** for the smaller apartment.

# 4. Nana: admiration, category failure, and the first expansion of Hachi's emotional world

Hachi's relationship with Nana changes what she thinks desire can be.

The first important point is epistemic caution. The manga gives Hachi explicitly romance-adjacent language, but it also repeatedly marks that language as analogy, counterfactual, or category uncertainty. Future Hachi says her admiration was:

> **「かなり恋に近いものだったと思います」**

and describes the period as:

> **「とても幸福な初恋みたいだったよ」**

She later thinks:

> **「もしもナナが男だったら 一世一代の恋が出来るのに」**

These are not neutral friendship formulations. They are also not sufficient by themselves to settle the categorical Nana/Hachi question. That belongs to Document 04.

For Hachi's individual development, the more important fact is that Nana breaks the simple hierarchy in which romantic partner automatically occupies every highest-value position. Hachi eventually tells Nana:

> **「彼氏よりずっと大事だよ ナナは！」**

She becomes jealous of Misato, wants joined hands to remain joined, imagines Nana inside her future home, and continues addressing Nana from the future regardless of whom she is romantically involved with.

The result is not “Hachi discovers her true partner and all men were mistakes.” The stronger conclusion is that Hachi's emotional world becomes **plural**. A spouse, a former lover, a child, a friend, a chosen family, and the person who feels like home can occupy different axes of importance without being reducible to one ranking.

This plurality is one of Hachi's greatest capacities. It is also one of her recurring sources of conflict because she often tries to convert plural attachments into a single coherent story.

---

# 5. Being seen: Hachi's shame is not the same thing as innocence

Hachi's comic presentation can make her look less self-aware than she is.

Volume 4 provides one of the sharpest corrections. She recognizes that she is no longer the “pure” person she wants Nana to see and says, in effect:

> **「もう純粋じゃなかった」**  
> **「純粋なフリをした」**

She performs innocence because she does not want Nana to dislike her.

This is central to later relationships. Hachi's fear is not simply “people will discover I have had sex” or “I made a mistake.” It is that being fully known will make her unworthy of care. She can therefore experience a person who appears to accept the unidealized self as extraordinarily safe.

This is part of Takumi's power over her. By Volume 14 Hachi can say:

> **「タクミはありのままのあたしを受け入れてくれた」**

That statement is Hachi's belief, and it should not be converted into an objective absolution of Takumi. But it names something real about why the bond persists. Takumi often seems to know that Hachi is sexually complicated, jealous, ordinary, dependent, angry, acquisitive, frightened, and imperfect without requiring the idealized purity she fears losing in Nana's or Nobu's eyes.

Nobu later complicates that fear by demonstrating that disclosure does not necessarily destroy love. Hachi's growth depends partly on discovering that being known need not require either perfect innocence or surrender to the person who already knows the worst.

---

# 6. Shoji: betrayal teaches Hachi that emotion and communication are not the same thing

Shoji is important not because he is Hachi's “wrong first boyfriend,” but because the betrayal becomes an early lesson in how relationships fail when people assume meaning will communicate itself.

Hachi is betrayed. That fact should remain firm. Her later self-criticism does not make the affair her fault. Yet she eventually becomes capable of holding several propositions together:

- Shoji loved her at some point;
- Hachi was imperfect in the relationship;
- Sachiko became a real person rather than an abstract rival;
- Shoji still chose betrayal rather than honest closure;
- the relationship should not resume;
- and knowing more about what happened can stop the betrayal from poisoning every earlier memory.

Volume 11 develops this into a theory of memory damage. When Hachi lacks an account of the ending, later betrayal spreads backward and contaminates the entire past. Closure does not restore romance; it restores **historical differentiation**.

Volume 13 applies the same insight to Nobu. Hachi realizes that Nobu could not know explanations she never gave him. The mature correction is not “feel less.” It is:

> **If another person cannot access the information inside you, the moral meaning you assign to your silence is not automatically the meaning they receive.**

That lesson is one of Hachi's largest transitions from romantic interiority toward adult relational practice.

# 7. Work: Hachi's ordinary competence is easy to underestimate

Hachi is not career-centered in the way Nana is. The manga does not treat this as equivalent to incompetence.

Across the series she works, loses jobs, seeks work, performs temporary labor, thinks about income, develops service skills, and later has skilled paid work in kimono dressing. Volume 6 specifically shows her enjoying short-term food-demonstration work. Volume 16 gives her an increasingly adult philosophy of professional art: sincerity alone is not enough; audience, craft, market, and obligation also matter. Volume 18 establishes that future Hachi is busy at New Year because of **「着付けの仕事」**.

That late fact matters disproportionately. It prevents the final Hachi model from treating marriage to a wealthy man as the endpoint of her economic identity. The text does not establish that she becomes financially independent of Takumi, nor should we invent such a conclusion. It does establish that she acquires a skilled occupation with real demand.

Hachi's relationship to work is therefore best described as **instrumental but meaningful**. She does not derive her deepest identity from career prestige. She derives satisfaction from being useful, socially competent, aesthetically skilled, and able to make material life function.

This is one reason domestic labor should not be analytically dismissed. Hachi's cooking, household organization, clothing knowledge, social mediation, childcare imagination, and later kimono dressing all belong to a continuous competence cluster: she is good at making embodied social life workable.

The ethical danger appears when usefulness becomes the price she thinks she must pay in order to deserve a place.

# 8. Home and light: why Takumi's world attracts Hachi

Volume 5 distinguishes two things Hachi wants that are often collapsed: **home** and **light**.

By then she already has a real home in 707. What she does not have is access to the extraordinary social world around Nana: professional music, beauty, celebrity, talent, famous adults, money, and the sensation of standing close to people whose lives appear larger than ordinary life.

Hachi later corrects an indiscriminate reading of her desire:

> **「誰でもよかったわけじゃない」**  
> **「同じ光の中にいたかった」**

She did not simply want any man. She wanted to be inside the same light.

Takumi initially condenses that aspiration. He is glamorous, competent, adult, professionally powerful, sexually confident, materially secure, and connected to the world Hachi already admires through Nana. Sexual desire, fandom, social aspiration, and insecurity overlap.

This is why reducing Hachi/Takumi to “she chose the rich bad boy” misses the psychological mechanism. Takumi offers not one good but a cluster of goods Hachi has long desired:

- access to the extraordinary world;
- confidence and decisiveness;
- material stability;
- sexual recognition;
- acceptance of an unidealized self;
- and later a viable household structure.

The fact that these goods are real is precisely what makes the power structure ethically difficult. If everything Takumi offered were fake, Hachi's attachment would require little explanation.

---

# 9. Nobu: reciprocal care and the possibility of ordinary happiness

Nobu becomes important because he meets a different Hachi need.

With Takumi, Hachi often feels recognized through sexual desire, adult competence, status, and decisive provision. With Nobu, she experiences uncomplicated happiness in being wanted and begins discovering reciprocity in care. Volume 7 is especially important: Hachi explicitly ends the Takumi relationship as she understands it, chooses Nobu, discloses shameful material, and discovers pleasure in supporting someone rather than only being supported.

The relationship is not merely a rebound. Nor is it a fully mature solution.

Hachi still uses fairy-tale grammar and can cast Nobu as the “real prince.” She remains vulnerable to making a partner proof that she has finally chosen correctly. Yet the attachment itself is genuine. Later Hachi can remember the full-moon night with Nobu as perhaps the happiest moment of her life without interpreting that memory as a command to restore the relationship.

This is an important adult capacity.

Shoji teaches that an ending need not make the whole past false. Nobu teaches that tenderness can survive after the future it once promised becomes impossible. Volumes 16 and 17 allow physical affection and contact without automatically restoring romance.

Hachi's mature relation to Nobu therefore becomes evidence that she can eventually distinguish:

> **the truth of a past love**

from

> **an entitlement to make that love the present again.**

That distinction becomes especially important in Chapter 83, where grief produces an embrace but not a romantic reset.

# 10. Necessity: Hachi's deeper vulnerability is not monopoly but irreplaceability

Volume 7 gives perhaps the most important sentence for Hachi's entire character arc:

> **「ナナを独り占めしたかったんじゃない」**  
> **「ナナに必要とされたかっただけなの」**

She did not want to monopolize Nana. She wanted Nana to need her.

The difference explains a large amount of behavior that otherwise looks inconsistent.

Hachi can tolerate other people in Nana's life more easily when she still possesses a distinct function. She can accept Takumi's absences more easily when she believes the household is the place he ultimately needs to return. She wants to be useful because usefulness feels like durable relational evidence. She is intensely affected by signs of being wanted, missed, chosen, or made central.

This is not identical to possession. In some respects it is gentler. Hachi often wants to help rather than control. But it can still distort care. If being needed proves worth, then another person's recovery may feel like one's own redundancy. Their self-sufficiency can become frightening. Their refusal of help can resemble rejection.

The final extant chapter returns precisely to this fault line. The question is no longer whether Nana is alive, whether Hachi cares, or whether they once shared a home. The pressure is whether Hachi still has a place if Nana no longer visibly needs her.

That makes necessity—not romantic exclusivity—the most useful longitudinal concept for Hachi.

# 11. Pregnancy: when desire becomes body, deadline, money, and institutional choice

Volume 8 is the largest material-adulthood hinge in Hachi's life.

Before pregnancy, Hachi's romantic decisions can still be imagined as highly reversible. Relationships end. Jobs change. Rooms can be left. People can return. Pregnancy makes choice embodied and time-limited.

The manga carefully prevents a simplistic motherhood story. Hachi initially wants the test to be wrong. She considers abortion and imagines returning to ordinary life. Maternal certainty is not presented as innate or immediate.

The ultrasound changes her subjective moral perception. Pregnancy becomes not merely a condition but **her child**. This is Hachi's experience and should not be universalized into a moral rule about pregnancy. What matters for her arc is that the decision to continue the pregnancy occurs **before** Takumi offers the full support structure.

That sequence protects Hachi's agency.

Takumi does not create her desire to keep the pregnancy. He changes what that desire can become materially.

Hachi then considers single motherhood and confronts the constraints:

- little money;
- unstable work;
- morning sickness severe enough to prevent ordinary labor;
- uncertainty about paternity;
- housing;
- childcare;
- Nobu's youth and career threshold;
- public and family recognition.

The story therefore transforms “choice” from an abstract declaration of freedom into a field of feasible and infeasible futures.

Hachi's decision is not well described as “choosing Takumi over Nobu.” She chooses the pregnancy first. She then accepts the support structure she believes can sustain the life she has chosen.

That choice has costs, including the likely destruction of the Nobu future closest to the ordinary marriage she had long imagined. The fact that she chooses the child anyway is strong evidence that Hachi can act against her own romantic fantasy when another value becomes more important.

# 12. Motherhood is chosen, then expanded beyond biology

Hachi's maternal identity develops in stages rather than appearing as a timeless essence.

First, pregnancy becomes a chosen commitment after initial avoidance. Then motherhood becomes practical: clinics, nutrition, bodily change, due dates, work interruption, housing, and legal recognition. Later it becomes social and ethical beyond biology.

By Volume 13 Hachi explicitly treats Shin as part of her family and functions as a maternal substitute. She wants his birthday celebrated because his birth deserves celebration. She worries about his sexual labor, dangerous relationships, safety, and future. The relationship also exposes limits: maternal feeling does not grant legal standing, omniscience, or authority over every choice. Volume 18 makes that concrete when Hachi wants to see Shin during crisis and institutions prevent immediate access.

Volume 21 then gives Hachi one of her clearest formulations of chosen parenthood:

> **「生まれて来る子は あたしとタクミの子なの」**  
> **「2人でそう決めたの」**

The unborn child is hers and Takumi's because they decided it.

The statement rejects biological determinism without pretending biology is irrelevant to everyone affected. Hachi herself immediately understands that choosing one family architecture has consequences for Nobu. Adult authorship is therefore neither “blood decides everything” nor “choice erases every competing claim.”

Hachi's late maternal form becomes broader still. She increasingly provides food, coordination, emotional translation, ritual, and ordinary continuity to a whole network. Motherhood and domesticity become part of a larger care practice rather than a private identity sealed inside wife-and-child household space.

# 13. Takumi: genuine love, real care, coercive power, and the danger of non-binary truth

Hachi/Takumi is where the character study most needs the method's refusal of false binaries.

Several facts must remain simultaneously true.

Hachi is genuinely attracted to Takumi. She later explicitly says she loves him. She values his competence. She experiences moments of tenderness and domestic happiness. She believes he accepts her unidealized self. She wants to build a household with him. She chooses marriage. She remains legally married in later future material shown by Volume 19. His money, mobility, decisiveness, pregnancy support, willingness to acknowledge the child, and public status provide real benefits.

The same relationship also contains severe asymmetry, information control, unilateral decision-making, sexual infidelity, and at least one encounter the stabilized sequential analysis classifies as rape/sexual assault. Later affection does not retroactively transform that encounter into consent.

This combination is not an analytical inconvenience to be resolved. It is part of what the manga is doing.

Hachi's mature insight in Volume 19 approaches this directly through the captor analogy: a person can become attached to someone who is both source of harm and source of relief, and the emotion can still be real. The governing principle is:

> **Authentic love does not certify an ethical relationship structure. An unethical relationship structure does not make authentic love imaginary.**

That distinction also protects Hachi's agency. Calling every affectionate scene false because coercion exists can inadvertently turn Hachi into a person incapable of having any interior response not dictated by victimization. Calling the relationship consensual or healthy because affection exists erases harm. The source supports neither simplification.

Hachi's task is therefore not merely to discover whether she “really loves” Takumi. She does. The deeper task is learning whether love can survive only by allowing his authority to define reality for her.

# 14. Marriage: the household becomes Hachi's authored project, not merely Takumi's enclosure

Volume 10 provides a critical correction to the passive-wife reading.

Hachi says:

> **「あたしは自分の家庭を持ちたいの！」**

She wants a household of her own.

The wording matters. The aspiration is larger than “I want to marry Takumi.” Hachi wants to create durable family form: child, home, shared routines, recognition, food, care, stability, and a future that can be inhabited rather than merely imagined.

By Volume 15, marriage is clearly something Hachi participates in as an act. She submits registration, calls herself married, imagines an ordinary family, and can sincerely say she is happy. This does not erase the unequal conditions under which the relationship formed. It means the mature analysis must distinguish **origin conditions** from **later acts of commitment**.

Marriage also gives Hachi institutional power she can later use. Volume 21's funeral scene is especially revealing. Public recognition as Takumi's wife gives her status with the press, and she uses that status to shield Nana. The institution that can enclose Hachi can also become a resource she wields.

This is a recurring pattern in her adulthood:

> **agency through structure rather than freedom from structure.**

Hachi rarely receives a clean outside position from which she can choose without cost. She becomes more capable of acting inside systems—marriage, household, money, celebrity, family, work—without pretending that acting inside them makes their asymmetries disappear.

---

# 15. Domesticity is a vocation, but Hachi can turn vocation into proof of worth

Hachi's domestic desire should be taken literally before it is interpreted politically.

She enjoys cooking. She cares about rooms, food, clothes, family routines, holidays, children's lives, guests, household atmosphere, and the emotional meaning of return. These activities are not merely imposed by Takumi. They precede him and survive beyond his immediate presence. The seaside house Hachi imagines for Nana proves that her domestic imagination is not reducible to heterosexual marriage.

At the same time, domesticity becomes dangerous when Hachi turns competence into relational currency.

Volume 12 gives the phrase:

> **「タクミのそばにいてタクミの役に立ちたい」**

She wants to stay beside Takumi and be useful to him.

The desire contains gratitude and reciprocity. She does not want to be merely supported. But because Hachi already equates being needed with durable value, **役に立つ** can become a condition of self-worth.

Volume 19 makes the structure strategic. Hachi wants to create a home Takumi desires to return to because she calls it her:

> **「勝算」**

her chance of winning.

Domestic care becomes simultaneously:

- love;
- skilled labor;
- gendered social script;
- family building;
- fear of abandonment;
- economic strategy;
- and competition with Reira or other possible women.

Calling the care “fake” because it is strategic would be wrong. Calling it purely selfless would also be wrong. Hachi's domesticity is most convincing when understood as overdetermined human action.

---

# 16. Material dependence: security can expand choice and make leaving more costly at the same time

Hachi's relationship with money is one of the clearest places where binary independence models fail.

Takumi's resources give Hachi real options she would otherwise struggle to access during pregnancy: housing, healthcare, transportation, legal recognition, childcare horizon, protection from immediate economic precarity. Those benefits matter.

The same resources also increase asymmetry. Once Hachi inhabits a large protected household, alternative futures become expensive to imagine. Volume 19's Shirokane house becomes a spatial metaphor: a huge home can suddenly feel cramped because every possible future must be made to fit inside the structure already built.

Yet Hachi is not unable to imagine exit. Volume 13 is decisive. She can picture returning to her parents, working, returning to 707, raising the child without Takumi, and refusing support if necessary. She directly asserts:

> **「あたしにはあたしのペースがありますから」**

I have my own pace.

This is a sophisticated autonomy claim because it concerns **time**, not only outcome. Hachi wants ownership over the process by which a decision becomes hers.

Takumi's characteristic strength is rapid conversion of uncertainty into plan. That can be useful in crisis. It can also deprive Hachi of the temporal space necessary for authorship.

The mature material conclusion is therefore:

> **Hachi is neither economically sovereign nor simply trapped by inability to imagine survival. Her adulthood is constrained authorship under conditions where security itself has opportunity costs.**

---

# 17. 707 after departure: homesickness, letter, ritual, and the survival of chosen home

When Hachi moves into Takumi's household, the series does not treat 707 as an obsolete adolescent stage.

Volume 9 gives her explicit **ホームシック**. She understands her departure as moral injury toward the group and imagines herself as a traitor. This is why returning is difficult even when she remains loved. Home can become inaccessible not because the door is literally locked but because shame makes the person feel she has forfeited the right of return.

Hachi's letter to Nana is therefore one of her most important acts of self-authorship. After a crisis in which Takumi speaks for her, controls information, and becomes the conduit through which others learn about pregnancy, Hachi creates a durable record in her own voice. She names what 707 meant, what she cannot undo, what Nana means, and what practical responsibilities she will continue.

Later volumes transform return into ritual. The group preserves 707. Fireworks and seasonal recurrence make it a timekeeping device. Future Hachi returns with children and keeps a place for Nana.

The apartment's final function is not nostalgia alone.

It is an institutionalized promise:

> **You can leave this household, fail inside other households, become famous, marry, have children, disappear, or change—and there remains a place whose meaning was created before those later identities consumed you.**

For Hachi, that is what home increasingly means.

# 18. Hachi's narrating voice: present exuberance and future self-critique are different registers

The manga gives Hachi two related but distinguishable voices.

Present Hachi is often exclamatory, feminine, humorous, self-dramatizing, rapidly associative, socially observant, and emotionally immediate. She converts experience into story almost as fast as experience occurs. She can be perceptive about another person's mood and wildly wrong about what that mood means for the future.

Future Hachi is quieter. Her direct address to Nana—often beginning from **「ねえ ナナ」**—uses temporal vocabulary of then, now, again, forever, return, waiting, and memory. She is more capable of causal correction. She admits that she talked about herself without listening on the train. She later recognizes that idealizing Nana distorted her perception:

> **「ナナを理想化した あたしの勝手な思い込みが / すでにあたしの目を大きく狂わせていたの」**

This is a major maturation because it moves beyond ordinary self-blame. Hachi does not merely say “I was stupid.” She identifies the mechanism by which her need for Nana to be strong made Nana harder to see.

Future Hachi is still not omniscient.

She speculates, revises, remembers emotionally, and sometimes narrates from incomplete information. Her greater maturity gives her a better interpretive position, not divine access to other minds or to events she did not witness.

That limit matters especially in the future Nana material. Hachi's fear, hope, or theory about Nana's absence cannot be promoted into fact simply because the narration comes later in time.

---

# 19. From fairy tale to ordinary time: Hachi's concept of happiness matures

Early Hachi wants a dramatic love whose form proves that happiness has arrived. Later Hachi repeatedly discovers that there is no stable endpoint at which relational uncertainty disappears.

Volume 12 stages the fairy-tale critique sharply. Hachi describes effort that **空回り**—spins without traction—and future Hachi calls her own efforts an **一人芝居**, a one-woman performance. The image is important because it identifies a problem with narrative scripts: Hachi can act intensely inside a role while assuming other people are participating in the same play.

Volume 13 gives a more mature alternative. Instead of a permanent “happy ending,” Hachi can accept that today is happy while tomorrow may contain tears. Happiness becomes temporal and revisable rather than terminal.

Volume 15 develops the scale change further. “Ordinary” family life becomes an achievement rather than an insult. Hachi can value a healthy child, meals, shopping, appointments, ordinary outings, and a usable home. The glamorous future she once associated with adult success becomes less important than continuity.

This does not mean she stops romanticizing. She continues to cast Nana/Ren through destiny language, continues to use rival grammar around Reira, and continues to interpret intense desire as reparative. Maturity in *NANA* is not purification.

It is the increasing ability to recognize a script as a script while still living emotionally inside it.

---

# 20. Protector fantasy: Hachi's care becomes ethically stronger when she accepts insufficiency

Hachi often answers love by trying to become useful enough to prevent loss.

That impulse is generous and dangerous.

She mediates romances, arranges meetings, carries information, protects reputations, investigates family history, shields friends, and tries to solve problems before being asked. Some interventions are genuinely helpful. Others cross boundaries or create new risk.

Volume 17 gives the clearest correction. Hachi's attempt to protect Nana from damaging family/media exposure reaches the limit of what she can competently carry alone. The resulting proposition is:

> **「一人で抱えきれなくても / 一人じゃない」**

Even if one person cannot carry everything, she is not alone.

This is one of Hachi's largest ethical developments.

Her earlier model often implied:

> If I really love you, I should be able to help enough.

The revised model is:

> I can matter without being sufficient.

That distinction prepares the continuation after Ren's death. Hachi cannot make grief disappear. She cannot undo the accident. She cannot single-handedly keep every person from self-destructing. Her adult competence becomes **coordination**, not omnipotence.

---

# 21. Hachi as mediator: domestic intelligence becomes social infrastructure

By Volume 20 Hachi has become one of the story's major causal organizers.

She coordinates Nana's birthday plans, understands Nana's desire to see Ren, urges Ren to contact her, offers to carry information if he cannot, gives access to Nana's location, monitors Takumi's condition, keeps track of Reira, participates in naming the child, and moves constantly between people whose direct communication has failed.

This is the mature form of a trait that existed from the beginning. Early Hachi is socially hungry and intrusive. Mature Hachi is socially connective and sometimes still intrusive.

Her strength is that she notices relational states and translates feeling into action. She knows who needs food, who should be called, who is being left out, where a gathering should happen, what an ordinary ritual might keep people together, and how to make emotionally difficult contact materially possible.

Her weakness is that she can mistake perceptiveness for authorization. Because she often understands what two people want before they say it cleanly, she can conclude that helping them toward that outcome is justified.

Nana/Ren becomes the clearest case. Hachi's mediation is often affectionate and perceptive, but later volumes increasingly insist that Nana and Ren's timing belongs to Nana and Ren. Future Hachi's fear that Nana may refuse her hand shows the more mature principle: understanding another person's likely desire still does not eliminate their right to answer differently.

---

# 22. Being accepted, being desired, and being missed: Hachi's three proofs of love

Hachi repeatedly looks for love in three overlapping signals.

First is **acceptance**: someone sees the unidealized self and remains. This helps explain Takumi's durable psychological importance and Hachi's statement that he accepts her as she is.

Second is **desire**: someone wants her intensely enough that she feels singular. Sexual desire, romantic pursuit, jealousy, and urgent longing can therefore carry enormous reparative force for Hachi.

Third is **absence**: if she is gone, does the other person feel the missing place?

Volume 19 makes the third explicit. Hachi's loneliness is not simply that Takumi works too much. It is that he does not appear lonely when she is absent. She wants evidence that her non-presence changes his interior world.

This is why Volume 20's conversation with Ren is ethically revealing. Hachi imagines that if Takumi had once desperately wanted to see her with the intensity Ren wants Nana, perhaps that knowledge could have allowed her to forgive everything.

The formulation should not be adopted as the manga's moral rule. It is Hachi's vulnerability.

> **She can mistake proof of intense desire for proof that harm has been repaired.**

Her late development begins to ask for something harder: not merely to be wanted, but to be related to in a way that leaves both people free to have needs beyond one another.

---

# 23. Gender and adulthood: Hachi is neither a failed career woman nor a completed housewife

Hachi's life makes simplistic independence metrics unusually unhelpful.

She is not a careerist. She wants marriage and motherhood. She enjoys feminine fashion, domestic labor, household organization, romantic symbolism, and being a wife. None of this makes her psychologically unserious.

Nor does choosing those roles mean the manga endorses every gendered condition surrounding them. Hachi's household labor can become an expected service. Her social status can depend on marriage. Takumi's public/professional mobility can contrast with her domestic containment. She can frame outings as things he **「許してくれた」**—allowed. Her dependence on his money changes the cost of conflict. Her attempt to become the home he returns to can become competitive feminine labor.

At the same time, Hachi increasingly develops forms of autonomy that do not look like Nana's:

- she sets boundaries;
- controls disclosure more skillfully;
- asserts her pace;
- imagines exit;
- acquires skilled paid work;
- uses wife status strategically;
- builds chosen family;
- supports art professionally without making self-expression absolute;
- and treats care as a practice that can be distributed rather than owned.

Her adulthood is therefore better described as **relational and material self-authorship**.

The question is not whether she becomes independent in the abstract. It is whether the domestic life she genuinely wants can become a life in which her own pace, knowledge, friendships, work, sexuality, and right to refuse remain intact.

---

# 24. Ren's death: care confronts the impossibility of rescue

Ren's death places Hachi inside the crisis she is least equipped to solve.

Her reflex is guilt. Because she spoke to Ren and participated in the relational chain around his movement, she imagines that she should have stopped him. The sequential analysis correctly classifies this as **character belief**, not objective causal responsibility. Ren remained an adult agent.

The guilt matters because it exposes Hachi's old care model at catastrophic scale. She has often believed that if she understands people well enough, connects them correctly enough, and becomes useful enough, she can keep the network from breaking.

Death demonstrates the limit absolutely.

Volume 21 then gives Hachi a counter-ethic through her conversation with Miu. Miu says her self-injury is not an attempt to die. Hachi nevertheless recognizes that self-destructive behavior can kill without suicidal intention. The resulting survivor formulation centers those who remain:

> **「負けられない」**  
> **「残された 大切な人達の為に」**

This is a major correction to Hachi's earlier romanticization of sacrificial intensity. Love is no longer proven by being willing to disappear with someone. Care also means accounting for what one's destruction does to people left behind.

Hachi still cannot save everyone. But she becomes better able to think of survival as relational responsibility rather than proof that grief is finished.

---

# 25. Wife status as resource: Hachi's adulthood often operates inside compromised structures

One of the strongest examples of Hachi's mature agency occurs at the funeral.

Earlier volumes often make “Takumi's wife” a label that narrows her public identity. The status organizes press attention, household expectations, and the legitimacy through which others understand her pregnancy and place in the celebrity world.

At the funeral, Hachi uses the same status to protect Nana from reporters. She can speak from a position the press recognizes and turn socially legible wifehood into a shield.

This is not a liberation narrative in which the institution becomes unproblematic because Hachi learns to use it. It demonstrates a more realistic kind of adulthood:

> **people often exercise agency through structures they did not fully design and that still constrain them.**

Hachi's marriage, money, family name, social connections, and celebrity adjacency all work this way. They can protect and confine. Her increasing maturity lies partly in learning to use available power without confusing access to power with moral authority over everyone else.

---

# 26. Future Hachi: waiting replaces pursuit, then permission replaces entitlement

Future Hachi's relationship to Nana supplies some of her most mature ethical language.

At first glance, waiting can look passive. But Volume 15 explains why she does not simply force a search or expose Nana's location: renewed publicity could injure Nana. Hachi preserves a place of return rather than claiming the right to produce return herself.

Volume 17 moves from non-searching toward tentative initiative. The question becomes whether Hachi may open the door from her side.

Volume 19 sharpens the ethical problem further. Hachi fears:

> **「伸ばしたこの手を 振り払われる事が」**

that the hand she extends may be brushed away.

This fear is painful, but it is also mature. Early Hachi often treated love as permission to enter, arrange, help, or restore. Future Hachi can imagine that Nana may have an answer of her own—including refusal.

The reunion problem therefore becomes:

> **Can Hachi seek Nana without treating finding her as entitlement to recover the old relationship?**

This is one of the strongest signs that her ethics of care has moved beyond rescue fantasy.

---

# 27. Chapters 81–84: Hachi begins separating love from indispensability

The continuation after Ren's death brings Hachi's necessity problem back into direct view.

Chapter 81 first appears to repeat the old pattern. Hachi wants to remain with Nana and the others. She wants to be spoiled like a child and cry freely. Instead she suppresses herself and returns home because she believes Takumi will have nobody if she does not.

The important point is that this is Hachi's belief, not an objective fact about Takumi's available supports.

Chapter 83 revises the premise. Hachi thinks that Takumi's refuge may not necessarily be her. Chapter 84 then supplies observational pressure: Takumi is coping through work; Hachi is not central to the work-focused conversation; Reira remains a major emotional responsibility; and the restored Spanish interval leads Hachi to suspect that Takumi may love Reira as well.

Across three chapters the logic changes:

> **I must go because he has nobody**

becomes

> **perhaps I am not the refuge he needs**.

That is not evidence Hachi stops loving Takumi. It is the beginning of a separation between love and indispensability.

The same development appears with Nobu. In Chapter 83 Hachi and Nobu hold each other through grief. The intimacy is real. The scene does not require romantic restoration. The recovered Chapter 84 material makes Hachi's restraint clearer: she recognizes that moving toward Nobu while she wants to stop thinking and drift would hurt him again. She refuses to use him as an escape route from unresolved marriage and grief.

This is a meaningful maturation from the younger Hachi who often allowed the next relationship to organize the ending of the previous one.

---

# 28. Domesticity after Ren: Hachi's care becomes portable rather than marital

The continuation also gives Hachi's domesticity a final important transformation.

She cooks, organizes meals, cares for grieving people, and helps sustain the dormitory community around Nana. Her competence no longer functions primarily as wife-service inside Shirokane.

It becomes communal.

This matters because it reveals what was always larger than marriage in Hachi's domestic vocation. Food, shelter, routines, atmosphere, and practical attention are her way of turning relationship into inhabitable space. The skill can be used for boyfriend, husband, child, friend, band, grieving community, or chosen family.

The strongest mature interpretation is therefore not “Hachi was meant to be a housewife.” It is:

> **Hachi is a maker of social home.**

Marriage is one institution through which she tries to exercise that ability. 707 is another. Motherhood is another. Friendship and crisis care are others.

Once care becomes portable, Hachi can begin imagining personal value that is not entirely dependent on one man's need for her service.

---

# 29. The extant endpoint: what Hachi understands and what she still does not

By Chapter 84, Hachi understands substantially more than the young woman who came to Tokyo.

She understands that:

- romantic intensity does not guarantee a future;
- an ending does not make earlier love unreal;
- domestic desire can be genuine without making every domestic structure safe;
- money and housing change what choices are feasible;
- pregnancy and parenthood require decisions beyond romantic ranking;
- biological uncertainty does not prevent socially chosen family;
- love for Takumi can be real while harm remains real;
- Nobu can remain precious without requiring restoration;
- silence does not communicate intention;
- another person's autonomy includes the right to refuse help or reunion;
- protecting someone can become controlling or intrusive;
- being useful does not require being sufficient;
- and grief cannot be solved by competent caretaking.

She has also become capable of recognizing her own interpretive distortions. Future Hachi can admit idealization, narratorial self-dramatization, failed rescue, and the possibility that what she once called fate or effort did not mean what she thought it meant.

But several problems remain unresolved.

She still gives enormous emotional weight to being wanted. She still fears replacement. She still sometimes organizes care around the hope of becoming irreplaceable. Her marriage's future emotional form remains incomplete. Her suspicion about Takumi and Reira is not equivalent to complete knowledge. Her future relation to Nobu remains open without evidence of conventional reunion. Her future relation to Nana remains defined by waiting, approach, and the fear of refusal.

Most importantly, she has not yet fully answered the question Chapter 84 places at the edge of the published story:

> **If Nana no longer needs Hachi in order to function, can Hachi still believe she has a legitimate place beside her?**

That question is larger than Nana/Hachi. It reaches the core of Hachi's self-worth.

# 30. Developmental map: what actually changes in Hachi

## Stage 1 — Romance gives ordinary life a plot

Hachi seeks dramatic love because being chosen makes the future legible. She can recognize impossibility while still needing the story.

## Stage 2 — Migration and 707 reveal practical self-authorship

She saves, moves, works, shares costs, and helps construct a home based on privacy plus mutual aid rather than romantic merger.

## Stage 3 — Nana expands the emotional category system

Hachi discovers a bond that does not fit the ordinary hierarchy of boyfriend, friend, roommate, fan, or family. She becomes capable of plural attachment, though not yet of interpreting it without romantic scripts.

## Stage 4 — Betrayal produces self-critique and a need for better communication

Shoji's affair destroys the assumption that recognized relationship equals security. Hachi learns slowly that feeling, intent, and what another person can actually know are different things.

## Stage 5 — Takumi and Nobu expose the conflict between recognition, light, care, shame, and ordinary happiness

Hachi is drawn to Takumi's adult certainty and acceptance while finding reciprocal care and possible ordinary happiness with Nobu. She becomes more capable of choosing and disclosing, but remains vulnerable to narrative idealization.

## Stage 6 — Pregnancy converts choice into material adulthood

Body, time, money, work, housing, paternity uncertainty, and childcare replace abstract romantic ranking. Hachi chooses the pregnancy, then chooses a support structure.

## Stage 7 — Marriage becomes an authored household project under unequal conditions

Hachi chooses Takumi, family form, and ordinary domestic continuity without acquiring equal power or full information. Love and coercion coexist in the same relationship history.

## Stage 8 — Care becomes social competence rather than private romantic service

Hachi develops communication boundaries, chosen family, surrogate motherhood, professional skill, mediation, and the ability to use institutional status strategically.

## Stage 9 — Rescue is revised into distributed care

`一人じゃない` replaces the fantasy that Hachi must personally carry everyone. She learns that insufficiency is not abandonment.

## Stage 10 — Future Hachi learns the ethics of possible refusal

Waiting, non-exposure, and fear of the outstretched hand being brushed away demonstrate that love no longer automatically grants access.

## Stage 11 — After Ren, love begins separating from indispensability

Hachi recognizes that Takumi may not need her as refuge, refuses to use Nobu as an escape route, and makes care communal. The endpoint asks whether belonging can survive when necessity decreases.

# 31. Counterreadings and hypothesis stress-test

## Counterreading A — Hachi is simply “boy-crazy” and never fundamentally changes

**Why tempting:** romance structures much of her early identity; she repeatedly becomes attached, narrativizes men, and remains highly sensitive to being chosen.

**What it explains:** her rapid idealization, romantic scripts, fear of abandonment, and some poor decisions.

**What it misses:** savings, work, household competence, chosen pregnancy, direct boundary speech, later skilled labor, maternal/communal care, closure with Shoji, non-restorative tenderness with Nobu, distributed-care ethics, and future respect for possible refusal.

**Verdict:** too thin. Romantic susceptibility persists, but the forms of agency and care become substantially more adult.

## Counterreading B — Takumi simply traps Hachi and her later choices are not really hers

**Why tempting:** the relationship contains large economic/status asymmetry, information control, unilateral decision-making, infidelity, and sexual violence.

**What it explains:** why security can become enclosure and why Hachi's options narrow.

**What it misses:** Hachi chooses pregnancy before Takumi's support; explicitly says she loves him; wants a household; can imagine exit; asserts her own pace; submits the marriage registration; remains legally married in later material shown; and derives genuine domestic satisfaction.

**Verdict:** ethically attentive but agency-erasing if made total. The correct model is constrained authorship inside a coercively asymmetric relationship.

## Counterreading C — Nobu was objectively the correct choice and Hachi's tragedy is simply choosing the wrong man

**Why tempting:** Nobu offers affection, reciprocal care, and a version of ordinary happiness closer to Hachi's early dream; Hachi later remembers their happiness vividly.

**What it explains:** the emotional cost of the pregnancy crisis and why Nobu remains meaningful.

**What it misses:** uncertain paternity, money, Nobu's age and career, Hachi's unwillingness to demand sacrifice, her independent desire to keep the child, her genuine love for Takumi, and the fact that later tenderness does not automatically restore the relationship.

**Verdict:** converts a material tragedy into a shipping verdict. The text does not establish an objective alternate timeline.

## Counterreading D — Hachi's domesticity is false consciousness or surrender to gender norms

**Why tempting:** she is economically dependent, performs extensive domestic labor, values wifehood, and sometimes organizes worth around usefulness to Takumi.

**What it explains:** the gendered asymmetry and strategic dimension of homemaking.

**What it misses:** domestic desire predates Takumi; the seaside-house fantasy includes Nana; Hachi genuinely enjoys cooking and home-making; domestic competence later becomes communal; and future Hachi also possesses skilled paid work.

**Verdict:** inadequate. Gender scripts shape Hachi's domestic vocation but do not exhaust its meaning.

## Counterreading E — Motherhood fixes Hachi's immaturity

**Why tempting:** pregnancy forces practical decisions and accelerates adult responsibility.

**What it explains:** why Volume 8 is a genuine developmental hinge.

**What it misses:** Hachi initially considers abortion, continues romantic idealization after pregnancy, remains insecure about being needed, makes intrusive interventions, and keeps learning long after choosing motherhood.

**Verdict:** motherhood catalyzes growth; it does not purify personality.

## Counterreading F — Future Hachi is the authoritative narrator who finally explains what everything meant

**Why tempting:** she knows later outcomes, repeatedly corrects younger Hachi, and speaks in reflective language.

**What it explains:** why future narration is a privileged source for retrospective correction.

**What it misses:** Hachi still lacks information, speculates about others, idealizes, and cannot know the cause or final meaning of Nana's absence merely because she narrates later.

**Verdict:** future Hachi is epistemically improved, not omniscient.

## Counterreading G — Nana is secretly Hachi's only “real” love and every male relationship is displacement

**Why tempting:** explicit romance-adjacent language, narrative centrality, 707, jealousy, future address, red thread, waiting, and Chapter 84's necessity question.

**What it explains:** why Nana/Hachi cannot be reduced to ordinary friendship.

**What it misses:** Hachi's genuine love for Nobu and Takumi, sexual/romantic desire toward men, chosen marriage, parenthood, and the text's repeated maintenance of categorical ambiguity.

**Verdict:** premature. Document 04 will adjudicate the Nana/Hachi relationship independently. Hachi's character study should establish plurality rather than use one relationship to erase all others.

# 32. Final character thesis

Komatsu Nana begins *NANA* looking like the less autonomous of the two protagonists.

She wants romance.
She wants a boyfriend nearby.
She wants marriage.
She wants a home.
She loves clothes, food, social approval, domestic fantasy, and emotionally legible roles.
She can be impulsive, jealous, sexually confused, financially precarious, idealizing, intrusive, and terrified of being left.

It would be easy to read the entire series as the education of a foolish girl into realism.

That is not what the extant manga supports.

Hachi's early desires are not simply discarded as childish. Instead they are progressively **materialized**.

Home becomes rent, rooms, privacy, cooking, keys, travel, security, and return.

Romance becomes communication, consent, betrayal, paternity uncertainty, sexual history, jealousy, and the difference between being wanted and being safe.

Marriage becomes law, money, status, household labor, public identity, compromise, and an institution Hachi can both be constrained by and use.

Motherhood becomes bodily choice, medical time, chosen family, surrogate care, and responsibility without omnipotence.

Work becomes not glamorous self-realization but practical competence, service, craft, and eventually skilled paid labor.

Friendship becomes chosen home, moral witness, jealousy, repair, ritual, distance, and the right of another person to refuse reunion.

The central continuity is that Hachi wants to matter.

As a young woman she most often experiences that need through being chosen romantically. As her life expands, she tries to secure value by being useful: cooking, caring, arranging, protecting, mediating, mothering, waiting, and making herself into the place others return to. This produces some of her greatest acts of love and some of her most serious boundary failures.

Her deepest growth is therefore not the acquisition of responsibility by itself. Hachi was capable of responsibility much earlier than her comic image suggests. The deeper growth is learning the **limits of responsibility**.

She cannot prevent every betrayal.
She cannot make every relationship last.
She cannot communicate through silence.
She cannot determine another person's choice because she understands them well.
She cannot rescue Nana by force.
She cannot keep Ren alive retrospectively.
She cannot be Takumi's only refuge by moral effort.
She cannot make Nobu's pain disappear by refusing to speak.
She cannot make a future safe simply by working hard enough inside the role she has chosen.

What she can do is increasingly concrete.

She can choose.
She can speak.
She can write.
She can work.
She can cook.
She can earn.
She can mother.
She can apologize.
She can preserve a room.
She can use social status to protect somebody.
She can call other people when she cannot carry something alone.
She can let an old love remain true without reclaiming it.
She can keep a door open without dragging another person through it.

By the future material, Hachi has moved far from the young woman who expected a relationship to supply a happy ending. Yet she has not become cynical about connection. If anything, she values ordinary connection more intensely because she knows it can fail.

The strongest final formulation through Chapter 84 is therefore:

> **Hachi's adulthood is the transformation of desire into practice. She begins by wanting love and home as narratives that will prove she has been chosen; she gradually learns to build love and home through material acts whose value does not depend on a perfect ending. Her domesticity is genuine vocation, her marriage is both chosen and ethically compromised, her motherhood is authored rather than automatic, and her social intelligence becomes a form of infrastructure for other people's lives. The unresolved final movement is whether she can stop treating indispensability as the deepest evidence of worth. Chapter 84 leaves her at the threshold of a harder form of belonging: being allowed to remain beside someone not because that person is broken enough to need rescue, but because both people continue to choose connection while remaining separate.**

That is the Hachi model the remaining synthesis should inherit.

# 33. Baseline propositions frozen for later documents

The following propositions should be treated as the current Phase-3 Hachi baseline unless later primary-source verification requires correction:

1. **Hachi's early romance fixation is best understood as a search for narrative legibility and chosen value, not merely sexual or romantic frivolity.**
2. **Her Tokyo migration already contains real self-authorship:** saving, delay, work intention, and practical planning complicate the stereotype that she simply follows Shoji.
3. **707 is Hachi's foundational model of home because it combines privacy, mutual aid, shared costs, ordinary labor, and reciprocal presence.**
4. **Nana expands Hachi's relationship categories beyond ordinary boyfriend/friend hierarchy; categorical interpretation of Nana/Hachi remains reserved for Document 04.**
5. **Hachi's central relational vulnerability is the desire to be needed and therefore irreplaceable, not merely the desire to monopolize another person.**
6. **Shoji and later Nobu teach Hachi that feeling, intention, communication, and historical truth are distinct.**
7. **Hachi possesses genuine work competence and later skilled paid labor; lack of career centrality should not be confused with lack of adult economic capacity.**
8. **Pregnancy is the major material-adulthood hinge. Hachi chooses continuation before Takumi's support makes the choice materially easier to sustain.**
9. **Motherhood is chosen, not automatic, and later expands beyond biology into surrogate/chosen-family care.**
10. **Hachi genuinely loves Takumi. That fact does not erase coercion, information asymmetry, infidelity, or sexual violence within the relationship history.**
11. **Hachi's marriage is an authored household project under unequal conditions, not adequately described as either pure free choice or pure captivity.**
12. **Domesticity is a genuine Hachi vocation, but usefulness can become a dangerous condition of self-worth.**
13. **Material security expands some choices while making exit and alternative futures more costly; Hachi's mature agency is often agency through structure.**
14. **Future Hachi is a more self-critical narrator but not an omniscient one.**
15. **Hachi's care matures from rescue fantasy toward distributed care; `一人じゃない` is a major correction to the belief that love requires personal sufficiency.**
16. **Her mature social role is mediator and maker of social home, though mediation can still cross autonomy boundaries.**
17. **Nobu remains a real past love without the text establishing conventional romantic restoration.**
18. **Ren's death teaches the limit of retrospective rescue and strengthens an ethic of survival for people who remain.**
19. **Chapters 81–84 begin separating Hachi's love for Takumi from the belief that she must be his indispensable refuge.**
20. **The final extant necessity question is whether Hachi can belong beside Nana without Nana's brokenness being the condition that makes Hachi necessary.**
21. **The hiatus leaves Hachi's destination unresolved:** the final emotional form of her marriage, her future relation to Nobu, the full reason and outcome of Nana's separation, and whether Nana/Hachi can reconnect remain open.

These propositions provide the Hachi side of the structural foundation required for `04_NANA_HACHI_RELATIONSHIP_INTIMACY_CHOSEN_FAMILY_AND_QUEER_AMBIGUITY.md`, which must analyze the central dyad without allowing either protagonist's independent character model to disappear inside the relationship.
