# 02 — Osaki Nana: Selfhood, Love, Art, and Dependency
## How Nana attempts to become autonomous without becoming alone

### Scope and governing question

This document is the definitive Phase-3 character study of **大崎ナナ / Osaki Nana** across the complete extant primary-fiction corpus: Japanese tankōbon Volumes 1–21 and the uncollected continuation through Chapter 84. It inherits the chronology and structural propositions stabilized in `01_SERIES_ARCHITECTURE_CHRONOLOGY_AND_VOLUME_PROGRESSION.md` and routes disputed claims through the frozen volume-by-volume evidence layer rather than silently replacing the sequential readings with a retrospective personality summary.

*NANA 7.8* is deliberately excluded here. Its profiles, interview material, and editorial framing will be introduced later as paratext, after the manga-derived character model has stabilized. The anime is not evidence for this document.

The governing question is:

> **How does Nana attempt to become autonomous without becoming alone?**

The shortest answer is that she never solves the problem by choosing one side.

Nana's independence is real. It is not merely a pose concealing dependency. She repeatedly refuses to let romance determine her geography, insists on preserving her music, resists being reduced to Ren's lover, converts humiliation into professional ambition, learns to negotiate with the industry rather than merely despise it, and eventually accepts a solo path because she can understand autonomy as carrying relationships within a self-authored choice rather than escaping from them.

Her dependency is equally real. She is not secretly self-sufficient. She needs Ren, Hachi, Yasu, BLAST, the stage, and eventually an audience, but she needs each of them differently. Her most damaging relational impulses arise not because she cannot imagine autonomy, but because she wants autonomy for herself while often experiencing other people's autonomy as a threat to her own security. She can intellectually understand that Ren, Hachi, Yasu, Nobu, and others possess independent centers of life while emotionally longing to become indispensable enough that they will never leave.

The mature character thesis is therefore:

> **Nana is a self-author whose greatest strength and deepest vulnerability come from the same refusal to let another person define her. She builds an identity through music because inherited home has failed her and romantic fusion terrifies her; yet because she has so little confidence in permanence that is not actively chosen, she repeatedly turns voluntary choice into a test of love. Her development is not a movement from dependence to independence. It is a movement from defensive self-sufficiency and possessive attachment toward the harder possibility of reciprocal dependence among separate persons.**

That development remains incomplete at Chapter 84.

---

# 1. Nana before Tokyo: abandonment, self-authorship, and the problem of inherited home

Nana's early presentation invites a simple contrast with Komatsu Nana. Hachi looks dependent, romantic, ordinary, domestic, and eager to follow people. Nana looks autonomous, severe, artistically driven, sexually self-possessed, and resistant to conventional femininity. The manga spends the rest of its run proving that the contrast is useful but insufficient.

The decisive fact about Nana's autonomy is that it grows in a life where inherited continuity is unusually weak.

Her father is absent from the family structure available to her. Her mother leaves her upbringing. Her grandmother becomes the caregiver who actually raises her, and that grandmother dies when Nana is fifteen. Volume 4 makes explicit what Volume 3's blunt **「あたしには故郷などない」** already suggested: Nana's claim to have no hometown is not simply punk disdain for provincial roots. It describes a real deficit of stable inherited home.

This matters because it changes the emotional meaning of nearly every later place and person Nana calls home.

Someone with a stable family house can leave and still imagine return as a default possibility. Nana's life does not offer that confidence. Home is therefore not something she begins with and must escape. It is something she repeatedly has to **construct from chosen people, work, rooms, and promises**.

This helps explain why her self-authorship is both admirable and defensive. She has learned, correctly, that nobody else is guaranteed to build continuity for her. Yet the same lesson can make dependence feel humiliating and another person's departure feel existential.

Volume 20 supplies the most devastating retrospective frame. Nana's mother, Misuzu, explains that she did not sacrifice the life she wanted in order to remain Nana's mother. The juxtaposition with adult Nana's desire for total sacrifice is psychologically powerful: the person who should have prioritized the child did not, while the adult child longs for proof that someone else will do the opposite.

But the evidence rule is crucial:

> **Nana does not hear Misuzu's confession.**

The scene is explanation available to the reader, not therapeutic insight available to Nana. We should therefore say that the manga makes Nana's possessive hunger historically intelligible, not that Nana consciously knows its cause or has resolved it.

The distinction protects her temporality. Nana is not a psychological diagram walking around with the reader's later knowledge. Much of the tragedy is that she can become unusually self-aware about *what* she wants without possessing equivalent knowledge about *why* the wanting is so absolute.

# 2. Ren and the first lesson: love is real, but it does not get to decide Nana's life

Nana's relationship with Ren is already complex before Tokyo.

Volume 1 does not describe her attraction as innocent romantic absorption. Nana herself retrospectively names the mixture as:

> **「嫉妬が入り混じった羨望と 焦燥感 そして欲情」**

Envy, impatience, and desire are present from the beginning. Ren is lover, artistic stimulus, object of erotic desire, and a figure whose talent and trajectory activate Nana's own ambition.

That mixture is fundamental. Nana does not merely fall in love with a man and then reluctantly discover a career. Love and artistic selfhood are entangled at origin.

The domestic life that follows matters just as much. Volume 1 explicitly describes living with Ren as precious enough to feel dreamlike. The mature Nana should therefore never be rewritten as someone who always preferred independence to domestic partnership. She knew the pleasure of shared ordinary life and lost it.

The first great test arrives when Ren decides to go to Tokyo with Trapnest.

His instruction is not domination:

> **「おまえは おまえの好きに生きりゃいいさ」**

Live as you like.

Yasu makes the principle even more explicit:

> **「ナナはレンの飼い猫じゃねぇぞ」**  
> **「それはナナが決める事だ」**

Nana is not Ren's pet cat. Nana decides.

These Volume-1 anchors are important because later possession imagery does not emerge in a story that lacks an autonomy ethic. The autonomy ethic is present from the beginning and is voiced by the people closest to Nana. Her later contradictions are therefore genuinely contradictions, not retroactive thematic inventions.

Nana does not follow Ren.

The decision hurts. She is lonely. But she allows loss to produce a new form of self rather than immediately collapsing geography into romance. Nobu's invitation—**「ナナ！ おれのバンドで歌って！」**—routes the overflow of post-separation feeling back into voice. The narration's formulation that overflowing feeling becomes voice establishes music as something more than career planning.

This first sequence gives the template for much of Nana's life:

1. a relationship matters enormously;
2. the relationship threatens to determine her future;
3. Nana refuses total determination;
4. the refusal produces loneliness;
5. music becomes the form in which she can remain a self without pretending the attachment did not matter.

That is the original Nana solution.

The entire series will test whether the solution can survive success, reunion, possession, grief, and death.

# 3. Independence is not comfort with isolation

Volume 2 closes the door on one of the easiest misreadings of Nana.

She directly says:

> **「あたし ずっと 一人で寂しかった」**  
> **「もう二度と 独りぼっちは嫌だよ……」**

The girl who refused to follow Ren is lonely.

That does not invalidate the refusal. It clarifies it.

Nana does not pursue independence because she wants a life without attachment. She pursues independence because she cannot accept attachment as sufficient grounds for surrendering self-authorship. Those are radically different motivations.

This distinction becomes one of the most important longitudinal corrections in the series. At several points Nana herself treats the alternatives as though they are:

> stand alone

or

> burden somebody.

Yasu repeatedly challenges that binary. Hachi disrupts it through ordinary domestic life. BLAST complicates it by making artistic identity collective. Ren complicates it by remaining both beloved and independent. Future Nana complicates it by surviving in physical separation without becoming socially empty.

Nana's actual developmental problem is therefore not learning that people need people. She already knows the pain of loneliness.

Her problem is learning:

> **how to need people without making need either shameful dependence or a demand for guarantee.**

# 4. Roughness as self-authorship, armor, and intimacy

Nana's roughness is one of the most visible elements of her characterization, but it is analytically weak to reduce it either to "tough girl" styling or to compensation for hidden fragility.

It performs several functions at once.

First, it is genuine identity. Nana likes punk, masculine-coded bluntness, cigarettes, black clothing, aggressive humor, and a style of speech that distinguishes her sharply from Hachi's more conventionally feminine register. The manga does not treat this as a costume she must eventually outgrow in order to become emotionally healthy.

Second, roughness protects her from pity. Later celebrity and crisis volumes repeatedly show that Nana can accept care from particular people while resenting being converted into a generic victim. She wants concern that recognizes *her*, not sympathy that turns her into an image of damage.

Third, roughness is a language of intimacy. Nana often shows affection through teasing, insult, orders, physical proximity, cigarette-sharing sociality, practical action, and the refusal to become ceremonially sentimental. With Nobu, Yasu, Shin, Ren, and Hachi, bluntness often indicates that formality is unnecessary.

Fourth, roughness can conceal unspoken tests.

Nana often resists asking directly for what she wants. Volume 8 makes the mechanism unusually legible in her difficulty asking Yasu to accompany her. She does not want to command another person to abandon a life for her. That is ethically sympathetic. But the desire can become:

> **If I do not ask and you choose me anyway, I will know I matter.**

Autonomy thus becomes a relational test.

This is one of Nana's most consequential defensive structures. She is often proud enough not to demand openly and needy enough to experience the absence of unsolicited choosing as evidence that she ranks second.

The problem is not that other people fail to read her mind. The problem is that Nana can make unreadability part of the proof she requires.

# 5. Music as voice: the first self Nana can trust

Music is the most durable self Nana builds.

It begins as emotional overflow made audible. It becomes sociality through Nobu and BLAST. It later becomes ambition, profession, public identity, livelihood, responsibility, refuge, leverage, and finally a structure that may keep her alive.

The development is important because "music saves Nana" is true only if the phrase is allowed to become more complicated over time.

## 5.1 Art as indirect disclosure

Volume 4 provides an early key. Hachi hears BLAST's songs as saturated with endurance, being left behind, and barely standing. The analysis appropriately resists treating every lyric as literal autobiography, yet the formal juxtaposition is strong:

> **Nana can sing abandonment publicly more easily than she can explain private vulnerability conversationally.**

Art gives her emotional disclosure without requiring direct supplication.

That is a perfect medium for a character who fears both abandonment and dependence.

## 5.2 Reira and the wound of comparison

Nana's response to Reira's voice punctures the fantasy that artistic selfhood makes her invulnerable. Reira can make Nana feel hopelessly outclassed.

The rivalry matters because Nana's artistic identity is not merely expressive. It is evaluative. She wants to be good enough, distinctive enough, successful enough, and eventually powerful enough that the music belongs to her on terms she can defend.

This makes Reira more than "Ren's bandmate" or romantic threat. She is evidence that Nana's strongest identity can itself become insecure.

## 5.3 The stage as completion

By Volume 9 Nana can say, in effect, that she has nothing but singing and that only the stage makes her **完全体**—complete.

That formulation is both triumphant and alarming.

It is triumphant because Nana has found a domain in which she can feel whole through an activity she authors. She is not waiting for Ren or Hachi to assign meaning to her.

It is alarming because the language of completion creates another dependency structure. A self that can exist fully only onstage remains vulnerable to canceled tours, industry control, bodily failure, scandal, and the ordinary impossibility of performing forever.

The manga refuses to choose between these readings. Nana's artistic dependency is healthier in some respects than possessive dependence on another person because performance involves active skill, labor, and self-authorship. It is still dependency.

## 5.4 Singing becomes responsibility

Volume 10 adds another layer. Nana looks at BLAST and understands that other people's futures now run through her singing. Nobu's guitar, Shin's instability, the band's labor, management, staff, and audience convert the stage from private self-completion into collective livelihood.

This is maturation because Nana's art is no longer imagined as purely personal purity.

It is also dangerous because Nana can convert responsibility into another reason she is not allowed to stop.

## 5.5 Vocation can narrow morality

Volume 18 makes the danger explicit. Nana admits that the tour matters more to her than what happens to Shin.

The statement should not be softened into "she is under stress." It shows that artistic vocation can become morally narrowing. The dream that saves Nana can also make another person's crisis feel secondary to the continuity of the stage.

At the same time, cancellation demonstrates why the tour has acquired such psychic weight. When the performance route disappears, Nana does not merely lose work. She loses orientation.

The mature synthesis therefore rejects both romantic extremes:

- music is not a pure healing force;
- music is not merely avoidance.

It is a life-structure with real ethical costs.

# 6. BLAST: not background band, but distributed selfhood

BLAST is crucial because it prevents Nana's artistic identity from being reducible to the solitary singer.

Nobu, Yasu, Shin, and the wider professional apparatus become parts of the conditions under which "Nana the singer" exists. This is why her later solo transition is psychologically difficult even though the girl who came to Tokyo once imagined independence in more atomized terms.

The band gives Nana several things at once:

- artistic medium;
- peer recognition;
- work;
- social belonging;
- a reason to remain in motion;
- a public identity not defined entirely by Ren;
- people toward whom she feels responsibility;
- and a field in which her charisma becomes materially consequential.

BLAST also teaches her that selfhood can be collective without becoming fusion.

Band members remain separate persons with instruments, wounds, families, obligations, and desires. Nana cannot literally absorb them into herself without destroying the very ensemble that makes her voice possible.

That makes BLAST a practical counterexample to some of Nana's most totalizing romantic fantasies.

The band's professionalization later complicates the lesson. Once contracts, management, schedules, media strategy, protected residences, and money enter the picture, Nana experiences the group not only as chosen artistic family but as institution. The institution can sustain her and constrain her simultaneously.

This is why her later solo project does not mean "Nana finally becomes truly independent." It means that she learns to transfer the relational knowledge of BLAST into another form.

Volume 18 provides the strongest formulation. She can be physically alone while carrying people inside the choice. Her closing capacity to sleep holding a dream is not a return to the lonely Volume-1 girl. It is a new kind of independence:

> **the self can act alone without pretending it was made alone.**

# 7. Ren after reunion: intimacy without merger

The Tokyo reunion does not restore the pre-Tokyo household.

This is one of the most important facts about Nana's development.

She still wants Ren. Volume 5 removes serious doubt about that. But she no longer treats desire as automatically authorizing cohabitation. She wants BLAST, her career, her pride, her public identity, and the Tokyo self built after separation.

Her refusal to merge households immediately should therefore not be dismissed as immaturity or pride standing in the way of "true love." Pride does cause problems, but here it also protects a hard-won distinction:

> **loving Ren is not the same as becoming Ren's life.**

Nana's image of returning to "that house" only after getting older, losing pride, and becoming tired of singing is revealing. Domestic permanence is deferred into a future in which artistic struggle has ended.

This exposes an unresolved binary in her imagination:

> artist now, home later.

The series never proves that these identities must be incompatible. Nana fears they may be.

The relationship with Ren consequently becomes a laboratory for whether intimacy can survive two adult vocations.

At its best, the answer appears possible. Ren can love Nana without demanding immediate surrender of BLAST. Nana can spend nights with Ren and still practice with the band. They can desire each other while occupying different professional worlds.

At its worst, the old fusion fantasy remains alive underneath the arrangement.

# 8. The death pact: romantic fusion as Nana's most dangerous fantasy

Volume 2 reveals an early exchange:

> **「もし あたしが死んだら 一緒に死んでくれる？」**  
> **「いいよ」**

The death-together fantasy should neither be treated as throwaway goth styling nor promoted into a literal contract governing every later act.

Its importance is conceptual.

Nana's most extreme romantic fantasy imagines love proven by the abolition of separateness. If one dies, the other follows. No independent future remains in which the survivor can choose another life.

This is the logical endpoint of possession.

Yet *NANA* repeatedly positions Nana's actual growth against that fantasy. She refuses to follow Ren to Tokyo. She builds BLAST. She develops a bond with Hachi stronger in some dimensions than the Ren bond. She learns from Yasu that care need not interfere. She becomes professionally capable. She begins to see that other people have priorities beyond her. She eventually articulates the danger of wanting everything from Ren.

Volume 20 makes the fantasy conscious:

> **「全てを犠牲にして あたしを愛して欲しい」**

She wants him to sacrifice everything.

Then:

> **「いつかレンの全てを 奪い尽くしそうで 怖いんだよ」**

She is afraid she may take all of him.

This is not romantic self-celebration. It is self-recognition with fear.

The distinction matters enormously. Nana does not arrive at moral clarity by ceasing to desire totality. She arrives at a point where she can see that the desire threatens the person she loves.

Ren's death in Volume 21 does not fulfill the pact. It destroys it through asymmetry.

Ren dies. Nana remains.

The old fantasy imagined that nobody would be left behind. The reality makes Nana the one left behind.

The tragedy is therefore not "they were destined to die together." It is that Nana must confront a world in which even total love cannot eliminate the survivor's separateness.

# 9. Yasu: dependency without erotic fusion

Yasu is essential because he demonstrates that Nana's dependency system cannot be explained solely through romance.

She turns to him in crisis. She trusts his judgment. She uses him for regulation. Volume 6 makes the dependency explicit through her own language:

> **「あんたに甘え過ぎだね」**

She leans on him too much.

The relationship has a parental dimension that Nana can partially name. This does not make Yasu literally her father or reduce his own subjectivity to a caretaker role. It means his function in Nana's emotional system is unusually infrastructural.

When she is flooded by jealousy, conflict with Hachi, uncertainty about Ren, work pressure, or bodily distress, Yasu is one of the people she expects to remain structurally available.

The significance of Yasu is not merely that he is reliable. He becomes Nana's most important model of **care without possession**.

Volume 8 explicitly links Nana's aspiration to become a "normal friend" to her observation of Yasu. He cares intensely without making care an entitlement to control the cared-for person's decisions. His example gives Nana an ethic she can understand before she can fully practice it.

The later volumes then revise the relationship again.

Nana begins to ask not only what Yasu gives her, but what she has given him. In Volume 18 she recognizes the asymmetry and experiences genuine joy when she can do something for him and receive his thanks.

That is a major developmental step because it transforms dependency from extraction into reciprocity.

Her problem with **甘える / amaeru** changes accordingly. The immature version of the problem is:

> if I need someone, I am burdensome.

The more mature version becomes:

> a relationship in which I only take is incomplete, but receiving care itself is not shameful.

Volume 19 sharpens the lesson further. Yasu tells her, in effect, that disappearing in order not to burden people can itself burden them. Once a person is loved, unilateral self-removal does not restore moral neutrality.

Nana must therefore learn a difficult relational truth:

> **other people's care is part of their agency too. Refusing all care can become another way of deciding for them.**

# 10. Hachi: the first close female friendship Nana does not know how to classify

The full Nana/Hachi relationship belongs in Document 04. This document should not preemptively settle whether the bond is best categorized as friendship, chosen family, romantic love, queer intimacy, domestic partnership, or some combination whose categories shift over time.

What matters here is what Hachi does to **Nana's self-concept**.

Volume 4 supplies an essential biographical correction:

> **「親しい女友達っていなかった」**

Nana has not previously possessed a mature repertoire for close female friendship.

Hachi is therefore not simply a new member of an established friendship category. Nana is learning the category while living it.

That helps explain why her reactions can be so intense and why the relationship repeatedly spills into languages that do not fit ordinary friendship comfortably.

Apartment 707 matters because Hachi's care enters through ordinary acts rather than heroic rescue. Food, furniture, conversation, shopping, sleep, cleaning, waiting, returning, and watching each other create a home whose strength comes from repetition.

For Nana, whose inherited home is fragile or absent, this is profound.

Hachi becomes incorporated into the autobiographical beginning of Nana's adult Tokyo life. Volume 8 fixes their meeting on March 5, Nana's twentieth birthday. Her professional new beginning and Hachi's arrival become temporally inseparable.

Hachi also offers a form of intimacy Nana does not receive from Ren or Yasu. Ren is lover and artistic peer. Yasu is protector and regulator. Hachi is domestic witness, admirer, irritant, nurturer, emotional audience, and person who can make a room feel inhabited.

This is why loss of Hachi's ordinary presence becomes so destabilizing even after Nana has reunited with Ren.

Ren is not enough to regulate Nana's loneliness.

That finding is decisive.

# 11. Pet, collar, yard: why Nana's tenderness and possessiveness cannot be cleanly separated

The pet language surrounding Hachi is one of the clearest places where Nana's care and control become entangled.

At first the language is comic and affectionate. Hachi is Hachikō. The dog image fits her exuberance, loyalty, appetite, emotional transparency, and tendency to wait for affection.

But the metaphor develops teeth.

Volume 6 makes Nana's possessive feeling explicit. Volume 7 gives the famous revision: Nana supports Hachi/Nobu partly because it would let her keep Hachikō **"free-range in my own yard."**

The formulation is psychologically exact.

Nana has learned that Hachi cannot be caged. She can imagine freedom. But she still imagines the field of freedom as belonging to Nana.

Volume 8 takes the self-awareness farther. Future Nana describes deliberately keeping some distance because she feared how strongly she wanted to put a collar on Hachi. Withdrawal is therefore not always evidence of weak attachment. Sometimes distance is Nana's attempt to prevent herself from acting on the desire to bind.

This is an important correction to simple attachment models.

Nana is not unaware that possession can be wrong. She often knows it and tries to behave better than she feels.

That ethical fact matters even before Document 07 performs the full moral analysis.

Her development is frequently behavioral before it is emotional:

> she still wants monopoly;
> she does not always act as though monopoly is justified.

That distinction is one of the reasons Nana remains morally interesting rather than merely possessive.

# 12. "First love" and the limits of Nana's categories

Volume 8 gives the most important self-description of Nana's attachment to Hachi before the late-series future material.

Nana compares herself to a boy newly learning **恋**—romantic love—and describes the feeling as like a first love, while explicitly denying that the struggle is **性欲**, sexual desire.

The combination should be preserved exactly rather than flattened.

It establishes that:

- Nana herself experiences the attachment as exceeding ordinary friendship categories available to her;
- she reaches for explicitly romantic vocabulary;
- she distinguishes that vocabulary from sexual desire;
- and the manga does not therefore grant us permission to convert "romantic-adjacent" into a simple categorical statement about sexual orientation or conventional couplehood.

For Nana's character study, the deeper significance is not the label. It is that Hachi forces her to confront a form of love for which her existing scripts—lover, bandmate, protector, family, friend—are inadequate.

That inadequacy drives later control fantasies.

When a relationship has no secure social script, being **needed** can begin to substitute for having a recognized role.

Volume 7's distinction—Hachi wanted to be needed rather than possess Nana—belongs to Hachi's interiority, but Nana develops the mirror problem. She wants Hachi free, yet wants proof that Hachi's freedom will keep returning to her.

This is why the Nana/Hachi bond becomes so important to Nana's autonomy problem even when this document defers the full dyad analysis.

# 13. Rescue: Nana wants to be the person who can save without needing to be saved

Nana repeatedly casts herself as protector.

She wants Hachi protected from humiliation. She arranges joy for her. She wants Nobu to be good for her. She wants to become successful enough that Hachi can look at her and return. She sometimes imagines herself as the hero of Hachi's story.

The rescue fantasy is loving.

It is also asymmetrical.

Volume 10 makes the problem explicit. Nana answers Hachi's real request—keep singing—and then adds a project Hachi did not request: become so impressive inside Hachi's narrative that Hachi will come back.

The distinction is crucial:

> responding to another person's expressed desire

is not the same as

> deciding what that person's life should mean.

Future Nana performs the correction herself. Present Nana casts herself as Hachi's hero. Future Nana says she can no longer be that hero, while Hachi remains the heroine of Nana's own story. The revision does not diminish the bond. It relinquishes jurisdiction over Hachi's narrative.

That is one of Nana's clearest mature ethical developments.

She begins to understand that being central to someone's life does not authorize authorship of that life.

The same correction later enters her relation to rescue more generally. Nana's deepest problem is not simply that she wants to help people. It is that rescue can provide a role secure enough to make abandonment less frightening. If another person needs Nana, Nana knows where she belongs.

Chapter 84 therefore lands directly on this wound from the opposite side: if Nana appears to recover, Hachi fears that Nana may no longer need **her**.

The series ends with both protagonists facing versions of the same problem:

> **Can love survive becoming unnecessary as a rescue function?**

# 14. Professional humiliation and the transition from purity to strategy

Nana's career arc is sometimes described as punk authenticity being threatened by commercialization. The mature text supports a more complex reading.

Nana is never economically disinterested.

Volume 8 explicitly has her describe singing as a **means of living**. She wants scale, money, audience, stage, and professional success. That does not make the art insincere. It means she understands music as work before the industry fully captures her.

The media attack around Ren initially wounds her because it makes her publicly legible as **Ren's woman** before she is secure as Nana Osaki, singer.

This is an identity injury.

Her response in Volume 10 is significant: she decides to use the connection rather than preserve a fantasy of a perfectly clean path to recognition. She converts humiliation into ambition.

That shift is maturation, not corruption.

The same development culminates in the solo project. Nana wants punk, but she also understands that sales can become bargaining power. Being number one is not necessarily the final artistic value; it can be leverage with which to defend the conditions of the music she wants to make.

The early Nana often protects selfhood by refusal.

The later Nana increasingly learns to protect selfhood through **negotiation**.

That is a more adult autonomy because the world cannot always be kept pure by saying no. Sometimes autonomy requires understanding institutions well enough to create room inside them.

---

# 15. Celebrity: when the public possesses the image Nana wanted to author

Celebrity intensifies Nana's central contradiction because she wants recognition without surrendering ownership of self.

The early singer can transform a dining table into a stage for Hachi. The later singer exists inside an apparatus of labels, publicity, security, paparazzi, fan demand, magazine narratives, and corporate strategy.

Success makes Nana visible.

Visibility does not mean she controls what is seen.

The media repeatedly reduce complicated relations to simpler stories:

- Nana as Ren's woman;
- BLAST versus Trapnest;
- engagement as celebrity event;
- Hachi as Nana's former roommate;
- private relationships as public evidence.

This is especially difficult for Nana because public authorship resembles relational possession at institutional scale. A photograph, headline, or marketing strategy can decide what her life means without asking her.

The paradox is that Nana also depends on that public.

An audience sustains the career that sustains her identity. Fame gives her money and leverage. It turns BLAST into a livelihood. It makes the dream larger than a private coping mechanism.

Celebrity is therefore not simply an external villain. It is another dependency relation Nana must learn to negotiate.

She cannot become fully herself without other people's attention, yet other people's attention can turn her into an object.

That tension mirrors her private life almost perfectly.

---

# 16. Marriage to Ren: authentic desire captured by institutional form

Volume 11 gives Nana unusual directness:

> **「結婚して」**

Nana proposes marriage to Ren.

The initiative matters. Whatever the music industry later does with the engagement, the personal origin is not fake. Nana herself wants to formalize the relationship.

This complicates any simplistic model in which her punk selfhood is opposed to marriage or domesticity. Nana can want marriage without wanting to become economically or artistically absorbed into Ren.

The institution then demonstrates the larger *NANA* pattern: authentic desire and instrumental use can coexist.

Marriage may help them live together legibly. It can protect both people after scandal. It can produce a reassuring public story. It can be consumed as celebrity spectacle.

Nana recognizes the degradation when something intimate becomes **「ただの見世物」**, merely a spectacle.

The correct conclusion is not that the marriage is therefore false.

It is that formal recognition cannot guarantee ownership of the meaning of one's own relationship.

Ren later distinguishes form from bond by suggesting that legal registration matters less than being able to remain with Nana. Nana's development similarly requires distinguishing:

- the desire to be bound;
- the social form of the bond;
- the public use of the bond;
- and the fantasy that formalization can prevent loss.

Ren's death ultimately proves the last guarantee impossible.

# 17. The body: the part of Nana that refuses pure self-authorship

Nana's body becomes increasingly important as the series progresses because it places limits on a self-concept built around will, toughness, and performance.

## 17.1 Panic and bodily interruption

The hyperventilation/panic episodes matter because Nana is used to converting distress into function.

She can sing through jealousy.
She can work through humiliation.
She can turn loss into ambition.
She can make roughness perform stability.

The body interrupts the conversion.

A stress-linked physical crisis cannot be solved simply by deciding to be tougher. Nana's hostility toward psychiatric implications is revealing because it threatens her preferred self-understanding: if the body can expose distress she has not consciously authorized, selfhood is less sovereign than she wants it to be.

## 17.2 Sex, contraception, and motherhood

Nana's reproductive position also resists stereotype.

She uses contraception. She is serious about career timing. She is afraid she may not be capable of motherhood. She also wonders whether pregnancy itself could awaken a desire to give birth.

The correct model is neither anti-maternal punk rejection nor hidden conventional femininity.

It is:

> **career commitment + reproductive self-control + genuine uncertainty about motherhood.**

This matters because Nana's identity is repeatedly misread when toughness is treated as proof she lacks ordinary domestic or bodily desires.

## 17.3 Grief becomes stillness

After Ren's death, the body takes another form of refusal.

Nana does not produce the expected spectacular grief. She becomes still. Time stops subjectively. She does not reach for Ren's hand at the viewing.

The manga does not establish one single internal explanation. Shock, dissociation, fear of tactile finality, and inability to make death material are compatible readings.

What matters is that the body now communicates what Nana cannot organize into speech.

The woman whose voice can fill stages becomes nearly voiceless when the most important loss can no longer be converted into performance.

---

# 18. Future Nana: survival after the failure of the old self

The future material prevents Nana's present-day collapse from being treated as her final state.

By Volume 16 the reader is shown Nana alive abroad. The public rumor that BLAST's Nana is dead is therefore false within the future frame available to us. Later photographs further support her survival.

The fact of survival does not solve the mystery.

The text does not establish:

- why she left;
- when she left;
- how long she has been away;
- why she has not returned to Hachi;
- whether she intends permanent separation;
- or what final form her relationships take.

The hiatus makes those destinations unavailable.

What the future does establish is psychologically more important than a mystery solution.

Nana continues to sing.

She is capable of ordinary embodied connection with at least one person in the future setting.

She is not socially empty.

And Volume 18 makes explicit that she has experienced suicidal ideation. She thinks of the sea as a place where death is available and describes the thought that she can die at any time as paradoxically helping her continue another day.

This should be called what the text supports: **suicidal ideation**, not a speculative diagnosis.

The most important future statement is directed toward Hachi:

> **「あんたに救われたこの命を / 捨てるなんて出来ないじゃない」**

Nana experiences her life as having been saved by Hachi and therefore as something she cannot simply discard.

The line is moving, but it should not be sentimentalized into cure.

It creates two truths at once:

- recognition by another person can help make survival possible;
- survival experienced as debt to another person can itself become burdensome.

The manga does not reveal how fully Nana eventually converts gratitude into self-owned desire to live.

That unresolved distinction belongs at the center of any mature account of future Nana.

---

# 19. "How to live" replaces "how to die"

Volume 18 contains one of Nana's clearest philosophical revisions:

> **「どう生きるかが きっと大事なんだよ」**

How one lives is what matters.

This does not mean the death imagery disappears. Present Nana still romanticizes dying with Ren and dying onstage. Future Nana still lives close enough to suicidal thought that death remains an imagined exit.

The development is therefore not purification.

It is a shift in criterion.

The early romantic fantasy asks whether love is total enough to justify shared annihilation.

The later Nana begins to ask whether a life can be shaped in a way worth continuing.

That is a much harder question because it lacks the dramatic finality Nana often finds aesthetically compelling.

Death can prove everything in one gesture.

Living requires repeated maintenance.

This is why the late series' movement toward cooking, work, gratitude, friendship, saying thank you, accepting care, and singing another day is ethically important. Nana's mature challenge is not to discover one grand reason to live. It is to tolerate the ordinary accumulation of reasons that never become absolute.

---

# 20. Volume 20: self-recognition without release

Volume 20 is Nana's most important introspective volume because she can finally articulate the symmetry she has resisted.

She knows:

> Ren has something more important than Nana.

She knows:

> Hachi has someone more important than Nana.

She knows:

> Nana herself has a dream she refuses to surrender.

Then she asks:

> **「自分だって 譲れない夢があるのに / どうして あたしはそれが許せないんだよ」**

Why can she not accept in others what she claims for herself?

This is not a small insight.

It means Nana's central problem can no longer be explained as lack of empathy or inability to conceptualize separateness. She sees the structure clearly.

She still hurts.

The distinction between cognition and affect becomes decisive. Nana has reached a level of self-knowledge at which she can identify the contradiction without being able to dissolve the attachment system that produces it.

Her fear of taking everything from Ren shows that the insight has moral force. She is not simply saying, "I know this is unreasonable, but I deserve it anyway." She is afraid of becoming destructive.

At the same time she says:

> **「あたしを救えるのは やっぱり自分の夢だけだ」**

Only her own dream can save her.

Again, the line must be held in tension.

The dream is genuinely self-authored. It is not merely repression. But the insistence on self-rescue also appears when relational life feels unmanageable.

By Volume 20 Nana can recognize both halves of herself:

- the person who wants everybody to remain free enough to choose her;
- the person who fears she will not survive what they choose.

She has not yet learned to live peacefully inside the contradiction.

---

# 21. Ren's death: when the impossible guarantee fails absolutely

Volume 21 does not resolve Nana's attachment problem philosophically. It subjects it to catastrophe.

Ren dies while acting inside obligations that exceed the Nana/Ren dyad. The reader knows that his immediate destination involved Reira; Nana does not receive that complete information. That asymmetry must remain intact.

The significance is not that Ren "chose Reira over Nana." That would simplify a crisis into romantic ranking.

The significance is that Ren remained a person whose life contained relationships, work, addictions, obligations, fear, and action beyond Nana.

Nana's fantasy of absolute priority cannot survive the fact of death.

Her first response is revealing: she talks about work.

This is not mere professionalism. It is an emergency attempt to re-enter the identity whose script she still understands.

If she is Nana the singer, there is a next task.

If she is Nana whose lover is dead, there is no established script.

Yasu forces the second identity into visibility.

The event also exposes the limits of substitution. Hachi, Yasu, BLAST, Nobu, and others remain. Nana is not literally alone. Yet Ren occupied a singular composite position:

- lover;
- erotic object;
- musical peer;
- co-origin;
- domestic memory;
- rival source of autonomy;
- abandonment wound;
- imagined future;
- imagined death partner.

No one else can simply replace that configuration.

The late-Nana question therefore becomes:

> **Can she reorganize dependency without replacing Ren with another absolute?**

The extant text does not answer.

---

# 22. Chapters 81–84: apparent vitality is not yet recovery

The continuation gives Nana some of the ordinary behaviors grief initially suspended.

She eats.
She becomes irritated.
She jokes.
She participates in group life.
She sings again with Nobu accompanying her.

These are meaningful changes.

They do not prove recovery.

Yasu supplies the explicit adversarial reading: Nana can look as though she is better while perhaps instead having given up on caring about things she previously fought to control.

That counterreading is essential because it prevents the series from converting restored function into inner resolution.

For Nana's character arc, this distinction echoes earlier episodes in which functioning concealed distress:

- roughness can conceal need;
- work can conceal collapse;
- stage completion can conceal dependency;
- professional ambition can conceal humiliation;
- apparent independence can conceal loneliness.

Chapter 84 therefore ends the extant character arc in a deliberately unstable state.

Nana has re-entered ordinary life enough that Hachi can fear becoming unnecessary.

But the manga has not established that Nana herself has reconstructed a durable reason to live, a stable relation to Ren's death, or a non-possessive model of connection.

The last available Nana is neither "broken forever" nor "healed."

She is functioning inside unresolved grief.

---

# 23. Gender presentation: Nana's autonomy is not a refusal of femininity

Nana's gender presentation is deliberately disruptive within the cast.

Her punk silhouette, blunt speech, cigarettes, physical confidence, dark clothing, and frequent refusal of conventional softness distinguish her sharply from Hachi's romantic/domestic femininity and Reira's polished celebrity femininity.

Yet the manga repeatedly prevents style from becoming essence.

Nana can:

- desire men;
- want marriage;
- imagine domestic return;
- fear pregnancy and still wonder about motherhood;
- enjoy jewelry and engagement symbolism;
- be emotionally dependent;
- be jealous of other women;
- act protectively toward Hachi;
- occupy conventionally masculine-coded rescue positions;
- and remain uncertain about the exact category of her love for Hachi.

None of these facts cancels the punk identity.

The more useful reading is that fashion and gender presentation are forms of authorship. Nana builds a body that communicates control, seriousness, erotic confidence, subcultural belonging, and distance from the conventional "good girl" image.

The danger is to treat that authored surface as proof she has transcended needs culturally coded as feminine—home, reassurance, marriage, being chosen, being cared for.

She has not.

*NANA* gains much of its force from refusing the proposition that toughness and need are opposites.

---

# 24. Nana's dependency system is distributed, not singular

A mature Nana model should resist asking, "Who is the person Nana really needs?"

The answer changes depending on what kind of need is meant.

**Ren** provides erotic love, artistic mirroring, shared origin, domestic memory, and the most totalizing fusion fantasy.

**Hachi** provides domestic recognition, ordinary home, emotional audience, first-close-female-friendship experimentation, and a bond Nana herself later treats as stronger than Ren's in at least some relational dimensions.

**Yasu** provides regulation, safety, non-erotic infrastructure, paternal continuity, and a model of care without interference.

**Nobu** provides musical origin, peer affection, loyalty, and a form of past that remains alive without demanding fusion.

**Shin** helps make Nana responsible for someone younger and more vulnerable, while also exposing the limits of her artistic priorities.

**BLAST** provides collective vocation, belonging, labor, and a self larger than private romance.

**The stage** provides completion, agency, audience, money, and a reliable role.

**The audience** eventually provides social recognition and the material conditions of the career, while also threatening to consume Nana as public image.

The distributed model matters because it explains why no single relational "solution" ever cures Nana.

Reunion with Ren does not eliminate need for Hachi.
Hachi's love does not eliminate need for Yasu.
Yasu's care does not replace BLAST.
BLAST does not eliminate sexual/domestic longing.
Success does not eliminate rivalry.
Marriage does not eliminate fear.
The stage does not eliminate bodily vulnerability.

Nana is not a puzzle whose correct partner will make her whole.

She is a person whose self is constituted through multiple relationships and practices.

Her growth depends on accepting that fact without turning any one node into an absolute guarantee.

---

# 25. The central contradiction: Nana wants freely chosen devotion that behaves like inevitability

Nana's deepest contradiction can now be stated precisely.

She dislikes coercion directed at herself.
She does not want another person to decide her route.
She resists being owned.
She values the right to choose work, residence, timing, and relational form.

She also longs for a form of love so total that the beloved's choice becomes functionally guaranteed.

This is why her strongest fantasy is not simple domination.

She does not usually want to force Ren or Hachi to stay through explicit command.

She wants them to be free—and to use that freedom to choose her absolutely.

That is psychologically understandable and structurally impossible.

If another person is genuinely free, their choices cannot be guaranteed in advance.

Nana's attachment problem therefore sits exactly at the border between love and autonomy:

> **she wants proof of love that only freedom can provide, but the existence of freedom means the proof can always change.**

Her unspoken tests, pride, jealousy, pet/collar imagery, hero fantasy, desire for sacrifice, and terror of being left behind all emerge from attempts to solve this impossibility.

The mature Nana increasingly recognizes the contradiction.

She has not overcome it by Chapter 84.

---

# 26. Developmental map: what actually changes in Nana

Nana does change substantially across the extant narrative, but not through a clean sequence from unhealthy to healthy.

## Stage 1 — Self-authorship through refusal

Early Nana learns that love does not automatically determine geography. She remains behind when Ren leaves and returns to music.

**Strength gained:** agency.

**Cost:** loneliness can become proof that autonomy means being alone.

## Stage 2 — Chosen home and distributed attachment

707, Hachi, BLAST, Yasu, and reunion with Ren teach Nana that several bonds can coexist.

**Strength gained:** connection without immediate surrender of vocation.

**Cost:** the more people matter, the more opportunities for abandonment and jealousy appear.

## Stage 3 — Possession becomes conscious enough to regulate behavior

The pet/collar/yard sequence makes Nana aware that she can want to bind Hachi. Yasu becomes an explicit model of caring without interference.

**Strength gained:** emotional desire and ethical behavior can be separated.

**Cost:** restraint often takes the form of withdrawal and unspoken tests rather than direct communication.

## Stage 4 — Professional identity becomes adult strategy

Nana accepts money, market power, publicity, and later solo leverage as parts of artistic life.

**Strength gained:** autonomy becomes negotiational rather than merely oppositional.

**Cost:** vocation can become a new absolute and can narrow concern for others.

## Stage 5 — Rescue fantasy is revised

Present Nana imagines herself as Hachi's hero. Future Nana relinquishes the role while keeping Hachi central to her own story.

**Strength gained:** recognition that love does not authorize authorship of another person's narrative.

**Cost:** Nana still seeks secure roles that make her place in relationships undeniable.

## Stage 6 — Reciprocity begins to replace one-way dependence

Nana recognizes how much she has leaned on Yasu and discovers pleasure in giving back. She learns that receiving care does not necessarily make her weak.

**Strength gained:** dependency can become reciprocal.

**Cost:** the old shame around needing people remains active.

## Stage 7 — Self-recognition without emotional release

Volume 20 lets Nana articulate the unfairness of wanting other people to sacrifice priorities she herself will not surrender.

**Strength gained:** unusually strong self-knowledge.

**Cost:** insight does not dissolve the need.

## Stage 8 — Catastrophic asymmetry

Ren's death destroys the fantasy that total love can guarantee shared destiny.

**Potential developmental demand:** survive without replacing Ren with another absolute.

**Status:** unresolved.

## Stage 9 — Function after catastrophe

Chapters 81–84 return Nana to food, irritation, humor, companionship, and song.

**Potential developmental demand:** construct ordinary life rather than merely resume function.

**Status:** unresolved.

This is why the character should not be summarized as "learning independence."

She begins independent in one important sense.

What she has to learn is **interdependence without engulfment**.

# 27. Counterreadings and hypothesis stress-test

A mature character study should make the strongest competing interpretations visible rather than writing as if Nana has one secret essence.

## Counterreading A — Nana is simply abandonment-driven

This explains a great deal: her mother, absent father, grandmother's death, sensitivity to being left, totalizing desire, and home instability.

It is still insufficient.

Nana's ambition, artistic taste, competitiveness, punk identity, pleasure in performance, strategic professional growth, and concern for artistic quality cannot be reduced to attachment compensation. The dream predates several later relational crises and remains genuinely self-authored.

**Verdict:** abandonment is foundational context, not total explanation.

## Counterreading B — Nana's independence is only defensive performance

There is strong evidence for defensiveness. Pride, reluctance to ask, withdrawal, and fear of dependency all support it.

But the manga repeatedly shows real agency: she does not follow Ren, builds BLAST, makes professional decisions, proposes marriage herself, accepts solo work on her own terms, and develops market strategy.

**Verdict:** independence is both authentic value and armor. Calling it only armor erases Nana's achievements.

## Counterreading C — Ren is Nana's only true love; everything else is secondary

Ren occupies an irreplaceable configuration in Nana's life and remains the clearest erotic/romantic partner.

But the text itself complicates hierarchy. Nana's bond with Hachi later becomes stronger than Ren's in at least one explicit retrospective formulation. Yasu satisfies needs Ren does not. BLAST and the stage provide identity no lover can substitute for.

**Verdict:** Ren is singular, not exhaustive.

## Counterreading D — Hachi is Nana's "real" romantic partner and Ren is a mistaken heterosexual attachment

The manga provides unusually strong romantic-adjacent language for Nana/Hachi, including **恋**, first-love analogy, domesticity, jealousy, rescue, and future centrality.

It also preserves Nana's sexual/romantic desire for Ren, explicitly distinguishes her Hachi feeling from **性欲**, and never converts the dyad into a conventional declared couple in the extant text.

**Verdict:** this document should not resolve the category. Document 04 must hold the ambiguity rather than replacing one reduction with another.

## Counterreading E — Nana is fundamentally selfish

The evidence for selfishness is real. She can prioritize her tour over Shin, wish for total sacrifice, imagine Hachi inside her own yard, and cast herself as another person's hero.

She also repeatedly recognizes her own unfairness, restrains impulses she regards as possessive, gives care without credit, takes responsibility for others, and becomes increasingly concerned with reciprocity.

**Verdict:** selfishness is one tendency within a person capable of moral self-observation and behavioral revision.

## Counterreading F — Work is merely avoidance

After Ren's death, work clearly functions as emergency identity. Earlier, performance can also metabolize pain that Nana cannot discuss directly.

Yet music is not created by the crisis. It is constitutive of Nana from the beginning and produces skill, community, livelihood, pleasure, authorship, and meaning.

**Verdict:** work is both refuge and vocation. The analysis fails if it treats either function as unreal.

## Counterreading G — Chapter 84 proves Nana is recovering

Eating, joking, irritation, and singing are meaningful signs of re-engagement.

Yasu's counterreading is explicit: visible animation may coexist with surrender or inner collapse.

**Verdict:** recovery is possible but not established. Function and resolution remain distinct.

---

# 28. What Nana understands by the extant endpoint—and what she still does not

By Chapter 84, Nana has accumulated forms of self-knowledge that the Volume-1 Nana did not possess.

She knows that:

- loving another person does not make that person property;
- she can become jealous even while believing jealousy is unfair;
- she wants forms of sacrifice she cannot ethically reciprocate without giving up her own dream;
- Hachi matters in a category ordinary friendship does not fully explain for her;
- Yasu's care has become something she must learn to reciprocate rather than merely draw upon;
- singing can be livelihood and bargaining power without ceasing to be art;
- art cannot protect the body from every crisis;
- public recognition can misrecognize the private person;
- marriage can be authentic and still become spectacle;
- she can live alone without being wholly alone;
- and "how to live" matters more than the dramatic beauty of how to die.

What she does **not** demonstrably know is equally important.

The extant text does not establish that she understands:

- the full relation between her mother's abandonment and her demand for total priority;
- how to ask directly for care without turning the request into a test;
- how to tolerate being important without being primary;
- how to grieve Ren without replacing the lost absolute with work, Hachi, Yasu, or another structure;
- how to transform survival from debt into desire;
- why future Nana remains separated from the 707 network;
- whether she can return without making return a restoration of the past;
- or what final balance she reaches between art, home, and relational plurality.

Those absences are not failures of the analysis.

They are the story's unfinished horizon.

# 29. Final character thesis

Osaki Nana is often compelling because she looks like a character who already possesses the answer many coming-of-age protagonists seek.

She has style.
She has talent.
She has erotic confidence.
She can say no.
She refuses to follow a man simply because she loves him.
She chooses Tokyo for herself.
She builds a band.
She becomes famous.
She survives humiliation.
She can turn emotion into art.

The manga's deeper achievement is to show why none of those accomplishments makes dependence disappear.

Nana's life asks whether a person can become fully self-authored without becoming sealed off from others.

Her early answer is to protect the self through refusal.

Her middle answer is to distribute need across lover, friend, band, protector, home, and stage.

Her most dangerous answer is to seek proof that the people she loves will choose her so absolutely that their freedom ceases to feel risky.

Her mature insight is that this demand is impossible and potentially destructive.

Her late tragedy is that insight arrives before she has learned how to live comfortably without the guarantee.

Ren's death makes the problem irreversible. No amount of possession could have prevented mortality. No marriage could guarantee shared timing. No death pact can save the survivor from being the survivor. No career can immediately translate grief into meaning.

Future Nana nevertheless remains alive.

That fact matters more than any single theory about why she leaves.

She sings. She forms at least some ordinary connection. She thinks about death and does not yet take it. She remembers Hachi's saving care. She continues existing in a world where the old structures have failed.

The strongest final formulation through Chapter 84 is therefore:

> **Nana's autonomy begins as the refusal to let love author her life. It matures when she begins to understand that being authored by nobody is not the same thing as being free. Freedom for Nana increasingly requires the ability to receive care, give care back, preserve vocation, tolerate other people's separate priorities, and remain connected without turning connection into ownership. Her tragedy is that she can understand this ethic before she can fully inhabit it. Her unresolved future is the question of whether survival can eventually become a self-authored life rather than merely the continuation of debts, dreams, and absences.**

That is the Nana model the remaining synthesis should inherit.

# 30. Baseline propositions frozen for later documents

The following propositions should be treated as the current Phase-3 Nana baseline unless later primary-source verification requires correction:

1. **Nana's independence is authentic and defensive at the same time.** Neither side should erase the other.
2. **Inherited home deprivation is central to her attachment structure, but not a total explanation of her artistic identity.**
3. **Music is simultaneously voice, vocation, livelihood, collective responsibility, refuge, bargaining power, and dependency structure.**
4. **Ren is singular but not exhaustive:** lover, artistic peer, co-origin, domestic memory, abandonment wound, and fusion fantasy.
5. **Yasu demonstrates non-erotic dependency and becomes Nana's clearest model of care without possession.**
6. **Hachi transforms Nana's understanding of intimacy and home, but the categorical interpretation of the dyad remains reserved for Document 04.**
7. **Nana's possessiveness becomes increasingly conscious; moral development often occurs through behavioral restraint before emotional purification.**
8. **The central autonomy contradiction is that Nana wants others to be free enough to choose her and wants their choosing to be permanent enough to feel inevitable.**
9. **Professional growth moves Nana from autonomy-through-refusal toward autonomy-through-negotiation.**
10. **Her body repeatedly limits the fantasy of sovereign will, especially through stress responses, reproductive uncertainty, grief, and future suicidality.**
11. **Ren's death destroys rather than fulfills the death-together fantasy.** The late ethical problem is survival after asymmetrical loss.
12. **Future Nana is alive in the scenes shown; the cause and ultimate meaning of her separation remain unresolved.**
13. **Visible functioning after Ren's death is not equivalent to psychological recovery.**
14. **The mature developmental target is not independence from others but reciprocal interdependence without engulfment.**
15. **The hiatus leaves Nana's trajectory legible but her destination unknown.**

These propositions provide the Osaki Nana side of the structural foundation required before the corpus can proceed to Hachi's independent character study and, only afterward, the central Nana/Hachi relationship synthesis.
