# 01 — Series Architecture, Chronology, and Volume Progression
## How 『NANA』 changes shape across the complete extant narrative

### Scope and governing question

This document is the first mature structural synthesis of the frozen sequential *NANA* corpus: Japanese tankōbon Volumes 1–21, followed by the uncollected continuation through Chapter 84. It does not incorporate *NANA 7.8* or other retrospective paratext; those materials are deliberately reserved for the later supplemental-material phase. It also does not use the anime as evidence for the manga.

Its governing question is:

> **What kind of story does 『NANA』 become as it progresses?**

The answer is not that the manga abandons one genre and becomes another. Rather, its initial forms are repeatedly retained and reclassified by later experience. The provincial romantic comedy does not disappear when the story becomes a music-industry drama. The domestic roommate story does not disappear when Apartment 707 ceases to be the protagonists' ordinary home. The romance plot does not disappear when pregnancy turns love into a problem of money, housing, law, work, and reproductive time. The celebrity story does not erase the private relationship story; it industrializes it. The tragedy of Ren's death does not begin a wholly new manga; it makes literal the series-long fear that beloved people can become absent faster than a shared future can be revised.

The strongest architectural description is therefore developmental:

> **『NANA』 begins as two parallel stories about young women trying to author themselves through love and migration; becomes a story of chosen domesticity, music, and overlapping relational worlds; turns adulthood into a material test of romantic fantasy; turns artistic success into an institutional and media pressure on private life; then allows retrospective narration to convert the present into memory before the characters know what will be lost. By the extant endpoint, the central problem is no longer simply who loves whom, but how people remain connected after possession, rescue, marriage, fame, grief, and death have all failed as guarantees of permanence.**

That progression is the organizing argument of this document.

The evidence-routing convention is:

> **this synthesis → frozen Document 15 → volume analysis → evidence ID → original source locator.**

Short evidence IDs are cited where they are especially load-bearing. The purpose is not to reproduce the twenty-two sequential close readings but to make their cumulative architecture visible.

# 1. Master chronological orientation

## 1.1 Two origin stories before there is a shared story

Volume 1 is structurally unusual because the title's central pair does not yet exist as a pair. Komatsu Nana and Osaki Nana receive separate origin narratives. Each woman is already organized around attachment, but neither woman's attachment provides a complete answer to the question of who gets to author her life.

Komatsu Nana repeatedly narrativizes romance. She can recognize that a relationship has no future and still experience separation as catastrophic. Her early imagination is cinematic: what she wants is not only affection but the feeling of living inside a romance recognizable as a romance. Tokyo initially enters her story as the place another person leaves for. The city is an external destination capable of reorganizing her emotional world before it becomes her own chosen destination.

Osaki Nana appears at first to offer the opposite model. She has a rougher self-presentation, artistic vocation, and a much more explicit refusal to let romantic attachment automatically determine geography. Yet Volume 1 prevents independence from being read as emotional detachment. Her life with Ren is precious enough to feel dreamlike; her attraction is explicitly mixed with desire, envy, urgency, and artistic identification. Ren tells her to live as she wishes, and Yasu insists that Nana is not Ren's pet and must decide for herself. Nana's eventual return to singing after Ren's departure therefore matters because she does not preserve autonomy by ceasing to love him. She preserves it by building a self that can survive love's failure to determine a shared route. [V01:E006–E010]

This establishes the series' first deep structural opposition:

> **attachment can be real without being sovereign.**

Everything that follows will complicate that principle.

## 1.2 March 5, 2001: coincidence becomes a shared chronology

The two narratives intersect on the train to Tokyo. The manga later supplies a hard date: Nana Osaki identifies meeting Hachi as occurring on **March 5, 2001**, her twentieth birthday. [V08:E007]

That date is more than trivia. Before March 5, the two protagonists inhabit parallel biographies. After March 5, the work possesses a shared clock.

The train gives Hachi the language of fate: two women of the same age, on the same train, traveling to the same city, both named Nana. Apartment 707 converts that coincidence into material structure. The landlord's description of the apartment arrangement explicitly combines separate rooms and privacy with emergency mutual aid and economic practicality. The home is neither romantic fusion nor purely transactional co-tenancy. It is a designed compromise between independence and proximity. [V02:E001, E003, E007 and Phase-2 V02 backfill]

This distinction is foundational. Hachi's earlier fantasy with Shoji imagines happiness as caring for a boyfriend. 707 instead creates a household where neither woman is legally or romantically obligated to the other, yet each can become indispensable to the other's daily life.

The series' famous language of fate is therefore immediately paired with infrastructure:

> **fate may explain why Hachi thinks they met; rent, rooms, keys, food, work, habit, and mutual return explain how the relationship acquires a life.**

## 1.3 The main present: approximately one year of extraordinary compression

The principal present-tense narrative runs from the March 5, 2001 meeting to Ren's death on **March 4, 2002**, the night before Nana Osaki's twenty-first birthday, and then into the immediate post-death continuation of Chapters 81–84. The exact date of every intermediate event is not equally recoverable from the frozen evidence layer, and this synthesis should not manufacture precision where the manga does not require it. What matters structurally is the compression.

Within roughly a year, the cast undergoes:

- migration to Tokyo;
- creation and loss of homes;
- first serious professional opportunities;
- infidelity and breakup;
- reunion with former lovers;
- overlapping romantic triangles;
- pregnancy;
- reproductive decision-making;
- marriage planning and family negotiation;
- BLAST's transformation from local band to commercial property;
- escalating Trapnest and BLAST celebrity;
- paparazzi intrusion;
- management restrictions and protected housing;
- public narratives about private relationships;
- substance dependence and psychological collapse;
- and finally Ren's death.

The speed is important. Characters repeatedly make life-defining decisions without the temporal distance that later narration possesses. The mature synthesis must therefore resist judging twenty-year-old Hachi or Nana as if they already know the consequences that future Hachi and future Nana remember.

## 1.4 The future does not arrive after the present; it contaminates it from early on

The second timeline begins long before the main chronology reaches catastrophe. Future Hachi addresses Nana from early volumes. The address is initially elegiac without providing a complete explanation. Ordinary moments become memories before the reader knows why they matter. The Tama River, the 707 table, Nana's voice, hands held after a breakup, strawberry glasses, fireworks, Christmas decorations, keys, rings, postcards, photographs, and rooms acquire a double temporal existence:

1. they are objects or experiences in the present;
2. they are also future evidence that a form of life once existed.

The structural consequence is that *NANA* never permits the present to be fully innocent. Happiness is often framed as already rememberable.

By Volume 12, the future is no longer only a voice-over register. It becomes a physically depicted social world: Hachi returns to 707 for the summer fireworks; people gather; a child calls her mother; a matching yukata is waiting for Nana; the room is organized around Nana's absence. [V12:E004]

By Volume 16, future Nana is shown directly alive, long-haired and singing in an unidentified English-speaking venue marked `NAGORAD`, while other characters within the story still repeat the rumor that BLAST's Nana is dead. [V16:E011 and the V16 visual sequence]

By Volume 17, Hachi and Shin possess photographs that strongly corroborate Nana's survival; Nana's 707 key was never found among the belongings she left behind; Christmas at 707 remains a ritual of waiting. [V17:E009, E018–E019]

By Volume 18, the manga reveals future Nana's suicidality and her attempt to continue living, including her explicit understanding that Hachi once saved her life. [V18:E009, E026, E041]

By Volume 19, future Hachi's fear has changed. She does not merely fear failing to find Nana. She fears that Nana may reject the hand she extends. [V19 opening future sequence]

These fragments do not provide a complete later chronology. Their **publication order of revelation** is clearer than their **in-universe calendar order**. That distinction must be preserved.

## 1.5 March 4, 2002: catastrophe destroys the old future rather than completing it

Ren's death is the hardest chronological anchor in the late main narrative. Volume 20 ends by activating an old promise about dying together and Nana's fear of being left behind, but it is not yet entitled to turn that language into fact. Volume 21 does. Ren dies on March 4, 2002. Nana survives.

The importance of the event is not simply that a major character dies. The narrative explicitly treats the death as destruction of the characters' existing future architecture. The old blueprint cannot be used. The future becomes blank. Nana's subjective time stops. [V21:E016, E034]

The asymmetry matters. The early death-together fantasy implied that ultimate love might erase the difference between two persons through shared death. The actual event does the opposite: one person dies and another must remain. Volume 21 consequently begins shifting the work from the fantasy of total relational proof toward an ethics of survival for those who would otherwise be left behind. [V21:E023, E025]

## 1.6 Chapters 81–84: the narrative after the blueprint

The uncollected continuation does not provide a new stable future. It depicts people trying to function after the future has become unusable.

Hachi wants to remain with Nana and the others, wants to be allowed to collapse and cry, but initially returns toward Takumi because she assumes he will have nobody if she does not. [C81:E002–E004] She then begins revising that assumption: Takumi's refuge may not necessarily be her. [C83:E006]

At the BLAST dorm, Hachi's domestic competence becomes grief infrastructure. Food, errands, ordinary absurdity, and presence keep time moving without solving grief. Nobu and Hachi embrace without using physical consolation as authorization to restore their old romance. Nana eventually eats, jokes, becomes angry, and sings with Nobu's guitar—but Yasu explicitly offers the counterreading that apparent animation may be surrender rather than recovery.

The recovered Chapter 84 interval adds a second unresolved future: Hachi tells Shin that she must speak with Takumi about **their future**, while also recognizing that grief cannot be used as an excuse to drift back toward Nobu. The final surviving page then turns Nana's apparent improvement into an old question in new form: if Nana no longer seems broken enough to require rescue, is “Takumi's wife” still needed beside her?

The extant narrative therefore ends with neither reunion nor separation, neither divorce nor reconciliation, neither recovery nor death. It ends on a relational problem:

> **Can Hachi have a place beside Nana that does not depend on Nana needing to be saved?**

That question belongs fully to Document 04. Here, its architectural importance is that the manga's endpoint returns to a problem first made explicit in Volume 7: the difference between possessing someone and needing to be needed by them.

# 2. Movement I — origins, migration, and 707
## Volumes 1–3: parallel biographies become a shared domestic world

The first movement establishes the fundamental grammar from which the later series grows.

### 2.1 Volume 1: two forms of attachment, two forms of self-authorship

The split-volume structure prevents the reader from treating one Nana as the other's supporting character. Each protagonist arrives with a complete relational history, wound structure, and model of adulthood.

Komatsu Nana tends to build identity through imagined relational futures. She is capable of insight but often only after desire has already reorganized her behavior. Osaki Nana tends to defend selfhood through refusal, vocation, and controlled presentation, yet that defense coexists with profound dependency needs. The apparent opposition—dependent Hachi versus independent Nana—is therefore unstable from the beginning.

The volume's real opposition is subtler:

> **Hachi is more willing to let attachment author her future explicitly; Nana is more willing to suffer in order to preserve the appearance and reality of self-authorship.**

Later volumes will reveal dependency inside Nana and increasing authorship inside Hachi, but Volume 1 must retain its historical asymmetry.

### 2.2 Volume 2: fate requires architecture

The train encounter supplies myth. Apartment 707 supplies repeatability.

The shared apartment is the first major structural invention of the manga because it permits several things at once:

- intimacy without courtship;
- interdependence without formal kinship;
- financial cooperation without complete pooling;
- privacy through separate rooms;
- emergency access without constant access;
- and a daily life not organized by the boyfriend role.

That is why 707 eventually survives as a symbolic center after it ceases to be an ordinary residence. Its later ritual power is not arbitrary nostalgia. It was the first place where the Nanas built a relationship not supplied to them by romance, family, band hierarchy, or law.

Volume 2 also introduces future Hachi as an internal critic of younger Hachi. She remembers talking mostly about herself and not listening enough to Nana. [V02:E004] The future voice therefore does not merely mourn. It revises.

### 2.3 Volume 3: romance and home diverge

Volume 3 is the first time the Nana/Hachi relationship receives explicit, qualified romantic language from future Hachi: `かなり恋に近い`, `初恋みたい`, and the counterfactual `もしもナナが男だったら 一世一代の恋`. Phase 2 verified these formulations against the Japanese pages rather than leaving them as inherited paraphrase.

The point is not to resolve the dyad as a conventional romance. The point is that the work itself refuses the safety of an ordinary friendship label almost immediately.

At the same time, Shoji and Sachiko demonstrate another principle: no formal category guarantees ethical behavior. Care, attraction, weakness, secrecy, attempted boundaries, and betrayal coexist. The Hachi/Shoji relationship can be socially legible and still fail as home. Nana/Hachi can be socially illegible and already function domestically.

Volume 3 therefore leaves Movement I with a crucial reorganization:

> **the person Hachi is romantically attached to and the person who increasingly feels like home are no longer the same person.**

That divergence drives the next movement.

# 3. Movement II — relational formation and music-community consolidation
## Volumes 4–7: breakup redistributes attachment while BLAST becomes a social world

This movement is not merely “the BLAST formation arc.” BLAST's musical consolidation and the Nana/Hachi domestic consolidation occur simultaneously. The band gives Nana a public vocation and a peer network; Hachi becomes increasingly embedded in that network even though she is not a musician. The result is a chosen social ecology in which music, home, friendship, romance, and aspiration stop being separable.

### 3.1 Volume 4: breakup does not leave an emotional vacuum

Shoji's betrayal and the breakup are the first major relationship collapse after the shared narrative begins. Structurally, the key question is where Hachi's attachment goes afterward.

The manga answers directly. Hachi experiences jealousy over Misato's closeness to Nana and explicitly says that Nana is more important than a boyfriend. [V04:E016, E022] Future Hachi remembers wanting to keep holding Nana's hand indefinitely. [V04:E033]

This does not prove a conventional romance. It proves that the emotional energy lost from the boyfriend category is not simply returned to individual independence. The Nana/Hachi bond becomes more central precisely because another socially recognized relationship has failed.

Nana's own longing for a lively family atmosphere also becomes explicit. [V04:E027] The person who performs independence most visibly is therefore also the person for whom the improvised household carries unusually high emotional stakes.

### 3.2 Volume 5: home and light become different needs

Volume 5 refuses the idea that 707's success solves Hachi's insecurity. She possesses a real home and still wants proximity to the “light” around Nana: BLAST, Trapnest, artistic talent, celebrity, Ren, Yasu, Reira, Nobu, and Takumi.

Future Hachi later corrects the idea that “anyone would have done.” She wanted to be in the same light. [V05:E019]

This matters because Takumi does not enter the story only as a man Hachi finds attractive. He enters as a portal into a world Hachi has already idealized through Nana. Nana arranging access to Takumi is interpreted by Hachi as love. [V05:E006] The gift is emotionally generous and structurally dangerous: Nana brings the glamorous world Hachi admires directly into 707.

The series therefore begins separating three things that Hachi often wants to merge:

- having a home;
- being loved;
- and feeling extraordinary.

No one relationship can reliably satisfy all three.

### 3.3 Volume 6: love becomes an argument about possession

By Volume 6, the manga stops leaving its relational philosophy entirely implicit. Shin describes the human tendency to want the whole of another person. Nobu proposes an ideal of non-extractive love and immediately admits jealousy makes the ideal difficult. Takumi tells Hachi he does not want to give her to anyone and wants her to remain his. Ren has similar possessive impulses toward Nana.

What had looked in early volumes like personality-specific behavior becomes a general problem:

> **Can intimacy become important enough to structure a life without turning another person into property, proof, or emotional infrastructure?**

The movement also intensifies the role of wish fulfillment. Tanabata fills the volume with desires, and several wishes begin to come true. The result is not uncomplicated happiness. *NANA* increasingly becomes a story about what happens **after** the desired thing is obtained.

### 3.4 Volume 7: possession becomes necessity

Volume 7 refines the problem. Future Hachi distinguishes wanting Nana all to herself from wanting to be needed by Nana. That difference is one of the central lexical and psychological pivots of the series.

Hachi also appears to solve one romantic ambiguity by ending the Takumi relationship as she understands it and choosing Nobu. This is genuine progress. She makes a decision, states boundaries, and chooses someone she likes reciprocally.

Yet two structural warnings remain.

First, the old self cannot simply be repainted out of existence. Hachi's removal of the Takumi poster and repainting of the wall become a visual model for the persistence of history under apparent change.

Second, BLAST's dream is beginning to come true at the same moment Hachi feels herself disappearing in the crowd. Professionalization gives Nana what she wants and threatens the social scale on which Hachi's intimacy with Nana was built.

Movement II therefore ends with both women apparently gaining what they wanted:

- Hachi has chosen a loving romantic partner;
- Nana's band is becoming professionally viable.

The next movement demonstrates why fulfilled desire is not the same as stable life.

# 4. Movement III — romantic divergence and domestic fracture
## Volumes 8–10: pregnancy forces private desire into material and public form

Volume 8 is the largest non-death structural hinge in the series. Before pregnancy, the central relationships can still be imagined primarily through feeling, choice, loyalty, jealousy, and desire. After pregnancy, every major abstraction acquires material constraints.

### 4.1 Volume 8: autonomy acquires a body and a budget

Hachi's pregnancy transforms self-authorship into a practical question of:

- bodily condition;
- medical time;
- uncertain paternity;
- work;
- income;
- housing;
- childcare;
- family legitimacy;
- career;
- and who is actually willing and able to support which outcome.

This is not a repudiation of the earlier autonomy principle. It is its maturation.

A person may have the right to choose, but choices are made inside structures. The manga therefore shifts from asking only **“What does Hachi want?”** to asking **“What makes a desired future livable?”**

The opening future image of Hachi imagining a large house with a permanent place for Nana makes the contrast especially sharp. Hachi's desired adulthood is not simply husband + child. It contains an imagined return-place for Nana as well. The pregnancy crisis therefore does not replace the Nana/Hachi axis with the Takumi/Nobu question. It forces these relational worlds into collision.

### 4.2 Volume 9: private crisis becomes administration

Takumi's defining late-series competence becomes visible here: he can convert chaos into logistics rapidly. Marriage becomes a timetable, family notification, housing, security, money, and media strategy. These are not fake solutions. They solve real problems.

But the same disposition repeatedly treats unilateral authorship as efficiency. The volume's sexual-assault sequence is therefore structurally important, not an isolated moral exception. Takumi's ability to act decisively for a relationship and his willingness to override another person's autonomy emerge from the same confidence that he can define the necessary reality. [V09:E014, E016–E017]

The 707 fracture becomes concrete. Nana tests whether distance will make Hachi need her again; the empty room becomes evidence that Hachi's life is moving into another structure. Strawberry glasses demonstrate the difference between replacement and continuity: a materially identical object cannot carry the history of the lost one.

Meanwhile paparazzi convert the intimate network into public information. The private story has become legible to strangers.

### 4.3 Volume 10: everyone is living inside competing stories

Volume 10 makes narrative authorship explicit through `物語`, hero, and heroine language.

Present Nana imagines herself as the hero of Hachi's story. Future Nana later relinquishes that role while saying Hachi remains the heroine of her own story. [V10:E086, E089–E090]

This is an architectural turning point because the work begins actively separating:

- the story a person tells about another;
- the story another person tells about herself;
- the story the public tells;
- and the reality the reader can reconstruct from distributed viewpoints.

Nana imagines Hachi trapped inside Takumi's domestic system. Hachi, however, tells others that she wants the child, loves Takumi, and wants to attempt the household. Nobu imagines himself removed from Hachi's story while Hachi still remembers their happiness and asks about him. The media converts fragments into simplified public narratives.

By the end of Movement III, “romantic choice” is no longer an adequate category. Hachi's adulthood is being produced by the interaction of love, pregnancy, security, legal structure, harm, desire, and practical possibility.

# 5. Movement IV — pregnancy, marriage, and professionalization
## Volumes 11–13: adulthood becomes maintenance rather than an ending

Although pregnancy begins in Volume 8, Volumes 11–13 reveal what its consequences mean over time. At the same moment, BLAST and Trapnest increasingly inhabit institutional rather than purely artistic worlds. Protected housing, management decisions, contracts, schedules, press, and public roles become ordinary conditions of life.

### 5.1 Volume 11: repair is not restoration

Volume 11 asks what it means to rewrite a damaged story.

Hachi's encounter with Shoji is crucial because it rejects a common romantic narrative mechanism: honest confrontation does not necessarily restore the relationship. Hachi realizes that what continued to hurt was not only betrayal but the inability to know where the truth of their shared past ended and the betrayal began. Memory itself had been contaminated.

The resulting principle is mature:

> **repair may mean recovering truthful memory without recovering the relationship.**

That logic becomes increasingly important to *NANA* as a whole. Later, the future will ask whether Nana/Hachi can reconnect without recreating 707, whether grief can be lived without restoring a pre-death world, and whether love can remain real after its original form has become impossible.

The volume also makes professional protection resemble confinement. Shin calls the protected residence a cage; celebrity security provides safety while reducing self-authored movement. [V11:E029]

### 5.2 Volume 12: home becomes ritual architecture

Volume 12 is the point at which the future social world becomes materially legible.

707 is no longer simply where the Nanas live. It has become where people return to wait. Fireworks recur. A child is present. Nana's absence is structurally real even though its cause remains unknown.

This converts the apartment through several states:

> **rental property → shared home → lost ordinary life → public/scandal coordinate → reunion site → ritual of waiting.**

The shift matters because it demonstrates that home in *NANA* is not reducible to continuous cohabitation. A place can become home through accumulated relationship, and later become the material practice by which relationship survives absence.

The same volume destabilizes Hachi's Cinderella vocabulary. She can still imagine climbing toward an ideal adult form, but future Hachi questions the fairy-tale mechanism and calls some of her own attempts a futile one-woman play. Marriage, money, and family may be valuable; they are not self-executing guarantees of happiness.

### 5.3 Volume 13: happiness loses its finish line

Volume 13 makes the argument explicit: `幸せのゴールなんてあるのかな`—is there a finish line for happiness? [V13:E017]

The question emerges after the manga has repeatedly delivered what earlier Hachi would have called endings: Tokyo, boyfriend, celebrity access, Nobu's love, marriage prospects, pregnancy, wealth, family, music success. None produces a permanent state beyond further choice.

Future Hachi's distinction between `結ぶ` and `繋ぐ` gives the movement its mature relational grammar. Human bonds are not things to tie into a permanent knot; they must be kept connected, without becoming `がんじがらめ`, completely bound. [V13:E146–E148]

This language retroactively clarifies much of the series architecture:

- 707 worked because it connected without total fusion;
- romantic possession fails because it treats connection as ownership;
- marriage can stabilize material life but cannot guarantee reciprocal interiority;
- band membership can create shared fate but can also become institutional entrapment;
- future waiting can preserve a route without forcing a return.

Movement IV therefore changes the meaning of adulthood. Adulthood is not reaching the correct final form. It is maintaining relationships, obligations, bodies, work, and selfhood without assuming that any arrangement will maintain itself.

# 6. Movement V — celebrity, media, and competing forms of adulthood
## Volumes 14–17: private truth becomes difficult to know as every relationship acquires observers

This movement takes place in a world where the cast is no longer merely becoming famous. Fame has reorganized the conditions under which they can know one another.

### 6.1 Volume 14: appearance, fact, and durable reality separate

Volume 14 repeatedly insists that appearances, facts, and durable relational truths are not interchangeable. Characters have asymmetric access to information. Public images distort. Private partners conceal. Industry roles become contractual. Fandom can support and invade. Ren's substance use and Reira's dependency become harder to manage precisely because professional competence cannot substitute for person-level knowledge.

The volume's insistence on accepting facts “as they are” is not simple realism. The problem is that characters frequently do not possess the same facts.

This becomes central to the late series: much relational suffering comes from acting decisively on partial models of another person.

### 6.2 Volume 15: people can remain inside one another while misunderstanding one another

Volume 15 moves the epistemic problem inward. Future Hachi can still hear Nana's voice inside herself but asks whether Nana can hear hers. [V15:E004]

The series therefore distinguishes memory from reciprocity. A person can remain psychologically present without any guarantee that the relationship remains mutually available.

Future Hachi also acknowledges that she idealized Nana. [V15:E039] This is one of the most important retrospective corrections in the entire corpus because it prevents the mature synthesis from simply adopting Hachi's loving image of Nana as objective truth.

Ren, meanwhile, explicitly recognizes his history of overriding Nana's will. [V15:E121] Again, later self-knowledge does not erase earlier behavior; it changes what the cumulative model can say about the character's awareness.

### 6.3 Volume 16: the future split becomes dramatic irony

Volume 16 directly shows future Nana alive. This changes the epistemic structure of the series.

Before this point, Nana's absence could support multiple ontological possibilities. Afterward, the reader knows something important that some characters do not: Nana survives into the depicted future and sings. The rumor that she is dead becomes demonstrably false from the reader's perspective. [V16:E011]

This does **not** answer:

- why she left;
- where exactly she is;
- how long she has been there;
- whether she intends permanent separation;
- whether she knows she has been found;
- or whether reunion will occur.

The future is clarified and made more mysterious simultaneously.

The volume also gives Nana an important relational revision in the present: she can imagine connection without sameness—`もう同じじゃなくてもいい`, followed by the desire to connect more properly. [V16:E048–E049] This is a major movement away from earlier attachment logic in which closeness often depended on matching, exclusivity, or irreplaceable need.

### 6.4 Volume 17: evidence creates a right to hope, not a right to recover

The future photograph becomes a bridge between Nana's survival and the waiting network. Hachi and Shin possess evidence; the 707 key remains missing; the annual and seasonal rituals continue.

Yet the volume's care language moves away from solitary rescue. Hachi's protective model becomes `一人じゃない`: not being alone. Yasu explicitly de-emphasizes relationship labels and emphasizes trust. [V17:E137, E147, E149]

This is an architectural correction to the early series. Initially, extraordinary bonds often appear to derive force from singularity: the one boyfriend, the one great love, the one person who understands, the one rescuer, the one home. By the late series, the healthiest emerging model is increasingly **distributed care**.

That shift prepares the ethical terrain of the final movement.

# 7. Movement VI — mature relational instability and the expanding future frame
## Volumes 18–21 + Chapters 81–84: from how to die together to how to remain alive separately and connected

The final movement is where the series' long-standing relational problems become existential.

### 7.1 Volume 18: the death fantasy is converted into a life question

Future Nana's suicidality is made explicit. She thinks of the sea as preserving the possibility of death and paradoxically finds that the availability of death allows her to try living another day. [V18:E009, E026]

She also says that she cannot simply discard the life Hachi once saved. [V18:E041]

The structural importance is not that Hachi becomes Nana's sole reason to live. The evidence does not support that reduction. The importance is that a relationship can become an obligation **to continue existing** without becoming ownership of the future.

Volume 18 explicitly moves from questions of how to die toward how to live. This reverses one of the earliest Nana/Ren romantic fantasies and foreshadows the moral asymmetry of Volume 21.

### 7.2 Volume 19: reaching becomes ethical only when refusal remains possible

Future Hachi fears that Nana may reject her outstretched hand. This is a major maturation of Hachi's attachment model.

Earlier Hachi repeatedly wanted signs that she mattered, was chosen, was needed, or occupied a secure relational role. Future Hachi now understands that searching for Nana does not automatically entitle her to Nana's return.

The volume's present relationships mirror the same problem. Hachi rejects total sacrifice as proof of Ren's love. Takumi can describe dependency as being trapped inside another person's world while still building controlling architectures himself. Shin refuses to promise Reira a future he cannot yet truthfully guarantee. Yasu frames connection as reciprocal responsibility rather than total dependence.

The late series is therefore not becoming anti-attachment. It is trying to imagine attachment whose reality does not depend on eliminating another person's answer.

### 7.3 Volume 20: self-recognition without release

Volume 20 brings Nana's contradiction into explicit speech. She knows Ren has priorities beyond her. She knows Hachi has people beyond her. She knows she herself has a dream she will not surrender. She still cannot tolerate the emotional implication of that plurality. [V20:E019]

Her desire for Ren to sacrifice everything is therefore not simply ignorance. It is a demand she can recognize as impossible and still feel. [V20:E024]

This is one of the clearest examples of *NANA*'s psychological method: self-knowledge does not automatically generate self-mastery.

Reira's refusal to sing also matters structurally. Music, which began as a route toward selfhood, has become professional leverage. Her only available form of resistance is to stop producing the thing the institution needs from her. [V20:E015]

The old death pact and the fear of being left behind close the volume as prospective signals rather than established outcome. [V20:E027]

### 7.4 Volume 21: Ren dies; the shared future does not

Ren's death on March 4, 2002 literalizes “being left behind.” Nana's time stops. The group's future blueprint becomes blank. [V21:E014, E016, E034]

Yet the volume rejects the idea that self-destruction requires a conscious wish to die. Hachi sees the link between habitual self-harm, drug use, and potentially fatal behavior without suicidal intent. The emerging counter-principle is survival for those who remain. [V21:E023, E025]

This is not a clean moral lesson attached to Ren's death. The manga does not reduce him to a warning. It preserves love, addiction, professional pressure, responsibility, vulnerability, and accident as overlapping causal structures.

The numbered-volume endpoint then states that Nana's future absence remains the condition around which the others' future cannot fully restart: `ナナがいないと / 始まらないの`. [V21:E036]

Crucially, the line establishes absence, not death and not cause.

### 7.5 Chapters 81–84: after catastrophe, ordinary life becomes emergency technology

The continuation refuses a heroic recovery arc.

People eat because food exists. Hachi cooks because cooking is something she can do. Nobu plays guitar because sound can enter a room where speech fails. Bodies hold one another because words cannot stop trembling. Nana becomes animated enough to complain about beef and sing into a bottle, while Yasu warns that she may simply have stopped caring.

The narrative therefore distinguishes **behavioral animation** from **renewed investment in a future**.

Hachi's development also becomes increasingly important. She begins by treating herself as a necessary refuge for Takumi. She later recognizes that she may not be his refuge, refuses to use Nobu as an escape, and acknowledges that her marriage requires a future conversation she does not want to have.

The final question about whether “Takumi's wife” is still needed beside Nana returns the entire extant architecture to one of its oldest concerns:

> **If connection is real, must it be justified by indispensability?**

The hiatus leaves the question open.

# 8. Future chronology as presently recoverable

The future material should be divided into **facts established**, **strongly supported relationships among facts**, and **gaps that remain open**. The manga reveals the future nonlinearly, so a perfectly dated future timeline cannot currently be produced without inventing transitions.

## 8.1 What is established

### A. Hachi becomes a mother and remains connected to the 707 network

Future Hachi is shown at Room 707 during the Tama River fireworks. A child calls her mother. The old social network returns to the apartment. A yukata is prepared for Nana. The room functions as an annual waiting place. [V12:E004 and surrounding future sequence]

Later future material also shows Hachi managing children, work, and family obligations; Volume 20 depicts a boy named Ren in the Hachi/Takumi household addressing Takumi as Papa. [V20:E003]

These facts establish continuing family structure. They do not by themselves settle every paternity or marital-state question raised by the present narrative.

### B. Nana is absent from the 707 network

Nana's absence is repeatedly established through direct address, waiting rituals, an unused room, the missing key, prepared clothing, photographs, and the group's desire for her return.

The cause is not established.

Absence therefore remains a **fact**, while the explanation remains an **open proposition**.

### C. Nana survives into the depicted future

Volume 16 directly shows long-haired Nana alive and singing at an English-speaking venue. This overrides the in-story rumor of her death for the reader. [V16:E011 plus the direct visual evidence]

Later photographs corroborate that survival for Hachi and Shin. [V17:E018]

The manga does not establish that every character knows the same information at the same time.

### D. Future Nana experiences severe suicidality but continues living

The snowy-sea material establishes that Nana went far away while thinking about death and that the availability of death could paradoxically make another day survivable. She explicitly links her continued life to the life Hachi once saved. [V18:E009, E026, E041]

This is evidence about future Nana's psychological state. It is not proof of the exact event that caused her departure.

### E. Hachi eventually understands approach as a request rather than a claim

By Volume 19, Hachi fears Nana may reject her hand. This indicates a future consciousness substantially different from the younger Hachi who frequently equates being needed with relational security.

The future search therefore becomes ethically conditional:

> **finding Nana would not itself settle whether Nana wishes to return.**

## 8.2 What can be strongly inferred but should remain qualified

The future fragments strongly support the idea that 707 has become a ritualized preservation structure for the old network rather than an ordinary household. They also strongly support the idea that Nana is physically separated from the group for a substantial period, long enough for children to grow and for repeated annual rituals to form.

The reader can also reasonably infer that the social network knows or comes to believe Nana is alive after the photographs circulate. What remains less clear is who knows when, what they do with the knowledge, and whether Nana knows the extent to which she has been found.

The future material further suggests that the meaning of “home” has split:

- Hachi has a family household and continuing practical obligations;
- 707 remains a memorial/relational home;
- Nana lives elsewhere and sings elsewhere;
- the possibility of return remains symbolically concentrated in a key and a door.

## 8.3 What is not established

The extant text does **not** establish:

- the precise cause of Nana's eventual departure from Japan or the 707 network;
- the exact timing of that departure;
- the exact country or city of every future Nana scene with sufficient confidence to convert contextual cues into a hard locator;
- whether Nana and Hachi actually reunite after the future photographs;
- whether Nana accepts or refuses Hachi's approach;
- the final state of Hachi and Takumi's marriage;
- whether Hachi and Nobu ever resume a romantic relationship;
- the final long-term state of Reira and Takumi;
- the complete future of BLAST and Trapnest;
- or the ultimate destination Yazawa intended for the story.

The hiatus therefore creates a specific epistemic condition:

> **trajectory is available; destination is not.**

Document 09 will analyze the future temporal system in greater formal detail. For Document 01, the essential point is that the future timeline does not merely foreshadow plot. It changes the genre-temperature of the present. The reader experiences ordinary life under the pressure of future loss.

## Included bonus and special material

The collected volumes also contain bonus pages, `淳子の部屋`, and special chapters such as the NAOKI, NOBU, and TAKUMI material. These materials can add character prehistory, tonal counterpoint, and metatextual framing, but they should not be flattened into the primary chronological line above.

In architectural terms:

- **main-story chapters** determine the primary progression;
- **character special chapters** can reconstruct earlier history or secondary perspective;
- **淳子の部屋 / omake material** can reveal framing and self-parody but does not automatically possess the same evidentiary status as dramatic main-story events.

This distinction becomes especially important once Document 13 introduces *NANA 7.8*: supplementary framing may illuminate the manga, but it must not retroactively dictate what the sequential fiction “really meant.”

# 10. Major retrospective corrections

A mature structural synthesis should not merely summarize what later volumes establish. It must also preserve the historical interpretations that were reasonable before later evidence arrived. The following are the most important cumulative corrections.

## 10.1 “Independent Nana” was never “Nana does not need people”

Volume 1 establishes Nana's autonomy directly: Ren leaves the decision to her; Yasu rejects the idea that she is Ren's pet. But the same volume establishes that life with Ren was precious and that singing becomes a route out of post-separation pain.

Later volumes therefore **complicate**, rather than overturn, the early independence reading.

The mature formulation is:

> **Nana's independence is genuine self-authorship and, increasingly under stress, a defensive technology against abandonment.**

Document 02 will develop this further.

## 10.2 Hachi's migration is more self-authored than “she follows a boyfriend to Tokyo”

Volume 2 explicitly establishes that Hachi stayed behind and saved money for Tokyo rather than impulsively following Shoji. [V02:E002]

Later dependency does not retroactively erase this agency.

The structural correction matters because Hachi's adulthood should not be narrated as a linear path from pure dependency to autonomy. Agency and dependency coexist from the beginning, just as they do in Nana.

## 10.3 707 is not merely symbolic “found family”; it is a material design for bounded intimacy

Early readings can romanticize 707 as destiny. Phase 2 strengthens a more precise account: 707 works because it combines affordability, private rooms, separate selves, and mutual aid.

Its later transformation into ritual home is powerful precisely because the original arrangement was so practical.

## 10.4 Nana/Hachi romantic ambiguity is textual, not merely fandom projection

Volume 3's Japanese backfill confirms that future Hachi herself uses explicitly qualified romantic comparison: “close to love,” “like a first love,” and the counterfactual about Nana being a man.

That evidence does not prove a conventional romance. It does invalidate a reading in which romantic/queer ambiguity is entirely external to the text.

The mature dyad analysis must preserve both propositions:

1. the work structurally and linguistically exceeds ordinary friendship categories;
2. the extant manga does not finally assign a single conventional romantic category.

## 10.5 Hachi's Shoji breakup becomes more than a betrayal plot

Volume 11 retrospectively reveals that the unresolved aftermath damaged Hachi's access to her own happy memories. The correction is not that Shoji's betrayal was less serious. It is that **truthful confrontation matters even when restoration is neither possible nor desirable**.

This becomes a model for late-series relational repair.

## 10.6 Hachi's attraction to Takumi is not adequately explained by indiscriminate need

Volume 5's future correction—she wanted to be in the same light—makes the attraction partly aspirational. Hachi's access to Takumi is inseparable from her entrance into Nana's extraordinary music/celebrity world.

This does not excuse later harm or eliminate desire. It changes the architecture of the desire.

## 10.7 Nobu is a genuine choice but not a magical counterfactual solution

Volume 7 establishes Hachi's real choice of Nobu. Later volumes preserve jealousy, memory, compatibility, and tenderness.

But pregnancy, timing, idealization, material insecurity, and Nobu's own limitations prevent the mature series from treating him as the simple healthy answer that fate denied Hachi.

By Chapter 83, Hachi and Nobu can physically console one another without treating grief as authorization to resume romance. That is a more mature form of their connection than either “true love lost” or “relationship fully over.”

## 10.8 Pregnancy corrects abstract accounts of freedom

Volumes 1–7 often allow autonomy to appear as a problem of will: follow or remain, call or do not call, choose Takumi or Nobu, stay or leave.

Volume 8 does not disprove those choices. It reveals the missing variables:

> **body + time + money + work + housing + childcare + legal recognition + available care.**

From this point on, the manga's adulthood cannot be analyzed through emotional preference alone.

## 10.9 Takumi's competence and Takumi's coercion are both real

The series repeatedly refuses a simplifying moral economy in which a harmful person must be incompetent at care or a competent person must therefore be safe.

Takumi can solve material problems rapidly. He can also use the same unilateral certainty to dominate information, decisions, sexual access, housing, and relationship architecture. Volume 9's sexual assault must remain classified as such; later marriage or affection cannot retroactively create consent.

The mature architecture therefore requires a two-level claim:

> **competence can create real security; security can also become the infrastructure through which control becomes difficult to resist.**

## 10.10 Nana's “hero of Hachi's story” self-concept is explicitly corrected by future Nana

Present Nana imagines herself as Hachi's hero. Future Nana says she can no longer be that hero while Hachi remains the heroine of Nana's story. [V10:E086, E089–E090]

The correction is vital because it demonstrates that Nana's rescue fantasy is not simply validated by narrative centrality.

The late series increasingly asks people to relinquish roles that make another person's life intelligible by placing oneself at its center.

## 10.11 Marriage is neither false love nor narrative closure

Hachi's marriage to Takumi contains material security, affection, chosen commitment, coercion, inequality, family aspiration, and later unresolved distance.

Volumes 12–13 explicitly dismantle Cinderella closure and the idea of happiness as a finish line. The mature series therefore treats marriage as an evolving institution and relationship, not as proof that earlier romantic questions were answered.

Chapter 84's future-conversation line keeps that status open.

## 10.12 Future Nana's absence does not mean death

Early future narration makes absence feel grave enough that death is an imaginable hypothesis. Volume 16 directly overturns that ontological uncertainty for the reader by showing Nana alive.

The correct cumulative statement is:

> **Nana is absent from the future 707 network and alive in at least the future scenes shown; the cause, duration, and final outcome of the separation remain open.**

This is one of the clearest examples of why the project must distinguish prospective signal from established fact.

## 10.13 Hachi's memory of Nana is loving but not omniscient

Future Hachi is frequently perceptive and is structurally privileged as narrator. Volume 15 nevertheless has her identify her own idealization of Nana.

Future narration must therefore be treated as **retrospective character testimony**, not authorial omniscience.

This correction protects the series from being reduced to whichever interpretation future Hachi happens to articulate most beautifully.

## 10.14 The death-together fantasy is not fulfilled by Ren's death

Volume 20 revives the old death pact and Nana's fear of being left behind. Volume 21 makes Ren's death real.

A superficial reading could call that fulfillment of tragic romantic destiny.

The actual architecture is the opposite: the pact fails through asymmetry. Ren dies; Nana lives. The narrative then develops a survivor ethic oriented toward those who remain.

> **The tragedy is not that they finally die together. It is that one person must learn—or fail to learn—how to live after the fantasy of shared annihilation becomes impossible.**

## 10.15 Apparent recovery after Ren's death remains disputed

Chapter 84 gives Nana appetite, irritation, humor, and singing. Those behaviors are significant. Yasu's explicit counterreading prevents them from being converted into proof of psychological recovery.

The extant story therefore ends with a distinction that applies to the entire series:

> **observable function is not identical to inner resolution.**

A person can work, sing, cook, marry, joke, care for others, or appear socially competent while remaining structurally unwell.

# 11. What kind of story 『NANA』 has become by Chapter 84

The architectural evolution can now be stated more precisely.

At first, *NANA* can be mistaken for two coming-of-age romance stories that collide in Tokyo. That description is not wrong. It is simply incomplete.

Apartment 707 then makes it a domestic relationship story. BLAST and Trapnest make it a music and ambition story. Shoji, Ren, Nobu, Takumi, Reira, Shin, Yasu, Miu, and the larger ensemble make it a comparative study of relational forms. Pregnancy makes it a material-adulthood story. Marriage and housing make it an institutional story. Professionalization makes music a labor story. Paparazzi and management make private identity a media story. Future narration makes ordinary life an archive. Ren's death makes the archive catastrophic. The continuation makes care itself subject to ethical scrutiny.

Yet these are not successive replacements. The early questions survive inside the later genres.

Hachi's first desire to be chosen survives inside marriage, motherhood, and future searching.

Nana's first refusal to follow Ren survives inside celebrity, possessiveness, grief, and eventual separation.

707's practical privacy survives inside the later dream of a home to which Nana can return.

The early train's language of fate survives, but future Hachi eventually learns that connection cannot be secured by tying another person down.

Music begins as voice and self-authorship, becomes community, then career, then celebrity commodity, then leverage, then room-scale consolation after death.

Romance begins as imagined destiny, becomes jealousy and betrayal, becomes material choice, becomes legal structure, becomes memory, and finally becomes one relational form among several ways people can remain inside one another's lives.

The series' architecture therefore moves from **formation** to **maintenance**.

Its early characters ask:

> Who will I become? Who will choose me? Where will I live? Whom do I love?

Its later characters increasingly confront:

> How do I keep living inside choices whose consequences I cannot undo? How do I care without owning? How do I remain connected when the old form of the relationship is gone? How do I allow another person to have a life whose center is not me? What does home mean when nobody can simply return to the version of it that memory preserves?

That is why the future frame is not merely a mystery about where Nana went. The disappearance matters because the entire manga has trained the reader to understand connection as something maintained through ordinary acts. Future absence removes those acts and leaves objects, rituals, memory, photographs, songs, and waiting to carry the relationship instead.

The extant endpoint makes the architecture almost painfully symmetrical.

Volume 2 created a home in which two women could be close without one owning the other.

Volume 7 asked whether Hachi wanted to possess Nana or merely be needed by her.

Volume 13 said bonds should be kept connected without becoming binding.

Volume 19 allowed Nana the possibility of refusing Hachi's future hand.

Volume 21 destroyed the group's shared future.

Chapter 84 asks whether Hachi can still belong beside Nana if Nana no longer appears to need her.

The unfinished status prevents a final answer. It does not prevent a mature statement of trajectory:

> **Through Chapter 84, 『NANA』 increasingly treats love not as the discovery of the person who can guarantee permanence, but as the unstable practice of remaining connected to separate persons whose autonomy, wounds, ambitions, obligations, and mortality make permanence impossible to guarantee.**

The tragedy of the series is that nearly every major character wants some form of permanence.

Its maturity lies in progressively dismantling the mechanisms by which they try to force permanence into existence:

- possession;
- indispensability;
- marriage as ending;
- wealth as safety;
- artistic success as wholeness;
- shared death as proof;
- rescue as ownership;
- memory as perfect knowledge;
- and return as something another person can be required to perform.

What remains is less comforting and more durable:

> connection must be repeatedly made without any guarantee that it will stay in the form one wants.

That is the kind of story *NANA* has become at the point where publication stops.

This document establishes the coordinate system for the specialist studies that follow. It intentionally stops before resolving questions assigned elsewhere.

`02_OSAKI_NANA_CHARACTER_SELFHOOD_LOVE_ART_AND_DEPENDENCY.md` should now ask how Nana's autonomy, abandonment, artistic vocation, possession, and survival develop inside the architecture mapped here.

The present document therefore freezes the following structural propositions for Phase 3:

1. **March 5, 2001 is the key convergence point** at which separate biographies become a shared narrative clock.
2. **Apartment 707 is the central early architecture of bounded intimacy**, later transformed into ritual home and archive.
3. **Pregnancy is the largest material-adulthood hinge** because it forces choice into body, money, time, law, work, and available care.
4. **Professionalization and celebrity do not replace private relationships; they institutionalize and expose them.**
5. **Future narration creates a second temporal plane** in which present life is experienced as memory before its loss is understood.
6. **Future Nana's survival is established; the cause and ultimate meaning of her absence are not.**
7. **Ren's death on March 4, 2002 destroys the old future rather than completing a death-together romance.**
8. **The continuation replaces heroic recovery with maintenance, distributed care, and unresolved grief.**
9. **The central longitudinal movement is from connection-as-fate or possession toward connection-as-maintenance among separate persons.**
10. **The hiatus leaves trajectory without destination.** Any later synthesis that converts these trajectories into final outcomes exceeds the extant evidence.

These propositions are now the structural baseline for the remainder of Phase 3.
