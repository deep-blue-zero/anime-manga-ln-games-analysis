# 『進撃の巨人』Volume 26 Deep Reading
## Victory becomes captivity: Liberio’s extraction, Sasha’s death, the Volunteers’ hidden prehistory, and Eren’s conversion of survival into self-command

## 1. Central thesis and volume role

Volume 25 ended at the moment when the categories inherited from the early manga had become unusable. Humans were now attacking Titans as terrifyingly efficient predators; civilians existed inside the same battle geometry as soldiers; Eren understood Reiner more accurately than ever and nevertheless transformed beneath him; and the opposing imperatives `殲滅しろ` and `生き延びろ` had ceased to map cleanly onto monster and victim.

Volume 26 asks what follows when that moral collapse produces a tactical success.

Its answer is not triumph.

The Paradis force wins the immediate Liberio operation. The War Hammer Titan is captured through Eren’s adaptation; the Marleyan harbor and fleet are devastated by Armin’s Colossal transformation; Zeke is extracted; surviving Scouts board the airship and escape. Measured narrowly, the operation succeeds.

But the chapter titled 「勝者」 refuses to let tactical success define victory. The winners carry dead comrades into the airship. Sasha is shot after the main battle has apparently ended. Gabi and Falco are captured and brought into the same enclosed space as the people they have learned to call devils. Zeke, whom the battle presented as an enemy commander, is revealed to be part of the extraction plan. Eren, who made the operation possible, is not welcomed home as an uncomplicated victorious commander; he is physically restrained, rebuked, and eventually imprisoned.

The volume therefore establishes a governing distinction:

> **A successful operation can simultaneously be a strategic gain, a political loss of control, a moral catastrophe, and an internal command crisis.**

That distinction matters especially for Eren. V25 showed that recognition does not automatically inhibit violence. V26 adds a more institutional corollary: conviction and strategic foresight do not automatically confer command legitimacy. Eren’s unilateral action gives Paradis leverage because the Scouts cannot simply abandon him once he has entered enemy territory and forced the crisis. Yet this same leverage is coercive toward his own side. Hange and Levi’s anger is not reducible to squeamishness about violence. It concerns who gets to decide that everyone else must enter the battle.

The volume’s second major movement is retrospective. Chapter 106 「義勇兵」 turns backward three years and reveals that Paradis’s apparent sudden technological and strategic maturation has a hidden history. Marleyan ships were captured. Anti-Marleyan Volunteers arrived through Zeke’s plan. Yelena, Onyankopon, Nicolo and others supplied technical knowledge, weapons, information, food practices, and strategic proposals. The island’s contemporary capacity is therefore not self-generated isolationist development. It is the result of contact with people produced by the same imperial world Paradis had previously imagined only as an external enemy.

This yields the volume’s second thesis:

> **The enemy world is also the source of the knowledge required to resist the enemy world.**

That is not merely technological. Nicolo’s food changes social relations; Onyankopon articulates a plural world populated by peoples and technologies the island has never known; Yelena’s group turns captured enemy infrastructure into a channel of exchange. The line between infiltration and cooperation becomes unstable because every transfer of useful knowledge also creates dependency on people whose motives cannot be fully verified.

Chapter 106 then recontextualizes the entire Liberio operation. Zeke’s cooperation is not a surprise created on the airship. It belongs to a multi-year clandestine structure whose core proposition is that the Founding Titan, when brought into contact with royal blood, may permit use of the Wall Titans’ `地鳴らし` as strategic deterrence. At the V26 boundary, this proposal remains a plan mediated through Zeke and the Volunteers, not a transparent objective fact about every future action.

The final image condenses the volume’s deepest transformation. Eren stands before a prison mirror and tells himself:

> `戦わなければ 勝てない`
>
> `戦え`
>
> `戦え`

The grammar is crucial. Earlier Eren often directed imperatives outward—toward enemies, comrades, or the world. Here he becomes both commander and commanded. Survival logic has been internalized as recursive self-discipline.

That produces the volume’s third thesis:

> **Eren’s freedom discourse is increasingly inseparable from self-coercion. He presents himself as the one who keeps moving, but Volume 26 ends by showing that continued movement must now be commanded from within.**

The volume therefore converts “victory” into three forms of captivity:

1. the Scouts are strategically bound to the consequences of Eren’s initiative;
2. Paradis is materially bound to external knowledge and to Zeke’s uncertain plan;
3. Eren is physically imprisoned while psychologically reproducing the command to fight.

# 3. Narrative architecture and causal close reading

## 3.1 Chapter 103 — 「強襲」: the attack becomes a system

Chapter 103 begins inside the battle V25 had already morally destabilized. Its title is not simply “attack” but 「強襲」: an assault whose defining feature is sudden concentration and operational violence. The chapter’s form matches the word. Individual duels are continually reclassified as parts of a coordinated system.

Eren’s fight with the War Hammer Titan is no longer the isolated spectacle of one Titan facing another. The Scouts provide anti-Titan intervention; Pieck’s Panzer unit supplies suppressive fire; Porco becomes a mobile counterattack; Zeke enters as a strategic Titan asset; Marleyan and Paradis infantry operate vertically through buildings and rooftops. The battlefield is an integrated weapons environment.

### Eren’s mechanistic adaptation

The strongest character-model evidence for Eren in the chapter is not aggression but iterative problem-solving. V25 established that he recognized the War Hammer bearer by noticing the anomalous cable connection between the Titan body and the operator. V26 extends this through repeated attempts to solve the crystal problem. The hardened shell cannot simply be bitten open by Eren’s Titan jaws. The enemy’s body becomes an engineering constraint.

Eren’s combat cognition is therefore increasingly model-based:

1. observe anomaly;
2. infer mechanism;
3. test response;
4. revise when the response fails;
5. appropriate another actor’s capability if necessary.

The key point is not that Eren is “smart” in the abstract. His reasoning is strongest under direct material pressure when the problem can be transformed into an actionable mechanism.

### Pieck and the Panzer unit: relationalized military infrastructure

Pieck’s bond with the Panzer unit is given emotional consequence when the Scouts dismantle the unit protecting her. V25 had already shown that she treats these soldiers as people with whom she has social familiarity, not interchangeable equipment. Chapter 103 turns that ordinary familiarity into combat grief.

This matters for the series’ broader anti-abstraction work. Military platforms that appear from a distance as “Cart Titan + guns” are, from Pieck’s position, a network of known human partners. Destroying capability also destroys relationships.

### Porco’s rage and the loss of Titan invulnerability

Porco’s V25 shock that humans intend to kill him “while remaining human” carries directly into V26. His aggression is intensified by the death and injury around him. The Scouts’ anti-Titan competence means inherited Titan status no longer guarantees battlefield dominance. Porco responds to this altered hierarchy emotionally as well as tactically.

### Zeke’s entrance is deliberately legible as hostility

At this boundary, the reader still sees Zeke participating in Marley’s defense until the operation later reveals another layer. This is important for prospective discipline. His visible battle conduct should not be retroactively rewritten as transparent cooperation from the chapter’s first page. The volume’s later reveal changes what the reader can infer about motive, but the scene’s initial epistemic structure remains part of the experience.

## 3.2 Chapter 104 — 「勝者」: winning is defined by extraction, not moral closure

The chapter title creates an interpretive trap. A reader arriving from a conventional battle manga might expect 「勝者」 to designate the side that defeats the enemy’s strongest fighters. Isayama instead makes victory ambiguous at every scale.

### Armin’s Colossal Titan and the harbor

Armin’s transformation near the Marleyan harbor destroys military infrastructure on a scale no ordinary soldier could produce. The image of the Colossal Titan rising over the devastated port deliberately recalls the series’ foundational visual grammar while reversing position. Paradis once experienced the Colossal Titan as the incomprehensible external force that opened its world to catastrophe. Armin now inhabits that silhouette from the attacking side.

This is not a simple claim that Armin “becomes the same as Bertolt.” The volume preserves distinctions of motive, target, context, and institutional position. But visual inheritance matters. Armin’s body has become a weapon whose activation necessarily operates at city-destroying scale.

The ethical consequence is not erased by strategic utility. Armin later acknowledges the possibility that communication might have existed while also recognizing that he has now personally participated in catastrophic destruction. His role cannot remain the pure dialogic alternative to Eren’s violence.

### Eren converts Porco into a tool

The War Hammer crystal presents an unsolved material problem. Eren discovers the solution by using the Jaw Titan’s own hardened jaws as a crushing mechanism. He physically positions Porco between himself and the crystal and forces the Jaw’s bite to break the shell.

Formally, the sequence is one of the volume’s most brutal acts of instrumentalization. Porco is not merely defeated; his body and inherited Titan capacity are repurposed as a tool for Eren’s consumption of another person.

The sequence extends V25’s theme of Titan bodies becoming military platforms. It also demonstrates a specific Eren rule:

> **When an obstacle cannot be overcome directly, Eren will reclassify an opponent’s capability as usable infrastructure.**

This is tactically adaptive and ethically chilling. It models the enemy first through affordance—what can this body do for the objective?

### Reiner returns because someone calls for him

Reiner’s partial transformation occurs after Gabi and Falco cry out for him to save Porco. This continues the relational tether established in V24, where responsibility toward the Warrior candidates interrupted his suicide attempt. The volume does not show generalized psychological recovery. It shows activation under a specific relational demand.

That distinction matters for reconstruction. Reiner may remain exhausted, dissociated, or death-seeking while still mobilizing when someone whose survival he feels responsible for calls him into action.

### Extraction creates the true operational endpoint

The Scouts’ airship is the real victory condition. Once the force can escape with Eren and Zeke, staying to annihilate every enemy would increase risk without serving the immediate operation. The chapter therefore frames withdrawal as part of victory.

Yet escape generates a new vulnerability. The airship is slow enough and low enough for Gabi to pursue it; the physical corridor that saves the Scouts also permits the battle’s violence to follow them upward.

## 3.3 Chapter 105 — 「凶弾」: the battle ends, causality does not

「凶弾」 means more than an ordinary bullet. The title marks the shot as disastrous, ominous, fate-laden in consequence. The chapter is built around delayed causality: an operation that appears finished produces its most emotionally decisive death during extraction.

### Gabi’s grief becomes authored grievance

V25 showed Gabi moving from inherited anti-Paradis ideology toward direct bereavement as friends, soldiers, and familiar places were destroyed. Chapter 105 completes that transition into action. She does not board the airship because Marleyan propaganda mechanically tells her to. She chooses pursuit after witnessing devastation that now belongs to her own memory.

This is a major character-state change.

Earlier Gabi could understand “island devils” through received history. After Liberio she also possesses first-person evidence: these people attacked her home and killed people she loved. That evidence is incomplete as a total history, but it is emotionally authentic.

The manga thereby complicates ideological correction. Showing Gabi that Paradis contains human beings will not automatically erase the fact that some of those human beings actually participated in the destruction she experienced.

### Falco follows Gabi despite better causal knowledge

Falco has more information than Gabi. He heard Eren and Reiner’s basement conversation. He knows Reiner participated in the original Wall breach and that Eren explicitly described people across the sea as fundamentally similar to those within the Walls. He therefore possesses stronger counterevidence against a simple devil ontology.

Yet Falco still follows Gabi onto the airship.

This demonstrates that knowledge does not mechanically determine behavior. Attachment can override safer judgment. Falco’s protective orientation toward Gabi repeatedly draws him into risks generated by her decisions.

### Sasha’s death is structurally anti-climactic

Sasha is not killed in a grand duel, a sacrificial charge, or a heroic last stand. She is shot aboard the extraction craft after the operation’s decisive combat has largely ended. The scene rejects the expectation that major death arrives only at the narrative peak of battle.

Her final `肉` is especially important because it returns Sasha from soldier-symbol to ordinary appetite. The word connects the dying body to one of her oldest comic and domestic signatures. The effect is not merely humor in tragedy. It collapses the distance between wartime casualty and the person who ate, stole food, shared meals, teased friends, and imagined ordinary abundance.

That is why the death has disproportionate force for Connie and Jean. They are not losing “the marksman.” They are losing a specific everyday companion.

### Jean refuses a monocausal blame story

Jean’s response is one of the chapter’s most mature ethical positions. He is furious, grieving, and willing to identify Eren’s unilateral initiative as causally important. At the same time, the chapter does not permit the entire chain to be reduced to one person. Gabi fired the shot. The Scouts attacked Liberio. Marley created the Warrior system and declared war. Eren forced the timing. Zeke’s plan shaped the operation. Each causal layer remains real.

Jean’s function across the corpus continues to be resistance to morally convenient simplification. He can criticize Eren without pretending that simply refusing Eren’s action would have dissolved the surrounding strategic problem.

### Levi’s violence toward Eren is disciplinary rather than enemy-directed

Levi’s kick on the airship resembles earlier moments in which violence inside the Corps marks hierarchy and accountability. Eren is not treated as a sovereign whose success immunizes him from judgment. He has become indispensable, but not legitimate by indispensability alone.

This distinction is central to the volume:

> **Indispensability creates leverage; it does not create rightful authority.**

### Zeke’s presence reclassifies the battle

The reveal that Zeke is aboard the airship changes the operation’s meaning. What looked like the capture or defeat of an enemy Titan becomes an extraction. His visible battlefield role was part of a deeper plan involving the Volunteers and Paradis.

But the reveal does not make Zeke trustworthy. Levi’s hostility, the physical restrictions placed on participants, and the later political context preserve suspicion. Cooperation is operational, not equivalent to moral or interpersonal trust.

## 3.4 Chapter 106 — 「義勇兵」: the island’s future was imported from the enemy world

The chapter’s backward movement is architecturally decisive. Rather than allowing Sasha’s death to close the volume in pure grief, Isayama asks how the present operation became possible at all.

Three years earlier, Marleyan survey ships approaching Paradis entered a changed environment. The island now possessed Titans, captured ships, and emerging military capacity, but lacked modern knowledge of the wider world. The Anti-Marleyan Volunteers become the bridge.

### The Volunteers destabilize the enemy category

Yelena, Onyankopon, Nicolo and their comrades originate within or under Marley’s imperial system, yet they are not reducible to Marleyan state purpose. Some are conquered peoples; some possess anti-Marleyan commitments; some cooperate because of Zeke; some become socially entangled with islanders.

This is the political analogue of V25’s `海の外も壁の中も同じなんだ`. The outside world is internally heterogeneous.

But Volume 26 adds a harder implication: recognizing heterogeneity does not solve the security problem. The island must decide whether to trust people whose information it desperately needs and whose motives it cannot independently verify.

### Technology enters as relationship, not abstraction

Onyankopon is particularly important because he makes technical asymmetry socially legible. The islanders encounter unfamiliar firearms, ships, communications, engineering, and global diversity through people rather than diagrams alone.

Technological modernization therefore becomes relational dependence. Paradis cannot simply “catch up” by deciding to modernize. It requires teachers, captured equipment, industrial learning, language, and time.

The word `時間` is strategically central. Zeke’s proposal is valuable in part because the island’s primary deficit is not courage or willingness but developmental time.

### Nicolo and food create low-stakes contact

Nicolo’s cooking scenes matter disproportionately for character reconstruction because V26 is otherwise crisis-heavy. Food produces one of the volume’s clearest noncombat forms of cross-group contact. Paradis soldiers encounter an enemy-world person not through interrogation or battle but through taste.

Sasha’s enthusiastic appetite has a double function. It preserves her established low-stakes behavior while showing how quickly an allegedly alien culture can become attractive through ordinary embodied experience.

After Chapter 105, these scenes are retrospectively painful within the same volume boundary. The manga has already shown Sasha die; Chapter 106 then restores her to a table, alive, hungry, and socially expansive. Chronology creates mourning through regained ordinariness.

### Zeke’s proposal combines deterrence with dynastic coercion

The Volunteers explain a plan centered on the Founding Titan, royal blood, and the Wall Titans’ `地鳴らし`. The underlying strategic logic is deterrence: demonstrate a limited capacity sufficient to prevent immediate invasion, then use the resulting interval to modernize Paradis until it no longer depends on Titan monopoly.

At V26, this is a proposal, not verified omniscient strategy.

Its apparent rationality also hides a bodily cost. If royal blood is required across generations, the plan transforms particular human bodies into strategic infrastructure. The series has repeatedly shown institutions converting inherited bodies into weapons; the proposed solution risks reproducing that structure under a different flag.

The volume does not yet settle how Paradis will adjudicate this problem. It does establish that strategic survival may be purchased by transferring coercion onto a smaller set of bodies.

### Armin’s dialogue ethic becomes self-implicating

Armin continues to imagine that communication may matter. But after Liberio, dialogue cannot be posed as the clean alternative to violence from which he stands morally outside. His Colossal transformation has killed at scale.

This strengthens rather than invalidates his dialogic orientation. It removes innocence from it.

Armin’s emerging position can be formulated as:

> **Because violence has already implicated us, communication is necessary not to preserve purity but to prevent implication from becoming permanent destiny.**

That is a more mature and more fragile position than early-series idealism.

### Eren before the mirror: command turned inward

The chapter returns to the present and ends with Eren imprisoned. His body is isolated from the group whose survival he claims to pursue. The mirror divides and doubles him visually. He grips his own hair and repeats:

`戦わなければ`

`勝てない`

`戦え`

`戦え`

Several readings remain live at V26:

1. **determination** — Eren is preserving resolve against confinement and opposition;
2. **self-discipline** — he must actively command himself to continue rather than merely expressing spontaneous desire;
3. **internalized war logic** — the binary fight/win has become a governing cognitive structure;
4. **self-division** — the mirror staging suggests commander and commanded may no longer be psychologically identical positions;
5. **performance/rehearsal** — Eren may be consolidating a chosen role through repeated language.

The volume does not authorize selecting only one.

What it does establish is that the language of agency has become coercive toward the self. Eren is “free” enough to choose continued struggle, yet the choice is narrated as something he must repeatedly order himself to enact.

# 4. Character-state deltas

## 4.1 Eren Jaeger — strategic initiative without institutional consent

### Starting state

At the end of V25, Eren has already crossed the threshold from clandestine infiltrator to initiator of open battle. He understands the humanity of the people around him and acts anyway.

### Pressure

- War Hammer resistance;
- Marleyan counterattack;
- extraction timing;
- dependence on the Scouts’ rescue;
- strategic need to obtain Zeke;
- post-operation accountability.

### Choice and action

Eren repeatedly prioritizes objective completion. He adapts tactically, uses Porco to break the crystal, consumes the War Hammer bearer, and continues until extraction becomes possible.

### New state

Volume 26 strengthens four rules.

**Rule 1 — recognition does not imply de-escalation.** V25 established this interpersonally with Reiner; V26 operationalizes it across an entire battle.

**Rule 2 — Eren is willing to impose strategic commitment on allies by creating irreversible facts.** His supporters cannot freely choose whether to rescue him once his action begins without accepting his loss and the resulting strategic consequences.

**Rule 3 — Eren’s problem-solving is highly mechanistic under direct pressure.** He identifies affordances and repurposes enemy capabilities.

**Rule 4 — his self-authorship increasingly requires self-command.** The mirror sequence makes continued struggle something he orders himself to perform.

### Open contradiction

Eren frames himself as someone who moves forward because he chooses to. Yet the language `戦え` suggests that this movement is no longer effortless expression of desire. Agency and compulsion are beginning to overlap without yet becoming identical.

---

## 4.2 Armin Arlert — dialogue after becoming the weapon

Armin’s most important delta is moral positioning.

Earlier volumes repeatedly associated him with imaginative alternatives, communication, and the possibility that unknown worlds contain more than enemies. Volume 26 does not erase those traits. It places them inside the body of the Colossal Titan.

His transformation destroys the harbor and kills at scale. Consequently, any future appeal to dialogue must come from a participant in mass violence, not an untouched observer.

That makes Armin more analytically useful, not less. His question becomes whether someone already implicated in catastrophe can still choose to limit future catastrophe.

Negative constraint: do not model V26 Armin as a pacifist. He remains willing to employ enormous violence when he judges it necessary.

---

## 4.3 Mikasa Ackerman — protection remains distinct from endorsement

V25 established the crucial rule that Mikasa can protect Eren while condemning his civilian killing. V26 maintains that separation during extraction and aftermath.

Her attachment does not make her politically identical to Eren. Nor does criticism terminate attachment. This is precisely the kind of relationship-conditioned evidence required for later simulation: behavior toward Eren cannot be predicted from a one-dimensional loyalty variable.

---

## 4.4 Jean Kirstein — grief without surrendering civilian distinction

Jean’s response to Sasha’s death strengthens his role as one of the corpus’s clearest proportionality-sensitive soldiers.

He is capable of anger toward the Marleyan children who have boarded the airship, but he does not collapse immediately into retaliatory killing. His prior V25 conflict with Floch over civilian distinction remains relevant. Sasha’s death gives him a direct personal reason to abandon that distinction, which makes his restraint more diagnostic.

Jean also recognizes Eren’s role in forcing the operation without claiming Eren alone caused everything. His judgment tends to preserve multiple causal layers even when emotionally costly.

---

## 4.5 Sasha Blouse — ordinary identity survives military death

Sasha’s death is one of the strongest demonstrations so far that low-stakes behavior is not analytically secondary.

Her final `肉` matters because the meaning of the loss depends on the long accumulation of ordinary appetite, embarrassment, humor, family background, hunting competence, friendship, and food behavior. A purely strategic character ledger would badly under-model why her death affects the group.

Chapter 106’s flashback to her delight in Nicolo’s cooking deepens this. Food becomes continuity across the time cut: the person dying in Chapter 105 is restored in Chapter 106 as someone vividly alive within ordinary desire.

---

## 4.6 Connie Springer — bereavement through shared ordinary history

Connie’s grief is especially bound to Sasha because their relationship has been built through long-duration peer familiarity rather than a single ideological bond. His reaction should therefore be modeled as the rupture of routine companionship.

This is another case where ordinary-social evidence predicts crisis response better than abstract shared values alone.

---

## 4.7 Gabi Braun — propaganda becomes experience

Gabi begins the volume with a worldview already shaped by Marleyan categories. By Chapter 105, she has acquired something propaganda alone could never provide: personal bereavement caused by the enemy category.

This strengthens her commitment in the short term because ideology and lived evidence now reinforce each other.

A later ideological challenge would therefore need to address both. Simply proving that islanders are human cannot negate the fact that islanders participated in the violence she saw.

Her decision to pursue the airship also demonstrates extreme action orientation under grief. She converts affect rapidly into mission behavior.

---

## 4.8 Falco Grice — protective attachment overrides superior information

Falco possesses the strongest cross-boundary perspective among the Warrior children because he heard Eren and Reiner’s conversation. Yet he follows Gabi into danger.

This is a powerful negative result against rationalist character modeling: more accurate information does not guarantee safer or more conciliatory action when attachment is at stake.

Falco’s defining behavior remains protective substitution and accompaniment. He repeatedly chooses exposure to danger rather than allow Gabi to face it alone.

---

## 4.9 Reiner Braun — relational tether persists

Reiner’s partial return to battle validates the V24 finding that the candidate children function as a specific anti-suicide tether. He mobilizes when Gabi and Falco call for help.

This should not be generalized into renewed will to live. The evidence supports a narrower rule: **responsibility toward specific younger dependents can temporarily override Reiner’s self-annihilating state.**

---

## 4.10 Zeke Jaeger — strategic ally without trust

V26 radically reclassifies Zeke’s operational position while preserving epistemic uncertainty about motive.

He has apparently coordinated with the Volunteers and Paradis over multiple years and enters the airship as an extracted participant rather than defeated enemy. Yet Levi’s conduct and the command environment show that cooperation is heavily constrained.

The correct state label at V26 is therefore not “ally” without qualification. It is **strategically cooperating high-risk actor under coercive supervision**.

## 4.11 Yelena — ideological mediator and asymmetric information holder

Yelena enters as a conduit for Zeke’s plan and as a central figure in the Volunteers. Her composure and ideological intensity are visible, but V26 does not yet provide enough independent evidence to determine whether she should be modeled primarily as anti-Marleyan revolutionary, Zeke loyalist, opportunist, or some more specific combination.

Confidence should remain provisional.

---

## 4.12 Onyankopon — world-expanding contact figure

Onyankopon’s value is partly informational and partly social. He gives Paradis access to technological and geographic plurality without reducing himself to exposition.

He is evidence that the outside world contains conquered peoples and interests not aligned with Marley’s state. This complicates any future model in which “the world” behaves as one agent.

---

## 4.13 Nicolo — enemy category eroded through embodied ordinary life

Nicolo’s cooking creates a low-stakes domain in which mutual recognition occurs without requiring ideological agreement first. Sasha’s response to his food is especially important because it is immediate, bodily, and socially contagious.

At V26, this does not prove deep political reconciliation. It proves something narrower: ordinary practices can generate attachment and familiarity across enemy boundaries faster than formal ideology changes.

# 5. Relationship-state analysis

## 5.1 Eren / Armin / Mikasa — the trio is no longer a unified decision-making unit

The childhood trio remains emotionally connected but no longer shares a common path to action.

- Eren acts first and forces the operational environment.
- Mikasa protects him while condemning aspects of his conduct.
- Armin participates in the rescue plan and catastrophic violence while still imagining communication.

The relationship therefore shifts from shared aspiration to **co-presence under strategic divergence**.

This is not yet a clean rupture. The more precise model is that affection, dependence, disagreement, and coercive consequences coexist.

## 5.2 Jean / Sasha / Connie — ordinary friendship becomes grief infrastructure

Sasha’s death demonstrates how long-duration low-stakes interaction becomes the substrate of wartime mourning. Jean and Connie’s reactions cannot be reconstructed from military role alone.

## 5.3 Gabi / Falco — asymmetrical knowledge, symmetrical exposure

Falco knows more; Gabi drives more. Yet Falco repeatedly enters her chosen risks. Their relationship is therefore not leader/follower in a simple competence sense. Gabi supplies action momentum; Falco supplies protective persistence and counter-perspective.

## 5.4 Paradis / Volunteers — cooperation under epistemic dependence

Paradis needs what the Volunteers know. The Volunteers need Paradis as the only polity capable of using the Founding Titan against Marley. This is mutual dependence without symmetric information.

Such relationships are inherently unstable. Each party has reason to cooperate and reason to fear manipulation.

# 6. Institutional and political reading

## 6.1 The Liberio operation as command crisis

A central mistake would be to analyze the operation only by outcome.

Tactically:

- key Marleyan forces are damaged;
- the War Hammer Titan is captured;
- Zeke is extracted;
- the Scouts escape.

Operationally:

- the airship extraction works;
- Armin neutralizes the harbor;
- Eren’s combat adaptation succeeds.

Strategically:

- Paradis gains Titan capability and Zeke;
- Marley is wounded but not eliminated;
- international consequences remain open.

Politically inside Paradis:

- Eren’s unilateral initiative exposes a crisis of civilian/military authority and command control;
- the leadership is forced to respond to a plan it did not freely choose in the moment;
- success may increase Eren’s leverage precisely because it proves his indispensability.

Ethically:

- civilian deaths remain real;
- Sasha and other Scouts die;
- enemy children become direct participants in retaliation;
- strategic gain cannot retroactively make every means justified.

These dimensions must remain separate.

## 6.2 The Volunteers as modernization infrastructure

The chapter reveals that Paradis’s new military competence is relationally imported. This matters because modernization narratives often erase the human network that carries knowledge.

Ships, firearms, radio, engineering, cuisine, geography, and strategic theory arrive through particular people. Every technical gain therefore has a trust architecture.

## 6.3 Zeke’s deterrence proposal and the reproduction of inherited-body politics

The proposed Rumbling deterrent seems, at first glance, to offer a way to buy time while avoiding immediate world-scale war. Yet it depends on inherited Titan powers and royal blood.

The institutional danger is continuity with the very systems the manga has criticized:

- Marley turns Eldian bodies into weapons;
- the Reiss system turns royal inheritance into political machinery;
- the proposed Paradis strategy risks turning royal descendants into strategic assets.

The flag changes. The body-politics may not.

# 7. Ethical and philosophical reading

## 7.1 Can coercion be legitimate when it works?

Eren creates a hard case because his unilateral action appears to produce results that the island desperately needs.

The strongest defense of Eren at V26 is consequentialist:

- Marley has declared war;
- waiting may worsen Paradis’s position;
- Zeke’s window is limited;
- Eren’s action acquires the War Hammer Titan and secures Zeke;
- the island lacks time.

The strongest criticism is institutional:

- Eren makes the rescue obligation irreversible before obtaining collective authorization;
- his comrades bear risks he effectively chooses for them;
- civilian casualties become part of the imposed cost;
- strategic success can normalize future unilateral coercion.

The volume does not settle this dispute. It makes it unavoidable.

## 7.2 Grief as a source of moral knowledge and moral distortion

Gabi’s grief is real knowledge: people she knew are dead because an enemy force attacked. It is also incomplete knowledge: the event does not explain the entire causal history that produced the battle.

The series therefore resists two bad positions:

1. grief is irrational and should be ignored;
2. grief supplies a complete moral theory of the conflict.

Grief gives standing to pain without granting omniscience.

## 7.3 Dialogue after guilt

Armin’s position is philosophically important because the manga removes the fantasy that dialogue belongs only to the innocent. After the harbor attack, he cannot argue for communication from outside violence.

That makes the question harder and more realistic: can perpetrators, participants, and compromised actors still build forms of recognition that interrupt future violence?

## 7.4 Freedom and self-coercion

Eren’s mirror sequence destabilizes any simple opposition between freedom and external coercion.

If a person internalizes the imperative to fight so completely that they must continually command themselves, is that pure self-authorship? It may be. But self-authorship can itself become rigid.

The volume therefore strengthens the project’s earlier revision:

> **agency is not equivalent to liberation, and self-authorship is not equivalent to ethical or psychological freedom.**

# 8. Visual and manga-form audit

## 8.1 Chapter-title progression

The four titles form a compressed argument:

`強襲` → `勝者` → `凶弾` → `義勇兵`

The sequence moves from action, to claimed outcome, to destabilizing consequence, to hidden enabling history.

The architecture therefore refuses linear triumph. What looks like victory is wounded from inside, then retrospectively shown to depend on people previously absent from the reader’s causal model.

## 8.2 The Colossal visual reversal

Armin’s Colossal Titan over the harbor is one of the strongest visual reversals in the corpus. The body that once represented invasion from the reader’s original side of identification is now occupied by a protagonist and directed outward.

The image does not say “the sides are morally identical.” It says the visual grammar of terror is transferable.

## 8.3 Eren, Porco, and forced tool use

The “nutcracker” sequence is formally grotesque because bodily agency is layered:

- Porco tries to resist;
- Eren controls the spatial relation;
- Porco’s jaw performs the mechanical action;
- the War Hammer bearer’s body is broken and consumed.

Three persons become one weapon system through coercion.

## 8.4 Sasha and the rifle line

Sasha’s death is staged through ordinary ballistic geometry rather than Titan spectacle. The reduction in scale matters. After pages of giant bodies and city destruction, one rifle shot becomes narratively larger than many explosions.

## 8.5 Flashback warmth after death

Chapter 106’s social scenes with food, Volunteers, and islanders are visually less compressed than the battle material. Because the reader has already seen Sasha die, her presence in these ordinary panels acquires retrospective fragility.

The chronological reversal produces grief by giving back what has already been lost.

## 8.6 The final mirror

The mirror is an unusually explicit visual device for internal division. Eren addresses a reflected self while touching and controlling his own body. The final page does not present freedom as open space. It presents a confined room, plumbing, brick, mirror frame, and repeated imperative.

The volume that began with external assault ends with an internal command cell.

# 9. Japanese voice and lexical notes

## Eren

The final `戦わなければ / 勝てない / 戦え / 戦え` is clipped, imperative, and self-directed. It lacks explanatory cushioning. The structure is causal first—if one does not fight, one cannot win—then imperative. The self is reduced to an operational rule.

## Sasha

`肉` functions as a minimal dying utterance with maximal accumulated character history. Its analytical importance depends on recurrence, not lexical complexity.

## Reiner

His return is less important for extended speech than for response to being called. At this stage, relational triggers remain stronger predictors of action than generalized declarations of purpose.

## Gabi

Her speech remains high-intensity and action-coupled. Ideological vocabulary and immediate grief increasingly overlap rather than remaining separate registers.

## Volunteers

Chapter 106 introduces technical and strategic vocabulary into the island’s discourse: modern weapons, ships, technology, time, royal blood, and deterrence. The language of survival becomes more geopolitical and developmental.

# 13. Adversarial rereading

## Counter-reading 1: Eren’s coercion is simply justified by the results

The operation secures enormous strategic gains. A defender can argue that ordinary institutional consent was impossible under conditions of urgency and infiltration.

This reading remains live. It fails, however, if it treats success as retroactive authorization. The manga explicitly gives us Levi/Hange anger, Sasha’s death, civilian cost, and Eren’s confinement. Those signals make legitimacy a separate question from effectiveness.

## Counter-reading 2: Armin has abandoned his earlier ethics

His harbor transformation could support a cynical reading that dialogue was always naïve and power ultimately decides everything.

The chapter does not support that conclusion. Armin continues to think in communicative and alternative-seeking terms. What changes is his innocence, not necessarily his value hierarchy.

## Counter-reading 3: Gabi is merely indoctrinated

This becomes increasingly inadequate after Liberio. Indoctrination remains causally important, but her hostility now has direct experiential reinforcement. Any later change would have to metabolize real grief, not just false history.

## Counter-reading 4: the Volunteers prove trust solves the conflict

Their presence does demonstrate cross-boundary cooperation. It also creates dependency and infiltration risk. The volume refuses both xenophobic closure and naïve cosmopolitan certainty.

## Counter-reading 5: the final mirror proves Eren has lost freedom

Too strong at V26. The scene certainly complicates self-authorship, but self-command can still be an expression of chosen commitment. The correct state is OPEN between determination, internalized compulsion, self-division, and role rehearsal.

# 14. Volume-level synthesis

Volume 26 is the point at which *Attack on Titan* stops allowing “victory” to function as a stable moral or political category.

The Scouts win in Liberio, but their victory is inseparable from the fact that Eren compelled their participation by acting first. Eren wins the War Hammer Titan, but he does so by converting another person’s body into a tool. Armin saves the operation, but through a Colossal transformation that places him inside the visual lineage of the terror that began the series. The airship escapes, but Sasha dies there. Gabi loses the battle, but gains a grievance she now owns more personally than the ideology she inherited. Zeke appears to be defeated, but is actually extracted. Paradis appears to have independently crossed the technological gap, but Chapter 106 reveals that its new competence depends on outsiders from within the enemy world.

The result is not moral equivalence. The result is causal entanglement.

Every side increasingly exists inside the other side’s history:

- Reiner helped shape Eren’s language of persistence;
- Eren’s attack gives Gabi new reason to hate Paradis;
- Marley’s empire produces the Volunteers who help Paradis resist Marley;
- Zeke’s royal-blood connection may enable Paradis’s deterrent;
- Bertolt’s Colossal power becomes Armin’s weapon against Marley;
- Porco’s Jaw becomes the mechanism by which Eren takes the War Hammer;
- Nicolo’s food becomes part of Sasha’s ordinary social world;
- Sasha’s death then becomes part of the emotional world that will confront Gabi and Falco.

This is why the volume’s chronology matters so much. Chapter 105 gives us irreversible loss; Chapter 106 then moves backward and shows the social world that made the loss meaningful. The narrative does not merely explain strategy. It restores ordinary contact after death, making the past feel more alive precisely because it cannot be recovered.

The final mirror converts the entire political problem into a personal grammar.

`戦わなければ 勝てない`.

At one level, this is the oldest logic in the series. Fight or be destroyed. Act or lose freedom. Refuse passivity.

At another level, the speaker has changed. Eren is no longer a child staring beyond a Wall, nor a recruit shouting at an external enemy. He is an imprisoned strategic actor telling his own reflected image to keep fighting.

Volume 26 therefore leaves the project with a sharper question than “Is Eren free?”

The better question is:

> **What happens when the pursuit of freedom becomes a discipline that the self must enforce upon itself?**

The volume does not answer. It makes that problem load-bearing for Volume 27 and for the approaching approximately-75% checkpoint.
