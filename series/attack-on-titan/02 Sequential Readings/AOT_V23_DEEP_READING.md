# 『進撃の巨人』Volume 23 Deep Reading
## The enemy becomes a society: inherited militarization, the good-Eldian bargain, Reiner's divided homecoming, and a weapon system approaching obsolescence

## 1. Central thesis and volume role

Volume 22 ends with Eren looking across the sea and asking whether killing the enemies on the far side would finally make the people of Paradis free. Volume 23 answers that question neither by immediately returning to Eren nor by supplying a philosophical rebuttal. It moves the reader across the sea and makes the category **“the enemy” structurally unusable as a complete description**.

That does not mean the volume reveals that Marley is harmless, that the conflict is a misunderstanding, or that everyone bears equivalent responsibility. On the contrary, Volume 23 gives some of the clearest evidence yet of Marleyan imperial coercion. Eldians are used as expendable infantry, Titan holders, political scapegoats, airborne living bombs, and a racialized population whose conditional social advancement depends on military usefulness. Marley has pursued colonial expansion under the shelter of Titan supremacy, and its own officers now admit that this dependence allowed conventional military development to stagnate.

The shift in focalization therefore performs a more difficult operation than simple humanization:

> **It converts the enemy from a category into a social system.**

Inside that system are officers, conscripts, Warrior holders, children competing to inherit Titans, families hoping military distinction will improve their status, traumatized veterans, prisoners, rivals, siblings, and people whose relationship to state ideology ranges from wholehearted internalization to private reservation. Human interiority does not erase political structure. Political structure does not erase individual agency.

The four chapters progressively narrow the distance between reader and Marley-side life.

- 「海の向こう側」 begins in industrial trench warfare with Falco disoriented beneath artillery and Gabi defining herself through the duty to destroy the island's “devils.”
- 「マーレの戦士」 shows Marley converting Subjects of Ymir into battlefield materiel while modern anti-Titan weapons expose the declining strategic monopoly of that system.
- 「闇夜の列車」 moves from military planning into hospital rooms, Warrior conversation, train celebration, rivalry, humor, care, and Reiner's attempt to redirect Falco toward saving Gabi.
- 「壁の中の少年」 brings the Warriors into Liberio homes and then into Reiner's childhood, revealing that the ideological reproduction visible in Gabi is not new. Reiner once used nearly the same logic: if he became a Warrior and defeated the island devils, he could save Eldians, reunite his family, and become someone worthy of pride.

This produces the volume's governing irony. The children are being taught to inherit Titans precisely when the Titan-centered world order is beginning to expire. At the strategic level, Marley knows anti-Titan artillery, naval guns, and future aerial warfare are eroding the supremacy on which its empire was built. At the domestic level, families still treat inheritance of a Titan—and therefore a thirteen-year death sentence—as honor, security, ethnic redemption, and upward mobility.

The system is thus reproducing itself **after its material foundations have already begun to fail**.

Reiner becomes the human bridge across this contradiction. He is celebrated at home as a loyal Warrior and living proof of service. Yet his speech about the 104th cannot sustain the demon category without leaking recognition. He describes the potato incident, selfish classmates, overly serious classmates, reckless classmates, and the ordinary irritating differences of people he actually lived beside. The wording is superficially framed as evidence of island depravity; the content is affectionate social memory. When the narration culminates in 「色んな奴らがいて そこに俺達もいた」, his earlier soldier life is not retrospectively exposed as fake. It remains a psychologically active human world inside the adult Warrior.

Volume 23 therefore strengthens one of the V01-V19 checkpoint's most important identity findings: **a role can be politically instrumental, socially performed, and still become psychologically real through repeated conduct and relationship**.

The volume's central thesis is:

> **Marley sustains its empire by teaching subordinated people to treat the inheritance of violence as the inheritance of dignity, while Reiner's return demonstrates that human recognition survives inside that machinery and can become a source of trauma rather than liberation.**

A second thesis follows:

> **The strategic future and the ideological future are out of sync. Marley knows Titan supremacy is ending; its children are still being trained to sacrifice their lives to prove that supremacy.**

# 3. Narrative architecture and causal close read

## 3.1 Chapter 91 — 「海の向こう側」: freedom imagery inside a trench war

The first human image of the post-ocean world is not a diplomat, civilian city, or omniscient map. It is Falco lying dazed in the dirt while artillery shakes the battlefield.

Before he fully reconstructs where he is, he reaches toward a bird and tells it to fly away—somewhere far—because it has wings. The image matters because the series' freedom vocabulary has migrated again. In V22, the sea ceased to guarantee freedom. In V23, flight becomes the obvious capacity Falco lacks. Yet the bird should not be overread as proof of later metaphysics at this boundary. Here it establishes a child's dissociated wish for distance from a battlefield whose context he momentarily cannot remember.

His cognition is rapidly restored by relationship and peer explanation. Colt calls him by name and identifies himself as his brother. Udo checks whether Falco remembers that the war has lasted four years. Gabi jokes that they may have to explain the entire operation again, while Zofia tells him simply to listen. This is important character-model evidence: Falco's confusion is **state-dependent concussion evidence**, not evidence that he is habitually slow.

The battle's strategic structure is contemporary industrial warfare. Marley needs Fort Slava and the nearby naval position to end the conflict. Trenches, armored trains, heavy artillery, naval guns, airships, and anti-Titan weapons occupy the battlefield. Titans are still decisive, but they now exist inside a combined-arms system rather than above technological history.

### Warrior candidacy as competitive inherited mortality

The children explain why they are there. The front contains roughly eight hundred Eldian soldiers who are explicitly treated as a force that can be spent. The Warrior candidates are present because Magath has turned the frontline into a final examination for succession.

Gabi assumes she will inherit the Armored Titan. When Falco needles her for speaking as though the other candidates barely count, she says the difference is not simply performance score but **覚悟**—resolve.

Her formulation is one of the volume's central ideological documents. She intends to carry the fate of the Eldians, destroy the island devils who cause their suffering, prove to the world that the remaining Eldians are “good Eldians,” and free the people of the internment zone.

At the V23 boundary, this cannot responsibly be summarized as “Gabi is stupid” or “Gabi is a passive propaganda victim.” She is a child inside a coercive informational system, but the scene gives her active reasoning. Her model is:

1. the world fears Eldians because of historical/Titan danger;
2. Paradis contains the genuinely evil branch responsible for continuing that danger;
3. continental Eldians can distinguish themselves through loyalty and sacrifice;
4. proving that distinction can purchase collective liberation;
5. inheriting a Titan is therefore not merely personal glory but a public burden.

The structure is tragically self-subordinating because the state offering “good Eldian” recognition is the same state maintaining racial segregation and consuming Eldian bodies as weapons. But internal coherence matters for reconstruction. Gabi should not be simulated as repeating slogans with no model underneath them.

### The armored train and humanitarian hesitation as a tactical resource

Gabi proposes destroying the armored train with seven bundled grenades. She argues that if one Warrior candidate is worth more than eight hundred soldiers, then risking herself to save the unit is precisely the kind of value judgment the Warrior system claims to make. She also links success to proving she deserves the Armor.

The method is ethically revealing. She removes visible military identification and approaches in civilian-like clothing with her hands raised. The enemy soldiers recognize that she may be a child and hesitate. Magath explicitly notes the international-law problem surrounding disguised combatancy and the practical importance of whether witnesses survive.

Gabi then rolls the explosives beneath the armored train and destroys it.

The sequence does several things at once:

- demonstrates genuine tactical creativity;
- demonstrates extraordinary willingness to accept personal risk;
- shows how the Warrior system rewards children for treating self-endangerment as proof of worth;
- weaponizes the enemy's hesitation to kill an apparently noncombatant child;
- introduces international law as a real constraint that can nevertheless be manipulated;
- makes “child innocence” itself part of battlefield geometry.

Falco's response is equally diagnostic. Once artillery resumes, he runs out after Gabi instead of preserving himself in the trench. His behavior is protective before it is ideological. The volume will later clarify that his desire to inherit the Armor is directed less toward status than toward preventing Gabi from receiving its shortened future.

### Titan success after the train is removed

Once Gabi has disabled the anti-Titan mobility threat, Porco's Jaw and Pieck's Cart can break the line. Their effectiveness is real. The volume does not pretend Titans have suddenly become useless. The important change is conditionality: their battlefield dominance increasingly depends on suppressing technologies specifically designed to kill them.

Chapter 91 therefore establishes the military problem that will govern the rest of the volume. Marley still possesses terrifying Titan force, but the force no longer sits outside technological competition.

## 3.2 Chapter 92 — 「マーレの戦士」: people as ammunition and victory as evidence of decline

The chapter title invites heroic interpretation and then systematically contaminates it.

### Eldians as aerial ordnance

Marley loads restrained, drugged Subjects of Ymir into an airship and drops them by parachute over the fortress. Zeke's scream transforms them during the descent. The human body is converted into a delivery mechanism whose value is measured through mass, height, impact survival, and destructive effect.

The operation is not metaphorically dehumanizing; it is administratively dehumanizing. People become a munition class.

The calculating language after impact is important. Roughly half the transformed bodies may have died from the fall because the altitude was too high, but the “mass bomb” still produced sufficient effect. This is not evidence that every Marleyan character shares identical moral affect. It is evidence about the military system's treatment of the bodies available to it.

The scene also complicates the word 「悪魔」. Watching the transformed Eldians slaughter enemy troops, Warrior candidates comment through inherited historical memory: this is what Marleyans' ancestors were supposedly subjected to. The state is manufacturing the visual evidence that sustains its own devil narrative. Eldian bodies are turned into monsters by Marley and then interpreted as proof that Eldians are monstrous.

### Falco and the enemy prisoner

Against that machinery, Falco supplies a small but high-value counterexample. He gives aid to a wounded enemy prisoner and insists that the man is no longer combat-capable. His conduct is partly rule-based: prisoners and the wounded remain within recognized constraints rather than simply becoming legitimate targets.

The prisoner nevertheless calls the Eldians devils. Udo translates the insult.

This matters because Volume 23 refuses an easy correction in the opposite direction. Anti-Eldian hatred is not shown as a belief held only by Marleyan propagandists. Foreign enemy soldiers can reproduce it too. The outside world is socially heterogeneous, but the stigma is transnational enough to shape behavior beyond Marley's borders.

Falco therefore acts humanely toward someone who actively despises his category. His procedural restraint does not require prior mutual recognition.

### Reiner and the wall trigger

When Reiner descends and surveys the fortress, a wall produces a flash of 104th memory. His thought is blunt: 「また壁かよ」, followed by the judgment that he is sick of walls.

This is strong evidence that Paradis has not been filed away as a completed mission. A neutral battlefield object activates relationship- and trauma-saturated memory. The new Marley-side present is therefore psychologically continuous with the soldier world rather than replacing it.

### Zeke, Reiner, and the cost of strategic closure

Zeke coordinates the Beast Titan's long-range destruction and attempts to finish the fleet. Reiner's Armored Titan ultimately shields him from naval fire, absorbing catastrophic damage.

The act is useful for the Reiner model even though it is not a clean test of the frozen mission-self-correction prediction. Reiner does not simply seek personal survival. He remains capable of body-as-shield commitment to the Warrior operation and its command structure.

The battle ends in Marleyan victory. Yet the narrative conclusion immediately deprives victory of triumph. The world has now seen that modern weapons can damage and defeat Titans. Marley has won a four-year war while demonstrating that the historical era in which Titan power alone decides war is ending.

This is the chapter's central strategic reversal:

> **Marley wins the battle and loses confidence in the system that made the victory possible.**

The next imperative is consequently to retake the Founding Titan quickly—not because Titan power is eternally supreme, but because Marley needs time to rebuild conventional military strength before that supremacy disappears.

## 3.3 Chapter 93 — 「闇夜の列車」: a state discovers it is late, while its Warriors begin going home

The chapter starts with military elites performing an unusually candid audit of their own empire.

### Titan supremacy produced strategic complacency

Marleyan officers note that the state has been relying on Titan power while pursuing colonial expansion. Other nations spent that same period developing anti-Titan weapons. The Fort Slava battle proves that human military technology can now break what used to appear invulnerable.

The most important claim is not “Titans are useless.” The Cart, Jaw, Beast, and Armor have just demonstrated the opposite. The claim is historical:

> **Titan advantage is no longer a stable monopoly.**

The officers anticipate warfare moving into the air. The line about “devils of the earth” being left to look upward is formally linked to Falco's opening bird image. Wings are not only a freedom symbol. They are becoming the next technological military domain.

### Zeke's proposal: buy time through renewed Paradis conquest

Zeke asks permission to speak and proposes resuming the Paradis operation immediately to recover the Founder. His strategic reasoning is coherent with the military audit: Marley needs time to modernize, and securing the island/Titans could fill the gap during reconstruction.

He also makes the proposal personally legible to Marley. He has only one year left in his term. Grisha Jaeger is framed as the “abomination” whose action should be ended by the son who once exposed him. The state praises Zeke as the child who proved loyalty by reporting his parents.

The scene is diagnostically important because public motive and private information are visibly separated. Marley knows Zeke has unusual Beast abilities but does not know why. Colt points out that earlier Beast Titans did not share Zeke's ability to transform Subjects of Ymir dosed with his spinal fluid, command them with his scream, or enable movement under moonlight in the same way. He asks why Zeke is special despite supposedly lacking royal blood.

Zeke does not answer. Instead, he later jokes that his “secret” is an unusual way of wiping his ass.

This is excellent voice evidence. Casual vulgar humor is being used as **deflection around strategically decisive hidden information**. A model that translates Zeke's playful surface into openness would fail here.

### Marley models Paradis instead of treating it as unknowable

The meeting reconstructs the island's current Titan holdings from incomplete reports. Founder and Attack are linked to Eren Jaeger; Colossal and Female are uncertain from Marley's perspective. The military also recognizes that the island possesses threats not reducible to Titan shifters: ODM-equipped soldiers designed specifically to kill Titans and at least two Ackermans, understood as a powerful anomalous lineage.

This is a useful perspective reversal. Earlier volumes showed Paradis trying to infer Warrior capabilities. Now Marley is doing the same thing. Neither side possesses an omniscient model; each builds an adversary model from losses, reports, missing reconnaissance, and inherited fragments.

### Reiner and Porco: gratitude blocked by rivalry

Reiner wakes from a memory-saturated dream in the hospital. Porco says he let him sleep because it looked like an enjoyable dream. Reiner attempts to thank Porco for saving him during the battle.

Porco rejects the relational framing. He says he was saving the Armored Titan for Marley, not Reiner personally, and returns to the old grievance that he should have been selected for the original Paradis mission.

The exchange expands the Warrior ensemble beyond a single family-like unit. Rivalry, resentment, inherited sibling comparison, and professional interdependence coexist. Reiner's old team relationships cannot simply be projected onto Porco.

Porco has gained some memory access through inheriting the Jaw Titan formerly held by Ymir. His description of Ymir as a pitiable woman burdened by another person's name is also an important external corroborating angle on her history, though it remains memory-mediated and partial.

### Pieck: body habit as ordinary-life evidence

Pieck provides one of the volume's clearest low-stakes physical controls. After remaining in Cart form for roughly two months, she jokes that she is forgetting how to walk on two legs and uses a crutch / quadrupedal posture during readjustment.

This should not be inflated into a timeless personality quirk detached from state. It is precisely valuable because it is **body-state conditioned ordinary behavior**. Long-term Titan embodiment changes post-transformation habit.

### The train: war transitions into social life

The chapter's second half places the Warrior candidates and soldiers on a train home. Gabi recounts destroying the armored train in an exuberant, performative register and is physically hoisted by cheering soldiers. The scene is funny, affectionate, and politically disturbing at the same time: a child receives mass social reward for successful self-endangerment.

This is how militarization reproduces without every participant needing to be a cold ideologue. Celebration itself is part of the institution.

### Reiner -> Falco: coercive surface, protective intent

The train conversation between Reiner and Falco is one of the volume's most important character-model scenes.

Falco notes that Gabi is likely to inherit the Armor and that Reiner has two years remaining. Reiner draws the consequence out: if Gabi inherits, her life becomes sharply bounded. Falco asks whether Reiner is really all right with that.

Reiner suddenly adopts the disciplinary language of the system. He tells Falco that disparaging the honor of inheriting a Titan could be reported as disloyalty; Colt could lose the Beast succession right; the Grice family could be converted into Titan weapons and dropped from an airship. Falco immediately corrects himself in formal loyalty language.

Then Reiner asks the real question: does Falco want to inherit the Armor?

Falco answers: 「オレです」—he will inherit it.

Reiner makes the motive explicit: if Falco wants to protect Gabi, he must surpass her. On the final page of the exchange he says:

`お前がガビを救い出すんだ`

from `この真っ暗な俺達の未来から`.

The scene should not be modeled as Reiner simply threatening a child. The threat is real as a statement of system consequences, but Reiner uses the system's coercive language to force Falco to understand what is at stake and then entrusts him with an anti-system relational objective: prevent Gabi from inheriting Reiner's fate.

This is a major revision to any model of adult Reiner as merely resigned. He is participating in the Warrior system while covertly attempting to create a different outcome for the next generation.

## 3.4 Chapter 94 — 「壁の中の少年」: Liberio as another enclosure and Reiner as the child Gabi is becoming

The title performs the volume's deepest spatial revision. For most of the series, “the boy inside the Walls” would naturally evoke Eren. Chapter 94 applies the structure to Liberio.

Paradis was not the only enclosure.

### Homecoming through an internment gate

The train returns to Liberio. Gabi shouts that she has come home alive and calls the place her beloved hometown. Families flood the station, reunions occur, and the candidates return to ordinary social relations.

This domestic texture is analytically essential. The internment zone is oppressive, but it is also home in the psychologically meaningful sense. People can love a place that structurally confines them. The relationship between home and freedom is therefore more complex than “home good / wall bad.”

The chapter also establishes that conditions for Eldians may be even worse in some foreign states than under Marley. This does not absolve Marley; it prevents the analysis from turning Marley into the sole container of anti-Eldian prejudice. The outside-world problem is distributed.

### Falco's interpretation of Reiner

Falco thinks back to Reiner's warning and recognizes its likely intent. Braun, a model Warrior whose loyalty is publicly exemplary, may actually be trying to free Gabi from the war. Falco's social cognition is therefore stronger than a passive “kind boy” label suggests. He can infer a hidden protective motive from a deliberately harsh conversation.

### Wounded veterans and the cost hidden behind parade language

Liberio contains returned soldiers whose war damage does not fit triumphal rhetoric. A traumatized veteran collapses under an ordinary stimulus, and the children help rather than treating the man as a glorious abstraction.

The scene is brief but important. Military victory returns home as bodies and nervous systems, not only medals and newspaper claims.

### Reiner's family dinner: the demon narrative fails at the level of anecdote

Reiner's family expects a Warrior's account of the island devils. Karina and the other relatives inhabit the ideological bargain sincerely: the island must be defeated, Gabi's Warrior success is a source of pride, and Reiner's service is proof of honorable Eldian status.

Reiner begins speaking in the appropriate category language. He calls the island experience hell and describes its people as terrible.

But the examples do something different.

He tells the potato story. He describes someone who thinks only of himself, someone who thinks too much about other people, someone who charges forward without thinking, and people who continue moving whatever happens. The visual field restores Sasha, Connie, Jean, Eren, Armin, Mikasa, and the ordinary 104th ensemble.

The structure is almost comic. Reiner is supposed to prove demonic depravity; instead, he produces the recognizable irritations of classmates.

Gabi notices the mismatch. Her response asks, in substance: after five years among brutal devils, is *that* the horror he has to report?

Reiner finally arrives at the truth he can say without directly repudiating the ideological frame:

> `色んな奴らがいて そこに俺達もいた`

There were all kinds of people—and they were there among them.

This is among the strongest pieces of evidence in the series against reading soldier Reiner as nothing but cover behavior. Adult Reiner's memory does not preserve a synthetic “performance.” It preserves individuated classmates.

### The chapter's temporal fold: Gabi repeats young Reiner

After dinner, the chapter turns into Reiner's childhood.

His mother Karina organizes his aspiration around family repair and social recognition. If he becomes a Warrior, he can become an Honorary Marleyan and perhaps make a complete family possible. Young Reiner's motivation is therefore not reducible to state propaganda. It combines:

- desire for his father's recognition;
- desire to make his mother happy;
- desire for family unity;
- ethnic status aspiration;
- belief that defeating the island devils will save Eldians;
- desire to become a hero.

This is exactly why the Warrior system is durable. It does not need to create motives from nothing. It recruits ordinary attachment and redirects it into military succession.

Training differentiates the children. Annie excels physically. Bertolt performs strongly. Porco is openly contemptuous of Reiner. Marcel mediates. Reiner is not naturally the obvious elite candidate and internalizes the fear that failure will destroy his route to family legitimacy.

The chapter ends with young Reiner repeating a formulation structurally close to Gabi's present-day one: defeat the devils of Paradis, save the Eldians—indeed save the world—and become a son worthy of pride.

The parallel should be treated as **institutional reproduction, not genetic personality equivalence**. Gabi is not “young Reiner again” in every respect. She is more tactically gifted, more confident, and socially rewarded in different ways. What repeats is the ideological conversion mechanism.

The volume therefore closes not on an adult strategic plan but on the production of the child who will later carry it out.

# 4. Marley as a coercive dependency system

Volume 23 clarifies that Marley does not simply hate Eldians. It depends on them.

The state needs Eldians as:

- expendable conventional infantry;
- Warrior candidates;
- holders of the Nine Titans;
- transformed battlefield Titans;
- aerial mass weapons;
- symbolic proof that “good Eldians” can serve Marley;
- a demonized category against which Marleyan national identity can define itself.

This creates a structural contradiction. The population is treated as dangerous enough to segregate and inferiorize, yet indispensable enough that Marley's strategic survival is organized around extracting military value from it.

The Honorary Marleyan system turns this contradiction into incentive. A family can seek safety, pride, or status by volunteering a child for shortened life as a Titan holder. Violence becomes a route toward conditional dignity.

Gabi's ideology is therefore not merely an error located inside her head. It is the expected product of an institutional market in recognition.

# 5. The good-Eldian bargain

Gabi's formulation deserves separate treatment because it is likely to become a recurring interpretive trap.

Her desired future is not “Eldians should remain oppressed.” She wants liberation from the internment system. The proposed mechanism is proving that continental Eldians are morally distinct from the island devils.

This is an assimilation bargain built on three assumptions:

1. stigma is caused primarily by the bad behavior of another subgroup;
2. exemplary loyalty can prove the good subgroup deserves release;
3. the dominant state can be trusted to honor that proof once demonstrated.

Volume 23 supplies evidence for why the bargain is emotionally attractive and why it is structurally unstable.

It is attractive because Warrior service produces visible rewards: pride, status, family celebration, Honorary Marleyan recognition, military praise, and the belief that one's suffering serves a collective purpose.

It is unstable because Marley is the institution that defines the test, controls the reward, and materially benefits from the discrimination that makes the test necessary. The standard of “goodness” can therefore keep moving.

Nothing in V23 proves Gabi has already recognized that contradiction. Modeling her as if she has would erase the actual developmental boundary.

# 6. Falco as a procedural-humanitarian countermodel

Falco is not simply “the nice one.” V23 gives his gentleness structure.

He:

- reaches toward escape/freedom imagery before recovering battlefield context;
- grounds after concussion through relationships;
- disputes Gabi's assumptions without matching her performative confidence;
- runs into danger after her;
- aids an incapacitated enemy prisoner;
- accepts that the prisoner's anti-Eldian hatred does not eliminate his protected status;
- quickly understands the life-cost of Titan succession;
- infers Reiner's hidden protective motive;
- decides to compete for the Armor specifically to prevent Gabi from inheriting it.

His moral behavior is therefore procedural and relational. He does not require the enemy to like him before treating him as wounded rather than target. He does not reject the Warrior system in an abstract manifesto. He attempts to alter one concrete person's fate from inside it.

This is a stronger reconstruction basis than a generic kindness adjective.

# 7. Reiner: from role fracture to chronic integration failure

V23 is the largest Reiner expansion since the reveal-era volumes.

## 7.1 The soldier self remains socially real

The 104th anecdotes make it impossible to maintain a simple “Warrior true self / soldier false self” model. Adult Reiner remembers peers as individuated people and the visual form explicitly restores their ordinary social presence.

## 7.2 Trauma is triggered by material analogy

A wall on a foreign battlefield produces the thought 「また壁かよ」 and a 104th memory intrusion. Reiner's trauma does not require explicit mention of Paradis. Environmental structure can trigger it.

## 7.3 Loyalty and recognition coexist

He still serves Marley and protects Zeke in battle. Recognition of Paradis humanity has not automatically dissolved the mission.

## 7.4 Protection can use coercive language

With Falco, Reiner uses the terrifying consequences of disloyalty to force a correction, then pivots toward the private goal of saving Gabi from inheritance. His care is neither soft nor fully transparent.

## 7.5 He can imagine a future he does not know how to create himself

`この真っ暗な俺達の未来から` is a striking formulation. Reiner understands the Warrior future as dark, yet his solution is not direct rebellion. He entrusts Falco with a local intervention.

This suggests an adult Reiner model in which insight outruns perceived agency.

## 7.6 Childhood motive architecture

Young Reiner's desire to become a hero is attached to his father, mother, ethnic status, family unity, and a world-saving narrative. Later guilt should not retroactively erase the sincerity of those motives. The tragedy lies partly in the fact that they were sincere.

# 8. Zeke: public loyalty, hidden lineage, casual deflection

V23 adds several high-value constraints to the partial Zeke model.

He is able to speak deferentially and strategically before Marleyan command, presenting the renewed Paradis operation in terms the state understands: modernization time, strategic deterrence, inherited duty, and personal loyalty.

He also withholds a central fact about himself. Colt knows Zeke's Beast capabilities are unprecedented and asks why they differ from historical Beast holders despite Zeke supposedly lacking royal blood. Zeke does not disclose the lineage explanation available to the reader from the V21-V22 corpus.

His later bathroom joke demonstrates that humor can serve as secrecy management.

This expands the anti-caricature rule:

> Zeke's casualness may regulate affect, soften hierarchy, or deflect inquiry. It does not reliably indicate candor.

His stated desire to finish Grisha Jaeger's work is public evidence of motive, but V23 alone does not authorize treating it as exhaustive access to his private objective.

# 9. Technology and the end of Titan exceptionalism

Volumes 1-22 repeatedly expanded the Titan ontology. Volume 23 begins historicizing it.

The important question is no longer only “what can Titans do?” It is “how long will those capacities remain strategically dominant?”

Anti-Titan artillery can destroy shifters. Naval guns can devastate the Armored Titan. The armored train changes the battlefield until Gabi removes it. Officers anticipate air power moving war into a domain where land-based Titans may be structurally disadvantaged.

The Nine Titans remain extraordinary biological weapons, but they are no longer outside an arms race.

This is a major conceptual transition:

**Titan power moves from ontology toward military technology.**

Its metaphysical origin remains open, but its geopolitical value is now contingent on countermeasure development, doctrine, logistics, and technological timing.

# 10. Children, inherited missions, and the thirteen-year economy

The succession system converts childhood into a resource schedule.

A candidate does not merely inherit power. The candidate inherits:

- a fixed term;
- family expectations;
- political meaning;
- military obligation;
- predecessor memory fragments;
- the social status attached to becoming Honorary Marleyan;
- the expectation that shortened life is honorable.

Reiner's conversation with Falco makes the temporal violence explicit. Gabi's likely inheritance means a drastically shortened future. Falco's counterstrategy is to accept that burden himself.

The apparent choice is therefore already constrained by a system that makes **which child dies early** the field of agency.

That does not make their choices unreal. It makes them authored inside a coercively narrowed option set—the same agency distinction the V01-V19 checkpoint established elsewhere.

The strongest controls are:

- Gabi, Falco, Udo, and Zofia teasing one another in the trench;
- Gabi's pride and performative storytelling on the train;
- soldiers cheering and physically carrying her;

- Reiner's affectionate-negative descriptions of classmates;

It still does not justify unrestricted slice-of-life simulation for every Marley character. Gabi and Falco receive excellent first-volume social evidence, but only across a narrow temporal band organized around war and Warrior candidacy.

# 12. Japanese language and voice notes

## 12.1 Gabi: declarative self-authorship inside institutional ideology

Gabi's voice is fast, confident, comparative, and status-aware. She is comfortable saying she is the obvious successor and converting collective stakes into personal responsibility.

High-value anchors include:

- `私達を苦しめる あの島の悪魔共を皆殺しにする覚悟だ`
- `私は負けない`
- `私が収容区からみんなを解放する`

The repeated first-person structure matters. Indoctrinated content is being spoken through active self-authorship.

Her ordinary register is more playful and competitive: she mocks Falco, performs stories, enjoys praise, and rapidly switches from battlefield confidence to adolescent indignation.

## 12.2 Falco: direct concern, lower theatricality

Falco's written voice is comparatively plain and relational. He challenges Gabi without her grand public rhetoric and asks Reiner the dangerous question: is he actually okay with Gabi receiving the Armor?

Under threat from Reiner's formal warning, Falco can instantly reproduce institutional loyalty language. That is evidence of learned survival register, not proof that the formal speech is his preferred ordinary voice.

## 12.3 Reiner: institutional menace -> private entrustment

The Falco exchange demonstrates sharp code-switching.

Reiner first uses bureaucratic consequence language: report, loss of succession rights, family punishment, conversion into Titan weapons. Once Falco demonstrates understanding, Reiner compresses the private instruction:

- `お前が「鎧の巨人」を継承したいのか？`
- `ガビを守りたいなら お前がガビを超えるしかない`
- `お前がガビを救い出すんだ`

Adult Reiner's care is often directive rather than soothing.

## 12.4 Zeke: deference, strategic framing, vulgar deflection

Before command, Zeke can speak with controlled deference. With Colt, he is much more conversational. The joke about an unusual way of wiping himself demonstrates deliberate register shift to terminate inquiry.

## 12.5 Porco: rivalry without politeness padding

Porco's Reiner register is openly contemptuous and comparative. Even gratitude is rejected through role logic: he saved Marley's Armor, not Reiner. His voice repeatedly invokes what *should* have happened if he had been chosen instead.

## 12.6 Pieck: low-energy familiarity

Pieck's ordinary dialogue is relaxed and lightly teasing. The two-month quadrupedal habit is described without melodrama. This supports a low-friction familiar register but remains a small sample.

# 13. Relationship-conditioned analysis

## 13.1 Reiner -> Falco: rescue through succession competition

Reiner cannot simply tell Falco to reject the Warrior system. The social environment makes such speech dangerous and his own position remains inside the system. He instead creates a survivable instruction: compete harder, inherit the Armor, and thereby prevent Gabi from inheriting it.

This is protection through **redirected institutional ambition**.

## 13.2 Falco -> Gabi: affection expressed as substitution

Falco's goal is not to persuade Gabi through abstract debate. He intends to take the cost onto himself. This resembles other AoT relationships where protection first appears as bodily substitution, but V23 gives it a distinctive succession form.

## 13.3 Reiner -> Gabi: identification without simple projection

Reiner recognizes that Gabi is on the track he once occupied. He does not respond by giving her an autobiographical confession. He works indirectly through Falco. This suggests that shame/role constraint blocks transparent mentoring.

## 13.4 Reiner -> 104th: human memory survives ideological return

The family-dinner anecdotes are relational evidence, not merely nostalgia. Reiner's memory preserves distinct people, habits, irritations, and affection. That content survives even when the surrounding discourse expects the language of devils.

## 13.5 Reiner <-> Porco: inherited sibling rivalry

Porco's hostility is partly professional and partly inherited through Marcel. Titan memory adds another layer: Porco can receive fragments of a previous holder's life while remaining unable to access every memory he wants.

## 13.6 Zeke -> Colt: mentor/successor relation with asymmetrical disclosure

Zeke can be friendly and even confessional in tone while withholding the information that most explains Colt's future inheritance problem. Familiarity does not equal full informational equality.

Reiner's individualized memory of the 104th survives inside continued Warrior participation.

Young Reiner's family longing and Gabi's collective rescue desire become Warrior motivation through the same institutional channel.

# 16. Visual and formal analysis

## 16.1 Opening bird / closing enclosed child

Falco begins by looking toward a creature able to leave the battlefield vertically. The volume ends inside Reiner's childhood enclosure. Flight and wall are therefore paired as competing inheritance images.

## 16.2 Children against industrial scale

Gabi's small body is repeatedly framed against trenches, train armor, fortress walls, and artillery. Her courage is real; the composition simultaneously exposes the absurdity of a state outsourcing strategic salvation to a child.

The parachuted Eldians invert conventional airborne troops. They are dropped not to fight as people but to become Titans. Human verticality becomes ammunition.

## 16.4 Reiner and the repeated wall

The fortress wall visually triggers the earlier Paradis wall-world. The same geometric form carries different political meanings but the same trauma load for Reiner.

## 16.5 The train as transitional architecture

Chapter 93 uses the train to move from war to home while refusing a clean separation between them. The compartment contains military celebration, succession anxiety, coercive ideology, jokes, and Reiner's attempt to save Gabi.

## 16.6 Liberio walls

The title 「壁の中の少年」 makes the internment district formally comparable to Paradis without asserting political equivalence. Both generate people whose world models are shaped by enclosure.

## 16.7 Family dinner / 104th montage

The manga undermines Reiner's demon rhetoric through visual contradiction. His words claim depravity; the panels return ordinary cadet memories. Form adjudicates the unreliability of the categorical label without requiring Reiner to denounce it aloud.

# 20. Volume 23 synthesis

Volume 23 is the first volume in which the world beyond the sea becomes socially inhabitable rather than merely historically knowable.

Its deepest accomplishment is to make several propositions true at once:

- Marley is an imperial state structurally exploiting Eldians;
- Eldians inside Marley can sincerely identify with Marley;
- Warrior children possess real agency even when the option set is coercively constructed;
- anti-Eldian prejudice extends beyond one government;
- Titan power remains terrifying while becoming militarily obsolete;
- Reiner's loyalty remains operational while his recognition of Paradis humanity remains psychologically real;
- ordinary affection and humor can reproduce militarization just as effectively as overt terror;
- a person can call an enclosed district home without the enclosure ceasing to be coercive.

The most important first-half claim strengthened here is therefore not merely “the enemy is human.” Volume 19 had already made that insufficient. Volume 23 goes further:

> **The enemy is institutional, relational, developmental, and internally divided.**

The category “Marley” must now be decomposed into state strategy, military doctrine, racial hierarchy, Warrior institutions, family incentives, individual belief, and ordinary social life.

Reiner is the volume's bridge because he contains multiple layers simultaneously. The child who wanted to save the world, the Warrior who broke the Wall, the soldier who protected Connie, the divided man who could not integrate Marco's death, the adult who shields Zeke, the cousin celebrated at home, and the mentor who tells Falco to save Gabi are not mutually exclusive answers to “who Reiner really is.” They are historically real states connected by memory, relationship, institution, trauma, and choice.

The final tragedy is temporal. Marley already knows its Titan century is ending. Gabi does not. Young Reiner did not. The adults continue asking children to inherit futures whose strategic logic is already failing.

That is the strongest V23 revision to the series' inheritance problem:

> **People can be compelled to inherit not only someone else's burden, but someone else's obsolete solution.**
