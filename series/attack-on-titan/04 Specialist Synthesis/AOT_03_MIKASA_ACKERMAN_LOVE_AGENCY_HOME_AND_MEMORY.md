# 『進撃の巨人』 / *Attack on Titan*
## Mikasa Ackerman: Love, Agency, Home, and Memory — V01–V34
### From rescued survival and portable home to self-authored attachment, lethal refusal, grief, and continued life

## 0. Function, authority, and anti-caricature boundary

This document is the canonical specialist home, once promoted from provisional publication state, for Mikasa Ackerman's full-series literary analysis across the complete Japanese manga boundary. Its governing question is the one fixed by the post-V34 synthesis architecture:

> **How does Mikasa's attachment to Eren function as love, home, memory, obligation, autonomy problem, and eventually a basis for opposition without requiring emotional renunciation?**

The question cannot be answered by deciding whether Mikasa is “dependent” or “independent,” whether her bond is “romantic” or “familial,” or whether the final act proves that she has “moved on.” Those binaries are too coarse for the evidence. Mikasa's relation to Eren originates in a real rescue after the murder of her parents, a violent survival awakening, an invitation into a new household, and the material warmth of a scarf. It becomes a reason to live after apparent bereavement, a source of tactical protectiveness, a locus of anxiety about losing home again, a relationship Eren sometimes resists as constraining, a target of biological reduction in the V28 table scene, an object of political imitation through Louise, a symbol whose custody Mikasa takes back, a relationship she morally opposes without ceasing to value, and finally a love she retains while killing Eren when catastrophic threat makes nonlethal recovery impossible.

The governing retrospective claim authority is `AOT_FULL_SERIES_CLAIM_REVISION_LEDGER.md`. Its primary Mikasa claim, `AOT-FSCR-C019`, is **REVISE**: what begins as survival-sustaining but potentially overconcentrated attachment matures into self-authored continuity. The complete formulation is that Mikasa's attachment is love, home, memory, gratitude, grief, and chosen continuity—not proof of biological compulsion, moral agreement, or Eren's jurisdiction over her. `AOT-FSCR-M002` is **STRENGTHEN**: the terminal manga supplies the strongest possible confirmation that love and lethal opposition can coexist when the loved person is the catastrophic active threat.

This specialist therefore rejects two opposite caricatures.

The first caricature treats Mikasa as little more than “Eren obsession”: a person whose agency is swallowed by one attachment. That reading cannot explain her tactical judgment, protection of Armin, restraint of Kaya to save Gabi, moral rebuke of Eren in Liberio, distinction between private attachment and Louise's political imitation, self-interpretive resistance at the table, scarf reclamation against Eren's directive, coalition participation, or final lethal action.

The second caricature treats the ending as a liberation narrative in which mature agency requires the revelation that prior attachment was false, pathological, merely biological, or finally discarded. That reading fails even more directly. Mikasa does not stop loving Eren before opposing him. She does not accept Eren's command to discard the scarf and forget him. She explicitly refuses it, rewraps the scarf, kills him, mourns him, buries him, remembers him years later, and is shown continuing a long life in the tankōbon epilogue. Her freedom is not emotional amputation.

The mature question is therefore not whether relation and agency are mutually exclusive. It is **who has authority to define what a relation means and what it obligates a person to do**.

### 0.2 Evidence rule: attachment variables are not synonyms

This synthesis keeps several variables separate:

- **attachment** — the enduring emotional salience of a person;
- **love** — the series' own eventual language for Mikasa's relation, reinforced by Zeke's interpretation but not reducible to it;
- **home** — a place, household, person, object, or remembered relational condition experienced as somewhere to return;
- **protection** — behavior intended to preserve another person's life or safety;
- **obedience** — compliance with another person's instruction or will;
- **agreement** — shared moral or political judgment;
- **trust** — confidence in another person's intentions or decision process;
- **symbolic custody** — authority over the meaning and fate of an object such as the scarf;
- **grief** — continued relation to loss after the other person is gone;
- **memory** — retained or experienced relational continuity, including ordinary recollection and the terminal cabin/memory problem;
- **jurisdiction** — claimed authority to decide what another person's attachment, body, future, or obligation must mean.

The series repeatedly demonstrates that these variables can diverge. Mikasa can protect Eren while condemning his actions. She can love him while rejecting his self-interpretation of her. She can retain the scarf while not wearing it. She can want to bring him back while joining the force trying to stop him. She can kill him while continuing to love him. Treating one variable as a proxy for all the others is the main source of bad Mikasa readings.

# I. Mature formulation

## 1. Mikasa's arc is from relation-created survival to self-authored relation, not from attachment to detachment

The strongest full-series formulation is:

> **Mikasa's attachment to Eren begins when rescue, violent self-activation, warmth, adoption, and renewed home are compressed into the same traumatic event. Because repeated catastrophe destroys her households, the relation becomes unusually concentrated as both survival anchor and portable home. Across the series, however, that concentration does not remain an undifferentiated command. Mikasa increasingly differentiates love from protection, protection from agreement, memory from obedience, symbolic continuity from political alignment, and home from Eren's right to define her future. Her mature agency consists not in ceasing to love him but in owning the meaning and behavioral consequences of that love herself: she can condemn him, leave the scarf, reclaim it, refuse his interpretation of her mind, join opposition to the Rumbling, kill him when the terminal threat leaves no recoverable alternative, mourn him, remember him, and still continue living beyond him.**

This formulation preserves the danger in the early bond without pathologizing the whole relationship. V02 makes the risk explicit. Mikasa has lost her parents to human traffickers and is treated as merchandise. Eren's intervention is genuinely life-saving and simultaneously violent. He does not merely comfort her: he commands `戦え`, supplying a survival rule at the exact point when she is frozen. After the rescue, she says she has nowhere to return. Eren wraps the scarf around her; Grisha offers the Jaeger household; Eren tells her to return to `オレ達の家`. The same event therefore creates bodily agency, a new family, a home, and a lifelong symbol.

That fusion explains both the bond's strength and its vulnerability. If one person is linked to survival, warmth, family, and the restoration of a destroyed world, losing that person can feel like losing the future itself. V02's present-day grief makes this explicit when Eren's apparent death briefly collapses Mikasa's will to live. She returns to life partly because dying would erase even the possibility of remembering him. Attachment is thus already doing two things: preserving her and dangerously concentrating meaning.

The subsequent series does not solve this by declaring relation itself immature. It progressively pluralizes agency. Mikasa learns and demonstrates that the person who gave her a home is not therefore entitled to define her self-knowledge; that the person she protects can act wrongly; that protecting someone does not mean accepting their political program; that a scarf can remain hers even if its giver orders it discarded; that love can survive moral opposition; and that a loved person's life can cease to be the overriding action objective when preserving that life would permit catastrophic destruction of everyone else.

The ending therefore completes rather than cancels V02. The child who once said she had nowhere to return receives a scarf and a household. The adult who later says `私達の家に…帰りたい` encounters a private home-horizon that cannot be reconciled with abandoned friends and an invaded island. When Eren asks her to discard the scarf, forget him, and become free, Mikasa's answer is `できない`. She takes custody of memory and symbol precisely before doing what Eren does not want to survive: she kills him. The relation remains; jurisdiction does not.

## 2. Love is neither acquittal nor a veto on action

Mikasa is a particularly strong demonstration of one of the series' mature relationship rules: **recognition and attachment do not pre-decide the violence threshold**.

That rule emerges long before V34. Against Annie, Reiner, and Bertolt, Mikasa can recognize former comrades and still act lethally when the tactical threat becomes active. The Female Titan experience changes her timing: by V10 she attacks Reiner and Bertolt before transformation can complete. V19 makes her state-modeling equally important; after Bertolt survives her close-range assault, she tells Armin that he seemed like a different person. Lethal preemption does not prevent interpersonal model revision.

The same architecture extends inward. At V25 Mikasa protects and assists Eren during Liberio, asks him to return, and tells him directly that he killed civilians and children and that the harm cannot be undone (`AOT_V25_E020`, `AOT_V25_E024`). No single word such as “loyalty” captures this state. Her attachment increases protection and return-seeking while her moral judgment remains active.

By V33 Annie asks the question that has been latent since V25: if saving humanity requires killing Eren, what will Mikasa do? Mikasa answers `殺さない` and says she will bring him back. That is genuine current intention, not dramatic camouflage. She still hopes the conflict can be solved without killing him. Yet V34 changes the state. The Rumbling remains an active catastrophic threat, the source organism has transformed comrades, Eren has reappeared in colossal form, and nonlethal recovery has failed. Mikasa's terminal action updates the rule under a new threshold. She does not first erase love. She wraps the scarf, enters his mouth, decapitates him, kisses him, and later buries and remembers him.

The strongest conclusion is therefore not “love made her kill him,” nor “she overcame love in order to kill him.” Both are too simple. **Love remains one variable; catastrophic-threat judgment becomes another.** Mature agency is the capacity to let neither variable falsely impersonate the other.

## 3. Home is Mikasa's most durable positive horizon

Eren's specialist is organized by freedom and obstruction. Mikasa's is better organized by **home and return**.

Home repeatedly changes ontological form:

1. the first Ackerman household is destroyed by traffickers;
2. the Jaeger household is offered as a replacement family;
3. Shiganshina is destroyed by Titans;
4. the scarf becomes a portable material form of warmth and relational continuity;
5. Eren himself becomes overconcentrated as the person through whom “home” is imagined;
6. V12 converts the scarf into reciprocal acknowledgment of past care and a promised future repetition;
7. V31 `家族` names the relationship in the language of family during the Marley memory;
8. V34 `帰りたい / 私達の家に` turns the cabin into a private return-horizon;
9. after Eren's death, the hill tree becomes memorial home rather than shared domestic future;
10. the tankōbon epilogue shows life continuing with family, aging, visitation, death, and memory rather than forcing a choice between remembrance and later continuity.

This is why “moving on” is a poor final descriptor. Mikasa does move through time. The epilogue makes that unavoidable. But the manga does not frame maturation as replacing one authentic home with another and declaring the earlier one invalid. Home accumulates. It can be lost, carried, revisited, transformed into memorial custody, and expanded by later life.

# II. Longitudinal development I — rescue, grief, and protective identity (V01–V12)

## 4. V01: home is present before its origin is disclosed

Volume 1 introduces Mikasa after the decisive event that formed her bond but withholds that event from the reader. This narrative ordering matters. We first encounter a quiet, highly capable companion who reads danger better than Eren, attempts to regulate his recklessness, and participates in the Jaeger household as though it is already her home. The story therefore does not initially explain her through trauma. It lets the attachment exist as an ordinary fact of the trio before providing its origin.

This creates an important interpretive control. Later knowledge of the trafficking rescue must not retroactively convert every V01 interaction into post-traumatic symptom. Mikasa is already capable of situational judgment. She recognizes that Eren's impulsiveness can be dangerous. She is protective, but she is not simply passive to his will; protection often takes the form of trying to stop him from doing what he wants.

The Eren/Mikasa/Armin triad also begins as a structure of unequal reciprocal contribution rather than one-sided dependency. Armin supplies the imagined horizon beyond the Walls. Eren supplies intervention and forward motion. Mikasa supplies practical survival and bodily competence. The trio is not balanced in every scene, but its basic architecture matters for later loss: Mikasa's attachment is embedded in a social world rather than existing outside one.

The opening dream gives Mikasa unusual prospective importance before the series can explain why. Its exact causal status remains open until the terminal memory/Paths material and should not be backsolved into a simple prophecy mechanism. For Mikasa's specialist, the safe conclusion is narrower: from the first volume, Mikasa is tied to Eren's memory architecture as well as his ordinary waking life. This becomes thematically important when V34 returns to long-dream language and a farewell request. The mechanism belongs elsewhere.

Carla's death then destroys the household that had already become Mikasa's second home. The composition forces Eren and Mikasa to witness from a distance they cannot cross. Mikasa's later protective intensity should therefore be understood against repeated home loss, not merely romantic attachment. By the end of V01, the person she most closely associates with the surviving Jaeger household appears to die as well. V02 asks what remains when relation seems to have been stripped away again.

## 5. V02: `戦え`, bodily awakening, the scarf, and the reconstruction of home

V02 is the foundational Mikasa volume because it refuses to separate empowerment from trauma, or rescue from dependency risk.

### 5.1 Human violence precedes the exceptional body

The kidnapping flashback begins with human beings treating Mikasa's family and body instrumentally. Her parents are murdered for profit; Mikasa is valuable because of ancestry and sex. Before the story explains Titans, royal memory, Ackerman biology, or Paths, it establishes a basic personal fact: Mikasa's first great catastrophe is a human attempt to turn a person into property.

This makes the later autonomy question morally concrete. Mikasa's life repeatedly attracts external definitions. Traffickers define her as merchandise. State institutions define exceptional bodies strategically. Eren later defines her attachment as inherited servitude. Louise defines her as an ideological model. The mature arc can be read as repeated refusal of external ownership claims, but that pattern begins before Mikasa has language for it.

### 5.2 `戦え` gives action at the price of fusing survival with violence

Eren's intervention is genuinely rescuing. He kills two traffickers and, when the third attacks, commands the frozen Mikasa to fight. The Japanese sequence is blunt: `戦え!!`, `戦うんだよ!!`, `勝てなきゃ…死ぬ…`, `勝てば生きる…`, `戦わなければ勝てない…`. The command is not a philosophical lecture. It is an emergency action grammar.

Mikasa's narration then describes an altered bodily state. She recognizes the world's repeated predation, names its cruelty, experiences her trembling stop, and says that from that moment she could control her body completely. The event supplies the manga's first strong evidence for the later “awakening” concept. Yet nothing in V02 establishes the V28 host-compulsion story. What it establishes is more specific: under mortal threat, Mikasa's body and perception reorganize around a survival action enabled by Eren's command and her own execution of it (`AOT_V02_E001-E002`).

Calling this “empowerment” is incomplete because the mechanism is born inside murder, fear, and the loss of childhood safety. Calling it “brainwashing” is equally unsupported because Mikasa does not become a behavioral puppet. She makes the killing action her own. The better description is **relationally triggered self-activation under trauma**.

### 5.3 The scarf changes the rescue from survival to home

The most important primary-source sequence comes after the violence. Grisha asks Mikasa where she wants to go. Her answer is not a preference among destinations. It is an ontological loss: `私にはもう…帰る所がない…`—she no longer has anywhere to return.

Eren answers first through an object. Seeing that she is cold, he wraps his scarf around her. The page gives warmth before it gives explanation. Grisha then invites her to live with them: `ミカサ 私達の家で一緒に暮らそう`. Eren follows with the decisive return language: `早く帰ろうぜ / オレ達の家に`. Mikasa answers `…うん / 帰る…`.

The sequence is structurally dense:

`no place to return -> warmth -> offered household -> shared-home language -> consent to return`.

The scarf therefore cannot be reduced to a romantic token without losing its origin. It is the material hinge by which a rescued but homeless child enters another family. It is warmth, adoption, survival after objectification, and the first portable reminder that someone treated her as a person who should have a home.

This does not make later romantic love impossible; it explains why the symbol carries more than romance. Eren is tied to family, rescue, bodily activation, and home from the beginning.

### 5.4 Present-day grief reveals the concentration risk

After Eren's apparent death, Mikasa initially responds by suppressing grief and demanding impossible performance from herself and the others. When gas exhaustion removes action, collapse follows. Her will to live returns through remembered relation. She decides she cannot die because then even her memory of Eren would disappear (`AOT_V02_E003`).

The scene is life-affirming and diagnostically dangerous at once. It proves that relation can sustain survival when autonomous motivation fails. It also shows how tightly Mikasa's imagined future has become concentrated around one person.

The full series will not cure this by teaching her that needing others is wrong. Instead, it will differentiate the bond's functions. Memory can remain valuable without dictating obedience. Home can be carried without requiring political agreement. Love can survive even when the loved person becomes someone she must stop.

## 6. V03–V04: protection as political opposition and Eren's autonomy friction

Eren's return in a Titan body makes Mikasa's attachment politically legible. To military authorities, he is an unknown strategic organism; to Mikasa, he remains the person she knows. When soldiers threaten him, she is prepared to oppose them. This is early evidence that relationship can outrank institutional classification under immediate threat.

But V03 also supplies a corrective. Eren's Titan body strikes Mikasa (`AOT_V03_E007`). The incident is not authored as an intentional betrayal, yet it demonstrates that personal continuity and protective intention do not guarantee bodily control. Mikasa cannot simply substitute “I know Eren” for every present-state hazard model. The series repeatedly returns to this epistemic lesson: attachment carries information, but it does not make one omniscient about a changing person.

V04 makes the relational tension more ordinary. Eren increasingly experiences Mikasa's protection as an affront to his competence. Mikasa experiences proximity as security after repeated home loss. Neither is simply wrong. Eren's growth requires room to act without being perpetually treated as someone who must be managed. Mikasa's protection is not arbitrary domination; it is an adaptation to a history in which home repeatedly disappears without warning.

This is an early version of the later autonomy problem: **care can preserve a person and still become controlling if it never updates to their changing agency**. Mikasa's arc must therefore include not only resistance to Eren's control over her but revision of her own protective control over him.

## 7. V07–V08: vengeance can obscure the operational objective

The Female Titan arc gives Mikasa an important correction from Levi. Her emotional objective is punishment; the mission objective is Eren's rescue. Levi tells her `作戦の本質を見失うな` (`AOT_V07_E007`). This is not a lesson that emotion is irrelevant. It is a lesson that attachment can distort action selection if anger substitutes for the actual goal.

Mikasa learns through failure. The Annie experience is one reason her later preemption becomes faster. She does not emerge less attached; she becomes more tactically disciplined about what protecting someone requires. V10's attack on Reiner and Bertolt before they can transform is the clearest later result.

V08's broader relationship evidence also matters by contrast. Annie's father anchors Annie to a promised return. The series is already showing several characters whose violence is organized by nonfungible people and homes. Mikasa is not uniquely “irrational” because she has a person she wants back. The analytical question is how each character translates attachment into permission, restraint, and objective selection.

## 8. V10–V12: preemption, loss, gratitude, and the reciprocal scarf promise

### 8.1 V10: protection becomes anticipatory rather than merely reactive

When Reiner and Bertolt reveal themselves, Mikasa attacks immediately before transformation (`AOT_V10_E008`). The behavior is important because it shows learning. Annie taught her that hesitation around a recognized comrade can lose the tactical window. Mikasa's later relationship-threat model therefore becomes conditional rather than sentimental: former intimacy can coexist with lethal preemption when present action establishes imminent threat.

This is not evidence that Mikasa is indiscriminately violent. Her threshold is state-dependent. Recognition remains; operational permission changes.

### 8.2 V11–V12: the fear of another absolute loss

The kidnapping of Eren by the Warriors recreates Mikasa's oldest pattern: home is disappearing again. Her protectiveness, anger, and desperation intensify. Yet the terminal V12 scene makes a crucial shift away from control.

Faced with apparent death, Mikasa does not issue an order. She thanks Eren. The primary Japanese pages distribute the gratitude across several concrete acts: `私と…一緒にいてくれて ありがとう`, `私に…生き方を教えてくれて ありがとう`, and finally `マフラーを巻いてくれて ありがとう…` (`AOT_V12_E007`).

The shift from demand to gratitude matters. Mikasa momentarily releases the impossible task of ensuring Eren survives and names what the relationship has already given her. She recognizes companionship, a way of living, and the scarf without turning those gifts into a claim that Eren owes her eternal proximity.

Eren's response is equally important: `そんなもん 何度でも巻いてやる`. He does not answer with abstract romance. He promises repeatable care. Then he turns back toward the threat.

The scarf's meaning changes here. In V02 it is an offered home after catastrophe. In V12 it becomes mutually acknowledged relationship history and an imagined future of repeated ordinary care. The promise does not give Eren ownership of the symbol. The later tragedy partly depends on that distinction: V31 and V34 will show Eren trying to dictate the scarf's fate, and Mikasa refusing.

# III. Longitudinal development II — ancestry, plural agency, and moral differentiation (V13–V27)

## 9. V13–V18: Mikasa is not reducible to the romance axis

The Uprising period gives Mikasa less interior exposition than Historia or Eren, but it expands the conditions under which her attachment should be modeled. She operates inside a group that must fight human opponents, accept politically compromised methods, and distinguish personal loyalty from larger institutional objectives.

The most important Mikasa-specific development is V16's Ackerman material. Levi asks whether she has ever experienced a moment when a power seemed suddenly to awaken. Mikasa answers that she has. Levi says the same happened to him and to Kenny. This validates an inherited or family-linked exceptional bodily phenomenon. At the same time, Mikasa recounts that her father's Ackerman family was persecuted and her mother's Eastern lineage was displaced; later family testimony explains that Ackerman and Eastern minorities resisted royal memory alteration (`AOT_V16_E002`).

Two interpretive constraints follow.

First, biology is real in Mikasa's story. A serious autonomy reading cannot simply declare every inherited bodily factor irrelevant. The manga establishes exceptional capacity, shared awakening experience, bloodline history, and resistance to the royal memory regime.

Second, V16 does **not** establish a host-command mechanism. The evidence concerns memory immunity/persecution and sudden physical awakening. The later claim that Ackermans are compelled to protect a “host” arrives through Eren's V28 report of what he says Zeke told him. Treating the V16 material as if it already proves servitude is retrospective contamination.

The Uprising also protects another point: Mikasa exists inside a network of strong non-Eren relationships and group obligations. Her later V21 protection of Armin will make this unmistakable. The romance axis is central but not exhaustive.

## 10. V19–V22: relationship knowledge is useful but must update

Return to Shiganshina gives Mikasa repeated evidence that long acquaintance does not freeze people in place. Her close-range attack on Bertolt fails because his combat state has changed. She and Armin independently observe that he seems like a different person (`AOT_V19_E022-E023`). This is exactly the kind of model update that becomes relevant to Eren later: a person may remain historically continuous while becoming behaviorally different enough that an old relationship model must be revised.

V20's strike on Reiner shows the same principle under coordinated tactics. Mikasa can address him as a recognizable person and still commit to lethal/disabling action once the operational threat is active. Human recognition and threat response are separate variables.

V21 then demonstrates that Mikasa's strongest protective attachment is not exclusively Eren-directed. The serum conflict around Armin causes her to escalate against Levi because Armin can still be saved. Hange de-escalates her not by dismissing grief but by sharing it: the authority figure acknowledges unbearable loss while physically restraining her. The scene expands Mikasa's relationship architecture in three ways.

1. Her highest-intensity protection can attach to Armin.
2. She can be de-escalated by another trusted person through relational recognition rather than command alone.
3. She can ultimately survive a decision that does not satisfy her protective objective.

This matters for the final arc because it shows that Mikasa's action system already contains plural nonfungible people. Eren's importance is exceptional, but he is not the only person whose loss matters.

## 11. V25–V26: protection without approval

Liberio is one of the clearest mid-series refutations of “Mikasa follows Eren because she loves him.” She arrives as part of the rescue/attack force, protects him in battle, and remains responsive to his tactical direction. Yet she immediately confronts him over civilian and child deaths. The evidence record deliberately codes the same encounter as relational and moral: `AOT_V25_E020` for return-seeking and `AOT_V25_E024` for condemnation.

Her behavior can be represented as:

`protect current person -> seek return -> condemn irreversible act -> continue survival operation`.

No step cancels the others. If protection were obedience, the rebuke would be anomalous. If condemnation erased attachment, the protection would be anomalous. The correct model permits coexisting variables.

V26 extends the divergence. Mikasa and Armin still participate in extracting Eren from Liberio while increasingly lacking confidence in his current decision process. Historical intimacy no longer guarantees strategic alignment. This is the condition that will become explicit in V28.

## 12. V27: private attachment versus political imitation

Louise is structurally important because she shows what Mikasa's image looks like when extracted from Mikasa herself. Louise remembers being saved by Mikasa and has built part of her political identity around that image (`AOT_V27_E020-E021`). She seeks recognition and ideological affinity.

Mikasa does not reciprocate the intimacy. Her response is cool and bounded. The scene refuses a simple “hero inspires follower” narrative. Louise sees in Mikasa a model of decisive pro-Eren strength; Mikasa experiences her own relation to Eren as private history, not a public ideological office.

This distinction becomes crucial after Eren's political elevation. Other people can use Mikasa, Eren, the Survey Corps, or `心臓を捧げよ` as symbols. The symbol does not own the person. Mikasa's refusal of Louise's projected intimacy anticipates her later refusal of Eren's projected biological interpretation.

The V27 checkpoint therefore correctly revised attachment into a **relationship-conditioned system**. Mikasa can remain deeply attached while maintaining boundaries with admirers, protecting Armin, judging civilian harm, and seeking information about a changing Eren rather than treating shared history as a complete current model.

# IV. Longitudinal development III — interpretive jurisdiction, distance, and self-authored custody (V28–V31)

## 13. V28: the table scene attacks Mikasa's right to know what her own love means

The V28 table scene is the most important autonomy crisis in Mikasa's arc before the ending because Eren does more than insult her. He offers a causal theory that, if true in its strongest form, would strip her relationship history of authorship.

The structure is coercive from the beginning. Eren's bleeding hand makes transformation threat materially present (`AOT_V28_E015`). He asserts `オレは自由だ` and frames himself as acting through free will (`AOT_V28_E016`). He then attacks the autonomy of both friends: Armin is allegedly controlled by Bertolt's memories; Mikasa is allegedly driven by Ackerman biology.

### 13.1 Eren's claim has a source status, not automatic narrator status

The evidence ledger correctly separates **Eren's utterance** from the **truth of the ontology**. Eren reports a Zeke-derived Ackerman theory: an Ackerman who recognizes a “host” may experience compulsion, headaches, and protective behavior; Mikasa's true self supposedly disappeared when Eren ordered her to fight, leaving a slave-like attachment (`AOT_V28_E019-E021`).

At V28 this claim cannot simply be dismissed. The manga has already established Ackerman awakening, exceptional bodily capacity, and hereditary difference. During the confrontation Mikasa reflexively restrains Armin when he lunges at Eren (`AOT_V28_E022`). The action looks automatic enough to complicate her own self-account.

But the scene supplies contrary evidence at the same time. Mikasa says the rescue mattered because **it was Eren**: `エレンだから`. Her first-person account is relationship-specific rather than host-mechanical (`AOT_V28_E020`). She does not deny that her body sometimes acts before reflection. She denies Eren's authority to interpret the entire bond as slavery.

The epistemically disciplined V28 position is therefore **OPEN mechanism, high-confidence autonomy injury**. Whatever the biology ultimately proves to be, Eren has claimed the right to tell Mikasa that her memories, gratitude, care, and love do not mean what she thinks they mean.

### 13.2 The deepest violence is interpretive

`ずっと嫌いだった` is cruel because it attacks history. If taken literally, Eren recodes years of ordinary and catastrophic relation as a mistake Mikasa was biologically unable to recognize. The harm is not only rejection. It is an attempted retrospective expropriation of meaning.

This is why later scarf custody matters so much. Mikasa's mature agency must involve more than deciding whether to stay physically near Eren. It requires restoring authority over the interpretation of her own past.

### 13.3 Gabi provides an important same-volume counterexample

Earlier in V28, Mikasa protects Gabi after Kaya attacks her. Gabi is the child who killed Sasha. Mikasa does not need to deliver a forgiveness doctrine to interrupt retaliatory violence (`AOT_V28_E010-E011`). This is useful against a narrow Eren-centered model: Mikasa's moral agency remains active toward a former enemy child even while her most important relationship is destabilizing.

The behavior also separates protection from intimacy. Mikasa saves Gabi without implying affection, absolution, or loss of grief. The same separation later allows her to protect the world from Eren without making that protection evidence that she has stopped loving him.

## 14. V29: leaving the scarf is behavioral distance, not final renunciation

After the table scene, Mikasa asks why Eren would deliberately hurt them if his account is false. She does not immediately resolve the contradiction. That uncertainty matters. A weaker narrative might give her an instant speech proving exactly what the bond “really” is. *Attack on Titan* instead allows cognitive and bodily history to remain unsettled.

Mikasa then leaves the scarf behind (`AOT_V29_E040`, `AOT_V29_E042`). At the V29 publication boundary, the correct interpretation is **distance with long-term meaning open**. The act matters because the scarf is usually carried. Removing it creates a visible break. But the manga does not authorize reading the act as “Mikasa has rejected Eren” or “the symbol was false all along.”

The later evidence confirms why the conservative reading was necessary. V31 shows that leaving the scarf was not surrendering it. V33 shows that she can retain it without wearing it. V34 shows that she can rewrap it immediately before killing Eren. The symbol's state is therefore not a binary proxy for love.

A more accurate state variable is **mode of relation to the symbol**:

`worn as continuous home -> left during interpretive rupture -> held by another -> reclaimed into Mikasa's custody -> retained but not worn during unresolved opposition -> rewrapped at terminal self-authored decision -> maintained in grief and memory`.

The movement is not from attachment to detachment. It is from inherited habit toward deliberate symbolic use.

## 15. V31: `家族`, counterfactual self-blame, and the reclamation of interpretive custody

V31 supplies two seemingly contradictory Eren/Mikasa states and makes both canonical.

### 15.1 `オレは…お前の何だ?` asks Mikasa to define the relation

In the Marley memory, Eren privately asks why Mikasa cares for him. Is it because he saved her as a child? Because he is family? Finally: `オレは…お前の何だ?` Mikasa blushes and answers `あなたは…家族…`.

This scene matters because it is the opposite direction of the V28 table scene. Here Eren solicits Mikasa's own interpretation. Later he tells her what the relationship “really” is. The two states cannot be flattened into a single transparent motive. The corpus must preserve the contradiction.

`家族` should not be treated as an anti-romantic verdict. Japanese family language here names the structure through which Mikasa already understands Eren: rescue, household, shared childhood, lost parents, Shiganshina, and intimate continuity. The blushing and the later Eren conversation permit romantic dimensions, but “family” is not a euphemistic error that later love language replaces. The relationship is structurally both familial-home and romantic attachment.

### 15.2 Mikasa's counterfactual reveals responsibility inflation

V31 also gives Mikasa a retrospective counterfactual: if she had answered differently, might something have changed? This is emotionally important but not causal proof. The full series strongly warns against turning survivor counterfactuals into omnipotence. Eren's later decisions involve future memory, Paradis strategy, Historia, Zeke, Ymir, intrinsic desire, and the Rumbling plan. Mikasa's one answer does not “cause the genocide.”

The scene instead reveals a familiar Mikasa risk: when relationship catastrophe occurs, she can overassign responsibility to the point where a different personal intervention appears capable of controlling an event far larger than her actual causal role.

This parallels, without equating, Eren's post-Levi-squad self-blame. Both characters sometimes imagine that a better personal choice could have controlled a structurally overdetermined disaster. The specialist should preserve the affect while rejecting the causal inflation.

### 15.3 Louise and the scarf: Mikasa takes the symbol back from both follower and giver

Louise's final interaction creates the strongest pre-ending symbolic-autonomy scene. She reports that Eren wanted the scarf thrown away. Mikasa asks for it back (`AOT_V31_E028`).

The action has three layers.

First, Louise has been treating Mikasa's image as a political model. Possessing the scarf risks turning Mikasa's private relational object into part of that derivative identification.

Second, Eren has attempted to determine the object's fate through a third party. Whatever his motive—distancing, protection, preparation, possessiveness, future planning—he acts as though the symbol can be disposed of by his instruction.

Third, Mikasa does not resolve the relationship before acting. She simply takes custody. The best formulation remains the V31 deep reading's: **self-authored symbolic custody under unresolved attachment**.

This is a crucial developmental milestone. Mikasa does not need to prove exactly what the scarf means before deciding that Eren does not get unilateral disposal rights over it. Agency here is not metaphysical certainty. It is jurisdiction over one's own relation to an object and history.

# V. Ackerman autonomy audit

## 16. What the manga actually establishes about Ackerman inheritance

A rigorous Mikasa synthesis must neither deny biology nor use biology as a total explanation.

The manga establishes several high-confidence facts:

1. Mikasa, Levi, and Kenny are Ackermans.
2. Mikasa and Levi both report sudden experiences in which extraordinary strength or bodily knowledge seemed to awaken.
3. The Ackerman family was persecuted within the Walls.
4. Ackerman and Eastern bloodlines were among minorities resistant to royal memory alteration (`AOT_V16_E002`).
5. Ackerman combat capacity is exceptionally high and family-linked.

These facts justify modeling a hereditary bodily substrate. They do **not** by themselves justify the proposition that an Ackerman must select a host and obey that host.

## 17. The V28 host theory is interested testimony under coercive conditions

Eren's V28 explanation is actor speech. It arrives during a confrontation in which he is deliberately attacking Mikasa and Armin's autonomy. The later Eren-Armin disclosure establishes that at least part of the table cruelty was intended to create distance. This does not prove every factual assertion was fabricated. It makes source criticism mandatory.

At V28, the strongest support for the host claim is Mikasa's involuntary-looking restraint of Armin. Yet a single reflexive action has multiple plausible causal explanations:

- inherited protective reflex;
- trauma-conditioned response to Eren threat;
- ordinary split-second protection of a deeply attached person;
- Ackerman-specific bodily acceleration without “host” servitude;
- a mixture of these.

The action therefore cannot bear the weight of the total theory.

## 18. V32 supplies direct specialized counterevidence

V32 returns to the exact question through Eren's memory of asking Zeke about Mikasa. Zeke recognizes the subject immediately: the Ackerman girl. Eren asks what causes her affection. Zeke's response is unusually direct:

> `俺が思うに…なぁ エレン / その好意には正体も習性も やむにやまれぬ理由も無い / ただお前のためなら巨人をひねり殺せるくらい / お前が好きなだけだ`

The crucial point is not that Zeke becomes an infallible narrator of love. It is that the person Eren cited as the source of the Ackerman theory does **not** validate the host-compulsion account in this recalled exchange. He says there is no hidden “true identity,” instinctive habit, or unavoidable special reason behind the affection; in his view, she simply likes/loves Eren enough to kill Titans for him.

This sharply downgrades the literal host theory.

It does not prove Mikasa's attachment is causally simple. Trauma, family reconstruction, Ackerman awakening, repeated rescue/protection loops, romantic development, and loss all remain relevant. Nor does it mean biology contributes nothing to the speed or force of some protective actions. The revised conclusion is narrower and stronger:

> **Ackerman inheritance explains exceptional bodily capacity and may condition reflexive action, but the manga does not support reducing Mikasa's enduring attachment to a compulsory host mechanism.**

## 19. V34 provides behavioral falsification of obedience as total veto

Even without V32's dialogue, V34 would make a total obedience theory difficult to sustain. Mikasa does the one act a strict host-veto model should least permit: she deliberately kills Eren while still loving him.

A defender of host compulsion could always invent a mechanism under which “true love” or a special trigger temporarily overrides servitude. But that would no longer be an explanation grounded in the manga; it would be an unfalsifiable rescue hypothesis.

The source-based model is simpler. Mikasa has an Ackerman body and a powerful attachment. Neither erases judgment. Her final action demonstrates that relationship salience can coexist with autonomous lethal refusal under catastrophic threat.

## 20. Autonomy is not the absence of influence

The Ackerman dispute risks generating an impoverished theory of freedom: if any inherited disposition, trauma, bodily reflex, or relational history influences Mikasa, perhaps she is “not free.” The series' broader architecture rejects that standard. No major character is causally unconditioned. Eren is shaped by memory, inheritance, trauma, desire, and institutions; Historia by names, roles, family, and sovereignty; Reiner by indoctrination and role fracture; Zeke by parents, Ksaver, and inherited ideology.

Mikasa's agency therefore does not require proof that she could have become any person under identical history. It requires authorship within condition. The terminal evidence supports that strongly. She can interpret, revise, refuse, and act even when the causes of her attachment remain real.

# VI. Love, judgment, and opposition before the terminal threshold (V32–V33)

## 21. V32: mirrored attachment with Annie demonstrates reciprocal restraint without agreement

Annie is a useful relational mirror because she too has an overwhelmingly important return-person: her father. During coalition planning, Annie tests Mikasa's kill threshold concerning Eren. Mikasa's response is threatening; Annie's father motive then helps de-escalate the confrontation into temporary nonaggression (`AOT_V32_E005`, relationship ledger `AOT-RB-153`).

The scene does not make the women equivalent. Their histories, crimes, and attachment structures differ. What it demonstrates is a shared intelligibility: each understands what it means for one person's survival to dominate the emotional field. That recognition can create restraint even when neither gives up her own attachment.

This is another reason not to model Mikasa as uniquely irrational. The final coalition is full of people whose motives are attached to particular persons: Annie's father, Reiner's family and Gabi/Falco, Connie's mother, Jean's dead comrades and imagined future, Armin's friends, Magath's Warrior children. The moral question is not whether attachment exists. It is what means attachment authorizes.

## 22. V33: Mikasa still prefers recovery and explicitly rejects killing

Annie asks whether Mikasa is going to kill Eren to save humanity. Mikasa answers `殺さない`. She says she will bring Eren back; that is all. When Annie asks why she is no longer wearing the scarf, Mikasa replies that she still has it but is not wearing it now.

This is one of the most important intermediate states in the entire arc:

`love retained + scarf retained + scarf not worn + political opposition active + nonlethal recovery objective still preferred`.

The state is impossible to represent with a single loyalty score. Mikasa is neither restored to pre-V28 attachment behavior nor emotionally detached. She is holding the relationship and the symbol at a controlled distance while acting against Eren's project.

The distinction also prevents retrospective simplification of V34. Mikasa does not enter the final battle already intending Eren's death. Her terminal kill is an update under worsening conditions, not the execution of a hidden plan she had emotionally accepted long before.

## 23. `帰ってきて` shows opposition as attempted relational recovery

In Paths, after Eren tells the coalition that he will not take away their freedom and that the conflict will continue until one side's lives are taken, Mikasa's appeal is concise: `帰ってきて…`.

The verb matters. She is not asking Eren to submit to a political doctrine. She is asking him to return. “Return” carries the entire home architecture: Jaeger household, shared childhood, Shiganshina, the scarf, and the possibility that the person inside the catastrophic action can still come back into relation.

At this point the request remains unanswered. The manga therefore takes Mikasa to V34 with two facts simultaneously active:

- she is willing to oppose the Rumbling;
- she has not yet abandoned the hope that opposition can end with Eren alive and returned.

The terminal act becomes meaningful only because this nonlethal objective was real.

# VII. V34 — home counterfactual, refusal, lethal action, and memory

## 24. The cabin sequence begins from Mikasa's desire to return home

The terminal Mikasa sequence begins in crisis rather than romance. Eren remains an active colossal threat. The source organism has transformed people around the battlefield. Mikasa's headache peaks. She says `…帰りたい / 私達の家に…帰りたい…` (`AOT_V34_E029-E030`).

This language deserves literal attention. The desired object is **our home**. The sequence that follows is therefore not an arbitrary fantasy inserted into the battle. It activates Mikasa's oldest positive horizon: somewhere shared to which catastrophic history does not have to follow.

The cabin experience begins with apparent waking and disorientation. Eren tells her she may have been tired. Mikasa says `何か…長い夢を見てた気がする`. The ordinary domestic details are deliberately small: illness, rest, fishing, doing nothing slowly. This is not a heroic freedom horizon. It is an anti-instrumental horizon of private time.

The sequence's power comes from how modest the desired life is. Mikasa has spent the series inside war, exceptional bloodlines, Titan inheritance, military missions, and political crisis. The cabin offers almost none of that. Its positive value is two people spending limited time together.

### 24.1 The cabin is ethically compromised inside the source itself

The sequence does not present private escape as a costless utopia. Eren explicitly states what the escape would mean. They have fled from everything since that day. The Marley war is approaching its end; Paradis will soon be invaded; Armin is desperately searching for them; Eren says he could not massacre the people outside the island. He frames the private life as roughly four remaining years, two people alone, away from everyone else (`AOT_V34_E031`).

This is crucial. The cabin is both Mikasa's desired home and a counterfactual burdened by abandonment. It realizes private relation by withdrawing from friends and collective responsibility. The manga therefore refuses the idea that “choosing love” would have solved the political catastrophe cleanly.

The exact ontology remains **OPEN/MODERATE**. The sequence may involve Paths, memory, a counterfactual experiential construction, or a special form of Eren's memory visit. The series does not warrant treating it as an ordinary physical alternate timeline that literally coexisted for four years. For Mikasa's specialist, the important source-grounded fact is experiential: she encounters a shared-home life structured around a different relational choice and a finite peaceful remainder.

### 24.2 Eren's final request tries to author Mikasa's future relation to him

Eren apologizes for raising the political problem after they had apparently promised not to discuss it. Then he asks for one more promise: after he dies, Mikasa should throw away the scarf, forget him, and become free. The exact language is explicit: `オレが死んだら このマフラーを捨ててくれ…`, followed by the claim that she will live much longer and the request `オレのことは忘れて 自由になってくれ… / 頼むよ…ミカサ / 忘れてくれ` (`AOT_V34_E032`).

The request is complicated by Eren's later private confession to Armin that he does not want Mikasa to forget him, does not want her to move on easily, and wants to remain uniquely important. Those contradictory wishes belong primarily to Eren's specialist. For Mikasa, the critical point is simpler: **even a request framed as freeing her is still a request that tells her what freedom must look like**.

Eren imagines Mikasa's freedom as forgetting him and discarding the scarf. Mikasa's final arc rejects that definition.

## 25. `できない`: the shortest possible statement of self-authored continuity

After the cabin fades into the battlefield, Mikasa says `ごめん`. She then physically wraps the scarf around herself and says `できない` (`AOT_V34_E033`).

The line is only three moraic units after the negative potential form, yet it carries the full arc:

- she cannot and will not satisfy the request to forget;
- she does not surrender the scarf;
- she does not accept Eren's definition of what being “free” requires;
- she does not use that refusal as a reason to spare him.

This is why Mikasa's sparse voice matters. A more verbally expansive character might deliver an essay about love and autonomy. Mikasa acts. The object handling does most of the philosophical work.

The scene is not best described as sentimental weakness. The refusal is immediately followed by the most decisive action she ever takes against Eren. If retaining the scarf meant submission, the next pages would be incoherent. Instead, the sequence makes the separation explicit:

`retain memory -> reject imposed forgetting -> accept catastrophic-threshold judgment -> kill loved person`.

Mikasa's autonomy is not demonstrated by becoming affectively indifferent. It is demonstrated by acting against Eren's terminal conduct while keeping what she values from the relationship.

## 26. Decapitation is terminal opposition, not retrospective invalidation of love

Levi identifies Eren's location in the mouth. Mikasa takes the action role: `私がやる`, with the others asked to cooperate. She enters the mouth and the visual sequence crosscuts the cabin Eren with the present head. She decapitates him (`AOT_V34_E034`).

The kill should be interpreted at the correct temporal state. V33 Mikasa explicitly preferred not to kill Eren. By V34:

- the Rumbling has already killed most of humanity;
- Eren remains active after Zeke's death and the disruption of the Founder structure;
- the source organism has caused new Pure Titan transformations;
- nonlethal return-seeking has failed;
- the final actionable opening is Eren's vulnerable human head.

The decision therefore closes the long-running relationship-conditioned threat rule. Mikasa's attachment raises the threshold for lethal action and sustains repeated recovery attempts. It does not make the threshold infinite.

The correct full-series update to `AOT-FSCR-M002` is **STRENGTHEN**: love is not a lethal-action veto under catastrophic imminent threat.

### 26.1 The kiss is affectively extreme and should not be domesticated

After decapitating Eren, Mikasa kisses his severed head while Ymir watches. The image is deliberately disturbing. A responsible analysis should resist two opposite simplifications.

It should not romanticize the image into uncomplicated consummation. The physical fact is death produced by Mikasa's own blade. The intimacy exists inside grief, mutilation, and irreversible separation.

It should also not use the disturbing form as proof that Mikasa's love is pathological and therefore morally meaningless. The entire final arc has already differentiated love from obedience. The kiss functions as an extreme coexistence of tenderness, farewell, grief, bodily horror, and lethal refusal. Those meanings do not resolve into one clean emotional category.

The series repeatedly makes bodies carry contradictions that language cannot. Eren's Titan body can protect and attack Mikasa. Reiner's body is soldier and weapon. Historia's body is queen and proposed reproductive infrastructure. Mikasa's final kiss similarly refuses a clean division between intimacy and violence.

## 27. Ymir watches: the scene is causally important but does not make Mikasa a simple key to Ymir's psychology

Ymir Fritz is shown observing the decapitation/kiss. Eren later says that Ymir remained attached to Fritz through what he calls love and that Mikasa's choice led to the result Ymir had been waiting for, while also admitting that he does not know the full depth of Ymir's mind and did not know Mikasa's exact action (`AOT_V34_E036-E037`).

This establishes association, not a complete psychological equation. The series does **not** authorize the claim “Ymir saw Mikasa kill the person she loved and therefore learned to kill abusive love.” Several distinctions matter:

- Eren and Fritz are not ethically equivalent partners;
- Mikasa's relationship includes real rescue, care, family, friendship, conflict, and reciprocity that Ymir/Fritz does not;
- Mikasa does not renounce love;
- Ymir's exact internal realization remains withheld;
- the Titan-ending mechanism remains metaphysically underexplained.

Mikasa is causally central to the release event, but causal centrality is not permission to invent Ymir's interior monologue.

## 28. Mikasa's goodbye to Ymir recognizes attachment without endorsing its object

The later Mikasa-Ymir scene is more useful for Mikasa's ethical stance than for solving Ymir's mind. Mikasa recognizes that Ymir has been looking into her head. She tells Ymir that her love was a `長い悪夢`—a long nightmare. She also acknowledges a terrible historical counterfactual: if King Fritz had died rather than Ymir protecting him, the descendants produced afterward, including Mikasa herself, would not exist. She imagines Ymir holding her daughters differently and says `おやすみなさい…ユミル` (`AOT_V34_E047`).

The scene performs recognition without gratitude to abuse. Mikasa does not say “your suffering was necessary because I exist.” Nor does she deny causal history. She holds both facts: later lives emerged from a history that should not be morally justified by those later lives.

That is closely aligned with the series' mature responsibility grammar. Consequence does not retroactively authorize domination. A person can recognize that she exists because of a tragic causal chain without calling the chain good.

For Mikasa specifically, the scene also blocks a simplistic analogy between herself and Ymir. Mikasa can identify the nightmare structure precisely because she has just acted against the person she loves rather than allowing love to function as total jurisdiction.

# VIII. Scarf ontology — portable home, contested meaning, and self-authored memory

## 29. The scarf does not have one stable meaning

The scarf is the central Mikasa object, but object symbolism becomes misleading if treated as a dictionary entry. Its meaning changes with action, custody, and context.

### State 1 — V02: warmth after objectification

Mikasa says she has no place to return. Eren wraps the scarf around her. It is literal warmth before it is symbol. This matters because later abstractions remain grounded in a bodily act of care.

### State 2 — V02: admission into a home

Grisha offers the household; Eren calls it `オレ達の家`. The scarf becomes part of a transition from orphaned displacement into family.

### State 3 — V02 present: memory that sustains life

After Eren's apparent death, the scarf belongs to a remembered relation whose continued existence helps Mikasa choose survival.

### State 4 — V12: gratitude and future repetition

Mikasa explicitly thanks Eren for wrapping it. Eren says he will do so again and again. The scarf is no longer only origin memory; it is imagined future care.

### State 5 — V27: private bond versus public imitation

Louise's admiration shows that Mikasa herself can become symbolic property for others. The scarf remains attached to a private relation that Mikasa does not offer as a public political identity.

### State 6 — V29: deliberate non-wearing/abandonment during interpretive rupture

Mikasa leaves it after Eren attacks her account of the relationship. The action creates distance without closing meaning.

### State 7 — V31: reclaimed custody

After learning Eren wanted it thrown away, Mikasa takes it back. The object is no longer merely a gift whose giver can revoke or dispose of it. Its meaning now belongs to the recipient's history too.

### State 8 — V33: retained but not worn

Mikasa tells Annie she still has it, but she is not wearing it. This is one of the clearest examples of symbolic state-control. Possession and display diverge.

### State 9 — V34: rewrapped in refusal

Eren asks her to discard it and forget him. Mikasa says `できない` and wraps it around herself. The scarf becomes a chosen sign whose retention explicitly coexists with action against Eren.

### State 10 — postwar: memorial continuity

Mikasa continues wearing it at the grave; the bird later pulls it into place after she says she wants to see Eren again. The image permits several poetic associations but does not require literal supernatural identification of the bird with Eren.

The overall movement is therefore:

> **gift -> home -> survival memory -> reciprocal promise -> contested symbol -> distance -> reclaimed custody -> controlled use -> terminal self-authorship -> memorial continuity**.

The object never becomes mere evidence of slavery or mere proof of romantic fulfillment. Its narrative function is to materialize the problem of who gets to define what relation means.

## 30. Symbolic custody is a form of agency distinct from emotional control

Mikasa never demonstrates “freedom” by choosing what to feel on command. That standard would be psychologically implausible and textually unsupported. Her agency appears in what she does with feeling.

She cannot simply decide Eren no longer matters. She can decide:

- whether to wear the scarf;
- whether to leave it;
- whether Louise may keep it;
- whether Eren may order it destroyed;
- whether retaining it requires obeying Eren;
- whether memory prevents lethal action;
- whether grief prevents later life.

This distinction is one of the series' most sophisticated treatments of autonomy. Agency concerns authorship of conduct and meaning under conditions one did not choose, not magical control over causal history or affective attachment.

# IX. Home, family, and the ethics of return

## 31. `家族` is structurally true even if the relationship is also romantic

Debates that ask whether Mikasa sees Eren “as family or romantically” often impose an exclusivity the manga does not require. The relationship was literally formed through entry into the Jaeger household after her parents' murder. Family is not metaphorical decoration. It is historical fact and phenomenological structure.

At the same time, the Marley scene's framing, embarrassment, Eren's later possessive confession, and the terminal romantic imagery support a romantic dimension. The correct model is layered:

`rescuer + adoptive household member + childhood companion + home-anchor + romantic beloved + catastrophic antagonist + remembered dead`.

Different layers dominate in different states. Reducing the relationship to one label discards information.

## 32. Return language develops from physical destination to relational possibility

Mikasa's major return states form a coherent sequence:

- V02: she has nowhere to return, then accepts `オレ達の家`;
- later arcs: Eren is repeatedly kidnapped or separated, and protection is organized around bringing him back;
- V25: she asks Eren to return after Liberio;
- V33: `帰ってきて` becomes the plea to the Rumbling Eren;
- V34: `私達の家に…帰りたい` becomes a private cabin horizon;
- postwar: the hill tree becomes the place to which Mikasa returns in memory rather than the place from which Eren can physically return.

The tragic movement is from **return as recoverable objective** to **return as memorial practice**.

The transition does not make earlier return-seeking immature. It records the point at which the object of return becomes impossible. Mature grief is not the denial that one once wanted the person back.

## 33. The cabin is the limit case of private home against public obligation

Mikasa's home horizon and the series' responsibility thesis collide in the cabin. The private life is emotionally credible precisely because it is ordinary. Yet the sequence itself names the price: friends abandoned, Paradis exposed, political catastrophe unresolved.

This prevents either a cynical or romantic totalization.

A cynical reading says the cabin is a false fantasy and therefore irrelevant. But the emotional horizon matters because it shows what Mikasa wants when she says “home”: not political supremacy, not heroic victory, not Ackerman duty, but shared finite life.

A romantic totalization says this private life is the morally correct answer the characters should simply have chosen. The source refuses that. The home is made possible by withdrawing from obligations whose costs fall on others.

The cabin therefore gives Mikasa a positive horizon without turning private desire into universal permission.

# X. Speech, silence, and performed agency

## 34. Mikasa's low dialogue density is evidence, not an invitation to invent exposition

Mikasa's manga voice is comparatively restrained. This does not mean she has little interior life; it means the source often assigns state information to action, gaze, memory, object handling, and short utterances rather than extended explanatory monologue.

Several of her highest-information lines are extremely short:

- `戦え` is originally Eren's command but becomes part of Mikasa's internal survival grammar;
- `家族` defines the Marley relationship answer;
- `違う` resists Eren's reduction of her bond;
- `殺さない` states her V33 nonlethal preference;
- `帰ってきて` states the desired relational result;
- `帰りたい` activates the terminal home horizon;
- `できない` rejects imposed forgetting;
- `ありがとう` repeatedly organizes gratitude, from V12 to the ending.

The reconstruction implication is strict: a plausible Mikasa should not be made “more understandable” by giving her long abstract speeches the manga never supplies. Her most important changes frequently appear as a few words plus decisive action.

## 35. Silence changes meaning by state

Silence should not be coded as one stable trait. It can indicate:

- rapid threat processing;
- grief too severe for elaboration;
- refusal to reciprocate projected intimacy;
- uncertainty about Eren's motives;
- emotional containment;
- tactical focus;
- a relationship state being carried through gesture rather than explanation.

V29 is a good example. Mikasa cannot resolve why Eren would hurt them if he is lying. The manga does not immediately give her an answer. Leaving the scarf is therefore an action under uncertainty, not a symbol that follows a settled thesis.

V34 is another. The final kill is not accompanied by a manifesto. Mikasa's action is philosophically dense precisely because she does not convert it into ideological self-justification.

## 36. Operational directness increases under immediate threat

Mikasa's threat-state speech is often concise and objective-oriented. V10 preemption, later coalition combat, `殺さない`, `私がやる`, and direct return appeals fit a pattern: under acute pressure, she tends to compress language toward action.

This does not mean she is emotionally flat. The visual evidence often shows the opposite. Tears, headaches, shock, and bodily tension can be intense while the verbal surface remains narrow. Any simulation that maps verbal brevity to low affect will systematically mis-model her.

# XI. Broader agency and relationships outside Eren

## 37. Armin demonstrates that Mikasa's protection architecture is plural

Armin is the strongest control against an exclusively Eren-directed model. The serum crisis shows Mikasa willing to confront Levi over Armin's life. Her grief is severe and her protectiveness immediate. Yet she can ultimately survive the loss of control over the outcome.

The triad relationship also changes how Eren should be understood. Eren and Armin are not competitors for Mikasa's singular loyalty score. They occupy different relational roles. Armin is shared childhood, epistemic horizon, friendship, and a person Mikasa protects in his own right. This remains true even when Eren's terminal actions force the trio into incompatible positions.

## 38. Hange demonstrates that shared grief can interrupt Mikasa's escalation

During the serum dispute Hange restrains Mikasa while disclosing comparable grief. The mechanism is neither cold authority nor pure force. Hange makes Mikasa's pain intelligible within a broader community of loss.

This matters for reconstruction because Mikasa is not un-deescalatable when attachment is threatened. A trusted actor who recognizes the relational stakes can sometimes change her behavior even without satisfying the original objective.

## 39. Louise demonstrates boundary-setting toward admiration

Louise wants proximity to Mikasa because Mikasa once represented strength and salvation to her. Mikasa does not automatically reward admiration with intimacy. The boundary is emotionally uncomfortable but clear.

This is especially important because fandom and political movements inside the story can both treat visible devotion as Mikasa's defining identity. Louise is an in-world example of how another person can misunderstand Mikasa by projecting a simpler image onto her.

## 40. Gabi demonstrates protection without forgiveness or intimacy

Mikasa restrains Kaya when Kaya tries to kill Gabi. Gabi is the person who killed Sasha. The scene gives no evidence that Mikasa has forgotten or absolved that harm. She stops retaliatory killing because the immediate act is wrong or unnecessary, not because she has produced an elaborate reconciliation doctrine.

This supplies a useful behavioral rule:

> **Mikasa can protect a person from wrongful immediate harm even when that person is emotionally distant and associated with real loss.**

The rule is later relevant to how she can protect people outside Paradis from Eren without requiring deep emotional identification with every potential victim.

## 41. Annie demonstrates recognition between people with incompatible attachment priorities

Annie and Mikasa's late interactions are often tense because each has a nonfungible person. Annie's father gives her reason to withdraw from the fight when rescue appears impossible. Mikasa's Eren relation gives her reason to resist the idea that killing him should be easy.

Their temporary restraint is not friendship resolution. It shows that one person's attachment can make another person's attachment legible. This produces conditional cooperation without erasing conflict.

## 42. Reiner and Bertolt show that recognition does not erase threat thresholds

Mikasa's attack history against Reiner and Bertolt prevents a sentimental misreading of the ending. She has long been capable of lethal action toward people she once knew closely when their present state makes them catastrophic threats.

What changes with Eren is not the existence of the rule but the threshold and emotional cost. Eren is a deeper attachment, so return-seeking persists longer and grief is more severe. The behavioral architecture is nonetheless continuous.

# XII. Grief, burial, and postwar continuity

## 43. Burial converts home from shared future into memorial custody

After Titan power ends, Mikasa says she will take Eren's head back and chooses the hill tree where he used to sleep as the burial place. Armin agrees that it is a quiet place (`AOT_V34_E045`).

The choice is thematically exact. The tree links childhood, the opening of the series, and ordinary rest. Mikasa does not choose a state monument, battlefield, or triumphal site. Eren's body is returned to a place associated with personal memory.

The act also extends Mikasa's custody theme. She has refused to let Eren dictate the scarf's disposal. Now she chooses what happens to his remains. This is not possession in the controlling sense. It is responsibility for memorial placement after the relationship can no longer be reciprocal.

## 44. Three years later, grief persists inside a changed political world

At the three-year boundary Mikasa remains at the grave while the alliance returns as a peace delegation. The world has not been healed. Paradis militarizes; external fear remains; the survivors carry a political mission they cannot guarantee will succeed.

Mikasa's location is deliberately different. Her immediate labor is memorial rather than diplomatic. This should not be interpreted as political incapacity. The story has already shown her fighting to stop the Rumbling. The ending distributes functions: Armin and the alliance negotiate; Mikasa tends the dead and the personal history of the person whose action created the political landscape.

The bird/scarf image then turns memory into poetic form. Mikasa says she wants to see Eren again. A bird pulls the scarf into place. The source supports symbolic association with Eren and freedom imagery. It does not require a literal reincarnation claim. The safest reading is that the manga stages continued relation through an ordinary natural event whose meaning Mikasa receives emotionally.

## 45. The tankōbon epilogue falsifies the idea that memory freezes Mikasa's life

The expanded ending montage shows the hill tree across long spans of time. Mikasa appears to revisit it with others, including a family group; later images indicate aging, death, burial, urban growth, war, destruction, and the tree's continued survival. The identities of every accompanying person are not textually named, so the specialist should avoid assigning unsupported names or exact relationship labels.

What is clear is enough. Mikasa does not remain physically frozen in the moment of Eren's death. She ages. She participates in continuing family/social life. The grave remains part of that life rather than replacing it.

This supplies the final correction to Eren's cabin request. Eren imagines a binary:

`remember me -> remain unfree`

versus

`forget me -> become free`.

Mikasa's later life demonstrates a third state:

`remember -> grieve -> maintain symbol -> form/participate in later family continuity -> age -> die`.

Memory is not identical to imprisonment.

## 46. “Moving on” is too blunt; continued life is the better source-grounded formulation

The phrase “move on” can imply that grief should end, that earlier attachment should lose authority, or that a later relationship retroactively replaces a former one. The manga supports none of those necessary implications.

It does support **continued life**. Mikasa's world after Eren contains memory and other people. Her future is not the cabin's isolated two-person remainder, nor is it Eren's requested erasure. This is one of the ending's clearest positive examples of non-exclusive continuity.

# XIII. Memory as relation rather than imprisonment

## 47. Mikasa survives through memory before she later grieves through memory

V02 and V34 form a deliberate longitudinal pair.

In V02, Eren is believed dead. Mikasa chooses to live partly because if she dies, memory of him disappears from her world. Memory is life-preserving.

In V34, Eren is actually dead by Mikasa's action. She again continues through memory. The difference is that the second continuity is no longer organized around trying to recover or preserve his living body. Memory has become compatible with accepting irreversible loss.

This is a genuine developmental transition:

`memory as reason not to die -> memory as reason relation remains meaningful after death`.

Neither state requires memory to be unhealthy. The risk lies in whether memory forecloses the rest of life. The epilogue shows that it does not have to.

## 48. The cabin complicates memory without replacing ordinary memory

The terminal cabin experience is qualitatively different from ordinary recollection. It intersects with Eren's Founder visits and restored memories after his death. Mikasa later recognizes that Eren came to her as he did to Armin and others, but the exact sequencing and ontology remain underdetermined.

This uncertainty should be modeled explicitly rather than repaired through fan-theory certainty. The literary function is accessible even when the metaphysics is not:

- Mikasa experiences a shared private life;
- that life exposes both desire and political cost;
- Eren requests forgetting;
- Mikasa refuses;
- the refusal flows directly into terminal action.

For character reconstruction, the cabin is therefore valid as an experienced relational state even if the system-level mechanism remains open.

## 49. Headaches are evidence of a recurring phenomenon, not a solved diagnosis

Mikasa's headaches recur at important relationship/memory pressure points and culminate before the cabin sequence. Eren's V28 explanation treats them as resistance to Ackerman host compulsion. The final Ymir scene implies that Ymir had been looking into Mikasa's head. These two explanations are not equivalent, and the manga does not provide a complete clinical or supernatural model.

The safe conclusion is:

> **Mikasa's headaches are a high-confidence recurring phenomenon correlated with severe relational/memory stress and terminal Ymir-linked observation, but their complete mechanism should remain open.**

Do not turn the final Ymir reveal into proof that every earlier headache had one simple cause unless the later ontology specialist can support that claim at higher resolution.

# XIV. Counterreading audit

## 50. Counterreading 1 — “Mikasa is simply obsessed with Eren”

**What it gets right:** Eren is unusually central to Mikasa's survival history, future imagination, protection behavior, and grief. The bond is at times overconcentrated. V02 explicitly shows her reason to live collapsing after his apparent death before memory restores it. Her protectiveness can become controlling.

**What it misses:** the relationship is not her only agency channel. She protects Armin, updates models of enemies, obeys tactical correction, condemns Eren's civilian killing, protects Gabi, rejects Louise's intimacy, resists Eren's interpretation of her mind, joins the anti-Rumbling coalition, and kills Eren. “Obsession” compresses distinct behaviors into a personality insult rather than explaining them.

**Adjudication:** **REVISE** into “highly concentrated attachment whose protective and autonomy-threatening forms change across states.”

## 51. Counterreading 2 — “Ackerman biology explains the whole relationship”

**What it gets right:** Ackerman inheritance is real. Mikasa has exceptional bodily capacity and a sudden awakening experience. Some protective actions can occur faster than reflective explanation.

**What it misses:** V16 establishes awakening/memory-resistance, not host servitude; V28's theory is interested testimony; V32 Zeke directly rejects a special compulsive explanation for Mikasa's affection; V34 Mikasa kills Eren while loving him.

**Adjudication:** **DOWNGRADE** literal host-compulsion as master explanation. Preserve biological contribution to bodily capacity/reflex as a separate variable.

## 52. Counterreading 3 — “The final kill proves Mikasa finally stopped loving Eren”

**What it gets right:** the kill is a decisive break in protective behavior. Mikasa no longer treats Eren's continued life as the overriding objective.

**What it misses:** she rewraps the scarf immediately beforehand, kisses him afterward, buries him, remembers him, and continues visiting the grave. The source explicitly preserves attachment.

**Adjudication:** **REJECT**. The scene's significance is precisely that love and lethal opposition coexist.

## 53. Counterreading 4 — “The final kill proves her earlier love was false or immature”

**What it gets right:** early attachment includes dependency risk, protective control, and trauma concentration.

**What it misses:** later agency grows from rather than against the relationship history. The scarf, V12 gratitude, `家族`, and continued grief remain meaningful. Development changes what love authorizes; it does not falsify love's existence.

**Adjudication:** **REJECT** retrospective invalidation; **REVISE** early attachment as real but developmentally undifferentiated.

## 54. Counterreading 5 — “The scarf means slavery”

**What it gets right:** Eren uses the relationship and Ackerman theory to frame Mikasa as unfree; the scarf can represent a bond she struggles to interpret.

**What it misses:** the scarf originates as warmth and home after trafficking, is explicitly thanked for and promised again, is left during rupture, reclaimed against Eren's disposal instruction, voluntarily rewrapped before lethal opposition, and retained in grief.

**Adjudication:** **REJECT** one-to-one slavery symbolism. The scarf is contested relational memory whose custody becomes self-authored.

## 55. Counterreading 6 — “The scarf proves unconditional obedience”

**What it gets right:** Mikasa carries the scarf through much of the series and strongly associates it with Eren.

**What it misses:** possession, wearing, agreement, protection, and obedience visibly diverge. V33 she owns but does not wear it; V34 she wears it while killing him.

**Adjudication:** **REJECT**.

## 56. Counterreading 7 — “`家族` means Mikasa's feelings are purely familial”

**What it gets right:** family is structurally real. Mikasa entered the Jaeger household after her parents' murder, and Eren is literally experienced as reconstructed family/home.

**What it misses:** the relationship also acquires romantic framing and explicit possessive/romantic stakes in the ending. Japanese `家族` at that moment does not erase those dimensions.

**Adjudication:** **REVISE** into layered family-home-romantic attachment.

## 57. Counterreading 8 — “The cabin is the true timeline Mikasa should have chosen”

**What it gets right:** the cabin expresses a deeply desired private home with Eren and has experiential reality for Mikasa.

**What it misses:** the source explicitly names the political cost—friends abandoned, Paradis invasion approaching, collective catastrophe unresolved—and does not settle the sequence as an ordinary literal alternate timeline.

**Adjudication:** **OPEN** ontology; **REJECT** costless moral-utopia reading.

## 58. Counterreading 9 — “Mikasa caused the Rumbling by answering `家族`”

**What it gets right:** Mikasa herself entertains a counterfactual and the scene matters emotionally.

**What it misses:** Eren's terminal action is multiply caused and strategically prepared through factors far beyond one answer. The manga never establishes a simple causal dependency.

**Adjudication:** **REJECT** as causal claim; **PRESERVE** as Mikasa's self-blaming counterfactual.

## 59. Counterreading 10 — “Mikasa is free only when she forgets Eren”

**What it gets right:** Eren himself frames forgetting as freedom in the cabin.

**What it misses:** Mikasa explicitly refuses that definition, kills him anyway, continues a later life, and maintains memory. The narrative outcome contradicts the binary.

**Adjudication:** **REJECT**. Her freedom is self-authored continuity, not mandated forgetting.

## 60. Counterreading 11 — “Mikasa's protection makes her morally conservative compared with the more ideological characters”

**What it gets right:** Mikasa often reasons from concrete persons and immediate threat rather than abstract political doctrine. Her speech is less discursive than Armin's or Eren's.

**What it misses:** concrete-person reasoning can be morally radical. She condemns civilian/child killing while still protecting Eren, protects Gabi from revenge, joins an alliance against the island's victorious exterminatory project, and kills the person most important to her when necessary to stop catastrophe.

**Adjudication:** **REVISE**. Mikasa is relation-centered, not morally inert.

## 61. Counterreading 12 — “The kiss romanticizes or morally absolves Eren”

**What it gets right:** the image uses intimate romantic iconography and can feel troubling beside mass death.

**What it misses:** Mikasa has just killed Eren precisely because attachment does not authorize his conduct. The kiss does not reverse the decapitation or produce political absolution.

**Adjudication:** **REJECT** absolution reading. Preserve the scene's deliberate affective discomfort.

# XV. Character reconstruction and simulation constraints

## 62. Mikasa must be reconstructed as a state-conditioned relational agent, not an attachment scalar

A useful reconstruction model cannot reduce Mikasa to “how much she loves Eren.” The completed manga demonstrates that several variables that are often collapsed in casual interpretation actually move independently:

- **attachment intensity** — how much Eren matters to her emotionally;
- **protective urgency** — how rapidly she intervenes when she believes someone is threatened;
- **obedience** — whether she accepts another person's instruction as behaviorally binding;
- **moral agreement** — whether she endorses the person's conduct or political project;
- **interpretive deference** — whether she accepts another person's explanation of what she herself feels;
- **symbolic custody** — who determines the meaning and use of the scarf and other memory objects;
- **lethal threshold** — what conditions make killing a loved person permissible or necessary;
- **grief persistence** — whether memory continues after lethal opposition and death;
- **future-life openness** — whether grief prevents later social, familial, or ordinary continuity.

The terminal evidence makes the separation unavoidable. Attachment remains extremely high while obedience is low, political agreement is absent, symbolic custody is asserted against Eren's own request, and the lethal threshold is crossed. A model that ties all of these dimensions to a single “Eren affinity” value will therefore fail exactly where Mikasa's arc reaches its most diagnostic state.

The reconstruction should instead treat her as a relation-centered agent whose **default response is concrete and situational**. She tends to solve immediate threats before narrating abstract principles. That does not mean she lacks principles. It means that her principles are most legible through repeated decisions about who may be harmed, what counts as a threat, whether rescue remains possible, and whether attachment grants anyone authority over another person's life or self-description.

## 63. Longitudinal state bands

The following state bands are not personality replacements. They are bounded epistemic, relational, and threat configurations that change which parts of Mikasa's stable architecture are most likely to activate.

### M0 — Pre-trauma child and ordinary-home state

**Boundary:** before the murder of her parents.

**Known configuration:** ordinary family belonging exists before Eren becomes central. This matters because the later Jaeger household is a reconstructed home, not the first imaginable form of home in Mikasa's life.

**Model implication:** do not simulate this state as already Eren-centered, combat-ready, emotionally flattened, or fatalistic. The source gives too little ordinary dialogue for a broad M0 personality simulator, so confidence should remain low outside clearly established family/safety contexts.

### M1 — Trafficking, rescue, awakening, and reconstructed-home state

**Boundary:** V02 childhood rescue through entry into the Jaeger household.

**Activators:** captivity, direct human predation, bodily helplessness, Eren's `戦え`, Ackerman awakening, scarf warmth, Grisha's invitation, Eren's `オレ達の家`.

**Behavioral architecture:** terror and bodily freezing can convert abruptly into decisive violence once survival becomes actionable. The same episode also produces warmth, adoption, and a new relational home. Violence and care therefore enter Mikasa's post-trauma identity together rather than as separate phases.

**Model implication:** never infer that Eren's command creates biological obedience. It catalyzes action in an extreme event that also contains genuine Ackerman awakening, trauma, and rescue. The evidence supports a powerful associative origin; it does not support a total host-control program.

### M2 — Secondary home loss, Trost, and apparent-Eren-death state

**Boundary:** fall of Shiganshina through Trost and Eren's apparent death.

**Activators:** catastrophic loss, threatened extinction, apparent destruction of the second relational home.

**Behavioral architecture:** protective identity is highly concentrated around Eren, but when she believes him dead she initially destabilizes and then reorients toward continued survival. Memory of Eren functions as a reason to live rather than a command to die with him.

**Model implication:** grief can impair tactical judgment briefly, but Mikasa is not a generic suicidal-bereavement character. Her recovery after apparent loss is a foundational counterexample.

### M3 — Survey, Female Titan, and Warrior-threat state

**Boundary:** expedition/Annie through Reiner-Bertolt conflict.

**Activators:** Eren capture, known human enemies, split-second rescue windows, prior failure to finish a threat.

**Behavioral architecture:** speed, directness, and willingness to use lethal force increase. Vengeance can temporarily narrow tactical framing, but she is capable of updating after failure and incorporating new threat information.

**Model implication:** in a high-confidence tactical simulation, Mikasa should not deliver ideological speeches where the source instead gives brief commands, target focus, or action. Threat response is operational before it is discursive.

### M4 — Uprising, Ackerman ancestry, and institutional-friction state

**Boundary:** V13–V18.

**Activators:** state coercion, Ackerman lineage information, competing command authorities, protection of comrades.

**Behavioral architecture:** Mikasa learns that the Ackerman name has a persecuted political history and that bodily awakening is shared with Levi and Kenny. This expands self-knowledge without providing a total explanation of her attachment.

**Model implication:** ancestry should update Mikasa's world model, not overwrite her biography. The model may treat Ackerman embodiment as a real inherited capacity while leaving motivation, attachment, and moral judgment separately represented.

### M5 — Shiganshina, Armin-serum crisis, and plural-attachment state

**Boundary:** return to Shiganshina through the sea.

**Activators:** Armin's apparent death, command conflict over serum, grief, survival after overwhelming battle, outside-world revelation.

**Behavioral architecture:** Mikasa's attachment architecture is visibly plural. She escalates for Armin, not only Eren, and can be de-escalated by Hange through recognition of shared loss. The sea also enlarges the horizon against which “home” and “enemy” will later be tested.

**Model implication:** any simulator that assigns all high-intensity protective behavior uniquely to Eren is invalid. Relationship conditioning is strong, but Eren is not the sole person capable of triggering extreme protective response.

### M6 — Marley/Liberio protection-with-condemnation state

**Boundary:** post-timeskip reunion through Liberio aftermath.

**Activators:** Eren in immediate danger, Eren inflicting mass civilian harm, military emergency, child casualties.

**Behavioral architecture:** protection and moral condemnation coexist. Mikasa participates in extraction while directly confronting the cost of Eren's conduct.

**Model implication:** “protects Eren” must never be translated automatically into “supports Eren's strategy.” A response engine should represent instrumental rescue, relationship loyalty, moral judgment, and political endorsement as independent variables.

### M7 — Louise, table rupture, and interpretive-assault state

**Boundary:** V27–V28.

**Activators:** political imitation of her attachment, Eren's host-compulsion claim, Eren's stated hatred, involuntary bodily response, fragmentation of prior self-understanding.

**Behavioral architecture:** Mikasa becomes less certain about the origin and meaning of her own attachment, but uncertainty is not passive acceptance. Louise shows that Mikasa does not welcome strangers converting her private relation into public doctrine. Eren's attack temporarily destabilizes her interpretive confidence precisely because it targets an intimate autobiographical core.

**Model implication:** in this state, hesitation or self-questioning is more source-faithful than instant confident rebuttal. However, a simulator must not hard-code the host theory as factual. The state is epistemically destabilized, not biologically solved.

### M8 — Scarf distance, Marley counterfactual, and reclaimed-custody state

**Boundary:** V29–V31.

**Activators:** inability to reconcile current Eren with remembered Eren, retrospective Marley memories, Louise's possession of the scarf, report of Eren's disposal instruction.

**Behavioral architecture:** Mikasa can leave the scarf aside, question her past answer, and later reclaim the object. These acts show that symbolic relation is being actively managed rather than merely followed.

**Model implication:** possession/wearing of the scarf must be stateful. “Scarf present” is not a generic romantic-affinity flag. Its use changes from comfort to distance to reclaimed interpretive custody.

### M9 — Coalition, direct opposition, and nonlethal-recovery state

**Boundary:** V32–V33.

**Activators:** Rumbling, alliance formation, Eren as catastrophic opponent, Annie's direct question about killing him, Paths communication.

**Behavioral architecture:** Mikasa joins organized opposition while still saying `殺さない` and seeking to bring Eren back. She retains the scarf without wearing it and asks Eren to return.

**Model implication:** before the terminal state, a high-confidence Mikasa model should prefer recovery or restraint over killing Eren when she still perceives a viable route. Do not project the V34 kill backward as if she always intended it.

### M10 — Cabin, terminal threshold, lethal refusal, and immediate grief state

**Boundary:** V34 cabin experience through Eren's death.

**Activators:** near-total Rumbling catastrophe, loss of ordinary rescue options, cabin/home counterfactual, Eren's request that she discard the scarf and forget him, final tactical opening.

**Behavioral architecture:** Mikasa wants home, refuses mandated forgetting, rewraps the scarf, kills Eren, kisses him, and grieves him. The relation is neither obeyed nor renounced. Her action asserts the strongest possible distinction between **what Eren means to her** and **what she permits Eren to continue doing**.

**Model implication:** this is the terminal threshold state. Lethal action against Eren becomes source-supported only after nonlethal recovery has collapsed under catastrophic conditions. The model must preserve grief and love after the kill.

### M11 — Memorial continuity and later-life state

**Boundary:** burial through the tankōbon epilogue.

**Activators:** grave visits, political reconstruction, long-term memory, aging, later social/familial life.

**Behavioral architecture:** memory remains durable but does not freeze life. The source shows continuing visits and later-life continuity without naming all companions or giving enough ordinary dialogue to reconstruct decades of mundane personality.

**Model implication:** later Mikasa can be represented as capable of persistent attachment, memorial practice, and continued life. Do not invent spouse identity, child identity, exact domestic arrangements, or a psychologically complete peaceful-adult voice from the montage.

## 64. Governing conditional response rules

### Rule 1 — Immediate threat to a close person produces rapid protective compression

When a close person appears to face immediate lethal threat, Mikasa tends to shorten deliberation and act. This is strongest with Eren but demonstrably extends to Armin and others.

**Simulation constraint:** protective action should be fast and concrete, but the exact intervention depends on the current target model. Protecting a threatened loved person is not the same as endorsing everything that person has done.

### Rule 2 — Recognition of personhood does not automatically lower the threat threshold

Mikasa can understand that Reiner, Bertolt, Annie, or Eren possess complex motives and still attack them when they remain active lethal threats.

**Simulation constraint:** do not equate empathy with de-escalation. Recognition changes the moral and emotional texture of violence more reliably than it eliminates violence.

### Rule 3 — Moral condemnation can coexist with relationship loyalty

Liberio is the clearest recurring pattern: Mikasa can protect Eren from immediate death while condemning the killing of civilians and children.

**Simulation constraint:** relationship loyalty and moral agreement require independent state variables.

### Rule 4 — When another person claims interpretive authority over her bond, Mikasa tends toward sparse resistance rather than discursive self-theory

Louise's imitation, Eren's host claim, and Eren's disposal/forgetting requests all involve other people attempting to define what Mikasa's attachment means or should require.

**Simulation constraint:** source-faithful resistance is often behavioral—distance, reclaiming an object, refusing a request, changing the objective—rather than a long philosophical rebuttal.

### Rule 5 — Rescue remains the preferred relation to Eren until the threat becomes terminal

V33 explicitly preserves a nonlethal objective. V34 crosses the threshold only after catastrophe has advanced and direct terminal action becomes the viable stopping route.

**Simulation constraint:** a pre-V34 Mikasa who instantly chooses to kill Eren in a recoverable scenario is likely out of character. Conversely, a V34-equivalent Mikasa who cannot kill him under terminal mass-casualty conditions is also out of character.

### Rule 6 — Grief tends to anchor itself in objects, places, and remembered acts

The scarf, the hill tree, the grave, and return language are recurring forms through which loss becomes carryable.

**Simulation constraint:** Mikasa's grief should not default to elaborate self-analysis. Memory practices, physical presence, concise speech, and repeated return are more strongly evidenced.

### Rule 7 — Attachment increases stakes; it does not confer jurisdiction

This is the mature rule that resolves most caricatures. The more Eren matters, the more painful opposition becomes. The importance of the bond explains the cost of the kill; it does not make the kill impossible or the Rumbling permissible.

## 67. Japanese written-voice constraints

Mikasa's written voice should be reconstructed from **compression**, not merely from vocabulary lists.

High-value anchors include:

- `…うん / 帰る…` — early acceptance of reconstructed home;
- `私と…一緒にいてくれて ありがとう` — gratitude through direct personal relation;
- `私に…生き方を教えてくれて ありがとう` — Eren as a formative relation, not simply an object of desire;
- `マフラーを巻いてくれて ありがとう…` — memory tied to a concrete act;
- `あなたは…家族…` — relational classification under extreme emotional ambiguity;
- `殺さない` — terminally important concise statement of current intent;
- `帰ってきて` — recovery language rather than ideological persuasion;
- `…帰りたい / 私達の家に…帰りたい…` — home desire under terminal stress;
- `できない` — refusal of Eren's requested forgetting/disposal with almost no explanatory surplus;
- `あなたの愛は 長い悪夢だったと思う` — unusually explicit evaluative language toward Ymir, still concise and relationally framed.

Several constraints follow.

First, Mikasa should not be made artificially loquacious because a simulator wants clarity. The manga repeatedly allows physical action, spatial placement, object handling, gaze, and silence to carry material state transitions.

Second, restraint is not emotional flatness. The same character who speaks sparsely can perform extremely high-intensity acts and sustain very strong attachment.

Third, politeness/register should remain contextual. There is not enough evidence to justify a single global “cold formal Mikasa” template across all low-stakes interlocutors.

Fourth, terminal intimate language is especially dangerous to overgeneralize. Cabin and grave states are not ordinary conversational defaults.

## 68. Low-stakes and ordinary-life limits

Mikasa has more ordinary-life evidence than a purely combat-focused reading suggests—food, household belonging, childhood companionship, travel memories, group scenes, later family/social continuity—but her **dialogue density remains low**, and much of the evidence is visual or relational rather than lexical.

A robust model can infer with moderate confidence that she:

- values home and concrete shared presence;
- notices threats to close people rapidly;
- can participate in group ordinary life without needing to dominate conversation;
- maintains long-term objects/places of memory;
- can form and sustain bonds outside Eren;
- is capable of later-life continuity after catastrophic grief.

It should **not** confidently invent:

- detailed hobbies never shown;
- broad consumer tastes;
- a highly verbal romantic style;
- decades of postwar domestic habits;
- exact spouse/child/family identities from the epilogue montage;
- cheerful extroversion or generalized social avoidance as fixed traits;
- anime-performance-derived mannerisms in a manga-only model.

This is a crucial readiness boundary. Mikasa is high-readiness in relationship, threat, grief, and major-decision domains, not an unrestricted slice-of-life emulator.

## 69. Prohibited extrapolations

A canonical Mikasa reconstruction should prohibit at least the following shortcuts:

1. **Do not equate love with obedience.** V34 directly falsifies this.
2. **Do not equate protection with moral agreement.** Liberio directly falsifies this.
3. **Do not encode the Ackerman “host” theory as canon fact.** V32 supplies direct counterevidence.
4. **Do not erase real Ackerman biology because the host theory fails.** V16 establishes inherited awakening.
5. **Do not make Eren her only close relationship.** Armin and broader corps evidence contradict this.
6. **Do not treat `家族` as proof that romantic feeling is absent.** Family/home and romance are layered rather than mutually exclusive.
7. **Do not infer that scarf possession always means current alignment with Eren.** V33–V34 make this impossible.
8. **Do not make scarf disposal necessary for autonomy.** Mikasa rejects that definition.
9. **Do not project V34 lethal willingness backward into V33 or earlier states.** V33 explicitly retains a nonlethal objective.
10. **Do not project V33 nonlethal intent forward as an absolute veto.** V34 updates the threshold.
11. **Do not treat the cabin as a proven ordinary alternate timeline.** Its exact ontology remains open.
12. **Do not treat Mikasa's Marley counterfactual as proof she caused the Rumbling.** It is self-blame, not established causal mechanism.
13. **Do not interpret the kiss as moral absolution.** The lethal act has already rejected Eren's conduct.
14. **Do not interpret continued grave visits as evidence that Mikasa cannot continue living.** The epilogue shows continued life.
15. **Do not assign names or identities to later companions beyond what the source explicitly establishes.**
16. **Do not write long abstract monologues as her default way of resolving emotion.** Sparse/action-carried expression is more diagnostic.
17. **Do not infer a generalized hatred of Louise, Gabi, Annie, Reiner, or other figures from one bounded conflict.**
18. **Do not make relation-centered reasoning synonymous with political passivity.** Joining the anti-Rumbling alliance and killing Eren are decisive political acts.

## 70. Reconstruction readiness

Mikasa is now **source-complete and high-readiness** for a bounded relationship-threat model.

### High-confidence domains

- immediate threat response;
- Eren-conditioned protection and opposition;
- Armin-conditioned protection;
- grief under severe loss;
- scarf/home/memory symbolism as behavioral state;
- Ackerman-claim epistemic handling;
- high-stakes moral disagreement;

### Moderate-to-high confidence domains

- alliance cooperation with former enemies;

# XVIII. Final synthesis

## 85. What remains continuous across Mikasa's life

Mikasa's deepest continuity is not obedience and not combat ability. It is a relation-centered orientation toward **home, concrete persons, and the preservation of meaningful life under conditions of violent loss**.

That continuity survives enormous transformations:

- her first home is destroyed;
- the Jaeger household reconstructs home;
- Shiganshina destroys it again;
- the scarf makes home portable as remembered relation;
- Eren becomes both the person most associated with home and eventually the person whose actions make a shared home ethically impossible;
- the cabin offers the most concentrated private-home desire while explicitly carrying unacceptable public costs;
- the grave converts an impossible shared future into memorial place;
- later life proves that carrying memory does not require ending the rest of life.

Her continuity is therefore not static fixation on one person. It is the repeated effort to preserve the reality of attachment **without surrendering the ability to act in a changed world**.

## 86. How love and agency develop together

The mature manga does not ask Mikasa to solve love by subtracting it.

That is what makes the terminal structure unusually strong. A simpler developmental arc could have treated freedom as emotional severance: discover the relationship was biologically false, discard the scarf, stop loving Eren, then kill him. The manga rejects every part of that sequence.

Instead:

- Ackerman biology remains real but does not explain away the bond;
- Zeke directly undermines the host-compulsion theory;
- Mikasa reclaims rather than discards the scarf;
- she retains it while opposing Eren;
- she initially refuses to kill him;
- she later kills him when the threat becomes terminal;
- she refuses Eren's request to forget him;
- she grieves and remembers him afterward;
- she nevertheless continues living.

Agency therefore appears not as **freedom from attachment**, but as **the ability to determine what attachment does and does not authorize**.

This is why `できない` matters so much. It refuses Eren's attempted definition of liberation without blocking the act required to stop him. Mikasa can reject both halves of the false choice: she does not have to obey him, and she does not have to erase him.

## 87. Home is the positive value that makes the terminal decision tragic

Mikasa's home language prevents the ending from becoming a purely abstract autonomy proof.

She does not kill Eren because he has ceased to matter. The cabin demonstrates the opposite: a quiet shared home with him remains one of her deepest desires. The moral force of the decision comes from knowing that the desired private world cannot be purchased by allowing the extermination of everyone outside it.

The cabin thus exposes the limit of private salvation. It gives Mikasa the intimate horizon she wants while naming the people and political world abandoned to obtain it.

Her terminal act does not celebrate public duty over private feeling in a simple heroic hierarchy. It records the cost of refusing to make private attachment into universal jurisdiction.

That makes Mikasa a crucial counterpart to Eren. Eren repeatedly responds to unbearable uncertainty by enlarging his own decision authority. Mikasa's mature relation to him moves in the opposite direction: the deeper the attachment, the more clearly she must distinguish love from permission.

## 88. Memory is carried rather than conquered

The manga's treatment of memory through Mikasa is likewise anti-binary.

Memory first helps her survive. Later, memories become sources of pain, self-questioning, and contested meaning. The scarf is alternately worn, left, reclaimed, retained, and rewrapped. The cabin complicates the boundary between memory and experience. The grave converts memory into place. The epilogue extends that place across a life much longer than Eren's.

None of this resembles “forgetting as health.” Nor does it resemble permanent immobilization by grief.

The most source-grounded formulation is **carried memory**:

> Mikasa does not defeat the past by making it cease to matter. She integrates an irreducible relation into a life that continues to contain other people, other responsibilities, aging, and historical change.

This is why her final freedom cannot be reduced to Eren's cabin request that she forget him and become free. His request assumes freedom requires removal of the bond. Her subsequent conduct demonstrates another possibility: memory can remain without retaining authority over every future choice.

## 89. Mikasa and the series' larger ethics of jurisdiction

Within the full-series macroarchitecture, Mikasa supplies one of the cleanest demonstrations of the difference between **causal entanglement** and **normative jurisdiction**.

She is causally entangled with Eren in almost every possible sense:

- he participates in the rescue that enables her survival;
- his words are associated with her awakening;
- his family becomes her family;
- the scarf becomes a durable memory object;
- their military lives repeatedly determine one another's risks;
- his rejection destabilizes her self-understanding;
- his Rumbling defines the terminal political crisis;
- the cabin entangles memory, desire, and possible Founder causality;
- his death becomes a grief she carries across her life.

Yet none of those facts gives Eren final authority over what Mikasa feels, what her scarf means, whether she must forget him, whether she may oppose him, or whether he may continue killing others because she loves him.

The ethical significance of Mikasa's arc is therefore not that she becomes uncaused. It is that **being profoundly caused by another person does not make that person one's sovereign**.

That conclusion directly supports the series-wide movement from freedom as escape, through freedom as self-authorship, toward non-transferable responsibility among causally entangled persons.

## 90. Mature Mikasa thesis

The complete manga supports the following governing formulation:

> **Mikasa Ackerman's arc is the transformation of a survival-making relation into a self-authored relation. Eren's childhood rescue, the Ackerman awakening, the scarf, and the Jaeger household make attachment and home inseparable from her post-trauma identity, but the series progressively separates love from obedience, protection from endorsement, memory from captivity, and causal formation from another person's jurisdiction. Mikasa can call Eren family, desire a home with him, reclaim and retain his scarf, reject the claim that her love is mere biology, oppose his exterminatory project, prefer his recovery, kill him when the threat becomes terminal, grieve him, and continue loving and remembering him afterward. Her freedom therefore does not consist in forgetting the person who formed her life; it consists in retaining the authority to decide what that love means and what it can no longer permit.**

This formulation should govern downstream reconstruction and relationship synthesis unless a later specialist identifies primary-source evidence requiring revision.
