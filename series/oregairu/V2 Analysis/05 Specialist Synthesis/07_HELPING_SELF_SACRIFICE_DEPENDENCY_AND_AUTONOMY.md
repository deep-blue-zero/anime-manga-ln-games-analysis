# 07 — Helping, Self-Sacrifice, Dependency, and Autonomy
## From solution ownership to answerable involvement and chosen interdependence

## 0. Function, scope, and governing thesis

*Oregairu* begins with an institution devoted to helping people solve problems and then spends fourteen main volumes interrogating what “help” actually authorizes.

That tension is present from the premise. Hiratsuka Shizuka decides that Hikigaya Hachiman needs correction and places him in the Service Club without his request. The club's stated ethic, however, is that people should become able to solve their own problems rather than merely having a more capable person perform life for them. The institution is therefore founded through a productive contradiction: **paternalistic entry into an ostensibly autonomy-preserving practice**.

The series never resolves that contradiction by adopting a simple request-only ethic or by rejecting intervention. It instead keeps producing cases in which all of the following may be true at once:

- someone genuinely needs help;
- the person cannot cleanly ask for it;
- a helper sees a mechanism accurately;
- the helper has good reason to care;
- the helper can produce an effective outcome;
- the helper bears substantial personal cost;
- and the method still takes too much ownership over another person's problem.

The V2 synthesis is therefore:

> **The central ethical problem of helping in *Oregairu* is not selfishness versus selflessness, dependence versus independence, or intervention versus nonintervention. It is authorship and jurisdiction. Care, insight, competence, promise, sacrifice, and love can all supply reasons to act without automatically supplying authority to decide. The series gradually differentiates legitimate influence from substituted choice, self-borne cost from collateral cost, reliance from incapacity, and self-authorship from isolation. Its mature positive model is answerable involvement inside chosen interdependence: people may advise, influence, support, compete, rely, refuse, fail, and remain attached while preserving the affected person's meaningful standing to answer, revise, and live with the result.**

The two Phase-4 ledgers give the compact formulas that govern this document:

> **Caring about someone ≠ having reason to act ≠ having authority to decide.**

and:

> **Autonomy is not solitude. Dependence is not falsity. The decisive question is whether involvement expands or displaces another person's authorship.**

This document therefore treats the architecture's required domains as one connected ethical system:

- request ownership;
- Hachiman's intervention methods;
- Yukino's competence and boundaries;
- Yui's mediation and preservation;
- immediate success versus long-term cost;
- self-sacrifice;
- humiliation;
- reputation;
- autonomy;
- reliance and dependence;
- the late `共依存` diagnosis;
- and chosen interdependence.

The architecture also imposes a central test:

> **Do not assume either that effective outcomes justify methods or that emotional cost proves methods never worked.**

That double refusal matters because *Oregairu* repeatedly makes ethically troubling interventions instrumentally successful. The critique would be much weaker if every manipulative plan simply failed. Hachiman often does understand the mechanism. His plans often do move people. Yukino's overfunctioning often does keep institutions alive. Yui's accommodation often does preserve communication. Haruno's provocations often do expose a real weakness.

The hard question is what kind of success leaves the people inside it still able to recognize the result as theirs.

# 1. Ethical vocabulary: five questions that must not collapse

The V2 Request / Intervention Ethics Ledger separates five questions that characters repeatedly conflate:

1. **Who owns the problem?**
2. **Who has reason to care or act?**
3. **Who authorized what kind of involvement?**
4. **Who chooses the method and bears its costs?**
5. **Who retains the right to define whether the result counts as help?**

These questions are related but nonidentical.

A friend may have reason to care without possessing authority to choose.

A requester may authorize advice without authorizing coercion.

A teacher may have institutional authority to assign work without possessing unlimited authority over private feeling.

A helper may consent to his own humiliation without consenting on behalf of friends who must absorb relational consequences.

A person may refuse direct help without refusing future relationship, rivalry, advice, or ordinary presence.

A result may be objectively beneficial in one domain while being experienced by the subject as a violation in another.

This is why “Did it work?” cannot be the sole ethical question.

## 1.1 Authorization is granular

One of the most important full-series gains is **authorization granularity**.

Permission to:

- care;
- listen;
- advise;
- investigate;
- participate;
- disclose information;
- change incentives;
- decide;
- coerce;
- or bear cost

are not interchangeable.

The series' most troubling interventions often begin from a real permission and silently expand it.

Tobe asks for help pursuing Ebina. That does not automatically authorize Hachiman to substitute his own confession and decide what form Ebina's rejection will take.

Yukino asks Hachiman to witness what she does. That does not grant blanket permission to rescue her whenever he predicts she will struggle.

A promise to help can create obligation and motive without preserving the exact intervention authority of the original situation indefinitely.

A client can authorize a Service Club request without authorizing every consequence for third parties whose lives are also implicated.

Thus the mature ethical question becomes:

> **What exactly was authorized, by whom, for which domain, under what information, and with what exit rights?**

## 1.2 Problem ownership is often plural

The Service Club frequently encounters multi-owner problems.

Kyoto is the clearest early case. Tobe owns his romantic desire. Ebina owns her romantic boundary. Hayama and Miura have legitimate stakes in group continuity. The Service Club owns none of their lives.

Likewise, the later triad cannot be treated as though any one participant owns “the relationship.” Hachiman can choose his desire. Yukino can choose hers. Yui can choose hers. None can privately author all three futures merely because they are willing to sacrifice their own.

Plural ownership therefore blocks a recurrent moral shortcut:

> **I am willing to pay the cost, therefore I may choose the plan.**

In relational systems, a cost is rarely perfectly private.

## 1.3 The helped person retains interpretive standing

A helper does not possess final authority to declare another person saved, freed, healed, or fulfilled.

This principle appears gradually but becomes indispensable by the late series. Hachiman often judges outcomes from the perspective of structural success: the group remained intact, the event ran, the client avoided humiliation, the relationship was protected, the choice environment changed.

But *Oregairu* repeatedly returns interpretive standing to the people who must live inside those outcomes.

Rumi's later reactions matter.

Iroha's ownership of the presidency matters.

Yukino's statement of what kind of help she does or does not want matters.

Yui's refusal to be protected through ambiguity matters.

The ethical endpoint therefore does not imagine a perfectly neutral helper. It requires a helper who remains **answerable to the subject's account of what the help became**.

# 2. The founding paradox: autonomy is taught through an intervention Hachiman did not request

Hiratsuka's decision to place Hachiman in the Service Club is ethically mixed from the beginning.

She is not responding to a recipient-authored request. She is using teacher authority, her own diagnosis of Hachiman's social and moral development, and a coercive school mechanism to create an environment she believes will benefit him.

From a strict noninterference model, the founding is difficult to justify.

Yet the series also treats Hiratsuka as one of its best later examples of adult intervention because her method evolves toward **question-preserving pressure**. She creates circumstances, introduces counterarguments, supplies expressive tools, and sometimes pushes forcefully, but her strongest interventions return the final answer to the student rather than installing her own.

The contradiction is important because it prevents the series from becoming libertarian in the thin sense of “never act without an explicit request.”

Children, students, friends, and distressed people may not always be able to formulate the request they need.

Institutions already distribute power.

Silence can preserve a harmful equilibrium.

Withholding advice can itself become an act with consequences.

The mature ethic must therefore handle **justified influence without converting influence into sovereignty**.

That problem is already visible in Volume 1.

## 2.1 Requests do not automatically create duty

Totsuka's early request gives an important positive control. Hachiman can refuse an unsustainable demand. A request creates standing for an answer; it does not automatically create an obligation to obey.

This matters later when characters treat promises, friendship, or romantic significance as though they erase refusal.

The right to ask and the right to say no must coexist.

## 2.2 Hachiman's early closure of Yui demonstrates interpretive paternalism

When Hachiman interprets Yui's kindness through the traffic-accident/debt model, he attempts to settle the relationship by deciding why she is kind and therefore what the correct resolution should be.

His move is framed as protection from false expectation. It also refuses Yui's standing to define her current motive.

This is an early version of a pattern that later becomes much more consequential:

> **Hachiman converts explanation into jurisdiction.**

He often sees a plausible mechanism and then treats the mechanism as permission to decide what should happen.

Document 02 has already established that this is one of the central ways his epistemology becomes ethical procedure.

# 3. Volume 4 — Rumi and the failure of both pure request ethics and unrestricted inference

The Rumi case is one of the most important ethical thought experiments in the series because it breaks two easy positions at once.

Yukino initially articulates a principled threshold:

> `彼女が助けを求めるなら`

The strength of this position is clear. Rumi should remain a subject rather than becoming an improvement project for older students. Help should not be imposed simply because competent observers dislike the situation.

But Yui identifies the problem with a strict request-only ethic: shame, age, group pressure, fear, and social exclusion can make desire difficult to formulate cleanly.

Thus:

> **absence of explicit request is not proof of absence of need.**

The result is not permission for unlimited paternalism. It is a harder obligation: helpers may need to notice constrained expression while remaining humble about what inference authorizes.

## 3.1 Hachiman's social-system disruption: ethically troubling and partly agency-producing

Hachiman's plan deliberately manufactures fear, breaks the coalition structure among the children, and creates conditions in which Rumi's prior isolation pattern can change.

Rumi does not authorize the intervention.

The older collaborators have substantial awareness of their own roles, but the children do not.

The old group form is destabilized.

Yet the decisive action Rumi takes is not simply scripted by Hachiman. The intervention creates an opening in which she acts beyond his exact design.

This is why Volume 4 cannot be summarized as either:

> manipulation works, therefore it is justified

or:

> manipulation is coercive, therefore no agency was produced.

The V2 classification is more difficult:

> **unauthorized social-system engineering with mixed agency outcome.**

The canonical lesson is that an intervention can create conditions for autonomous action without itself being fully autonomy-respecting.

That distinction becomes essential later. The series is often interested in **agency produced through compromised procedure**.

The correct response is not to deny the agency. It is to keep the compromise visible.

# 4. Volumes 6–6.5 — competence, substitution, humiliation, and self-sacrifice as control

The cultural-festival arc is the first large consolidation of the series' helping ethics because it places Yukino's and Hachiman's characteristic strengths next to each other.

Yukino overfunctions.

Hachiman engineers.

Both achieve results.

Both risk replacing the people they are ostensibly helping.

## 4.1 Yukino: substitutionary competence

Sagami asks for help functioning as cultural-festival chair. Yukino increasingly performs the role's work while Sagami retains the title.

This arrangement is understandable. Yukino is capable. The festival has deadlines. Other people depend on successful execution. Allowing avoidable failure for the sake of abstract principle would itself impose costs.

But the arrangement has a developmental consequence: the more Yukino performs, the fewer opportunities Sagami has to practice the role she nominally owns.

The institution also learns a dangerous lesson: when the formal owner is weak, the competent person will absorb the deficit.

Thus competence becomes **substitutionary**.

The ethical problem is not that Yukino is secretly controlling because she enjoys power. It is that capability can acquire jurisdiction by default.

A task gets done.

The subject becomes less capable of owning it.

This is why the late series must distinguish:

> **supporting capability**

from:

> **using one's capability to become the de facto owner of another person's responsibility.**

## 4.2 Hachiman: self-sacrifice is not automatically self-contained

Hachiman's committee provocation and rooftop recovery of Sagami reveal the corresponding problem in his method.

His reputation becomes an instrument.

He is willing to be disliked.

That willingness is real evidence of investment. It also produces a morally seductive appearance: if the primary injury lands on Hachiman and he consents to it, perhaps the plan is ethically clean.

The series eventually rejects that inference.

Self-sacrifice has **relational externalities**.

Yukino and Yui care about what happens to him.

Hayama is assigned rescuer status in a script he did not authorize.

Sagami is deliberately humiliated and emotionally cornered.

Other students are pushed into predictable roles.

Hachiman can consent to becoming the villain. He cannot thereby consent for everyone else to live inside the villain/rescuer/victim structure he constructs.

This is the full meaning of the V2 phrase **self-sacrifice as control**.

It does not mean every self-sacrificial action is secretly selfish or manipulative.

It means self-sacrifice can function as a control technique when the actor uses his own pain to purchase unilateral decision authority over a shared problem.

## 4.3 Restoring formal agency can still violate deliberative agency

The rooftop case is especially important because Sagami does eventually move. Hachiman's method restores her to the formal chair role.

That success should not be denied.

But the route is deliberately engineered through humiliation, shame, anger, predicted rescue, and social pressure.

The V2 ethical distinction is:

> **restoring formal agency does not erase violation of deliberative agency on the route to that restoration.**

A person may end in the correct office, make the required speech, or perform the needed act while having had the choice environment heavily commandeered.

This becomes a recurring question in later choice-architecture cases.

## 4.4 Volume 6.5 shows that rehabilitation is also procedural

The sports-festival material complicates the cultural-festival pattern by refusing to let Sagami remain a permanently fixed “failed client.” Different interventions give her further opportunities to own action, fail differently, and become legible beyond the rooftop crisis.

The point is not that later improvement retroactively justifies prior humiliation.

It is that ethical analysis must include **aftermath**.

The helper remains answerable for what happens after the immediate solution.

A mature helping model cannot treat the apparent resolution as the end of responsibility.

# 5. Volume 7 — Kyoto: technical success without adequate jurisdiction

The Kyoto confession case is the strongest early demonstration that **accurate prediction does not create authority**.

Hachiman reads several relevant desires correctly.

Tobe wants to confess.

Ebina does not want the romantic pressure to produce the expected couple outcome.

Hayama wants the group preserved.

The current social structure is fragile.

Hachiman's false confession prevents Tobe's confession from reaching its direct form and protects group continuity.

Instrumentally, the method is successful.

Ethically, it is much more complicated.

Tobe's speech opportunity is substituted.

Ebina's answer is indirectly managed.

Hayama's preference for group preservation becomes part of a plan he does not fully authorize.

Yukino and Yui experience collateral emotional cost.

Hachiman again uses his own reputation as the expendable instrument.

The key problem is not that he misunderstands everyone.

The problem is that **understanding becomes permission**.

The canonical rule from the Request / Intervention Ethics Ledger is therefore:

> **Accurate understanding of a relationship system does not confer jurisdiction over the people inside it.**

This is one of the most important corrections to any reading that admires Hachiman primarily for being right about how people will behave.

Prediction is epistemic competence.

Jurisdiction is an ethical relation.

They must remain separate.

# 6. Volume 8 — Iroha, choice architecture, and the asymmetry of Hachiman's autonomy ethic

The student-council election arc is a major advance because Hachiman explicitly articulates an anti-coercion principle in relation to Iroha:

> `一色を無理矢理生徒会長にさせる気はない`

He recognizes that his reasons do not authorize forcing Iroha to absorb the office and its costs.

That is genuine development.

But the same volume exposes a crucial asymmetry.

## 6.1 Choice architecture is better than substitution—but still ethically mixed

Hachiman changes Iroha's environment so that accepting the presidency becomes more attractive. He manipulates apparent support, rivalry, incentives, Hayama proximity, and fear of failure.

Iroha recognizes enough of the frame to participate strategically.

Formal choice remains.

This is better than simply forcing her into office.

Yet informational conditions are also manipulated.

The result is neither pure consent nor pure coercion.

The V2 category **choice architecture with deception** is useful because it refuses the false binary between free untouched choice and total domination.

Most real choices occur inside shaped environments.

The ethical issue is whether the shaping becomes covert ownership of the answer.

## 6.2 Hachiman protects Yukino and Yui by deleting their reason—and therefore their choice

The volume's sharper criticism occurs when Hachiman applies the autonomy principle unevenly.

Yukino says her candidacy is her own will.

Yui also authors her candidacy despite mixed motives.

Hachiman resolves the election problem in a way that removes the external need for either to run and then treats their candidacies as no longer necessary.

This is benevolent.

It is also option foreclosure.

Desire has been translated into necessity. Once necessity is removed, Hachiman behaves as though the desire has lost standing.

The scene exposes an important intimacy failure:

> **Familiarity can reduce respect for explicit preference when the helper believes he possesses privileged access to the person's hidden good.**

The people Hachiman cares about most can become the people whose answers he most readily overwrites because he believes he knows what will protect them.

Care intensifies motive.

It does not intensify jurisdiction in the same proportion.

# 7. Volume 9 — the ethical reversal: Hachiman becomes requester

Volume 9 matters not only because Hachiman says `俺は、本物が欲しい` but because the **grammar of intervention reverses**.

For much of the series, Hachiman asks:

> What is the real problem and how can I solve it?

Volume 9 forces him to ask instead:

> What do I want, and can I put that want before the others without controlling their answer?

The `本物` request is therefore an ethical event as much as an authenticity event.

Hachiman becomes requester.

He exposes desire rather than converting desire into diagnosis.

He does not know exactly what the request entails.

Yui prevents the conversation from collapsing prematurely.

Yukino accepts an unfinished request without pretending complete understanding.

The authorization is **ongoing and revisable**.

This is the first mature model of request as self-limitation of jurisdiction.

## 7.1 Asking is not weak control—it is relinquishment of control over outcome

Hachiman's previous methods often achieve certainty by designing the outcome.

Request changes the risk structure.

To ask is to concede that the other person may answer differently.

The helper becomes claimant rather than solver.

The person who speaks desire is now exposed to refusal rather than protected by technique.

That vulnerability is ethically productive because it returns causal significance to the other person.

## 7.2 `助けてね` is not blanket authorization

Yukino's later `助けてね` matters because it makes reliance speakable.

But V2 must resist reading the line as unlimited future permission.

Authorization remains domain-specific and temporally revisable.

The later prom conflict proves that a prior invitation to help cannot erase a later explicit boundary.

Promises and requests create history.

They do not create permanent ownership over the person's future decisions.

# 8. Volumes 10–11 — trust sometimes means not taking over

The post-`本物` volumes refine autonomy by demonstrating that intervention is not always the mature answer.

## 8.1 Hiratsuka's final-authorship principle

One of Hiratsuka's strongest recurring lessons is that the person at the center of a problem must retain the final act that makes the result theirs.

This principle does not demand abandonment.

A mentor can provide language, pressure, options, critique, and resources.

The key is not to convert pedagogical power into answer substitution.

Hiratsuka's mature adult role therefore becomes a positive model for the entire series:

> **intervention can be strong while remaining question-preserving.**

## 8.2 `信じて任せる`: non-intervention can be trust

Yui's Volume-11 emphasis on entrusting rather than taking over supplies another important positive control.

Sometimes help means refusing to solve what another person can own.

This is particularly important for Yukino, whose competence and burden-bearing habits are easily reinforced by others either substituting for her or treating her as the substitute for them.

But the lesson must remain bounded.

Non-intervention can be trust.

It can also be abandonment.

The mature ethic therefore asks why one is not acting, what the person has asked, whether they possess capability, and whether silence merely transfers responsibility.

## 8.3 Yui's preservation proposal is a warning against moralizing kindness

Yui's proposal to preserve “everything” is caring and self-authored.

It is also capable of pre-authoring the triad's future.

This prevents an overly flattering view of her relational intelligence.

Kindness does not automatically preserve agency.

A mediator can become controlling if maintaining harmony is allowed to outrank another person's right to force an incompatible answer.

The same is true in reverse for Hachiman's autonomy rhetoric: telling another person what independence requires can itself become paternalistic.

The series thus rejects both:

> preservation is always caring

and:

> separation is always autonomy-preserving.

# 9. Volume 12 — `依存`, `共依存`, and the danger of converting diagnosis into cure

Volume 12 makes dependency explicit.

Haruno's `共依存` diagnosis has genuine evidentiary force because several reinforcement loops are now visible:

- Hachiman derives self-worth and relational license from being needed and solving;
- Yukino can come to expect intervention and transfer difficult burdens;
- Yui can displace a decisive choice through Yukino's self-removal;
- the triad can use tasks, requests, and preservation to postpone direct answers.

V2 therefore does **not** dismiss the diagnosis.

But it refuses to let diagnosis determine ontology or remedy.

The governing limit is:

> `共依存は仕組みだ。気持ちじゃない。`

A mechanism can be unhealthy without proving that the feelings inside it are false.

## 9.1 Haruno sees a mechanism but does not acquire remedial sovereignty

Haruno's value is adversarial. She identifies patterns the protagonists would prefer to soften.

But diagnosing a mechanism does not grant the diagnostician exclusive authority to prescribe separation, independence, or proof of relational truth.

This is the same jurisdiction rule applied at a higher conceptual level.

Accurate social diagnosis does not create jurisdiction.

Accurate psychological diagnosis within the story does not create jurisdiction either.

## 9.2 Yukino's `私にやらせて`: autonomy as authorship, not solitary competence

Yukino's prom position is frequently misread as needing to prove she can succeed without Hachiman.

That formulation underweights what the earlier volumes have already shown: Yukino is capable.

The deeper issue is authorship.

Her request `私にやらせて` asks that the consequential act remain hers.

She wants to be able to own success, failure, regret, and relinquishment rather than have another person's competence erase those possibilities.

This is why the prom is not a test of whether Yukino can function alone.

It is a test of whether she can have a future-affecting choice recognized as hers before certainty is available.

## 9.3 Hachiman's promise supplies motive without resolving authority

Hachiman cites the old promise:

> `いつか、助けるって約束したから`

The promise matters. It explains why withdrawal feels morally false to him. It gives his involvement historical continuity and personal seriousness.

But the promise cannot simply override Yukino's current boundary.

This becomes one of the cleanest examples of **motive/authority separation**:

- he has reason to act;
- he has an old commitment;
- he has a personal stake;
- he does not have uncomplicated present authorization for direct rescue.

The ethical task is therefore method redesign, not pretending the motive disappears.

# 10. Volume 13 — dependency diagnosis becomes experiment

Volume 13 is the series' strongest attempt to test whether changing the structure changes the mechanism.

Yukino owns the prom.

Hachiman is refused as direct helper.

He creates an independent rival prom.

Collaborators receive much more information and meaningful participation than in his early manipulations.

The result is real development without procedural purity.

## 10.1 Direct-help refusal is not global relational refusal

This distinction is one of the major V2 gains.

Yukino can refuse Hachiman as direct helper while accepting him as rival.

A “no” to one role is not a “no” to all relation.

This is **granular authorization** in practice.

The mature helper must be capable of hearing:

> not like that

without translating it into:

> never be involved again.

Likewise, a person who wants continued relation cannot treat:

> I still care

as permission to return to the refused method.

## 10.2 The dummy prom shows improved collaborator ethics and persistent target manipulation

Hachiman's later institutional engineering is more sophisticated than his early methods.

He relies on collaborators more openly.

He shares information.

He assigns less unilateral cost.

He can thank and rely on others.

This is genuine improvement.

But the dummy prom still manipulates institutional choice architecture. Better collaborator authorization does not automatically cleanse target-level intervention.

This distinction matters because development in *Oregairu* is often **partial and domain-specific**.

Hachiman can become more ethical with collaborators before becoming fully answerable to the person whose future motivates the plan.

## 10.3 “I still want to act when she does not need me” weakens neededness as a total theory

Hachiman's realization that desire persists even when Yukino does not need rescue is developmentally important.

It means his attachment cannot be exhausted by functional necessity.

But V2 rejects the stronger inference:

> therefore the relationship is not codependent.

Wanting involvement without being needed demonstrates a motive beyond neededness.

It does not prove every reinforcement mechanism has disappeared.

The correct transition is:

> **STRENGTHEN the evidence that Hachiman's motive exceeds neededness; REJECT the claim that this alone disproves `共依存`.**

## 10.4 Yukino's self-authored closure demonstrates why authorship is necessary but not sufficient

Yukino's late request that attempts to settle the triad is genuinely hers.

This matters because V2 does not want to erase agency whenever a choice is painful or mistaken.

But the request also affects Hachiman and Yui's futures.

Thus:

> **self-authorship can still overreach other subjects.**

Autonomy is not simply “a person made the choice herself.”

Where lives overlap, autonomy ethics must remain plural.

---

# 11. Volume 14 — from rescue logic to reciprocal claim

Volume 14 does not prove that all earlier dependency concerns were false.

It changes the procedure under which relation continues.

## 11.1 Hachiman removes incapacity and neededness as his justification

The key dependency shift is that Hachiman no longer needs Yukino to be incapable in order to justify wanting involvement.

He can separate:

> **desire for involvement**

from:

> **need to be needed as the moral license for involvement.**

This is a major autonomy gain because it permits support to be chosen rather than medically or morally necessitated.

It also forces Hachiman to own his stake.

If Yukino can manage without him, then he cannot hide continued attachment behind a rescue function.

He must say, in effect, that he wants to remain involved because he wants the relation.

## 11.2 The bridge must not be sanitized

The bridge scene is ethically important precisely because it is procedurally imperfect.

Hachiman initially constrains Yukino's exit physically.

A later yes does not retroactively authorize that act.

The scene's ethical gain lies in the visible procedural transition:

1. physical constraint loosens and ends;
2. Yukino remains;
3. she interrogates the meaning of his claim;
4. Hachiman permits rejection or nonanswer;
5. Yukino refuses to let nonanswer substitute for her speech;
6. she makes a reciprocal request.

The movement is therefore:

> **coercive retention → continued presence → inquiry → refusal capacity → reciprocal claim.**

This is a far more useful model than “the confession proves everything Hachiman did was justified.”

Romantic success cannot launder procedure.

## 11.3 `一人で立てるのに` / `知ってる`: chosen support under acknowledged capability

The later hand exchange provides the cleanest positive image of the series' terminal autonomy model.

Yukino can stand alone.

Hachiman knows she can stand alone.

He offers support anyway.

She accepts anyway.

The ethical meaning is produced by the removal of incapacity as justification.

This is why **chosen interdependence** is stronger than either dependence or independence as the terminal description.

Support does not need to prove weakness.

Capability does not require refusal of support.

Closeness does not need to be licensed by crisis.

## 11.4 Yui's continued standing prevents romantic choice from becoming an autonomy purge

Yui's role matters equally to the final autonomy model.

A crude “healthy independence” ending could solve the romantic triangle by removing the nonselected person from the relational system.

The series does not do that.

Yui receives a painful answer and remains a subject with friendship, institutional standing, preferences, and future choices.

Her final Service Club request brings an unresolved problem into the institution without asking for false symmetry or disappearance.

The mature club can host a problem it cannot eradicate.

That is one of the strongest indicators that helping has ceased to mean “produce a clean terminal state.”

# 12. Volume 14.5 — the final autonomy correction: influence is not control, and noninterference is not purity

The true post-ending stories provide the best low-temperature test of the ethical model because the characters are no longer under the same crisis pressure.

## 12.1 Transparent pretext remains imperfect but usable

Iroha's `プレゼントは言い訳ですよ` makes the pretext visible.

Shared recognition improves answerability because the frame is no longer pretending to be the entire reason for contact.

But visibility does not automatically create perfect consent. Iroha still exerts pressure and moves toward plans before every answer is crisply formalized.

The point is not that mature characters stop influencing one another.

The point is that influence becomes **more legible and more answerable**.

## 12.2 The succession double bind rejects both control and abdication

Hachiman's anxiety over Komachi's stewardship of the Service Club is one of the most conceptually important final cases.

If he tells her to preserve the existing club, his attachment may become her obligation.

If he says only:

> `自分で決めればいい`

he may simply transfer responsibility while withholding relevant history, preference, and advice.

The mature realization is that both control and abdication can be autonomy failures.

A meaningful relationship creates legitimate reasons to speak.

The ethical task is not silence.

It is to state one's view without converting the view into ownership.

## 12.3 Yui gives a positive model of preference without jurisdiction

Yui can say that the club should recruit new members.

Her opinion is consequential.

It influences Komachi.

But Yui does not treat the opinion as the decision.

This is a mature form of relational influence:

> **speech without ownership.**

## 12.4 Komachi demonstrates influenced authorship

Komachi's eventual leaning is visibly shaped by Iroha, Yui, Hachiman, Yukino, institutional history, and her own relationship to the club.

That influence does not make the choice inauthentic.

Autonomy is not causal isolation.

A person is not less autonomous merely because others matter.

The relevant question is whether the person retains meaningful capacity to answer, revise, and own the result.

Thus the final autonomy rule is:

> **Influence is inevitable in meaningful relationships. The ethical task is to give honest advice, disclose one's stake, avoid substituting one's will, and leave the other person's answer revisable.**

# 13. Self-sacrifice: taxonomy rather than moral halo

One of the largest V1→V2 revisions is the treatment of self-sacrifice.

V1 was correct to identify Hachiman's willingness to injure his own reputation as a central pattern. V2 retains that insight but rejects self-sacrifice as the master explanation for every intervention problem.

Self-sacrifice is better understood as a **cost-allocation technique**.

## 13.1 Cooperative self-cost

A person may knowingly take on cost as part of a jointly understood plan.

This can be ethically ordinary when:

- collaborators understand the method;
- costs are proportionate;
- third parties are not silently assigned roles;
- refusal remains possible;
- and the self-cost does not purchase ownership over others.

## 13.2 Protective self-cost

A person may absorb hardship to shield someone else from a legitimate danger.

This can be defensible, especially where asymmetry of capacity or role responsibility matters.

But protection becomes paternalism when the subject's own judgment is erased simply because the helper can tolerate more pain.

## 13.3 Instrumental self-cost

Hachiman often uses his reputation as a tool because he believes he can absorb damage more cheaply than others.

This is rational cost optimization.

It is not automatically ethical authorization.

Resilience does not create permission to redistribute relational consequences.

## 13.4 Coercive self-sacrifice

The strongest failure occurs when self-harm becomes part of a social script designed to compel others into predictable roles.

The rooftop is the clearest case.

Hachiman's willingness to be hated helps make the plan work.

It also gives him control over a drama others did not choose.

Thus self-sacrifice can be morally admirable in motive and controlling in procedure at the same time.

## 13.5 Identity-reinforcing self-sacrifice

By the late series, self-sacrifice also has a dependency dimension.

Being the person who solves, absorbs, or suffers can provide identity, value, and relational license.

This is why “I bear my own cost” does not establish independence.

The solver may be dependent on the role of solver.

V2 therefore rejects:

> **self-sacrifice = selflessness = autonomy.**

The relevant questions remain authorization, cost distribution, authorship, and reinforcement.

# 14. Immediate success and long-term cost must be separate axes

The architecture's central test requires a two-axis model.

## 14.1 Outcome axis

Ask:

- Did the event happen?
- Did the group stabilize?
- Did the client gain an option?
- Did the office function?
- Did the person avoid a worse harm?
- Did the relationship continue?

## 14.2 Procedure/autonomy axis

Ask:

- Who defined success?
- Who authorized the method?
- Who knew what was happening?
- Who absorbed cost?
- Whose speech was blocked?
- Whose options were narrowed?
- Could refusal alter the plan?
- Could the subject revise the outcome later?

These axes can diverge.

| Case | Immediate effectiveness | Autonomy / procedure |
|---|---|---|
| Rumi social disruption | partial structural success | unauthorized, coercive, yet creates room for unscripted action |
| cultural-festival substitution | high operational effectiveness | weak developmental authorship for Sagami; overfunctioning reinforced |
| rooftop recovery | high immediate effectiveness | coercive and role-assigning despite self-cost |
| Kyoto false confession | high group-preservation effectiveness | unauthorized substitution across multiple stakeholders |
| Iroha election choice architecture | effective | improved formal choice but compromised information |
| V9 `本物` request | no clean immediate solution | major procedural improvement through requester vulnerability |
| V11 entrustment | deliberately less controlling | stronger trust when subject can own task |
| V13 rival prom | strategically effective | improved collaborator authorization; target/institution ethics remain mixed |
| V14 reciprocal claim | produces concrete answer | procedurally imperfect beginning, then strong answerability transition |
| V14 hand exchange | low-stakes practical help | strongest mature positive control: support under acknowledged capability |
| V14.5 succession advice | no guaranteed outcome | mature influence without ownership |

This matrix prevents two opposite mistakes.

First:

> **success does not retroactively authorize method.**

Second:

> **ethical imperfection does not mean the intervention had no beneficial effect.**

The series' moral seriousness depends on preserving both truths.

# 15. Hachiman's helping arc: from solver sovereignty to owned stake

Hachiman receives the most sustained ethical development because his intelligence makes unilateral intervention unusually tempting.

## 15.1 Early strength: willingness to see incentives others avoid

Hachiman's pessimism is often instrumentally useful.

He recognizes status pressure, hypocrisy, embarrassment, group preservation, fear of rejection, and incentives that more polite characters may prefer not to name.

The problem is not that his social model is always wrong.

The problem is that epistemic confidence can become moral jurisdiction.

## 15.2 Middle failure: self-sacrificial solver identity

As relationships deepen, Hachiman continues to justify methods through his willingness to absorb cost.

But the cost is no longer private.

People care about him.

His actions alter their trust.

His reputation affects the shared social world.

The more he becomes necessary as solver, the more solving also becomes a way to secure relational relevance.

This is where self-sacrifice and dependency intersect.

## 15.3 Volume 9: requesterhood as ethical growth

The `本物` request changes Hachiman's position from manager of other people's meaning to person with a claim that others can answer.

That is not the end of his overengineering.

It is the first durable alternative to it.

## 15.4 Volume 13: collaborator reliance

Hachiman's increased ability to rely on Totsuka, Zaimokuza, Iroha, Hayama, and others matters because it weakens the fantasy that the ethical alternative to dependence is solitary competence.

His `ありがとう、頼りにしてる` type of language is developmentally important precisely because it normalizes being supported without converting support into shame.

## 15.5 Volume 14: desire replaces rescue as license

The endpoint is not Hachiman giving up intervention.

He still engineers.

He still manipulates institutions.

He still acts strongly.

What changes is that he becomes more willing to say:

> **I want this.**

That disclosure removes some of the false neutrality of the solver role.

His desire can now be rejected as desire rather than hidden inside a theory of what somebody else needs.

The mature Hachiman is therefore not noninterventionist.

He has a more answerable relationship to influence, dependency, and authorship.

---

# 16. Yukino's helping arc: from corrective competence to authored boundaries and chosen reliance

Yukino's intervention ethic begins differently from Hachiman's.

She is more explicitly committed to preserving the subject's capacity to act.

But her competence creates a different danger: **substitutionary overfunctioning**.

## 16.1 Early ethic: correction over comfort

Yukino wants help to improve capacity rather than simply soothe.

That principle has real value.

Its weakness is that she can underestimate constrained expression and overestimate how easily another person can act once told the correct thing.

## 16.2 Cultural festival: competence becomes hidden ownership

Her ability keeps the institution functioning while Sagami's role hollows out.

This becomes a major lesson: one can believe deeply in autonomy while structurally training dependence through overfunctioning.

## 16.3 Later requests: reliance becomes personally meaningful

Yukino's progression from bounded practical requests to future-facing `助けてね`, then to explicit boundaries such as `私にやらせて`, demonstrates that reliance and autonomy are not opposites.

She can ask for help.

She can refuse a form of help.

She can later accept a different role for the same person.

That is more autonomous than either permanent self-sufficiency or permanent rescue.

## 16.4 V13 closure: self-authorship is not ethical infallibility

Yukino's self-removal proposal is hers.

V2 preserves that authorship.

It also preserves the fact that she attempts to settle too much for Hachiman and Yui.

This prevents autonomy from becoming a magic word that sanitizes any self-originated choice.

## 16.5 Terminal state: capability plus chosen support

The V14 hand exchange and V14.5 study/future material establish the mature formulation:

> **Yukino becomes more autonomous not by needing less relation, but by gaining greater authorship over the relation she chooses.**

---

# 17. Yui's helping arc: mediation, preservation, and the right to force an answer

Yui's relational labor often looks gentler than Hachiman's or Yukino's, but it raises the same jurisdiction question.

## 17.1 Early strength: constrained-expression sensitivity

Yui notices that people may need help they cannot formulate cleanly.

This corrects Yukino's early request threshold without requiring unlimited inference.

She is especially strong at creating low-threat openings for speech.

## 17.2 Mediation can preserve agency

Yui often translates, invites, names, and keeps a socially fragile participant present long enough to answer.

At its best, atmosphere work expands agency.

## 17.3 Preservation can become decision displacement

By V11–V12, the same skill can defer an answer whose cost Yui fears.

Her self-description `あたしは彼女に依存したの` is best understood narrowly as decision displacement in that context.

She allows Yukino's self-denial to carry a choice Yui is afraid to own directly.

This is a dependency mechanism.

It is not proof that Yui lacks selfhood or that the friendship itself is pathological.

## 17.4 Mature Yui: preference without ownership

Yui's later development consists not in becoming less relational but in allowing relation to carry disagreement.

She rejects rescue ambiguity.

She refuses Yukino's unilateral disappearance.

She asks for answers.

In V14.5 she can state a strong opinion about Komachi's club decision while leaving the decision Komachi's.

Thus Yui becomes one of the clearest positive models of **influence without jurisdiction**.

# 18. `共依存` audit: mechanism, not total ontology

The late codependency vocabulary is analytically useful only if kept precise.

## 18.1 What it explains well

### Hachiman's neededness loop

Helping supplies value, role, proximity, and a reason to avoid naked desire.

### Yukino's expectation loop

Repeated intervention can make it easier to assume Hachiman will somehow solve the difficult problem, reducing practice in authorship.

### Yui's decision-displacement loop

Preserving everyone can become easier when the decisive renunciation is routed through Yukino.

### Triadic avoidance

Tasks, rules, and preservation can substitute for direct choice.

### Mutual role reinforcement

Overfunctioning and underpractice can sustain one another.

## 18.2 What it does not establish

The diagnosis does not prove that:

- the feelings are counterfeit;
- all three are dependent in the same way;
- Yukino is generally incapable;
- Yui has no agency;
- Hachiman acts only to be needed;
- healthy reliance is impossible;
- separation is the unique cure;
- romantic choice certifies health;
- or Haruno possesses diagnostic sovereignty.

## 18.3 Why separation is an experiment, not the answer

Changing structure can be useful because it interrupts reinforcement and reveals hidden habits.

That is why Volume 13 matters.

But a separation test can itself become borrowed doctrine.

If a person withdraws because someone else has told them that independence is what genuine autonomy requires, the resulting “independence” may be weakly authored.

Thus the series eventually rejects:

> dependence bad / separation good

in favor of:

> **which form of involvement preserves capability, refusal, consequence ownership, and revisability?**

# 19. Autonomy is not noninterference

The completed V2 evidence rejects three tempting autonomy models.

## 19.1 Maximal independence

The idea that healthy people should stand alone fails against the text.

Hachiman grows by asking and relying.

Yukino can stand alone and still accept support.

Yui authors continued friendship after loss.

Komachi's secure reliance supports increasing independence.

The Service Club itself is meaningful because people become more capable through relation.

## 19.2 Pure noninterference

The idea that respecting another person means doing nothing unless explicitly asked also fails.

Rumi cannot cleanly request.

Teachers have duties.

Friends have legitimate reasons to warn and advise.

Silence can preserve coercive structures.

V14.5 explicitly shows that `自分で決めればいい` can become responsibility transfer.

## 19.3 Causal isolation

The idea that an autonomous choice must be uninfluenced by others is perhaps the weakest model of all.

People acquire values, language, information, fear, courage, and preferences relationally.

The relevant question is not whether influence occurred.

It is whether influence became substitution.

The mature standard is therefore **relational autonomy**:

> a person retains meaningful authorship while embedded in histories, obligations, roles, dependencies, and other people's preferences.

---

# 20. Chosen interdependence as the terminal positive model

“Chosen interdependence” is not a euphemism for “the dependency was healthy all along.”

It names a different relation to dependence.

The mature model includes:

- acknowledged capability;
- chosen reliance;
- bounded requests;
- granular authorization;
- meaningful refusal;
- consequence ownership;
- disclosure of material stakes;
- influence without answer substitution;
- willingness to revise roles;
- tolerance for different desires;
- and succession without sacred preservation.

It also remains imperfect.

The characters can still overengineer.

They can still pressure one another.

They can still misread.

They can still want incompatible things.

Chosen interdependence is therefore not a state of ethical purity.

It is a **procedure for living with mutual consequence without converting mutual consequence into sovereignty**.

## 20.1 Capability remains visible

The mature relation does not need one person to be weak in order for another to matter.

This is the importance of `一人で立てるのに` / `知ってる`.

## 20.2 Reliance can be ordinary

Support does not have to be crisis-coded.

Study, advice, gifts, institutional succession, practical help, and ordinary shared time can all involve reliance without implying pathology.

## 20.3 Difference remains visible

Autonomy is tested most strongly not when everyone agrees but when different desires survive without requiring deletion.

Yui's romantic loss, Yukino/Yui friendship, Hachiman/Yukino romance, and Komachi's succession all demonstrate that relation can continue without equal outcome.

## 20.4 The form remains revisable

V14.5's anti-`神聖視` correction is essential.

Even a relationship that has achieved better answerability becomes controlling if its current form is treated as sacred and imposed on successors.

A mature relationship must be capable of changing without interpreting change itself as betrayal.

---

# 21. Positive and negative helping controls

The full series provides enough contrast cases to state a practical ethical spectrum.

## 21.1 Strong positive indicators

A helping act becomes more defensible when it:

- responds to an explicit or reasonably inferred stake without pretending inference is certainty;
- states the helper's own motive where it materially matters;
- preserves a meaningful no;
- respects domain-specific boundaries;
- shares information needed for decision;
- expands capability or options;
- avoids unnecessary substitution;
- distinguishes collaborator consent from target consent;
- lets the subject own success, failure, regret, or relinquishment;
- accepts that the subject may judge the result differently;
- remains revisable after the immediate crisis.

## 21.2 Strong negative indicators

A helping act becomes more suspect when it:

- converts prediction into permission;
- treats love or familiarity as privileged access to hidden good;
- uses self-sacrifice to purchase control;
- silently assigns third-party roles;
- fabricates material conditions that determine choice;
- treats refusal as merely another obstacle;
- substitutes competence for another person's practice;
- hides the helper's personal stake behind neutrality;
- equates successful outcome with moral vindication;
- or preserves a relationship by deleting an inconvenient participant's answer.

No single indicator settles a case. The mature analysis remains multidimensional.

# 22. Canonical Document-07 propositions

The following propositions are frozen for downstream Phase-5 synthesis and Phase-6 adversarial audit.

### HELP-01 — Caring supplies motive, not sovereignty

**Status:** **STRENGTHEN**

Care may justify noticing, asking, warning, offering, or remaining available. It does not by itself authorize redesigning another person's options.

### HELP-02 — Reason to act is broader than explicit request

**Status:** **STRENGTHEN**

Emergencies, institutional duty, constrained expression, promises, friendship, and material changes can create legitimate reasons to act without a clean request.

### HELP-03 — Absence of request is not absence of need

**Status:** **PRESERVE / STRENGTHEN**

Rumi remains the central counterexample to a pure request-only ethic.

### HELP-04 — Accurate diagnosis does not create jurisdiction

**Status:** **STRENGTHEN**

This governs Hachiman's social prediction, Haruno's `共依存` diagnosis, and any later interpretive authority claim.

### HELP-05 — Self-borne cost does not authorize collateral cost

**Status:** **STRENGTHEN**

Hachiman can consent to his own reputational injury; that does not authorize assigning roles and relational costs to others.

### HELP-06 — Self-sacrifice is a cost-allocation mechanism, not the master moral category

**Status:** **REVISE / STRENGTHEN**

Some self-cost is cooperative or protective; some is coercive or identity-reinforcing. Authorization and externalities determine the ethical meaning.

### HELP-07 — Effective outcome and ethical procedure are independent axes

**Status:** **STRENGTHEN**

A method can work while remaining jurisdictionally defective; a procedurally respectful method can still fail empirically.

### HELP-08 — Competence does not confer ownership

**Status:** **STRENGTHEN**

Yukino's cultural-festival overfunctioning and Hachiman's tactical intelligence are the major cases.

### HELP-09 — Refusal is domain-specific but must have force

**Status:** **STRENGTHEN**

A refusal of direct help can coexist with rivalry, advice, presence, or future relation. But the refused method must actually change.

### HELP-10 — Promise creates reason, not perpetual blanket authorization

**Status:** **STRENGTHEN**

Hachiman's promise to Yukino matters ethically without erasing her V12 boundary.

### HELP-11 — Nonintervention can be trust or abdication

**Status:** **STRENGTHEN**

Context, capability, responsibility, and the subject's preference determine which.

### HELP-12 — Influence is compatible with autonomy

**Status:** **STRENGTHEN**

Autonomy does not require causal isolation. Advice, affection, persuasion, and history can influence an authored decision.

### HELP-13 — `共依存` is useful as a reinforcement map, not a total relationship ontology

**Status:** **STRENGTHEN**

Neededness, expectation, burden transfer, and decision displacement are real mechanisms; the label does not determine feeling or unique remedy.

### HELP-14 — Self-authorship is necessary but not ethically sufficient

**Status:** **STRENGTHEN**

A self-authored decision can still overreach other people whose lives it attempts to settle.

### HELP-15 — Reliance is not incapacity

**Status:** **PRESERVE / STRENGTHEN**

The V14 capability-aware hand exchange is the strongest positive control.

### HELP-16 — Capability is not sufficient for autonomy

**Status:** **STRENGTHEN**

A highly capable person may still have weak practice in authorship, boundary recognition, or consequence ownership.

### HELP-17 — The mature Service Club can host problems it cannot solve cleanly

**Status:** **STRENGTHEN**

Yui's final request demonstrates institutionalized continuing answerability rather than solution compulsion.

### HELP-18 — Chosen interdependence is the terminal positive model

**Status:** **STRENGTHEN**

Capability, reliance, refusal, influence, and revision coexist without making either independence or dependence a moral absolute.

### HELP-19 — Romantic reciprocity does not certify ethical health

**Status:** **STRENGTHEN**

The Hachiman/Yukino outcome must be judged through capability-aware support, reciprocal answerability, and revisability rather than romantic status alone.

### HELP-20 — The opposite of control is answerable involvement, not withdrawal

**Status:** **STRENGTHEN**

V14.5's succession material supplies the clearest final evidence.

# 23. V1 → V2 revisions in the helping/autonomy domain

The V2 reread preserves the first pass's strongest intuition—that Hachiman's self-sacrificial methods are ethically complicated—but revises several overcompressed formulations.

| Earlier shorthand | V2 transition | Current formulation |
|---|---|---|
| Hachiman's problem is self-sacrifice. | **REVISE** | Self-sacrifice is one technique within the broader problem of jurisdiction, role assignment, and substituted authorship. |
| If Hachiman bears the cost himself, the method is ethically contained. | **REJECT** | Relational and institutional externalities mean self-cost is rarely purely private. |
| Yukino's problem is dependence on Hachiman. | **REVISE** | Her authorship difficulty predates Hachiman and is shaped by family structure, competence, and overfunctioning; Hachiman becomes one later reinforcement mechanism. |

| Yui is the agency-preserving mediator. | **REVISE** | Her mediation often expands agency, but preservation can also displace decision; mature Yui preserves answer rather than mere harmony. |
| Haruno correctly diagnoses codependency, so separation is the ethical cure. | **DOWNGRADE / REVISE** | Diagnosis has mechanism-level value; cure prescription lacks sovereign authority and is not uniquely confirmed. |
| Healthy autonomy means needing people less. | **REJECT** | The terminal model is chosen interdependence under retained capability and revision. |
| Noninterference is the cleanest respect for autonomy. | **REJECT** | It can be trust, but also abandonment or responsibility transfer. |
| An explicit request authorizes the helper to choose the method. | **REJECT** | Request scope is granular; third-party standing and refusal remain independent. |

| Pain proves sincerity and therefore legitimacy. | **REJECT** | Pain can reveal investment but cannot certify method, authenticity, or authority. |
| Ending the relationship proves independence. | **REJECT** | Separation can itself be borrowed doctrine or self-removal; authorship matters more than distance. |
| Romance proves the dependency problem was solved. | **REJECT as proof** | The positive evidence lies in capability-aware support, answerability, and revisability. |

The strongest meta-revision is therefore:

> **V1 often described the moral problem through the helper's sacrifice. V2 relocates the analysis to the affected person's authorship.**

That shift preserves what was insightful about self-sacrifice while preventing Hachiman's suffering from remaining the center of every ethical evaluation.

# 24. Phase-6 adversarial questions

# 26. Final synthesis

*Oregairu* begins by making helping look comparatively simple.

A person has a problem.

The Service Club identifies the real issue.

A competent helper finds a way to move the situation.

But every part of that model becomes unstable as the series proceeds.

Whose problem is it?

What if the stated request is incomplete?

What if the helper correctly sees something the requester cannot say?

What if several people own different parts of the same conflict?

What if the most effective method humiliates someone?

What if the helper pays the visible cost but other people inherit invisible costs?

What if helping becomes the helper's identity?

What if refusing help is an act of authorship?

What if refusing one form of help is not refusing the relationship?

What if “independence” is somebody else's theory imposed on the person?

What if doing nothing merely transfers responsibility?

What if a person can stand alone and still want the hand?

The novels do not answer those questions with a single rule because the cases are genuinely different.

Instead, they progressively improve the **resolution** at which helping is judged.

Early Hachiman thinks primarily in outcomes and hidden mechanisms.

Yukino introduces the idea that help should preserve the subject's capability.

Yui demonstrates that explicit request cannot be the only gate because constrained people may not be able to ask.

The cultural-festival material shows how competence and self-sacrifice can become substitutionary control.

Kyoto proves that accurate social prediction does not grant jurisdiction.

The election arc separates formal choice from manipulated conditions and exposes Hachiman's asymmetrical respect for autonomy.

Volume 9 changes the helper into a requester and makes vulnerability itself an ethical procedure.

Volumes 10–11 show that trust can require non-intervention, but that preservation can also become control.

Volume 12 names dependency mechanisms without proving that the feelings are false or the cure is separation.

Volume 13 experiments with structural change, granular refusal, rivalry, and improved collaborator consent without reaching procedural purity.

Volume 14 removes incapacity and neededness as the justification for continued relation. Hachiman must own desire. Yukino must answer. Yui must remain a subject after nonreciprocity. The bridge remains ethically imperfect, but the scene visibly moves from constraint toward reciprocal claim. The later hand exchange supplies the cleanest positive control: support is offered and accepted **because relation is wanted, not because capability is absent**.

V14.5 then performs the final correction. The alternative to control is not silence. Hachiman can neither command Komachi to preserve the club nor wash his hands with `自分で決めればいい`. Yui can state a preference without owning the decision. Komachi can be influenced without becoming unauthored. The existing club can matter without becoming sacred.

The final ethical model is therefore neither selflessness nor independence.

It is **answerable involvement**.

A person can care strongly and still admit that care does not confer sovereignty.

A person can sacrifice and still ask whether the sacrifice assigns costs to others.

A person can be capable and still accept help.

A person can depend in one mechanism without having the entire relationship reduced to pathology.

A person can refuse a method without refusing relation.

A person can influence another without pretending influence is neutral.

A person can make a self-authored choice and still be criticized for overreaching someone else's authorship.

A relationship can make people mutually consequential without making independence the cure.

The mature question is not:

> **Do these people need one another?**

It is:

> **What does their need, care, competence, fear, promise, and desire authorize—and what must remain the other person's to answer?**

That is why chosen interdependence is the strongest terminal description.

It does not deny dependency mechanisms.

It does not romanticize reliance.

It does not assume every influence is benign.

It does not certify the ending as healthy because romance succeeds.

It names a more demanding condition: capability remains visible, refusal has force, motives can be owned, roles can change, support does not require incapacity, and no participant's theory of what is best is allowed to become final jurisdiction over the others.

The definitive V2 formulation is therefore:

> **Helping in *Oregairu* becomes ethical not when the helper becomes selfless enough, correct enough, or detached enough, but when involvement stops confiscating authorship. The series' mature alternative to rescue, control, and sterile noninterference is answerable involvement inside chosen interdependence: reasons to act can be strong without becoming sovereignty; influence can be real without erasing autonomy; reliance can be meaningful without proving incapacity; self-sacrifice can remain admirable without purchasing authority; and success counts most fully when the people who must live with the result can still answer that it is theirs.**

Compactly:

> **Help is most ethical when it adds capacity without confiscating authorship.**

And the negative rule:

> **Knowing what may happen to someone is not the same as having the right to make it happen—or prevent it—for them.**
