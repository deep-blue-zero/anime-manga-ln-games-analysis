# OREGAIRU V2 — Social Role / Performance Longitudinal Ledger

## 0. Purpose

This is the seventh architecture-defined Phase-4 longitudinal ledger.

Its purpose is to consolidate how *Oregairu* treats social roles, performance, persona, atmosphere, reputation, institutional office, public scripts, relational labels, pretext, and role ownership across the canonical V2 reread from Volume 1 through Volume 14.5.

The ledger exists because one of the easiest ways to flatten *Oregairu* is to treat its social world as a simple opposition between:

- a hidden true self; and
- an externally performed false self.

The V2 corpus does not support that binary.

Characters perform because social life requires legible forms. They use jokes, titles, reputations, politeness, hostility, public competence, strategic helplessness, group expectations, event structures, club rules, family roles, and deliberately indirect pretexts to make interaction possible. Some of those performances are manipulative. Some are protective. Some are coercive. Some are generous. Many are mixed.

The central question is therefore not:

> **Is this the character's real self or a mask?**

It is:

> **Who owns the role, what does it allow, what does it conceal, what costs does it distribute, can other people answer it, and can the person revise or refuse it?**

This document does **not** replace the future Japanese Voice Ledger. Speech register, sentence form, pronouns, honorifics, and linguistic characterization are mentioned only where they materially affect role construction. Detailed linguistic analysis belongs in `OREGAIRU_V2_JAPANESE_VOICE_LEDGER.md`.

It also does not collapse social performance into authenticity. The completed Authenticity / `本物` Ledger already establishes that performance is not the opposite of genuineness. The present ledger asks the narrower longitudinal question: **how do performed identities and social forms work?**

---

# 1. Executive thesis

The V2 reread supports the following series-level model:

> **Oregairu treats social performance as an unavoidable technology of relation rather than as evidence of falsity. Roles become ethically dangerous when they become compulsory scripts, unilateral role assignments, reputation traps, or substitutes for answerable desire. They become constructive when participants can recognize them as roles, inhabit them voluntarily, revise them, refuse them, use them as reversible pretexts, and allow the people affected to answer. The mature endpoint is therefore not the abolition of performance but increasingly self-authored, transparent, revisable participation in social forms.**

The series develops this argument in stages.

### Stage A — Roles make early relation possible

Volumes 1–3 are already anti-purity texts before `本物` becomes governing vocabulary.

- Hachiman's “loner” identity is partly lived experience and partly a defensive interpretive role.
- Yukino's competence is real, but “the competent corrector” becomes a safe way to participate without naming desire.
- Yui uses atmosphere, nicknames, friendship labels, and soft social framing to increase relational standing.
- Zaimokuza's theatrical chuunibyou persona is transparently constructed and still protects a sincere desire to write.
- Haruno's polished warmth is clearly cultivated but cannot therefore be declared wholly false.
- Hachiman and Yukino can enact lover-like banter precisely because the unreality of the role lowers the risk of conventional intimacy.

Roles initially function as **risk containers**.

### Stage B — Role assignment becomes power

Volumes 4–6.5 reveal that social roles can be imposed rather than chosen.

Hachiman increasingly treats people as positions in a social diagram:

- himself as villain;
- Hayama as legitimate rescuer;
- Sagami as the target whose humiliation will activate a group;
- peers as validating audience;
- Tobe's network as a resource capable of absorbing harassment through humor.

His predictions are often accurate. That accuracy does not authorize assignment.

At the same time, Meguri supplies the strongest positive countermodel yet: office can be real without sovereign competence, leadership can depend on others without becoming fake, and institutional authority can be distributed rather than substitutionary.

### Stage C — Preservation by performance reaches crisis

Volumes 7–9 expose the danger of performed continuity.

Hayama's group, Ebina's defensive persona, Hachiman's shadow administration, the election, the hollow post-election Service Club, and Tamanawa's committee all demonstrate different ways a social form can continue while authorship thins out.

The post-election club is the decisive negative control:

> the problem is not that the trio is “acting.” The problem is that the performed normality makes it harder for the members to answer what has changed.

Volume 9 then restores performance to a more legitimate role. Destinyland, requests, event structures, and the Christmas coda show that pretext can lower expressive risk without preventing later answerability.

### Stage D — Externally shaped roles can become owned

Volumes 10–11 dismantle the idea that social origin permanently determines identity.

Hayama is constrained by expectation but also identifies with parts of the expected role. Iroha's presidency was heavily engineered, yet practice, responsibility, revision, competence, and investment make the office increasingly hers. Valentine social cover permits acts that direct romantic signaling would make more difficult.

The key distinction becomes:

> **externally shaped role ≠ unauthored role forever.**

### Stage E — Diagnosis and institutional role can become new excuses

Volumes 12–13 add the explicit `依存` / `共依存` vocabulary and the prom conflict.

The characters can now use sophisticated language about autonomy, pathology, responsibility, and support. That language is itself another social technology. A diagnosis can reveal a mechanism, but it can also become a respectable script through which desire is displaced.

The prom arc similarly shows that institutional role does not eliminate personal motive. Yukino's prom leadership is real institutional authorship. Hachiman's dummy-prom operation is real institutional manipulation. Haruno's diagnostic authority is real social power but not narrator sovereignty. Hayama's role as socially legible good person remains partial rather than total identity.

### Stage F — Personal desire survives after respectable roles are stripped away

Volume 14 repeatedly dismantles role-based justifications.

Hachiman can no longer ground his continued involvement securely in:

- club duty;
- being needed;
- a promise;
- objective usefulness;
- pathology diagnosis;
- or institutional necessity.

He has to say that he wants to remain involved.

This is not an escape from social form. The relationship immediately generates new obligations, negotiations, future assumptions, family integration, and institutional continuity. The difference is that role no longer carries all the explanatory burden.

### Stage G — Volume 14.5 refuses to sanctify the successful form

The post-ending material supplies the terminal correction.

Iroha still uses transparent pretext. Yui remains socially and institutionally active after romantic non-reciprocity. Komachi becomes president without becoming “the next Yukino.” New mugs, porous membership, new recruitment possibilities, and Yukino teaching tea make office transmissible rather than identical.

Hachiman's `神聖視` self-correction is crucial because it shows that even a meaningful, chosen social form can become distorted if its present configuration is treated as sacred.

The mature endpoint is therefore:

> **not authenticity without roles, but relation capable of changing roles without treating change as betrayal.**

# 2. Social-role / performance protocol

## 2.1 Social role

A **social role** is a recognizable position whose expectations organize interaction.

Examples include:

- loner;
- popular-group center;
- “good guy”;
- friend;
- club member;
- club president;
- student-council president;
- older sister;
- daughter;
- teacher;
- requester;
- helper;
- villain;
- mediator;
- romantic pursuer;
- event organizer.

A role may be formal or informal.

It may be:

- imposed;
- inherited;
- volunteered for;
- strategically adopted;
- gradually internalized;
- partially owned;
- or actively resisted.

## 2.2 Performance

**Performance** means socially legible conduct shaped partly by audience, role, convention, or desired effect.

Performance is not synonymous with deception.

A performed act may be sincere. A spontaneous act may be coercive. A polished public persona may contain genuine investment. A blunt statement may still be strategically chosen.

## 2.3 Persona

A **persona** is a recurrent performed identity with recognizable cues.

Important examples include:

- Zaimokuza's theatrical author/chuunibyou identity;
- Ebina's exaggerated fujoshi identity;
- Iroha's calculated junior femininity and helplessness;
- Haruno's socially polished older-sister/public-adult presentation;
- Hachiman's ironic cynic / disposable villain self-positioning.

A persona may protect vulnerable desire, stabilize group interaction, invite misreading, or create coercive expectations.

## 2.4 Reputation

**Reputation** is accumulated social expectation attached to a person.

Reputation matters because it changes the cost of action.

Hayama's goodness, Hachiman's outsider status, Yukino's competence, Sagami's failure, Iroha's office, and Haruno's polish all become socially predictive resources. Characters can use reputations instrumentally, but reputations can also become traps.

## 2.5 Atmosphere

**Atmosphere** is the distributed social field that makes some actions easier, harder, safer, embarrassing, or nearly unthinkable.

Yui is especially skilled at reading and modifying this field, but she is not the only character who does so.

Atmosphere is not imaginary. It has causal force.

It can preserve face, produce exclusion, prevent speech, enable approach, or make nominally available choices socially unavailable.

## 2.6 Pretext

A **pretext** is a stated or structured reason that allows an interaction carrying additional motives.

Pretext ranges from healthy to coercive.

Healthy pretext tends to preserve:

- low stakes;
- exit capacity;
- mutual awareness;
- reversibility;
- later answerability.

Harmful pretext tends to:

- conceal a decisive objective;
- remove refusal;
- assign another person's role;
- or become the only permitted channel for the real relation.

## 2.7 Role ownership

A role is more strongly **owned** when the person can:

- recognize that the role is socially shaped;
- affirm parts of it as theirs;
- revise how it is enacted;
- reject components;
- leave or transform it without total identity collapse;
- answer for its consequences.

Role ownership is gradual rather than binary.

## 2.8 Role capture

**Role capture** occurs when a person becomes difficult to distinguish, socially or personally, from the service the role provides.

Examples include:

- Hayama being expected to remain Hayama;
- Yukino's competence encouraging substitution;
- Hachiman becoming attached to being the needed solver;
- Yui being treated as the harmony-preserver;
- Haruno being treated as the one who always sees through everyone.

Role capture can coexist with competence and sincere identification.

## 2.9 Unilateral role assignment

This is one of the ledger's most important negative categories.

A character does not merely interpret someone. They construct a plan in which another person must occupy a preselected social function.

Examples include Hachiman assigning roles in the cultural-festival rooftop operation and the Kyoto intervention's administration of what Tobe, Ebina, Hayama, and the group will be allowed to become.

## 2.10 Answerability

A performance becomes more ethically legible when affected people can answer it.

Answerability includes the practical ability to:

- reject the role;
- revise the script;
- disclose a different motive;
- refuse participation;
- object to the interpretation;
- renegotiate the institution;
- or continue in a changed form.

This is the bridge between the present ledger and the Request / Intervention Ethics Ledger.

# 3. Series-level role architecture

Across the series, social roles repeatedly solve one problem while creating another.

They solve **legibility**.

Without roles, characters may not know:

- why they are allowed to approach;
- what kind of help they may request;
- what level of intimacy is permitted;
- who owns a decision;
- what behavior is expected;
- how much rejection would mean.

But roles create **inertia**.

Once a relationship works through a particular form, the form can start substituting for the people inside it.

The recurring sequence is:

> vulnerable desire -> social role or pretext makes action possible -> repetition makes the role useful -> usefulness generates expectation -> expectation can harden into identity -> identity can begin to constrain authorship -> crisis forces role renegotiation.

This architecture applies differently to different characters.

Hachiman often uses roles to keep desire impersonal.

Yukino uses role and competence to make care actionable before it is emotionally nameable.

Yui uses social framing to make closeness survivable for others.

Hayama's role originates partly in expectation and becomes partly self-owned.

Iroha's office begins under manipulation and becomes increasingly hers.

Haruno's social polish gives her diagnostic access while also making her own desire harder to locate.

Komachi's succession finally demonstrates that an institution can transmit practices without transmitting identity.

# 4. V01–V03 — roles as containers for unsafe desire

## 4.1 Hachiman: the loner as experience and defensive role

Hachiman's outsider identity is not invented. He has real histories of exclusion, humiliation, failed expectation, and social distance.

But the V2 reread rejects treating “loner” as a transparent essence.

The role performs several functions:

- lowers expectations;
- makes rejection predictable rather than surprising;
- turns desire into social theory;
- gives him permission to observe without participating;
- converts vulnerability into cynical competence;
- and lets him treat nonmembership as chosen even where the history is more mixed.

His early social criticism is often perceptive. The problem is that the role can become a jurisdictional theory: because he stands outside, he imagines he sees the system more clearly; because he sees the system, he is tempted to believe he can decide what it means for people inside it.

The “loner” role therefore has both epistemic advantages and defensive costs.

## 4.2 Yukino: competent corrector before hidden wounded girl

The early V2 corpus explicitly rejects the easy reading that Yukino's competence is merely armor.

She is genuinely competent.

She also has a sincere reformist ethic. She believes people can improve through effort and that assistance should not flatter weakness.

The social role of “competent corrector” nevertheless becomes relationally useful to her.

It allows her to:

- help without naming affection;
- belong through work;
- make claims through club procedure;
- criticize without negotiating emotional reassurance;
- and rely on formal responsibility where informal intimacy is less practiced.

The role is therefore both real and protective.

That duality must remain intact.

## 4.3 Yui: atmosphere work is active agency

Yui's early performance problem is almost the inverse of Hachiman's.

She is socially responsive enough that her behavior changes with context. A shallow reading can convert that into lack of self.

The V2 reread instead shows atmosphere sensitivity functioning as active social intelligence.

Yui:

- notices who is being excluded;
- changes names and address terms to alter relational standing;
- creates opportunities for contact;
- translates harsh speech into survivable meanings;
- detects when a “solution” leaves the person unheard;
- and uses ambiguity to keep interaction possible.

Her risk is not that she has no self.

It is that atmosphere management can become so important that conflict-bearing desire is delayed.

## 4.4 Zaimokuza: constructed persona, durable desire

Zaimokuza is one of the earliest and cleanest controls against performance=falsity.

His theatrical identity is obviously artificial, derivative, exaggerated, and comic.

His desire to write is nevertheless durable.

The persona does not prove the desire false. It protects the desire from ordinary embarrassment and gives him a language through which he can continue practicing.

The series therefore establishes very early that a role can be ridiculous and still carry something real.

## 4.5 Haruno: polish is not proof of emptiness

Haruno enters as socially fluent, audience-aware, warm, provocative, and difficult to read.

Hachiman notices the calibration.

That observation is useful.

The inference that calibration means absence of sincere warmth is not established.

Her later family investment, loneliness, frustration, and desire for some demonstrably non-false answer prevent the early polished persona from being retroactively reduced to simple deception.

## 4.6 Hachiman and Yukino's lover-like performance

The early staged romantic dynamic matters methodologically.

Hachiman and Yukino can play with a lover-like or quasi-romantic role precisely because they regard the conventional interpretation as impossible.

The role creates safety through unreality.

This is one of the first demonstrations that performance can enable intimacy rather than negate it.

## 4.7 Names and roles alter relational risk

Checkpoint A establishes that labels matter because they change the obligations attached to interaction.

Terms such as friend, club member, nickname, or lover-like framing do not merely describe relation. They help produce the level of risk the characters can tolerate.

Hachiman tends to prefer narrower functional labels.

Yui tends to increase personal standing through naming.

Yukino often acts with greater intimacy than the labels she is initially willing to use.

The early Service Club therefore contains a persistent mismatch:

> **behavior outruns naming.**

That mismatch is not automatically dishonest. It is a developmental state.

# 5. V04–V06.5 — role assignment, reputation, and distributed leadership

## 5.1 Hachiman's villain role changes from posture to intervention mechanism

By the cultural-festival period, Hachiman's willingness to become the disliked person is no longer merely self-description.

It becomes an operational role.

He can use his own poor reputation as a resource because:

- reputational loss appears cheap to him;
- outsider hostility is expected;
- public blame can concentrate social cost;
- other people may become coordinated in opposition to him.

This is one reason his methods can work.

It is also one reason they become dangerous.

## 5.2 The rooftop operation is unilateral casting

The cultural-festival rooftop intervention is the clearest early case of Hachiman treating social roles as allocatable resources.

The plan effectively casts:

- Hachiman as villain;
- Hayama as righteous rescuer;
- Sagami as wounded center;
- Sagami's friends as social validators;
- the broader audience as reputational mechanism.

The operation succeeds at producing motion.

Its ethical defect is not simply that Hachiman hurts himself.

It is that he assigns everyone else a role without giving them meaningful authorship over the script.

This becomes a governing social-performance proposition:

> **self-assignment does not authorize other-assignment.**

## 5.3 Reputation can be causally useful without becoming morally sovereign

The V2 reread repeatedly shows that social reputation is real power.

Hayama's legitimacy matters.

Hachiman's disposability matters.

Sagami's standing matters.

Tobe's social network can convert hostility into humor.

But a prediction that a reputation will absorb an intervention's cost does not settle whether that person should be made to bear it.

## 5.4 Yukino's competence role begins producing substitution

Volume 6 also reveals the failure mode of Yukino's competence.

Because she can do the work, other people can stop doing it.

The role “competent person who will fix this” can become a social invitation to withdrawal.

Her problem is therefore not competence itself.

It is **role concentration**.

A role becomes unhealthy when the institution begins treating one person's exceptional capability as the default substitute for everyone else's participation.

## 5.5 Yui becomes an operational social actor

Yui increasingly refuses the role of passive emotional buffer.

She provides:

- translation;
- accountability;
- practical coordination;
- opportunity creation;
- and explicit correction of the idea that reliance must be dyadic or shameful.

Her significance is that social intelligence becomes labor rather than merely personality.

## 5.6 Sagami: public failure does not freeze identity

Sagami is a crucial reputation test case.

After public failure and humiliation, the series could have frozen her into the role of “the incompetent status-seeker.”

Volume 6.5 does not.

She remains:

- approval-sensitive;
- vain;
- defensive;
- mixed in motive.

She also:

- accepts another public responsibility;
- asks for cooperation;
- continues after social protection weakens;
- speaks for herself;
- accepts accurate recognition of effort;
- completes work.

The later role does not erase the earlier failure. The earlier failure does not permanently define what roles she may inhabit.

## 5.7 Meguri: presidency without total competence

Meguri becomes the strongest positive control in the early/middle series.

She openly depends on others.

Her presidency works because she coordinates rather than substitutes.

She distributes:

- tasks;
- recognition;
- memory;
- warmth;
- expertise.

She does not need to perform omnipotence in order for her office to be real.

This matters enormously for the later Hayama, Iroha, Yukino, and dependency material.

The early series has already shown:

> **leadership can be authentic, socially performed, dependent on others, and still self-owned.**

# 6. V07–V09 — preservation by social form

## 6.1 Kyoto: the friend group becomes an administered object

The Kyoto arc is the point where social performance becomes a crisis of governance.

The problem is not merely that Tobe wants to confess and Ebina does not want a romance.

The surrounding group has a form it wants to preserve.

Hayama, Hachiman, Yui, Miura, Ebina, and Tobe all have different stakes in that form.

Hachiman's intervention treats the group as something whose future can be modeled and administered.

That is exactly what the later V2 framework rejects as sufficient authority.

## 6.2 Hayama: smoothing as a genuine social good with a failure mode

Hayama's role is not “fake nice guy.”

His social centrality produces real benefits:

- face management;
- conflict moderation;
- inclusion;
- continuity;
- social translation;
- reputational shelter.

The failure mode appears when the role's preservation function becomes more important than the participants' ability to answer changing desire.

Hayama can sincerely want the group to continue and still help maintain a structure that delays authorship.

## 6.3 Ebina: persona as defensive adaptation

Ebina's exaggerated fujoshi persona is not mere comic garnish.

It helps her:

- keep romantic legibility at a distance;
- create a safe public identity;
- preserve access to the group;
- and redirect certain kinds of attention.

The persona protects something real: she values the current place.

But it also lets her shift the cost of boundary enforcement onto others.

This is a central V2 distinction:

> **a defensive persona may protect sincere attachment while still producing ethically problematic externalities.**

## 6.4 Tobe: shallow register does not equal shallow desire

Tobe's comic social style makes it easy to underread him.

The V2 corpus rejects that move.

His desire is consequential to him, and rejection would hurt.

Kyoto preserves his long-term ability to continue in the group while taking away the particular speech he intended to make.

Social ease therefore does not cancel personal stakes.

## 6.5 The election: formal role survives while lived relation hollows out

The student-council election creates a decisive institutional analogy.

The Service Club remains formally open after the immediate crisis.

The members can still:

- enter the room;
- make tea;
- sit in expected places;
- perform familiar conversational rhythms.

But the lived relation has changed.

This is why the post-election normality is the strongest negative performance example in the series to that point.

The normality is not wrong because it is staged.

It is wrong because the performance prevents the group from renegotiating the relationship that the old performance no longer adequately represents.

## 6.6 Iroha: imposed candidacy versus later office

Iroha begins as a striking role-ownership problem.

Her candidacy is not cleanly self-originated.

Others manipulate the choice architecture around her.

Yet the office is real once she occupies it.

By Volume 9 she begins to convene her own institution, make decisions, accept help, revise plans, and exercise authority.

The series therefore starts a longitudinal test:

> **can a compromised beginning become a self-owned role?**

The later answer is yes, conditionally.

## 6.7 Tamanawa: performative consensus

Tamanawa's committee is satire, but its analytical function is precise.

Its language organizes social process through:

- constant affirmation;
- abstraction;
- jargon;
- euphemism;
- responsibility diffusion;
- reluctance to negate.

The committee performs consensus.

The problem is not politeness.

The problem is that without meaningful negation, consent loses information.

A social process that permits only affirmative performance cannot reliably distinguish agreement from compliance.

## 6.8 Destinyland and the Christmas coda: pretext rehabilitated

The same phase also shows the positive function of indirect form.

An outing, game, request, event, or nominal reason can create a buffer in which interaction becomes possible without immediate total disclosure.

The key is reversibility.

If participants can still refuse, redirect, misunderstand, clarify, or later state more directly what they want, pretext can expand rather than reduce agency.

This is why the post-Volume-9 Christmas coda matters. Ordinary activity begins converting the breakthrough into habit without requiring the characters to abandon every social frame.

# 7. V10–V11 — role ownership, plural selves, and productive pretext

## 7.1 Hayama: socially prewritten answer

Volume 10 is the central Hayama role text.

He is constrained not because he has no choices in the formal sense but because social expectation makes one answer disproportionately livable.

His role contains:

- family expectation;
- peer expectation;
- academic prestige;
- moral reliability;
- public recognizability;
- his own investment in being the person others believe him to be.

This is not a simple prison.

Hayama explicitly identifies with parts of the role.

That matters because it destroys the hidden-self shortcut.

The question is not whether expected Hayama is fake.

It is whether Hayama can revise, refuse, or answer differently without losing all social continuity.

## 7.2 Hachiman and Hayama as inverse expectation systems

The two boys become useful comparators.

Hachiman protects himself by minimizing expectation.

Hayama protects social continuity by satisfying expectation.

Both systems can become defensive.

One makes the self socially unavailable.

The other makes the self socially overavailable.

Neither is reducible to honest versus fake.

## 7.3 Iroha: plural performance without hidden essence

Iroha becomes the strongest anti-hidden-self case in the series.

Her:

- strategic helplessness;
- performed femininity;
- vanity;
- laziness;
- competence;
- ambition;
- gratitude;
- flirtation;
- annoyance;
- enjoyment;
- institutional responsibility

coexist.

The trial-date material is especially important because the strategy is real **and** the enjoyment is real.

No useful analysis comes from choosing one as the “true” Iroha and declaring the other a mask.

## 7.4 Iroha's presidency becomes self-owned practice

The office's compromised origin does not determine its later ontology.

Iroha becomes more authoritative because she:

- does the work;
- learns procedures;
- manages people;
- makes mistakes;
- revises;
- asks for help;
- sets preferences;
- accepts responsibility.

This is one of the series' clearest examples of role appropriation in the positive sense: a socially engineered position becomes part of the person's authored life.

## 7.5 Valentine event: social cover can expand agency

The Valentine event is a positive pretext model.

The event structure lowers the signaling cost of actions that would be more romantically exposed outside the frame.

Participants can make chocolate, exchange attention, and inhabit ambiguous motives without every act functioning as a total confession.

The pretext does not eliminate meaning.

It creates a larger action space.

## 7.6 Undefined Service Club: anti-definition can become avoidance

Volume 10.5 asks Hachiman to describe the club publicly, and the problem exposes a tension.

The club is already socially real.

It has:

- repeated practice;
- institutional history;
- expectations;
- private knowledge;
- ordinary co-presence;
- external relationships;
- public visibility.

Yet the members struggle to define what it is.

Initially, refusal to overdefine protects ambiguity.

By Volume 11, permanent indefinition becomes risky because the relationship now contains nonidentical desire that requires consequences.

A role can therefore be too rigid **or** too vague.

The mature problem is not “define everything.” It is whether the current form remains answerable to the people inside it.

# 8. V12–V13 — diagnosis, family role, and the politics of institutional performance

## 8.1 Diagnostic language becomes a social role of its own

Once `依存` and `共依存` enter the text, the characters acquire a more sophisticated vocabulary for explaining themselves.

That vocabulary has real analytical power.

It also has social power.

A diagnosis can:

- assign positions;
- distinguish healthy from unhealthy conduct;
- justify withdrawal;
- authorize intervention;
- create shame;
- convert desire into pathology;
- make one speaker appear to occupy the role of the person who sees clearly.

This is why Haruno's diagnostic function must remain separate from narrator authority.

## 8.2 Haruno: the role of exposer is both valuable and limiting

Haruno often occupies the position of the person who punctures social comfort.

She sees:

- family pressure;
- relational avoidance;
- socially convenient explanations;
- expectation traps;
- the trio's reinforcement loops.

Her role has real value.

But the V2 reread also shows that Haruno has personal stakes in proving that some unambiguously correct ending exists.

The exposer is therefore not outside the system she diagnoses.

Her polished adult persona and critical authority coexist with:

- sisterly investment;
- frustration;
- loneliness;
- disappointment;
- personal longing.

## 8.3 Yukino: prom leadership as public authorship

The prom is not merely a romantic metaphor.

It is a real institutional project.

Yukino's role includes:

- proposal ownership;
- student leadership;
- negotiation with adults;
- resource coordination;
- responsibility for outcome;
- family confrontation;
- public accountability.

Her competence becomes healthier precisely because the office/project no longer requires her to do everything alone.

She can remain the author of the project while using other people's labor.

## 8.4 Hachiman: rival organizer and institutional manipulator

Hachiman's dummy prom is not simply “helping from the shadows.”

He constructs an alternative institutionally legible proposal designed to alter the choice environment around Yukino's real prom.

This is a role-performance operation at system scale.

It uses:

- public process;
- collaborators;
- institutional credibility;
- reputational assumptions;
- adult networks;
- apparent rivalry.

The method preserves more of Yukino's direct operational authorship than simply taking over her prom would.

It still manipulates the environment.

The social-role lesson is therefore graded rather than binary.

## 8.5 Hayama: socially legible goodness cannot carry proxy guilt forever

Hayama's later relation to Hachiman includes an attempted transfer of old regret.

Hachiman refuses it.

That refusal is important because Hayama's role as responsible, socially central mediator creates a temptation to treat his unresolved guilt as something the next socially appropriate actor should inherit.

The V2 corpus rejects that proxy logic.

A role can generate responsibility without making responsibility infinitely transferable.

## 8.6 Yui: preservation is no longer enough of a role

Yui has long been the character most capable of keeping relational channels open.

By the prom arc, that competence risks becoming a role trap if her function is simply “keep everyone together.”

She increasingly refuses that reduction.

She states desire, acknowledges selfish motive, challenges Yukino's self-removal, and later remains after receiving an answer that does not match her romantic desire.

Her development is not from social performance to authenticity.

It is from **atmosphere-preserving role concentration** toward a broader self who can preserve, desire, lose, object, and continue.

# 9. V14 — when roles stop being sufficient explanations

## 9.1 Hachiman's decisive movement is de-role justification

For much of the series Hachiman can explain involvement through socially respectable or self-protective roles:

- club member;
- helper;
- debtor;
- promised rescuer;
- useful solver;
- person Yukino needs;
- person responsible for a request;
- opponent in an institutional contest.

Volume 14 progressively removes these excuses.

The result is not a roleless self.

It is a self who can no longer pretend the role is the whole reason.

`俺は関わり続けたい` matters because first-person desire becomes answerable without needing an institutional mask to certify it.

## 9.2 Yukino converts asymmetrical role into reciprocal relation

Hachiman's first formulation of continued involvement still risks reproducing a helper/beneficiary asymmetry.

Yukino's response changes the structure by making a reciprocal claim.

The relationship can include support without fixing one person permanently as rescuer and the other as rescued.

This is a mature role revision:

> **the social function continues, but the hierarchy is renegotiated.**

## 9.3 Capability prevents the romance from becoming savior theater

The line pair built around Yukino being able to stand alone and Hachiman knowing that fact is essential.

If Yukino were incapable, Hachiman's continued support could be interpreted as role necessity.

Instead, capability remains explicit.

Support becomes something they choose to negotiate.

That is the difference between role capture and chosen interdependence.

## 9.4 Yui is not assigned the role of gracious loser

The final movement refuses a common genre simplification.

Yui does not become valuable because she blesses the chosen couple.

She retains:

- grief;
- desire;
- friendship standing;
- institutional standing;
- social presence;
- future claims.

Her role changes without her subjecthood being reduced to the function the romance requires.

## 9.5 The Service Club continues after non-equivalence becomes explicit

This is a crucial institutional result.

The club is not dissolved because the romantic geometry changes.

Nor does the old club form become proof that nothing changed.

The institution becomes a place capable of containing known non-equivalence.

That is a stronger social form than the post-election simulation of normality because the difference is now answerable rather than denied.

# 10. V14.5 — transparent pretext, succession, and anti-sanctification

## 10.1 Iroha's `プレゼントは言い訳ですよ`

Iroha's post-choice pretext is nearly maximally transparent.

She effectively names the pretext as pretext.

That matters because it shows that indirectness can be mutually legible.

There is still ethical grading to do: transparent pretext is not identical to perfect authorization.

But the scene decisively rejects the idea that an interaction becomes false merely because the stated reason is not the whole motive.

## 10.2 The social world is not purified by romantic choice

Hachiman remains embedded in relationships that include:

- flirtation;
- ambiguity;
- mixed motive;
- institutional obligation;
- teasing;
- attraction;
- friendship;
- boundary negotiation.

The ending does not solve social life by making all relations fully explicit.

It makes some previously hidden stakes more answerable while leaving ordinary plurality intact.

## 10.3 Komachi's presidency is succession without replication

Komachi becomes the new Service Club president.

The series does not require her to reproduce Yukino's presidency.

She receives:

- office;
- a mug;
- advice;
- inherited practice;
- institutional memory;
- relationships with prior members.

She does **not** receive a command to preserve the old emotional geometry.

This is the strongest terminal model of role inheritance in the series.

A role can be transmitted without identity being transmitted.

## 10.4 Porous membership undermines rigid institutional essence

Iroha receives a matching mug despite pointing out that she is not technically a club member.

This small institutional fact matters.

The Service Club has always had porous boundaries:

- student council ties;
- clients who become recurring participants;
- informal collaborators;
- adults;
- friends;
- successor members.

The institution's social reality exceeds its formal category.

## 10.5 `神聖視` is a role-form correction

Hachiman recognizes that he has begun to treat the existing club configuration as something sacred.

This is the final major correction in the social-role ledger.

Even a role that was once liberating can become a trap if its present form is treated as supreme.

Even a relationship that survived crisis can become unanswerable if continuity means reproducing the same people, objects, habits, and boundaries forever.

The post-ending institutional thesis is therefore:

> **continuity should transmit practices, not freeze configurations.**

## 10.6 Tea as transmitted practice

Yukino teaching Komachi tea crystallizes this model.

The practice has history.

It carries memory.

It may continue.

But future tea can be made by different people, differently, under a different president, in a different club.

Transmission preserves relation to the past without making imitation the price of legitimacy.

# 11. Character-specific social-role routes

## 11.1 Hachiman — from outsider role to role-aware participant

### Early

Hachiman's outsider identity gives him distance, social perception, and defensive safety.

### Middle

He begins using roles instrumentally:

- villain;
- solver;
- sacrificial target;
- hidden administrator;
- person who can be disliked cheaply.

The role becomes dangerous when he treats his own acceptance of it as permission to cast others.

### Post-Volume 9

He becomes more capable of asking rather than only inferring, but still uses roles to manage desire.

### Late

He strips away helper, need, pathology, and institutional necessity as sufficient explanations and owns continued involvement as desire.

### Terminal

He remains socially embedded and still uses rhetoric, jokes, formalities, and institutional categories. Growth is not role abolition.

It is greater awareness that roles are provisional tools rather than sovereign explanations.

**Terminal Hachiman proposition:**

> **The mature Hachiman is not finally “himself” because he stops performing; he is more answerable because he can distinguish his desires from the roles through which he expresses them and can recognize when a role has become a distortion.**

## 11.2 Yukino — competence, office, and relational authorship

### Early

Competence is real and socially useful.

### Cultural-festival phase

Competence risks becoming substitutionary role capture.

### Election / post-election

Formal office and personal will become entangled; others can interpret her through “what Yukino should do” faster than she can stabilize her own answer.

### Prom

Institutional authorship becomes a training ground for personal authorship. She can lead, rely, refuse help, accept rivalry, negotiate family power, and own consequences.

### Terminal

She can carry competence without treating self-sufficiency as identity proof.

**Terminal Yukino proposition:**

> **Yukino's development is not the discovery of a private self behind the competent role; it is increasing ability to own, limit, delegate, and combine competence with explicitly personal desire.**

## 11.3 Yui — atmosphere labor without reduction to mediator

### Early

Yui uses atmosphere and naming to build relation.

### Middle

She becomes increasingly operational and accountability-bearing.

### Crisis

Harmony preservation begins to conflict with her own desire and the others' need to answer changing stakes.

### Late

She states selfish desire, contests self-removal, receives nonreciprocity, and continues as friend and institutional actor.

**Terminal Yui proposition:**

> **Yui's social sensitivity is part of her agency, not evidence that she lacks a self. Her growth lies in refusing to let relational maintenance become the only role she is allowed to occupy.**

## 11.4 Iroha — from manipulated entrant to self-owned plural role

Iroha is the clearest positive role-appropriation case.

Her presidency begins through manipulation.

It becomes hers through practice.

Her performed femininity never disappears.

Her strategic behavior never disappears.

Her competence does not require those layers to disappear.

Her later transparency about pretext makes the structure more legible without purifying motive.

**Terminal Iroha proposition:**

> **A socially engineered role can become self-authored, and a self-authored role can remain performative.**

## 11.5 Hayama — expectation saturation and partial ownership

Hayama's role is partly externally imposed and partly sincerely his.

He values being dependable.

He also suffers from the cost of being socially prewritten.

The V2 correction is decisive:

> Hayama is not a “fake normie” whose true self is hidden underneath.

He is a person whose socially valued capacities have become entangled with expectations so dense that revision becomes expensive.

His mature issue is not mask removal. It is authorship under saturation.

## 11.6 Haruno — performed polish and diagnostic distance

Haruno's public fluency is one of her sources of power.

It lets her enter spaces, provoke reactions, control tone, and see how others respond.

But the role of “person who sees through everyone” can itself become a shield.

Her later interior stakes weaken the fantasy that she occupies a pure outside position.

**Terminal Haruno proposition:**

> **Haruno's performance gives her real insight but cannot exempt her from the same requirement of self-disclosure and answerability she demands from others.**

## 11.7 Komachi — framing, then succession

Komachi uses jokes, sibling familiarity, staged opportunities, and low-pressure framing throughout the series.

Her social engineering usually preserves exit capacity better than Hachiman's because she does not control the destination as tightly.

Her presidency then turns her into the terminal succession case.

She inherits authority without inheriting identity.

# 13. Institutional models

## 13.1 The Service Club — role as scaffold, then potential shrine

The Service Club begins as an adult-imposed structure.

It becomes chosen relational infrastructure.

Requests permit closeness before closeness is speakable.

The club's risk is that institutional duty can conceal personal desire.

The post-election crisis shows that preserving the room and routine is not enough.

Volume 14.5 then supplies the final correction: preserving the original configuration forever would itself betray the institution's living function.

**Model:** scaffold -> home -> avoidance structure -> answerable institution -> transmissible practice.

## 13.2 Hayama's group — preservation by smoothing

Strengths:

- continuity;
- face protection;
- social accessibility;
- conflict dampening.

Risks:

- delayed authorship;
- change treated as threat;
- unspoken vetoes;
- moral outsourcing.

## 13.3 Cultural-festival organization — role concentration

The cultural festival demonstrates how institutions fail when:

- formal office lacks capability;
- capable outsiders substitute rather than support;
- public reputation controls labor;
- emergency response legitimates coercive scripts.

Meguri provides the positive countermodel through distributed leadership.

## 13.4 Tamanawa's committee — consensus as performance

The committee demonstrates that procedural civility can become anti-deliberative if disagreement has no legitimate form.

## 13.5 Iroha's student council — supported ownership

By Volume 9 onward, Iroha's council increasingly shows:

- advice without substitution;
- office-holder ownership;
- permitted disagreement;
- practical revision;
- institutional learning.

## 13.6 Yukino's prom — institutional authorship under family pressure

The prom tests whether a student can own public work while using others' help and negotiating adult power.

Its significance should not be reduced to romance.

## 13.7 Yukinoshita family — social role before explicit desire

The family system assigns identities and expected futures through:

- competence;
- comparison;
- elder/younger sister roles;
- succession assumptions;
- public reputation;
- polite framing.

Formal freedom can therefore coexist with powerful social scripting.

Yukino's development requires not escaping social role altogether but learning to answer inside and against it.

# 14. Performance ethics matrix

| Dimension | Lower-risk form | Higher-risk form |
|---|---|---|
| Ownership | person knowingly inhabits or revises role | role is treated as fixed identity by self or others |
| Refusal | exit or objection remains meaningful | refusal carries identity-destroying or relationally catastrophic cost |
| Pretext | lowers expressive cost and remains reversible | conceals decisive objective or substitutes another's choice |
| Audience | audience understands enough to answer | audience is manipulated as a causal mechanism without standing |
| Reputation | reputation informs expectations | reputation becomes permission to assign cost or foreclose change |
| Institutional office | responsibility distributed with authorship retained | competence or hierarchy substitutes for participants' agency |
| Persona | protects desire while leaving revision possible | persona becomes only socially permitted self |
| Atmosphere | makes approach safer | makes disagreement or change socially impossible |
| Mixed motive | motives acknowledged as plural | one respectable motive used to conceal all others |
| Continuity | form adapts to changed relation | form preserved despite changed relation and blocks renegotiation |

This matrix should guide later specialist synthesis.

# 16. Cross-ledger reconciliation

## 16.1 With the Hachiman Epistemic / Narratorial Ledger

Hachiman often reads social roles accurately.

He can identify:

- expectation structures;
- reputation dynamics;
- face-saving behavior;
- group ecology;
- procedural emptiness;
- public/private asymmetry.

The epistemic ledger establishes that accurate social observation does not authorize decision.

The present ledger adds:

> **accurate role recognition does not authorize role assignment.**

This is the difference between understanding that Hayama's reputation has causal power and deciding Hayama must perform a role in one's plan.

## 16.2 With the Character-State Ledger

The character ledger already freezes the anti-essentialist result:

- performance can coexist with sincere desire;
- competence can be both trait and role;
- social sensitivity can be agency;
- imposed roles can become owned;
- role ownership can remain partial.

The present ledger generalizes those character findings into a social architecture.

## 16.3 With the Relationship-State Ledger

Relationships often begin through roles:

- club member;
- client/helper;
- friend-group member;
- junior/senior;
- sibling;
- office-holder;
- event collaborator.

The relationship ledger shows that mature relation becomes more answerable when the role stops monopolizing the meaning of the relationship.

The role can remain.

Its jurisdiction narrows.

## 16.4 With the Request / Intervention Ethics Ledger

Requests are themselves social forms.

They can:

- authorize help;
- create bounded roles;
- disclose problem ownership;
- give helpers a legitimate reason to act.

But requests can also become pretext for deeper personal motives.

The mature standard is not motive purity. It is whether scope, refusal, and revision remain meaningful.

## 16.5 With the Authenticity / `本物` Ledger

The authenticity ledger establishes that `本物` is not absence of performance.

The present ledger explains why.

Human social relation requires forms.

The ethical and literary question is whether those forms remain answerable.

## 16.6 With the Dependency / Autonomy Ledger

Role capture is one mechanism through which dependency becomes distorted.

Examples include:

- Hachiman needing to be needed;
- Yukino being expected to remain the capable one;
- Hayama being expected to remain socially reliable;
- Yui being expected to preserve harmony.

Autonomy therefore includes the ability to revise a role without the relationship treating revision as betrayal.

# 17. Canonical social-role / performance propositions

### SRP-01 — Performance is not equivalent to falsity

A socially calibrated behavior may carry sincere desire, care, responsibility, or pleasure.

### SRP-02 — Spontaneity is not equivalent to authenticity

Bluntness, impulse, or unfiltered speech does not automatically have greater truth value.

### SRP-03 — Externally shaped roles can become self-owned

Iroha is the strongest longitudinal example; Hayama provides the more constrained version.

### SRP-04 — Self-owned roles can remain performative

Ownership does not require erasing convention, audience awareness, or strategic presentation.

### SRP-05 — Accurate prediction does not authorize unilateral role assignment

Hachiman's cultural-festival and Kyoto methods establish this repeatedly.

### SRP-06 — Reputation is causal but not sovereign

Social standing changes what is possible but does not determine what ought to be demanded.

### SRP-07 — Atmosphere is a real field of agency

Yui's sensitivity to it is a form of social intelligence, not evidence of weak selfhood.

### SRP-08 — Pretext is ethically graded

Pretext can expand action when it lowers expressive cost while preserving refusal and later clarification.

### SRP-09 — Performed continuity can become relationally false

The post-election Service Club is the central negative example because the performance blocks renegotiation of changed reality.

### SRP-10 — Institutional office does not require sovereign competence

Meguri and later Iroha demonstrate distributed leadership.

### SRP-11 — Role capture can occur through socially rewarded competence

Yukino, Hayama, Hachiman, and Yui each show different variants.

### SRP-12 — A role can be partly imposed and partly owned

Hayama's Volume-10 state is the clearest case.

### SRP-13 — Mixed motive does not disqualify role ownership

Iroha's institutional competence remains real despite strategic, romantic, reputational, and convenience motives.

### SRP-14 — Social labels change relational risk

Naming does not merely describe a relation; it changes what can be expected from it.

### SRP-15 — Mature continuity requires role revisability

The terminal Service Club model is transmission rather than identical preservation.

### SRP-16 — `神聖視` is a social-form failure

A successful present arrangement becomes distorted when its current form is treated as sacred and immune to succession or change.

# 19. Strong counterreadings and stress tests

## 19.1 Counterreading: the series ultimately says masks are bad and honesty is good

This reading captures a real preference for answerability and direct speech at decisive moments.

It fails as a total model because:

- Zaimokuza's persona remains valuable;
- Iroha remains strategic and performative after growth;
- Valentine pretext expands action;
- Hayama's expected role is partly owned;
- Yui's atmosphere work remains valuable;
- institutional roles continue after the ending;
- the series does not demand total disclosure.

The stronger claim is:

> **roles become dangerous when they foreclose answerability, not merely because they are roles.**

## 19.2 Counterreading: Iroha proves manipulation is harmless if motives are sincere

No.

Iroha proves mixed motive can coexist with real care and role ownership.

The ethical grading remains necessary.

Low-stakes transparent pretext differs from high-stakes choice substitution.

## 19.3 Counterreading: Hayama's role is entirely coerced

The text does not support that simplification.

Hayama experiences expectation as constraining but also identifies with parts of the expected self.

## 19.4 Counterreading: the post-election club is fake because everyone is lying

The stronger V2 account is structural.

The familiar performance becomes false in function because it blocks renegotiation, not because every individual utterance is consciously deceptive.

## 19.5 Counterreading: Komachi restores the old Service Club

Volume 14.5 rejects this.

Komachi inherits office and practice, not a duty to reproduce membership, relational geometry, or meaning.

## 19.6 Counterreading: Hachiman finally stops hiding behind roles

Only partially.

He becomes more able to separate desire from role justification.

He still inhabits social roles, jokes, institutional relationships, family structures, and ambiguous interactions.

Growth is role literacy and answerability, not role transcendence.

# 22. Terminal freeze

The V2 social-role / performance conclusion is:

> **Oregairu does not ask its characters to escape performance and reveal a pure private essence. It asks whether socially performed selves can remain owned, answerable, revisable, and compatible with other people's authorship. Early roles protect unsafe desire; middle-series roles become tools of administration, reputation management, preservation, and coercion; post-`本物` material shows that externally shaped roles can become genuinely owned; the prom arc demonstrates that diagnosis and institutional office can themselves become scripts that obscure desire; and the ending strips away role-based excuses without abolishing social form. Volume 14.5 supplies the final institutional correction: even the successful Service Club must not be `神聖視`. People, offices, mugs, memberships, and relational geometry may change. What can persist is practice—attention, request, friction, care, disagreement, support, and the capacity to answer. The mature alternative to false performance is therefore not rolelessness but revisable participation.**

This terminal freeze generates the following concise model:

> **performed identity + ownership + refusal + revision + answerability -> inhabitable social role**

and its primary failure mode:

> **performed identity + compulsory expectation + unilateral assignment + blocked refusal -> role capture**
