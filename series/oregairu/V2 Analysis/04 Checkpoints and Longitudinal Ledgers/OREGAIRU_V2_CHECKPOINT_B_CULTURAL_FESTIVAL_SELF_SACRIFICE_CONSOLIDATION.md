# Oregairu V2 — Checkpoint B: Cultural Festival / Self-Sacrifice Consolidation

## Purpose of this checkpoint

This document freezes the state of *Yahari Ore no Seishun Love Come wa Machigatteiru.* after the commercial **Volume 6.5 sports-festival sequence**, using **Volumes 4–6.5** as the positive-evidence window and **Checkpoint A** as the frozen end-of-Volume-3 baseline.

It is not a summary of four books. Its purpose is to identify what has actually changed in the character, relationship, intervention, autonomy, and authenticity systems between the early Service Club and the end of the cultural-festival aftermath.

The most important methodological problem is vocabulary. Retrospective discussion tends to compress several distinct Hachiman behaviors into one label—usually “self-sacrifice.” The V2 reread does not permit that compression. By this checkpoint, the corpus supports at least four different intervention modes:

1. **structural social engineering** — changing an environment so behavior becomes possible or impossible;
2. **self-borne cost allocation** — voluntarily accepting humiliation, dislike, or damage as the price of an outcome;
3. **other-borne cost allocation** — assigning cost to another person because Hachiman predicts that person can absorb it;
4. **control through unconsented role assignment** — deciding in advance what social or emotional role another person will be made to occupy.

These can overlap, but they are not morally identical.

The same discipline applies to Yukino. Her movement cannot be reduced to “independent girl learns to rely on friends.” Checkpoint A already established that bounded reliance was possible by Volume 3. What Volumes 4–6.5 add is a much more precise failure and correction:

> **autonomous assistance → substitutionary competence → particularized requested reliance → increasingly distributed institutional competence.**

Likewise, Yui cannot be frozen merely as mediator or emotional glue. Across this window she becomes increasingly operational: she diagnoses barriers to requesting help, interrupts Hachiman when another person's agency is about to emerge, particularizes reliance, accepts bounded work according to capacity, confronts both Hachiman and Yukino, and differentiates when she will wait from when she will actively approach.

The checkpoint therefore asks a narrower question than the eventual full-series synthesis:

> **What does the series know, by the end of 6.5, about helping people without replacing them, bearing costs without claiming ownership of the solution, and relying on others without turning them into tools?**

# 1. Executive frozen thesis

Checkpoint A ended with the Service Club as an emergent relational institution: three people with incompatible self-protection strategies learning to remain in contact through requests, bounded reliance, and increasingly particular attachment.

Volumes 4–6.5 transform that early institution into an **ethical laboratory for intervention**.

The central development is Hachiman's.

At the end of Volume 3, his strongest defense is **expectation avoidance**. He grants little behavioral authority to favorable uncertainty because expectation creates exposure. Volume 4 makes the underlying fear explicit: an irreplaceable person is frightening because the relationship can no longer be cut without injury. He distinguishes chosen solitude from maliciously imposed isolation and becomes increasingly willing to intervene in structures that force people into unwanted social positions. Yet the Rumi operation still contains meaningful collaborator consultation; Hayama knowingly accepts the ugly plan, and Rumi's unscripted rescue of her classmates prevents Hachiman from owning the meaning of the outcome.

Volume 5 then turns the intervention problem inward. Hachiman recognizes that he projected an exceptional image onto Yukino and that the projection was not merely romantic: her solitary `超人性` was something he wanted to acquire. His repeated `勝手に`—expecting, idealizing, believing he understood, then becoming disappointed—identifies the unilateral structure of the error. The lesson he carries into Volume 6 is initially defensible: do not impose your image on someone else.

But he converts that lesson into a new moral rule:

> `失望していいのは自分に対してだけであるべきだ。傷つけていいのは自分だけだ。`  
> *Disappointment should be directed only at oneself. The only person one should be allowed to hurt is oneself.*  
> — V06-L02, printed p.25

This is the decisive checkpoint transition.

Hachiman's earlier expectation avoidance becomes **cost-allocation ethics**. If other people should not bear the cost of his expectations, he will bear the cost himself. If an institution requires a villain, he can become the villain. If someone needs rescuing and a socially ugly method appears effective, he increasingly believes his willingness to damage himself grants him jurisdiction over the method.

Volume 6 demonstrates why that principle is ethically unstable. In the committee provocation, his self-borne reputational cost exposes a real exploitation problem and the committee subsequently acts for itself. At the rooftop, however, he goes further: he assigns Sagami the role of wounded target, Hayama the role of righteous rescuer, himself the role of villain, and Sagami's friends the role of validating social audience—without their consent. His own willingness to suffer becomes part of what licenses him, in his own moral accounting, to manipulate everyone else.

That is the point at which **self-sacrifice becomes control**.

Volume 6.5 then prevents the checkpoint from equating Hachiman's problem with martyrdom alone. When harassment reaches his shoe locker, he moves the garbage into Tobe's locker because Tobe's social network can transform the aggression into a joke. The prediction is accurate; the authorization is absent. Hachiman's deeper intervention pathology is therefore not simply “he spends himself.” It is:

> **he tends to treat social costs as allocatable resources and accurate prediction as partial authority to decide who should bear them.**

The same four-volume window produces equally important corrections in the rest of the cast.

**Yukino** begins Volume 4 with a strong authorization principle: if Rumi asks for help, she will use every means available. Volume 6 then shows a failure not of weakness but of competence: while assisting Sagami, Yukino gradually performs the work that the requester was supposed to learn to do. Her ability becomes a mechanism through which other people can cease exercising responsibility. Hachiman's critique is anchored in Yukino's own earlier method: she used to help, then return the matter to the person's will. Yui's response is not abstract dependency language but particularized invitation—`誰かとかみんなとかじゃなくて...あたしたちを頼って？`—and Yukino eventually makes a direct request: `あなたを頼らせてもらっても、いいかしら` (V06-L39, p.305). Volume 6.5 then advances one step further: Yukino works through a plural institution rather than replacing it.

**Yui** develops from relational architect into relational operator. Volume 4 establishes that she understands why someone may want help yet be unable to request it. During Rumi's escape she physically prevents Hachiman from ending the operation too soon, preserving Rumi's opportunity to act. Volume 6 she becomes angry at both Hachiman and Yukino for failing to let her help, particularizes reliance, accepts limits on what she can do, and differentiates between waiting for Yukino and actively going toward Hachiman. She remains accommodation-prone, but “passive preserver” is no longer a defensible checkpoint description.

**Hayama** becomes Hachiman's clearest competing intervention intelligence. He is not simply “the socially normal good guy.” Volume 4 shows that he can knowingly participate in an ugly plan while betting on a different theory of human behavior. Volume 6 shows him searching for Sagami seriously and attempting direct persuasion before Hachiman's coercive method. He supplies a counter-gaze that increasingly asks not whether Hachiman's tactics work, but why Hachiman believes only these tactics are available.

**Sagami** becomes the most important test case for behavioral change without moral purification. Volume 6 exposes and humiliates her. Volume 6.5 refuses both absolution and permanent condemnation. She remains approval-sensitive, resentful, status-conscious, and mixed in motive. But she accepts another responsibility, apologizes, explicitly asks for cooperation, continues after social support weakens, speaks for herself when Hachiman's procedural model stalls, accepts Meguri's recognition without having her old failure erased, and finishes the work. Her relationship with Hachiman does not become warm. It becomes sustainable distance.

**Meguri** supplies the checkpoint's strongest positive leadership countermodel. She does not pretend to be self-sufficient. She depends on a capable student-council team, remains legitimate as leader, recognizes Sagami's effort without calling her excellent, and leaves continuation as Sagami's choice. Her model is neither solitary competence nor self-sacrificial manipulation. It is **acknowledged, distributed interdependence**.

The checkpoint's governing thesis is therefore:

> **By the end of Volume 6.5, Oregairu has distinguished solitude from exclusion, reliance from use, competence from substitution, effective intervention from authorized intervention, and self-sacrifice from moral innocence. The Service Club can now solve problems, but the deeper question is whether its members can do so without privately deciding what everyone else is allowed to want, know, risk, or become.**

# 2. Evidence boundary and checkpoint architecture

## 2.1 Baseline

Checkpoint A remains immutable. Its end-of-Volume-3 state supplies the comparison point:

- Hachiman: expectation avoidance more developed than self-sacrifice;
- Yukino: real competence, bounded reliance already possible;
- Yui: relational architect/constitutor;
- triad: emergent relational institution, no settled geometry;
- helping ethic: authorization, diagnosis, cost awareness, subject authorship;
- `本物`: absent as governing vocabulary in V1–V2, explicit but non-unified in V3;
- dependence pathology: not yet established.

Checkpoint B does not rewrite any of those findings.

## 2.2 Positive evidence window

Positive claims may be established by:

- Volume 4;
- Volume 5;
- Volume 6;
- commercial Volume 6.5's sports-festival sequence.

The 6.25/6.50/6.75 texts matter only for genealogy and provenance. Their orphaned supplementary stories do not become governing 6.5 evidence merely because they once shared a release lineage.

## 2.3 Explicit exclusion

No Volume 7 or later material may establish a Checkpoint B claim.

## 2.4 Why this checkpoint matters before Kyoto

Volume 7 will introduce a different problem: preserving group form through deception and indirect intervention. If the present state is not frozen first, later knowledge can make Volume 6 look as though it already contained the full Kyoto critique.

It did not.

At this boundary, Hachiman has learned that his methods hurt people and that self-harm is not ethically private. He has **not** yet demonstrated a stable alternative method. Yukino has practiced requested reliance, but the club has **not** yet shown it can consistently state personal desires directly. Yui has become more active, but the later triangle-preservation problem is not yet established. `本物` remains an unstable vocabulary, not a settled doctrine.

# 3. Chosen solitude versus imposed isolation — the first major ethical refinement

Checkpoint A preserved a crucial early truth: Hachiman's solitude has real value. Growth must not be narrated as “loner learns that being alone is bad.”

Volume 4 makes the necessary distinction explicit.

Hachiman initially tries to apply his own solitary experience to Tsurumi Rumi. He rationalizes nonintervention by treating solitude as something from which a person may learn. Then he corrects himself:

> `孤立すること、一人でいること自体は別にいいんだ。問題なのは、悪意によって孤立させられていることだ。`  
> — V4-L10, printed p.141

This is not a repudiation of solitude. It is a refinement of consent and causation.

### Frozen distinction

**Chosen solitude** can be:

- pleasurable;
- protective;
- reflective;
- autonomy-preserving;
- compatible with meaningful relationships.

**Imposed isolation** is ethically different when it is produced by:

- malicious exclusion;
- rotating sacrificial mechanisms;
- reputation cascades;
- fear of becoming the next target;
- social structures that make approach costly.

This matters because later Oregairu sometimes appears to oppose social connection to Hachiman's loner identity. Checkpoint B must preserve the more precise early claim:

> **The series is not trying to cure Hachiman of being alone. It is teaching him to distinguish voluntarily chosen distance from social arrangements that deprive people of choice.**

Yui strengthens this distinction. Her account of Rumi is causal rather than sentimental: someone may want to speak, want to become close, or want help, yet inhabit an environment in which doing so risks becoming the next excluded person. That means the absence of a request cannot automatically be interpreted as absence of desire.

Yukino supplies the opposite ethical concern: she is reluctant to define another person's good without authorization. Her rule—if Rumi asks for help, she will act—is strong precisely because it protects subjecthood. Its limitation is practical: shame, guilt, fear, or social coercion may be what prevents the request.

The four-volume checkpoint therefore freezes a double requirement:

> **Do not force sociality on someone merely because you think closeness is better. But do not mistake coerced silence or exclusion for chosen solitude merely because no request has been spoken.**

That tension becomes foundational for later Service Club work.

# 4. Hachiman — transition from expectation avoidance to cost-allocation ethics

## 4.1 Checkpoint A baseline: expectation avoidance

At the end of Volume 3, Hachiman's strongest defensive mechanism is still epistemic and relational:

- grant favorable possibilities little standing;
- assume particular care may have another explanation;
- avoid expectations that could later become humiliation;
- preserve the ability to exit.

Volume 4 says the quiet part aloud. Hachiman fears `かけがえのない存在`—an irreplaceable person—and imagines the safest relationship as one that can be cut easily without anyone being hurt (V4-L02–03, p.30).

This is not yet a martyr ethic. It is a reversibility ethic.

## 4.2 Volume 4: intervention becomes structurally aggressive

The Rumi case adds a new tendency: Hachiman increasingly believes he has the right to redesign a hostile social environment.

His distinction between `解決` and `解消` is decisive:

> `問題は解決しない……でも、問題の解消はできる。`  
> — V4-L28, p.227

He does not promise to create friendship or justice. He proposes to destroy the mechanism that continually produces a victim.

That plan is coercive toward the children, but it must not be retroactively rewritten as the mature rooftop pattern.

Important consent differences remain:

- Hayama knowingly participates and even asks Hachiman to direct the operation (V4-L32, p.228–229);
- the older collaborators know the plan is ugly;
- Rumi's later rescue of her classmates is unscripted;
- Yukino explicitly credits Rumi's own will (V4-L41, p.270).

So the correct checkpoint classification is:

> **engineered rupture with informed collaborator participation, nonconsensual environmental manipulation of the target group, and preserved subject agency in the decisive final act.**

This is a precursor to control, not yet the cleanest self-sacrifice-as-control case.

## 4.3 Volume 5: unilateral interpretation becomes self-indictment

Volume 5 is the bridge from expectation avoidance to self-directed moral accounting.

Hachiman recognizes that his model of Yukino was partly self-authored. The repeated `勝手に` is the key:

> `勝手に期待して勝手に理想を押しつけて勝手に理解した気になって、そして勝手に失望する。`  
> — V05-L039, approximately p.225–226

He identifies four unilateral acts:

1. expecting;
2. imposing an ideal;
3. believing he understood;
4. becoming disappointed.

Crucially, his idealized Yukino was not merely an admired girl. He says her `完璧な超人性` was something **he wanted to acquire** (V05-L033). Her apparent solitary consistency validated an aspirational model of himself.

The failure therefore threatens both a relationship and a philosophy of self.

## 4.4 Volume 6: the self becomes the designated legitimate victim

The opening of Volume 6 converts the Volume 5 lesson into a dangerous moral rule.

Hachiman correctly concludes that he should not impose an image on someone else. Then:

> `失望していいのは自分に対してだけであるべきだ。傷つけていいのは自分だけだ。`  
> — V06-L02, p.25

This is the first mature formulation of the checkpoint's self-sacrifice problem.

The rule sounds ethically conscientious because it rejects harming others for one's expectations. But it contains three false premises:

1. that self-inflicted harm is ethically private;
2. that willingness to bear cost validates the intervention that produced it;
3. that choosing oneself as victim avoids assigning roles to other people.

Volume 6 systematically disproves all three.

## 4.5 Hachiman's self-appointed jurisdiction

When Yukino's substitutionary competence begins failing, Hachiman says:

> `ならその問題の解決は俺が任されよう。`  
> — V06-L24, p.201

Nobody formally appoints him.

The wording matters because it captures the transition from diagnosis into private jurisdiction. He sees a real problem, knows an intervention is needed, and silently converts recognition of need into authority to choose the method.

That authority is often grounded in accurate social prediction. The problem is not that he is socially incompetent. The problem is almost the opposite: his predictions are sufficiently good that he increasingly treats predictive competence as permission.

## 4.6 Committee provocation: self-borne cost without total control

Hachiman publicly attacks the committee's labor asymmetry and becomes a common enemy.

This is ugly and unilateral. It damages his reputation and disappoints Meguri. But its ethical structure differs from the rooftop:

- he exposes a real structural problem;
- he bears the reputational cost himself;
- the committee then argues, proposes alternatives, chooses, and works;
- his provocation restores some collective agency instead of scripting every later action.

Checkpoint classification:

> **unilateral social engineering + self-borne reputational cost + partially agency-restoring outcome.**

Calling this simply “self-sacrifice as control” erases the meaningful difference between provoking a group into acting and assigning the group's individual members predetermined roles.

## 4.7 Rooftop intervention: self-sacrifice becomes control

The rooftop is the decisive threshold.

Hachiman wants Sagami to return and fulfill the chair role she asked to be helped to fulfill. That goal preserves an important form of responsibility.

The method does not preserve deliberative agency.

He deliberately engineers:

- Sagami as the wounded person;
- himself as the villain;
- Hayama as moral rescuer;
- Sagami's friends as validating support;
- humiliation and anger as motivational force.

He also tells Sagami `誰も真剣にお前を捜してなかった` even though Hayama has just explained that he was seriously searching and following testimony (V06-L44–45, pp.323–326). This is not ordinary narratorial uncertainty. It is a tactically useful falsehood presented as objective social truth.

Hayama's question—`どうして、そんなやり方しかできないんだ` (V06-L46, p.328)—is therefore not a complaint that Hachiman is ineffective. It is a moral challenge to the narrowing of his available methods.

Checkpoint classification:

> **coercive agency-restoration through unconsented role assignment, powered by self-sacrificial moral accounting.**

This is the first scene where the phrase **self-sacrifice as control** should be considered secure.

## 4.8 Hiratsuka identifies the relational externality

Hiratsuka's correction prevents the checkpoint from treating self-harm as merely Hachiman's personal problem:

> `誰かを助けることは、君自身が傷ついていい理由にはならないよ`  
> — V06-L49, p.343–344

She then explains that people who care about him are hurt when he is hurt (V06-L50).

This is a profound correction to Hachiman's cost ledger.

Consent to one's own pain does not make the cost private once one exists in relationships of care.

The ethical unit is no longer the isolated actor. It is the **relational field**.

## 4.9 Volume 6.5: martyrdom is only one form of cost allocation

The shoe-locker scene broadens the diagnosis.

Hachiman moves harassment directed at him into Tobe's locker because he predicts Tobe's dense friendship network will transform the incident into a joke. The prediction works. The narration still registers real distress beneath Tobe's comic performance.

Tobe did not consent.

The scene therefore proves that Hachiman's deeper pattern is not simply “I will always take the hit.” It is:

> **I will identify where the hit can be absorbed with least structural damage, then move it there.**

Sometimes the least structurally damaging target is Hachiman himself. Sometimes it is someone else.

The checkpoint's more precise concept is **cost-allocation reasoning**.

### Frozen Hachiman transition

| Stage | Governing defense | Ethical risk |
|---|---|---|
| Checkpoint A / V1–3 | Expectation avoidance | Denies favorable uncertainty and particular care behavioral standing |
| V4 | Structural rupture | Treats accurate diagnosis as authority to redesign hostile social systems |
| V5 | Self-indictment after idealization | Converts relational disappointment into unilateral personal failure |
| V6 opening | Self as only legitimate victim | Treats self-harm as ethically clean cost-bearing |
| V6 committee | Self-borne social engineering | Manipulates group affect while partially restoring group agency |
| V6 rooftop | Self-sacrifice as control | Assigns unconsented roles to others and uses pain as causal instrument |
| V6.5 | Generalized cost allocation | Moves harm to whoever he predicts can absorb it most efficiently |

The checkpoint conclusion is not that Hachiman becomes worse. He becomes **more capable, more self-aware, and more dangerous at the same time**.

# 5. Social engineering, self-sacrifice, and control — frozen taxonomy

The later synthesis should use the following distinctions unless subsequent evidence requires revision.

## 5.1 Social engineering

**Definition:** deliberately changing incentives, information, roles, reputation, or atmosphere so that a group or person behaves differently.

Social engineering is not automatically self-sacrifice and not automatically control in the strongest sense.

Examples:

- V4 Rumi operation;
- V6 common-enemy committee provocation;
- V6.5 safety/procedure strategy against athletic-club resistance.

Its ethical quality depends on:

- target consent;
- collaborator consent;
- reversibility;
- whether the engineered field expands or contracts subject agency;
- whether alternative methods were available;
- how costs are assigned.

## 5.2 Self-sacrifice

**Definition:** Hachiman knowingly accepts a cost to himself—reputational, relational, emotional, or social—in pursuit of an outcome.

Self-sacrifice describes **who bears cost**, not whether the method is ethical.

It can coexist with:

- generosity;
- avoidance;
- control;
- accurate diagnosis;
- coercion;
- ordinary responsibility.

## 5.3 Cost allocation

**Definition:** Hachiman treats harm as a variable that can be assigned to the person or position best able to absorb it.

This includes self-sacrifice but is broader.

The Tobe locker scene is the clearest checkpoint evidence that his framework is not intrinsically self-focused.

## 5.4 Control

**Definition:** the helper claims practical authority over another person's choices, social role, information environment, or emotional pathway without adequate authorization.

Control is strongest when Hachiman:

- decides what the real problem is;
- decides what outcome counts;
- decides what role each participant will occupy;
- deliberately narrows the subject's alternatives;
- uses accurate prediction to substitute for consent.

The V6 rooftop is the strongest example.

## 5.5 Why outcomes cannot settle the taxonomy

A method can work and remain coercive.

A method can fail and remain ethically sound.

A person can regain formal agency through a process that violated deliberative agency.

Volume 6 makes this distinction unavoidable:

> **Sagami returning to the chair role is an effective outcome. It does not retroactively authorize the humiliation used to produce it.**

Volume 6.5 then supplies the inverse lesson:

> **Sagami's unscripted speech succeeds precisely because it lies outside Hachiman's plan. Subject-authored action can be causally stronger than engineered procedure.**

# 6. Yukino — autonomy, substitutionary competence, and requested reliance

## 6.1 Checkpoint A baseline

At the end of Volume 3:

- Yukino's competence is real;
- her reform ethic is sincere;
- she can ask for bounded practical help;
- she does not equate all reliance with weakness;
- her central difficulty is not incapacity for reliance.

Checkpoint B must preserve that baseline.

## 6.2 Volume 4: authorization as ethical strength

In the Rumi case Yukino says:

> `彼女が助けを求めるなら、あらゆる手段を持って解決に努めます。`  
> — V4-L12, p.145

This position has a limitation—Rumi may be unable to ask—but it also contains a major ethical strength. Yukino refuses to presume unlimited authority over another person's life merely because she can identify suffering.

Her approach protects:

- problem ownership;
- explicit authorization;
- subjecthood.

## 6.3 Volume 6: competence mutates into substitution

The cultural-festival request exposes a new failure mode.

Sagami asks for help becoming an effective chair. As Sagami delegates and evades, Yukino increasingly performs the practical work herself.

The institution begins learning a destructive lesson:

> if Yukino is competent enough, everyone else can stop becoming competent.

Hachiman's critique is especially important because he cites Yukino's own prior practice:

> `最後は必ず本人の意志に委ねてきた`  
> — V06-L20, p.197

Yukino's mistake is therefore not “too independent.” It is **substitutionary competence**: assistance so effective that it removes the other's need to exercise authorship.

## 6.4 Yui particularizes reliance

Yui's correction avoids abstract slogans:

> `誰かとかみんなとかじゃなくて...あたしたちを頼って？`  
> — V06-L21, p.199

The language matters. She does not ask Yukino to embrace “dependence.” She asks Yukino to rely on **particular people who have voluntarily offered themselves**.

That changes reliance from weakness into relationship.

Yukino initially cannot do so immediately. Yui allows the deferral.

Later Yukino asks:

> `あなたを頼らせてもらっても、いいかしら`  
> — V06-L39, p.305

And Yui answers that she had been waiting to hear it.

This is the checkpoint's clearest positive reliance sequence:

1. need exists;
2. the helper is particular rather than generic;
3. permission is requested;
4. limitations can be stated;
5. burden is shared;
6. reciprocity remains possible.

## 6.5 Volume 6.5: from reliance on a person to plural institution

The sports-festival sequence takes another step.

Yukino does not merely “learn to ask Hachiman and Yui for help.” She increasingly permits work to remain distributed across:

- Meguri;
- Hayama;
- Yui;
- Sagami;
- committee members;
- herself;
- Hachiman.

This is a structural improvement over cultural-festival substitution.

The correct frozen formulation is:

> **Yukino increasingly permits an institution to remain plural.**

That is more precise than “Yukino becomes dependent.”

## 6.6 Autonomy is not the opposite of reliance

Checkpoint B now has enough evidence to reject a simple binary:

- self-reliance can be dignified;
- reliance can be trust-bearing;
- dependence can be role-compatible;
- assistance can become exploitation;
- competence can erase another person's agency;
- distributed dependence can preserve leadership.

The series is already asking **how** reliance is structured, not merely whether it exists.

# 7. Yui — from relational architect to operational social agency

Checkpoint A froze Yui as an early relational architect. Volumes 4–6.5 strongly confirm and expand that classification.

## 7.1 Volume 4: Yui diagnoses constrained desire

Yui explains that Rumi may want to speak or reconcile and still be unable to act because the environment punishes approach. She connects guilt, fear, and atmosphere rather than treating silence as preference.

This is social analysis, not merely empathy.

She understands that agency can be **present but constrained**.

## 7.2 Yui protects another person's opportunity to choose

During the Rumi operation, Yui notices Rumi preparing to act and physically prevents Hachiman from terminating the performance too early.

This is a small but important ethical act.

Yui does not choose for Rumi. She protects the **time window in which Rumi can choose for herself**.

That is almost the inverse of Hachiman's later rooftop method.

## 7.3 Volume 5: Yui offers an epistemic ethic of approach

When Hachiman moves toward withdrawal, Yui says:

> `でもあたしはもっと知りたい、な……。`  
> `お互いよく知って、もっと仲良くなりたい。`  
> `困ってたら力になりたい。`  
> — V05-L23–25, approximately p.208–209

She does not claim complete understanding. She proposes a process:

> more knowledge → greater closeness → better ability to help.

This is one of the checkpoint's strongest alternatives to both omniscient interpretation and defensive nonknowledge.

## 7.4 Volume 6: Yui becomes a source of accountability

Yui is angry with **both** Yukino and Hachiman for failing to let her help.

This matters because her care is no longer reducible to smoothing conflict. She is willing to create conflict when the existing atmosphere prevents reciprocal relation.

She also openly says she may like Yukino more than she realized (V06-L07, p.93), reinforcing that Yui–Yukino is a primary relationship rather than a derivative side of the triangle.

## 7.5 Waiting versus approaching

Yui's later distinction is especially important:

- Yukino is someone she will wait for because Yukino is trying to approach;
- there are people for whom waiting accomplishes nothing;
- toward such a person, `こっちから行くの`—she will go herself (V06-L35, pp.254–255).

Her gaze toward Hachiman makes the romantic coding strong, but Checkpoint B should freeze the broader behavioral point:

> **Yui uses different relational strategies according to the person and the obstacle.**

She is not simply “the one who waits.”

## 7.6 Volume 6.5: operational translation

The sports festival continues this movement.

Yui helps convert social reality into workable action without becoming the sole decision-maker. She can:

- identify what people are actually reacting to;
- move between groups;
- articulate feasible contributions;
- support Sagami without pretending the cultural-festival history vanished;
- cooperate with Yukino without requiring Yukino's total self-sufficiency.

### Frozen Yui state

By the end of 6.5, Yui should be described as:

> **a relational architect who is becoming an operational social agent: she reads atmosphere, but increasingly uses that knowledge to create choices rather than merely adapt herself to whatever atmosphere already exists.**

Her risk remains real: accommodation can still become self-suppression. But later “triangle preservation” language must not be imported backward as the total explanation of her behavior here.

# 8. Hachiman and Hayama — competing intervention intelligences

Checkpoint B firmly rejects two simplistic Hayama readings:

- Hayama as naïve nice guy;
- Hayama as purely hypocritical social conformist.

## 8.1 Volume 4: same experiment, opposite priors

Hachiman and Hayama participate in the same operation against the Rumi exclusion system.

Hachiman expects fear to reveal betrayal.

Hayama says:

> `本性というなら俺はそっちの本性を信じたい。根は良い子たちだと思うんだよ`  
> — V4-L31, p.228

The result gives both men evidence:

- the children initially betray one another, supporting Hachiman's model;
- Rumi then acts prosocially and saves them, preserving Hayama's possibility.

Neither theory is total.

## 8.2 Hayama accepts ugly means under constraint

Hayama's participation matters because it prevents the V2 reading from treating him as someone who simply refuses morally compromised action.

He can cooperate with Hachiman's ugly method when he thinks the situation warrants it. He asks Hachiman to direct the operation.

The difference is not “Hachiman acts, Hayama moralizes.”

The difference increasingly concerns **what each man believes coercive pressure reveals and when instrumentalization becomes unacceptable**.

## 8.3 Volume 6: direct intervention before coercive intervention

Hayama seriously searches for Sagami and attempts persuasion.

This becomes crucial counterevidence when Hachiman tells Sagami that nobody was seriously searching.

Hayama's later question—why can Hachiman only do it this way?—therefore carries evidentiary weight. Hayama has just demonstrated another method, even if that method failed to produce the immediate outcome.

Effectiveness alone cannot make Hachiman's method the only available category of action.

## 8.4 Volume 6.5: social legitimacy as useful but non-sovereign power

Hayama's presence helps get Sagami into the new chair role. Hachiman then insists he could have achieved the same result without him and that Hayama merely saved effort.

That line is revealing.

Hachiman prefers to interpret Hayama's help as **replaceable utility**. He resists turning assistance into relational indebtedness or acknowledgment that another person's distinct social power changed the field.

Checkpoint B therefore freezes Hachiman–Hayama as:

> **mutually accurate, ideologically opposed, operationally compatible rivals in social intervention.**

They are not friends in the ordinary sense. Their ability to recognize one another precisely is itself a meaningful relationship.

# 9. Sagami — humiliation, behavioral rehabilitation, and non-intimate repair

Sagami is indispensable to Checkpoint B because she prevents the analysis from treating growth as either inner purification or social forgiveness.

## 9.1 Volume 6: mixed motive, real failure

Sagami's motives include:

- growth;
- skill development;
- social approval;
- visible status;
- comparative position;
- successful festival participation.

The V2 method does not require selecting one as the “real” motive and declaring the others fake.

Her failure is nevertheless concrete:

- she retains visible office;
- transfers practical burden;
- weakens accountability;
- contributes to Yukino's overload;
- eventually abandons the role at the moment it most requires her presence.

Hachiman's rooftop intervention restores her formal chair function through humiliation.

That is an outcome, not a rehabilitation.

## 9.2 Volume 6.5: second opportunity without purification

Yukino proposes Sagami for the sports-festival chair role.

The recommendation is severe rather than forgiving. It creates another opportunity for responsibility.

The move carries paternalistic risk because Sagami's own desire is not the sole origin of the proposal. Yet Sagami retains meaningful refusal and later becomes visible owner of the position.

This makes the intervention ethically superior to the rooftop method even before its outcome is known.

## 9.3 Behavioral change is enough to matter

Sagami remains difficult.

She is still:

- approval-sensitive;
- status-conscious;
- resentful toward Hachiman;
- vulnerable to peer judgment;
- mixed in motive.

But she acts differently.

By the end of 6.5 she has:

- accepted exposed responsibility;
- apologized;
- asked explicitly for cooperation;
- promised attention to others' club burdens;
- continued under weakened support;
- expressed her grievance directly instead of disappearing into the role;
- accepted Meguri's recognition;
- chosen to continue;
- completed the post-event work.

This is why the V1 shorthand **rehabilitation, not redemption** survives.

## 9.4 Hachiman's theory of change becomes less binary

Hachiman continues resisting the idea that a painful event magically changes someone's essence.

That resistance contains a legitimate insight.

But 6.5 adds:

> `習慣化された行動はいつしか自身を定義するものになる。`

Repeated behavior can become habitual; habitual behavior can eventually participate in social identity.

This allows Checkpoint B to freeze a more mature proposition:

> **Growth need not require proving that an inner essence has been replaced. Stable new behavior can become morally and socially real while motives remain mixed.**

## 9.5 Sagami's unscripted speech breaks Hachiman's diagram

The planned procedural strategy stalls.

Sagami then says, in effect:

- she apologized;
- she is doing the work;
- she is trying to do it properly this time;
- she wants to know why that effort is not being recognized.

The crucial point is structural: Hachiman explicitly recognizes that the speech lies outside his plan.

Sagami stops being the **object** of rehabilitation and becomes the **speaker** of the grievance.

That shift changes the field more effectively than the procedural diagram alone.

## 9.6 The end state is strangerhood, not friendship

After the festival Sagami is still working.

She tells Hachiman:

> `ねぇ、そこ、どいてくんない`

He moves.

There is no apology scene, forgiveness scene, or friendship conversion.

The relationship has improved because they can sustain ordinary distance without reopening the wound.

This is one of Checkpoint B's most important relational categories:

> **ethical repair does not always require intimacy.**

# 10. Meguri — distributed leadership as the strongest positive countermodel

Shiromeguri Meguri becomes much more than a pleasant student-council president in this checkpoint.

## 10.1 She does not claim sovereign competence

Meguri openly recognizes that the people around her make her presidency workable.

She therefore violates one of the assumptions that traps both Hachiman and Yukino:

> leadership does not require proving that one could do everything alone.

Her authority survives acknowledged dependence.

## 10.2 Her model is distributed rather than substitutionary

Meguri coordinates:

- roles;
- people;
- institutional memory;
- social warmth;
- practical competence.

She does not need to become the best performer in every domain.

This is the opposite of Volume 6 Yukino's failure state, where extreme competence encourages everyone else to stop functioning.

## 10.3 Recognition without idealization

Meguri's treatment of Sagami is especially important.

She does not say Sagami has become excellent.

She says, essentially, that she sees Sagami trying and doing the work.

Then she leaves continuation as Sagami's decision.

This intervention contains:

- accurate recognition;
- no purified narrative;
- no forced self-esteem;
- no denial of past failure;
- no seizure of the choice.

## 10.4 Frozen Meguri model

Checkpoint B should treat Meguri as the clearest positive model yet of:

> **role-compatible dependence + distributed competence + accurate recognition + preserved choice.**

That model matters because later dependency language must be tested against examples the series has already presented as functional.

# 11. Healthy reliance, exploitation, and autonomy

Checkpoint B can now freeze a much richer dependency/autonomy taxonomy than Checkpoint A.

## 11.1 Reliance is not pathology

The strongest Japanese distinction comes from Hachiman himself:

> `頼ってるならまだいいですよ、単純に使っているだけの奴がいる`  
> — V06-L14, p.154

Even Hachiman distinguishes reliance from use.

That distinction is essential.

## 11.2 Healthy reliance

Healthy reliance in this checkpoint usually contains several of the following:

- need is stated;
- the helper is particular rather than abstract;
- refusal remains possible;
- limitations can be admitted;
- cost is visible;
- roles are bounded;
- reciprocity is possible;
- the recipient retains authorship.

Examples:

- Yukino asking Yui for help before the festival performance;
- Sagami later asking the Service Club for cooperation in 6.5;
- Meguri relying on her student-council team.

## 11.3 Exploitation / use

Use becomes visible when:

- the nominal requester keeps title/status while shifting burden;
- the helper's competence becomes assumed infrastructure;
- gratitude or relationship is absent;
- the helper's agency is treated as a resource;
- the recipient no longer has to develop the capacity the request was supposed to cultivate.

The cultural-festival treatment of Yukino is the checkpoint's clearest case.

## 11.4 Substitutionary care

Substitution differs from exploitation because the helper may sincerely want to help.

Yukino's error is benevolent:

- she can do the work;
- the event needs the work done;
- Sagami is failing;
- therefore Yukino does more.

But every task completed in place of Sagami can reduce Sagami's opportunity to become the person her own request ostensibly sought to become.

Benevolence does not guarantee autonomy.

## 11.5 Dependence compatible with leadership

Meguri supplies direct counterevidence to any later claim that needing others is itself disqualifying.

Her dependence is:

- acknowledged;
- distributed;
- non-possessive;
- productive;
- compatible with responsibility.

## 11.6 Hachiman's resistance to relational debt

Hachiman repeatedly prefers to describe other people's help as replaceable function.

His statement to Hayama in 6.5—that he did not truly need Hayama and merely saved effort—is an important example.

This preference protects Hachiman from indebtedness and particularity. If help is replaceable utility, it need not create a claim on him or revise his self-image as someone who can manage alone.

### Frozen reliance proposition

> **Oregairu has not established “dependency” as the problem. The problem is relation without authorship: using, replacing, owning, or silently assigning burden. Chosen interdependence is already repeatedly depicted as functional.**

# 12. `本物` chronology through Volume 6.5

Checkpoint B must keep the lexical history disciplined.

## 12.1 Volumes 1–2

No governing `本物` vocabulary.

Thematically adjacent issues exist, but the famous term has not yet become an organizing doctrine.

## 12.2 Volume 3 — lexical threshold

Checkpoint A froze three important but non-unified domains:

- authentic presentation;
- non-instrumental expression;
- particular/non-substitutable affection.

The term is active but not yet one theory.

## 12.3 Volume 4 — act-level genuineness inside compromised relation

The Rumi climax provides the clearest early hypothesis:

> a relationship dependent on sacrificing someone cannot be `本物`;
> yet knowingly extending a hand inside a relation one knows is `偽物` may itself be `本物`.

The crucial Japanese is:

> `偽物だってわかってて、それでも手を差し伸べたいって思ったなら、そいつは本物なんだろ、きっと。`  
> — V4-L35, p.265

Then Hachiman immediately hedges:

> `いや、知らねぇけど`

The checkpoint must preserve the hedge.

Volume 4 proposes **act-level authenticity without relation-level purification**. It does not settle the doctrine.

## 12.4 Volume 5 — authenticity problem without governing `本物`

Volume 5 is foundational to the later authenticity question but does not materially advance it through a major `本物` cluster.

Its governing vocabulary is:

- `知る`;
- `理解`;
- `解釈`;
- `期待`;
- `理想`;
- `噓`;
- `誠実`.

Its contribution is epistemic:

> human beings must act through incomplete interpretations, but those interpretations must remain revisable.

## 12.5 Volume 6 — Hachiman suspects effortful maintenance is counterfeit

The main conceptual occurrence is:

> `頑張って取り繕うのはきっと本物ではないから。`  
> — V06-L08, p.94

Hachiman suspects that a relationship requiring deliberate patching, adjustment, or maintenance is therefore not genuine.

The novel itself increasingly supplies counterevidence:

- Yui waits because Yukino is trying;
- Yui actively approaches where waiting is useless;
- Yukino practices asking;
- Hachiman and Yukino revisit misunderstanding;
- the club repairs awkwardness rather than treating effort as proof of falsity.

Thus Checkpoint B freezes a productive contradiction:

> **Hachiman suspects effort disqualifies authenticity; the narrative increasingly shows effort as the labor through which relation becomes durable.**

## 12.6 Volume 6.5 — no governing `本物`, but mixed motives become morally legible

The sports-festival sequence contains no governing `本物`/`偽物` cluster.

Yet it contributes an important precursor:

Sagami can:

- want recognition;
- want status;
- want success;
- want proof she is trying;
- remain resentful;
- and still develop behaviorally.

The action does not become meaningless because the motive is impure.

### Frozen honmono chronology

| Volume | Checkpoint status |
|---|---|
| V1 | No governing `本物` |
| V2 | No governing `本物` |
| V3 | Explicit threshold; multiple non-unified domains |
| V4 | Candidate act-level genuineness within acknowledged relational falsity |
| V5 | Epistemic revision problem; no major lexical advance |
| V6 | Hachiman suspects effortful maintenance is not genuine; narrative counterpressure grows |
| V6.5 | No governing lexical cluster; mixed motives and performed roles can still become socially real |

The mature doctrine must **not** be imported here.

# 13. Effective outcomes versus ethically authorized methods

This is the checkpoint's central normative distinction.

## 13.1 Outcome is not authorization

A method can produce the desired result while violating subjecthood.

Examples:

- Rumi's exclusion mechanism is broken, but the children were manipulated into the crisis;
- Sagami returns to the stage, but the rooftop method humiliates her and scripts Hayama without consent;
- Tobe's social network neutralizes harassment, but Tobe never agreed to receive the garbage;
- the sports-festival bureaucratic strategy pressures opponents through engineered incentives.

None of these can be evaluated by outcome alone.

## 13.2 Authorization is not outcome guarantee

Conversely, an ethically cleaner method may fail.

Hayama's direct attempt to find and persuade Sagami does not immediately solve the crisis.

That failure does not prove the method ethically inferior.

## 13.3 Formal agency versus deliberative agency

Volume 6 creates an important distinction:

Sagami ultimately returns and performs the chair role. In formal terms, agency has been restored.

But the emotional pathway to that action was deliberately engineered through humiliation and rescue.

So:

> **formal responsibility can be restored through a process that violates deliberative agency.**

## 13.4 Subject authorship as the strongest positive indicator

Across V4–6.5, the most ethically promising interventions are those in which the subject does something the helper did not fully script:

- Rumi rescues her classmates;
- Yukino explicitly requests Yui's help;
- Sagami speaks outside Hachiman's diagram;
- Sagami chooses to continue after Meguri's recognition;
- Hachiman and Sagami settle into boundary-based strangerhood rather than prescribed reconciliation.

A useful checkpoint principle is:

> **The helper's success is ethically strongest when the helped person can surprise the helper.**

Surprise is evidence that the subject has not been reduced to an instrument inside the helper's theory.

## 13.5 Recognition can enable agency without dictating it

Meguri's treatment of Sagami becomes the cleanest model.

She sees effort, names it accurately, does not erase failure, and then asks whether Sagami wants to continue.

Recognition changes the decision environment without taking possession of the decision.

# 14. Relationship-state freeze at the end of Volume 6.5

## 14.1 Hachiman ↔ Yukino

**Trust:** repaired relative to the Volume 5 rupture, but not complete.

**Disclosure:** increasing; both have begun revisiting prior interpretations rather than treating first judgments as final.

**Epistemic model:** markedly improved. The Volume 6 ending rejects complete understanding and moves toward repeated revision: `飽きもせずに問い直すんだ` (V06-L55, p.352).

**Reliance:** increasingly possible and particular, though Yukino's cleanest explicit request in Volume 6 is directed toward Yui.

**Conflict tolerance:** high. They can challenge each other's methods directly.

**Romantic coding:** meaningful but still not a declared relationship.

**Primary risk:** each can still use the other's strength to avoid stating personal desire—Hachiman through functionalization, Yukino through role and competence.

**Frozen shorthand:** **revised mutual recognition + high competence trust + growing tolerance for provisional understanding.**

## 14.2 Hachiman ↔ Yui

**Trust:** high enough for Yui to challenge him and for Hachiman to use her help operationally.

**Disclosure:** asymmetrical. Yui increasingly states concrete wants; Hachiman remains skilled at deflection.

**Future assumption:** stronger than Checkpoint A. Yui repeatedly imagines continued relation and active approach.

**Romantic legibility:** high, but explicit confession remains absent at this boundary.

**Care:** no longer plausibly reducible to debt or dog-accident origin.

**Primary risk:** Hachiman still prefers ambiguity when clearer information would demand an answer.

**Frozen shorthand:** **particular care + active pursuit from Yui + continued answer-avoidance from Hachiman.**

## 14.3 Yukino ↔ Yui

**Trust:** high.

**Affection:** explicit enough that Yui says she may like Yukino more than she realized.

**Reliance:** now directly enacted through request and acceptance.

**Difference:** increasingly productive rather than merely confusing.

**Physical/social ease:** strong relative to the early volumes.

**Primary risk:** Yukino may still accept Yui's help only after overextension rather than before it.

**Frozen shorthand:** **particular friendship becoming a practical interdependence.**

## 14.4 The triad

The triad remains an institutionalized relationship whose strongest language is often still work.

It is more emotionally particular than at Checkpoint A, but still avoids direct articulation of the full stakes.

Important developments:

- Yui actively asks for reciprocal reliance;
- Yukino begins practicing it;
- Hachiman can cooperate but still claims private jurisdiction over hard solutions;
- each member now experiences the others' injury or withdrawal as consequential.

The club is therefore no longer merely a convenient relational technology. It has become a **cost-bearing relational system**.

That does not yet establish pathological dependency.

## 14.5 Hachiman ↔ Hayama

**Recognition:** unusually accurate.

**Operational compatibility:** high.

**Ideological agreement:** low.

**Mutual respect:** real but often hostile.

**Primary dispute:** whether social intervention should trust ordinary prosocial capacity, how far coercion may go, and whether Hachiman's ugly methods are genuinely necessary.

**Frozen shorthand:** **competing social intelligences with strong mutual legibility.**

## 14.6 Hachiman ↔ Sagami

The relationship moves through:

> dislike → humiliation → continued antagonism → functional distance.

There is no friendship and no forgiveness requirement.

By the end of 6.5 they can occupy the same institutional world without repeatedly reopening the cultural-festival wound.

**Frozen shorthand:** **sustainable strangerhood.**

## 14.7 Sagami ↔ Meguri

Meguri supplies the recognition Sagami wants without rewriting Sagami as someone she is not.

That recognition enables chosen continuation.

**Frozen shorthand:** **accurate recognition → restored authorship without absolution.**

# 15. Service Club polity — what kind of institution has it become?

Checkpoint A described the club as adult-imposed institutional form becoming chosen relational infrastructure.

Checkpoint B adds that it has become a **miniature polity of unequal competencies and partially shared obligations**.

Its members now face questions that political institutions face:

- Who gets to define a problem?
- Who has jurisdiction?
- What counts as consent?
- Can competence become illegitimate power?
- Can a person voluntarily assume a cost that creates externalities for others?
- When does care become paternalism?
- How should unequal skills be distributed without creating dependency or replacement?
- Does successful governance require transparency?
- Can conflict itself be legitimate relational labor?

The Service Club's strength is that its members can increasingly correct one another:

- Hachiman identifies Yukino's substitutionary competence;
- Yui identifies both members' failure to allow reliance;
- Yukino recognizes Hachiman's useful contribution without endorsing his method;
- Hiratsuka supplies adult correction when the internal system cannot regulate Hachiman's self-harm.

Its weakness is that **requests still provide moral cover for desires and powers nobody wants to name directly**.

The cultural festival shows how easily “helping the client” can expand into private authority over everyone involved.

# 16. Japanese-language anchors that should govern later synthesis

Checkpoint B freezes the following formulations as high-value lexical anchors.

## 16.1 `孤立` versus chosen aloneness

`孤立すること、一人でいること自体は別にいいんだ。問題なのは、悪意によって孤立させられていることだ。`

The causative/passive structure matters: the ethical problem is being **made** isolated through malice, not simply being alone.

## 16.2 `解決` versus `解消`

Volume 4's distinction prevents outcome language from becoming imprecise.

`解決` suggests solving the underlying problem.

`解消` can mean removing or dissolving the condition without establishing a healthy replacement.

## 16.3 Repeated `勝手に`

Volume 5's fourfold repetition assigns unilateral authorship to Hachiman's interpretation cycle.

The problem is not just error. It is that he privately generated the expectation, ideal, certainty, and disappointment.

## 16.4 `傷つけていいのは自分だけ`

This sentence is the checkpoint's clearest lexical bridge into self-sacrificial moral accounting.

The later Hiratsuka correction directly contests it.

## 16.5 `頼る` versus `使う`

`頼ってるならまだいいですよ、単純に使っているだけの奴がいる`

This line should govern later dependency analysis. The text itself distinguishes reliance from instrumental use.

## 16.6 `あたしたちを頼って？`

Yui's pronoun choice particularizes reliance. The offer is relational rather than doctrinal.

## 16.7 `あなたを頼らせてもらっても、いいかしら`

Yukino's construction is permission-bearing. She does not seize help; she asks to be allowed to rely.

## 16.8 `誰も傷つかない世界`

Whether treated as irony, bitter joke, or distorted accounting, the sentence cannot function as reliable description. It should be read against the visible costs surrounding it.

## 16.9 `ちゃんと`

Sagami's repeated `ちゃんと` in 6.5 asks to be judged by current performance under a shared norm of responsibility rather than solely by the frozen cultural-festival narrative.

## 16.10 `本物ではない`

Hachiman's `頑張って取り繕うのはきっと本物ではないから` is explicitly hedged by `きっと`. It is a belief under pressure, not authorial theorem.

# 20. Checkpoint conclusion

Checkpoint A ended before any one member's self-protection strategy had become powerful enough to threaten the others on a large scale.

Checkpoint B ends after that threshold has been crossed.

Hachiman has become capable of solving problems by treating social pain as something that can be budgeted. That capacity is neither wholly vicious nor wholly heroic. His structural intelligence can expose coercive systems, create openings, and restore stalled institutions. His willingness to bear cost can protect people. But the same strengths become dangerous when he assumes that being willing to suffer gives him the right to decide what everyone else will suffer, believe, or become.

Yukino has discovered the opposite danger. Competence can become paternalistic not because it is false or weak, but because it is so effective that other people cease acting. Her growth is not from independence to dependence. It is from solitary or substitutive competence toward **plural competence**: letting other people remain necessary.

Yui increasingly supplies the relational technology that both of them lack. She notices when desire cannot become request, when waiting is meaningful, when waiting is useless, when another person's chance to act needs protection, and when a relationship has become important enough that “helping” should itself be reciprocal. Her risk is no longer invisibility to the analysis; it is what happens when this active relational intelligence eventually collides with desires that cannot all be jointly satisfied.

Hayama establishes that moral disagreement with Hachiman is not equivalent to social naïveté. Sagami establishes that a humiliating past need not monopolize later identity. Meguri establishes that needing others can coexist with legitimate leadership. Rumi and Sagami together establish that the strongest evidence of preserved subjecthood is often the moment when the subject does something the helper did not plan.

The most important philosophical movement is therefore not “Hachiman learns self-sacrifice is bad.” That would be too shallow.

It is:

> **The series is learning to distinguish the willingness to pay a cost from the authority to choose the transaction.**

A person may consent to hurt himself and still wrong others by making them participants in the bargain. A leader may be competent and still wrong others by replacing their agency. A relationship may require work without becoming fake. A flawed person may change behavior without becoming pure. A relationship may be repaired without becoming intimate. Reliance may be healthy while use is not.

By the end of Volume 6.5, the Service Club is therefore more capable than it has ever been and less ethically innocent than it was at Checkpoint A.

> **Checkpoint B governing sentence:**
>
> **By the end of Volume 6.5, the Service Club has learned that helping is not validated by effectiveness, sacrifice, competence, or good intention alone: the real ethical test is whether the people inside the solution remain authors of their own roles, costs, refusals, and continuations.**
