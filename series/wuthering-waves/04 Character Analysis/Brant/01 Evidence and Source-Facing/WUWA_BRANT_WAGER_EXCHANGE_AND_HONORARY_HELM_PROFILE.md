# Brant — a day's chores, a costly treasure, and an honorary helm

Brant's character quest stages several ways of asking what a desirable outcome costs. The fishing wager transfers a day's crew chores to the captain while inviting Rover to try a temporary role (`flow#/5236/3`). Later a painting and an inscription associate Drake's treasure with a broken heart, a curse and “equal exchange” (`6281/6`; `6282/1`). At the scripted post-adventure conversation Brant offers Rover Drake's map and names them an honorary captain (`6283/2`). These are **different** transactions with different evidence: a socially adjudicated bet, an interpretation of symbolic art, a quoted rule plus his inference, and a scripted gesture of recognition. Their juxtaposition can test his cost-accounting ethic, but it cannot prove the treasure literally consumes a heart, the crew had full knowledge of every danger, or Rover acquired permanent operational command [BRA-E41–E43; BRA-C37–C39].

## The fish is not caught by the two bettors

The early wager starts among crew members, not with Rover compelled into it. Battier proposes that, if his rod rises first, the duty crew get a day off and Brant handles cooking, deck cleaning and weapon maintenance (`Character_Brant_3_15–16`, technical speaker `50098`). Narration at `flow#/5236/3/13–17` presents a twitching rod, an ignored player warning and Rover's hauling of a large fish. A crewmate then identifies the rod as Battier's; Battier declares Brant loses and owes the chores (`_3_26–30`). The fish is physically hauled by Rover from Battier's rod, so the betting condition is socially interpreted rather than settled by a literal “one of the bettors caught it” test. Brant accepts the judgment, promises to be the duty sailor and later says he must scrub the deck (`_3_31`, `_3_44`). This supports a willingness to bear a finite, visible cost for a crew benefit. It does **not** show every promised task completed, or establish a general rule that Brant will always personally absorb every cost he creates [BRA-E41; BRA-C37].

Rover's possible one-day captaincy enters only after Battier suggests that the person who caught the fish could take the helm (`_3_32–34`, still crew speech). Rover can react with disbelief or ask about compensation. The raw `TalkSequence`/`SequenceTransitions` in `5236/3` sends those alternatives to separate Brant replies, `Character_Brant_3_37` (confidence in Rover) or `_3_38` (the ship handles recompense), then rejoins at `_3_39`: whether to accept the *one-day* title remains Rover's choice. EN's “We sail straight!” is less explicit about pay than ZH's “proper ship,” JA's practical answer and KO's legal-operation wording; it should not be used alone to certify a payment amount or flawless labor practice. His later dinner invitation is an offer of thanks, not recorded acceptance or an exclusive date. The two opening wager-explanation options also split and rejoin; neither pair belongs in one invented transcript. The [temporary-helm study](WUWA_BRANT_TEMPORARY_HELM_BRANCH_AND_CREW_VALUE_PROFILE.md) follows additional `5238/2`, `5239/3`, `5241/3` and `5244/3` alternatives. This new scene establishes *how the role arises* before those later tests [BRA-E37, E41; BRA-C33, C37].

## A painting poses a question; it does not collect a heart

At `flow#/6281/6` Brant identifies a captain figure, a coin, treasure on one side of a scale and a broken heart on the other. He asks Rover what the image means (`Character_Brant_61_1–3`). Three Rover options mean “how to obtain treasure,” “a warning,” or “I do not know” (`_61_4–6`). The raw `JumpTalk` targets take the three options to distinct Brant replies `_61_7–9` and each reply jumps back to TalkId 7 for his common continuation. The first reply entertains a heart-for-treasure reading; the second notes possible treasure-owner traps; the third expects further clues. Do not make all three interpretations a single progressive deduction by Rover or Brant [BRA-E42; BRA-C38].

In the common passage, Brant refers to a distant country said to weigh souls and wealth, wonders whether this could represent a kind of exchange, then speculates that perhaps Drake cared about fairness (`_61_10–12`). The repeated Chinese hedge `或许` and the Japanese/Korean possible-language matter: this is a conjecture about an image and an absent treasure owner, **not** Brant's settled doctrine or a factual verification of Drake's moral character. EN's “everything has a price” is a vivid interpretation of the scale; it cannot by itself transform a painted broken heart into a literal payable item. The later map's account of Drake's crew losses and Brant's challenge to Aldric provide reasons to revisit this early fairness hypothesis, not to pretend he already knew the final ledger here [BRA-E20/E31, E42; BRA-C11/C28/C38].

## The inscription is a source within the scene, not the scene's verdict

At `flow#/6282/1` Brant reads an inscription calling for equal cursing of treasure thieves and repeating a rule of exchange (`Character_Brant_65_1–3`). Those lines are Brant *quoting a local inscription*; the source supports that he encountered that wording, not that a curse visibly operated or that every possible exchange is just. His next inference is that a corresponding price might explain why the treasure has not been obtained (`_65_6`). Rover has two short option surfaces about curse versus rule and later about the rule's meaning (`_65_4–5` and `_65_8–9`). The raw `ShowTalk` object has no `TalkSequence` or option `Actions` at those prompts. It retains the text alternatives and intervening Brant lines, but does not itself prove which wording a given account selected or a more complex branch topology. A model should quote a selected option only after declaring the hypothetical route [BRA-E42; BRA-C38].

One localization difference is too large to flatten. ZH, JA and KO `Character_Brant_65_10` tell the traveler to follow sea currents and trade winds; EN instead says one must offer something back to the sea. The adjacent inscription and warnings still contain exchange language in every witness, but EN makes the following sea metaphor sound like a direct payment demand. At `_65_11` Brant warns conditionally that abandoning the rule may let desire consume a person and cost a life. This anticipates a moral danger; it is not independently observed punishment or a universal sea law. At `_65_12` he says he has an idea and asks Rover to trust him (JA frames the request more as leaving the task to him); both preserved Rover options are supportive. The action does not show a categorical refusal, a detailed executed plan, or who ultimately bore what price. Later evidence is needed before calling his proposal safe or fully informed [BRA-E43; BRA-C38].

This matters for the Aldric comparison. Brant's later objection to pushing the blood price of one's ambition onto others is a stronger behavioral claim than the inscription's “equal exchange” formula (`flow#/5248/5/7–9`; BRA-E29). A *nominally equal* exchange can still be coercive if the chooser and payer differ. Equally, his own coin-for-map gamble and bounty ruse remain ethically contestable; an inscription does not absolve them. The source supports a character who asks about prices and sometimes undertakes work, not a mathematically consistent law of value or a guarantee that every person around him could decline [BRA-C26–C28, C38].

## A day ends; recognition can continue without transferring command

The scripted `flow#/6283/2` opens after an adventure with Brant noting the day's end. Rover's two first options are to say the captain day is over or to return paperwork to him (`Character_Brant_71_2–3`). The first reaches his “time flew” reply; the second reaches a mock complaint about paperwork followed by his explicit “joking” acceptance (`_71_4–6`). The raw jumps rejoin before the offered object. Treating both responses as his continuous monologue would falsely make Rover voice two different attitudes. The scene then depicts Brant giving a memento, Rover identifying Drake's map in ZH, and Brant explicitly conferring an *honorary* troupe captain title with the map as token (`_71_7–11`). EN's Rover calls it a “scroll” before Brant calls it a map; that object-label discrepancy is a localization issue, not evidence for two gifts [BRA-E43; BRA-C39].

The three titles must stay separate: Brant remains the troupe's working captain, Rover was offered the contingent *one-day* role, and this closing exchange scripts an *honorary* title. The honor is meaningful recognition, but the source supplies no new permanent command protocol or exact decision rights over Battier, Tina and Roccia. Nor does an optional next-voyage question prove one is scheduled. Brant's answer promises readiness when another adventure calls; JA says the sea will decide, while EN/KO stress his availability. That is a continuing invitation, not a standing romantic commitment or an obligation to sail on every demand [BRA-E37/E43; BRA-C12/C31/C39].

## Reconstructive and falsification boundary

A useful Brant scene may let him honor a small bet, do a practical task for friends, ask another person to choose a temporary role, read a warning without pretending to have solved it, or offer a title after shared adventure. The line of continuity is *who can choose and who bears cost*, not a supposed magic law that all outcomes are fair. A model fails if it makes Battier's wager Brant's solo offer, assigns every `Character_Brant_*` key to him, fuses option replies, charges a literal heart on the painting alone, says a curse was seen, makes EN `_65_10` the universal sea rule, or grants honorary Rover permanent operational command. It also fails if it erases his genuine willingness to do chores or the scripted significance of the map and title. Direct route viewing, all-dub listening, a later account of the treasure's actual mechanism or a record of crew consent would test distinct parts of this reading; none is silently supplied by the present metadata.
