# Brant — a captain cannot pay a crew's price with a coin

The Drake quest asks what the language of *cost* conceals. Brant and Aldric both hear Toscanini's treasure tale, both want a better life beyond persecution, and both use a captain's position to organize other people. Brant's childhood answer is to lead people home so each can choose a life; Aldric's reported answer is to gain wealth sufficient to force an improvement (`flow#/5357/3/2–5`). That remembered dispute does not prove Brant never gambles or Aldric never cared about his men. It does set the terms by which the later expedition is judged: who chooses the destination, who bears danger, and who is allowed to keep the reward? The [continuous deep dive](WUWA_BRANT_CHARACTER_DEEP_DIVE_PRE_AV.md) gives the larger exile-to-return arc, and the [matrix](WUWA_BRANT_EVIDENCE_AND_FALSIFICATION_MATRIX.md) separates these source claims from their counterreadings.

## Four costs that should not be collapsed

First is the **cost of chosen navigation**. At `flow#/5248/5/7–9`, Brant says freedom to sail calls for captainly obligation and crew labor. The Chinese anchor rejects making someone else bleed or die for one's own wish; Japanese calls using another as a stepping stone unacceptable, Korean rejects sacrificing another, and English sharpens the charge into *tricking* men into paying. The later cave sequence includes actual deception by Aldric (`flow#/5350/5/13–19`), but the English addition should not be silently back-projected into every prior decision. The common ethic is not “no one may risk anything”; it is that a leader cannot appropriate another's risk to realize a private desire [BRA-E19, E29; BRA-C10, C26].

Second is the **historical cost of Drake's voyage**. Brant reads the map at `flow#/5357/3/12–16`: fog, disease and being lost reduced a treasure-bearing crew to a handful on the island; only one sailor escaped with the coin in the Chinese, Japanese and Korean witnesses. English says only one remained with it, a less explicit departure. The map is an in-world record as Brant interprets it, not our direct view of every event. It supports a catastrophic human price for that earlier expedition. It does *not* establish that Aldric's later crew suffered the same fate or that Drake's coin mechanically caused each death. The two crews occupy different narrative times [BRA-E20, E31; BRA-C11, C28].

Third is **Aldric's distribution of reward and exposure**. At `flow#/5350/5/9–10`, he invokes feeding and clothing his men while bargaining for access. At `flow#/5355/5/14–17`, after seeing apparently accessible riches, he has to be reminded of them and proposes giving each a small coin or share while retaining the rest. That contrast damages the reliability of his benevolent-captain presentation without proving his earlier hunger claim was fabricated from the beginning. His attempted transfer of immediate danger is concrete: in `5350/5/18–19` he distracts Brant and Rover, sends them against threats and proceeds inside. In `5355/5/7–17` he asks others to try the possibly cursed treasure first, then claims it when they appear unharmed. Brant's `5355/5/18–19` warning that freedom has a price is addressed to this man and moment; it is not a general wish that all treasure-seekers be punished [BRA-E30; BRA-C26–C27].

Fourth is **Brant's own bargain**. At `flow#/5357/3/16–21`, he reads the note's equivalent-exchange maxim and proposes that the coin he inserted was the price for the map. He admits he was not sure what he would receive and credits friends and courage. This is a narrower exchange than the historical losses he has just recited. A coin can be the price of *this scroll* in his account without somehow refunding Drake's dead or trapped crew, erasing the risks borne by Rover, or making Brant's every gamble safe. Equally, it would be wrong to invent that the map was acquired by a fresh sacrifice of Brant's troupe: the pinned lines identify a coin trade and uncertainty, not such a death. The asymmetry is the point—his moral language is tested by costs he can recognize but does not fully control [BRA-E20, E31; BRA-C10–C11, C28].

## The quest synopsis reports an end; the dialogue does not show its mechanism

After the cave danger, the Chinese `Character_Brant_70_2` says Brant did not expect Aldric to go back to pick up the money; Korean likewise says he returned to the cave for it. Japanese says he remained caught by money to the end. English interprets this more starkly as choosing treasure over his life. The immediately prior Aldric-only state is outside Brant's selected collection but present at pinned `wuwa://353f2eaed119bc9f680eab92807d20ac75a79b40/BinData/flowState/flowstate.json#/5356/Actions!/2/Params/TalkItems/0–1`; its `Character_Brant_68_1` and `_68_3` text keys are money-focused, not a death declaration. **Those direct dialogue rows alone do not settle the outcome.**

The broader pinned client-derived source does: `BinData/QuestTree/questtreenode.json#/45` is node `220030`, contains `QuestArray: [155000000]` for Brant's character quest, and points to `QuestTree_Summary_220030`. The four textmap records at `Textmaps/{zh-Hans,en,ja,ko}/multi_text/MultiText.json#/293839` give a retrospective synopsis. ZH says Aldric perished at the treasure site, KO explicitly says he died there, EN says he “meets his end”; JA says he was buried there, a less explicit death formulation. This is source-backed **reported outcome**, not direct footage of a body, exact death mechanism or proof that Brant personally saw it. The earlier version of this profile incorrectly called Aldric's fate globally unresolved because it stopped at the selected dialogue and adjacent Aldric-only row. A later contradictory client record could revise the report; a runtime capture could clarify staging. Neither uncertainty licenses ignoring the synopsis [BRA-E31 E36; BRA-C27].

There is another source-language fault line at `flow#/5355/5/16`. Aldric says in Chinese that he left the Fools' ship, and Korean similarly names that ship; English and Japanese instead say Pilgrim's Sail. The later four-language `flow#/5357/3/5` agrees that he left the Troupe of Fools and became a pirate. Without an independent location/ship continuity proof, the `5355` discrepancy should be recorded, not used to rewrite his chronology to a specific departure from the Pilgrim's Sail. This matters because a model of Brant's leadership must not silently turn a former troupe member into a childhood fellow passenger on the basis of two divergent localization witnesses [BRA-E30; BRA-C27].

## The captain's own unresolved test

The bounty arrangement shows Brant organizing an elaborate transfer of risk: troupe members turn him in, receive reward money, and he handles the escape (`flow#/5357/3/23–25`); earlier supper shows provisions bought with a bounty in his name (`FavorStory_120602_Content`). These lines establish a coordinated method and a material benefit, but not whether every member consented freely on every occasion, whether every escape was safe, or whether authorities harmed bystanders. His own scheme is therefore a live counterexample to any model that equates him with spotless procedure. Conversely, the source does not make it identical to Aldric's demonstrated attempt to take the lion's share and shift danger to others. Both are captainly uses of other people; the comparison must test information, participation, exit and reward distribution rather than merely call one man charismatic and the other greedy [BRA-E20, E26, E31; BRA-C10, C26, C28].

The final appraisal Brant gives Rover at `flow#/5357/3/7–10` names acceptance, understanding and respect for crew choices. That is his ideal and a relationship clue, not proof that every Rover route made them permanent captain or that Brant himself always passes the standard. In a hypothetical relationship or crossover crew, someone who asks how the crew is provisioned and who may say no is engaging the hardest part of his ethics; someone who only applauds an adventure or demands he abandon the troupe is not. His trust in “friends and courage” at `/21` is broad in Chinese, more directly addressed to Rover in English/Korean, and names his *own* courage in Japanese. The common textual support is faith amid an uncertain trade, not an exclusive romantic confession or a guarantee of success. A strong model can answer an Aldric-like proposal by stating the hoped-for benefit, the map's remembered losses, the actual available information, and each participant's choice—then accept that some danger or disagreement may remain.
