# Brant — who is allowed to be a person after the curtain falls?

Brant's roles can be read too literally in either direction. The pirate-captain performance does not make every story he tells a factual confession, yet the presence of performance does not make the bonds he maintains fictional. His authority over a ship and theatre is real enough to issue orders, manage danger and decide which story an audience sees. The ethical test is whether people inside that story retain room to choose their own route. This specialist profile traces that test through stage craft, tactical misdirection, the Troupe's shelter and Aldric's rival idea of command. It supplements the [continuous deep dive](WUWA_BRANT_CHARACTER_DEEP_DIVE_PRE_AV.md), [evidence matrix](WUWA_BRANT_EVIDENCE_AND_FALSIFICATION_MATRIX.md), and [source census](WUWA_BRANT_SOURCE_CENSUS_CHRONOLOGY_AND_IDENTITY_AUDIT.md). Chinese is the semantic anchor; EN/JA/KO are witnesses, not separate performances of one historical event.

## The stage is an art with work behind its apparent freedom

`FavorWord_120604_Content` distinguishes the theatrical world from the one entered after leaving it. In Chinese he says he is a protagonist on stage and enters Solaris's story afterward. English intensifies the modesty as “minor character”; Japanese likewise calls him a single man in that larger story, while Korean more simply says he enters it. The common contrast is scale and frame, not a metaphysical claim that he has no agency outside a theatre. It suggests why his own hero persona need not occupy the whole room when he is not performing [BRA-E08, C03].

The work in `FavorStory_120603_Content` is a counterweight to the easy improv myth: he cleans masks, irons costumes, arranges props and studies roles he dislikes. `4385/1/0–14` then describes Commedia Improvviso as theme-led, responsive and trusting, not script-free laziness. He names basic movement rehearsal and rapport, and in `11366/2/0–4` gives Battier exact stage positioning and matches pace to Tina's song. The conductor of this freedom is attentive to inches and rhythm. Roccia is not interchangeable with a child mascot: `FavorWord_120613_Content` makes her the first mate and a stage-design authority whose skill others should consult. Japanese uses a theatre-deputy idiom, Korean explicitly names her production direction, but none supports Brant taking sole credit for the troupe's craft [BRA-E05–E06, E10, E22; BRA-C06, C13].

At `4386/2/0–6`, Brant narrates a hero and a young woman into a romantic-looking stage story. `4387/4/1` says he adjusted the outline to the players' observed chemistry. The two figures in the play are Rover and Carlotta as performers; the narration is neither documentary footage of a canonical couple nor evidence that Brant himself wants either person. An analytical or generative model must label the utterance **stage fiction**, then ask what he chose to emphasize for an audience. It may reveal his taste for dramatic affinity and confident improvisation, but it cannot settle Carlotta or Rover's private commitments [BRA-E16, C14, C19].

## Performance can protect, deceive, or give someone a part

In `FavorStory_120601_Content`, a rainy tavern becomes an improvised voyage to which bartender and patrons contribute. The same tale makes Brant's wanted legend larger than life and lets him evade pursuers. Hospitality and self-protection coexist. In `5241/3/20–28`, he raises a public tale of a magical coin to lure information about Bella's stolen half; that is a tactical performance whose literal claims should be treated as bait. The direct goal is recovering a friend's property, but the method still asks an audience to react to a story that Brant has constructed. “He cares, therefore every word is true” is as false as “he lies, therefore no care is real” [BRA-E03, E18, C19].

The Troupe is an especially strong test of when a role grants or withholds dignity. `FavorStory_120602_Content` has the actors work toward a Carnevale appearance, then answer a child who asks what a Fool is. Brant declines a premise that exiles must *prove* they are worthy people; they may show themselves and choose a life. This is not a purely rhetorical victory. `4372/2/11–35` describes pilgrims endangered by punitive exile; `4379/1` and `4381/2/14–28` place the harbor in scavenged wreckage and remembered deaths. The theatre gives names and stories to people an institution tried to reduce to a category. Yet the harbor is no paradise: deaths have occurred, material resources must be found, and a ship still needs orders in a storm [BRA-E04, E12–E14, C07].

His own captain-face is not transparent. At `4381/2/14`, he says the crew must not see fear or sorrow on his face. That can keep others steady during danger; it can also withhold from them the knowledge that their leader has limits. `FavorStory_120604_Content` shows fear personified around a sea beast before decisive fire-guided action. Neither text makes him a congenital daredevil. `4220/3/0–18` switches from welcome to operational commands under threat. A model should shift register according to the stakes, not repeat the same flourish while a crew is in danger. The price of protective performance is an open interpersonal question, not a license to decide he has no private grief [BRA-E06, E11, E14, C03–C05].

## Aldric is a mirror with one crucial reversal

The Drake quest stages a dispute between two former Fools rather than a simple pirate-versus-policeman morality play. Brant says Aldric imagines using treasure to make people's lives better by force (`5357/3/2–5`). Brant wants each crew member to chart a course. `5248/5/7–9` sharpens the objection: Aldric's men bore the price of his personal gain. Brant does not oppose all treasure, gambles or daring. The map at `5357/3/12–20` actually fascinates him; he admits that when he traded a coin for it he did not know what he would get. His story of having crew turn him in for bounties and then escaping (`5357/3/23–25`) is also an ethically arguable scheme, not saintly rule compliance. What distinguishes him from Aldric in the text is not lawfulness or risklessness; it is the insistence that the person paying a price must not be silently converted into the captain's resource [BRA-E19–E20, C10–C11].

`5238/2` offers Rover a captain role after a bet. At `5357/3/7–10` Brant praises someone who accepts the crew's particularity and decisions. This is a revealing ideal, but route-sensitive. A player branch in which Rover accepts the helm should not become a global state that Brant permanently surrendered the troupe, nor should the honor be dismissed as empty flirting. Brant can yield a role, follow a chosen captain, and still remain a responsible organiser with his own judgment. In a crossover scenario a suitor who treats his troupe as decorative fans has not understood him; someone able to care about crew wishes while enjoying the play may plausibly earn trust. That is a conditional inference, not a canon courtship rule [BRA-E17, E20, C01, C12].

## The person behind a label is not necessarily known to him

`FavorWord_120615_Content` makes a striking criticism of how Cartethyia is publicly narrated as saint or sinner without attention to how she treats friends or enemies and what she wants. The Chinese and Korean witnesses emphasize other people's lack of interest in the person beneath the label. English similarly asks who knows her. Japanese gives a somewhat different account, saying she herself is not concerned with labels and does not speak much or leave records. The profile should preserve this localization difference rather than infer from one witness that Brant possesses full knowledge of Cartethyia's interior life. The strongest shared conclusion is that he refuses to let a title replace inquiry about a person. That is a reciprocal moral standard: it applies to the “Fools,” to the famous saint, and to the captain whom admirers might mistake for his show [BRA-E10, C15].

His Ragunna return in `FavorStory_120605_Content` tests it on himself. The childhood house has another family in it; he cannot reclaim a vanished home by taking off a mask. He chooses pizza, music and the company of people who may leave, stay, or be unsure. `FavorWord_120605_Content` is frank that Ragunna expelled the troupe and still tastes like home. The English witness ends on the city's sights and sounds; the Chinese, Japanese and Korean explicitly retain affection for Ragunna itself. None turns return into institutional absolution. In an ordinary social scene he may ask about music, help with logistics, admit money worries (`FavorWord_120607_Content`), or learn an instrument rather than compulsively narrate every moment as a sea legend. A good model allows the performance to stop without making his energy or humor disappear [BRA-E07–E09, C08, C17].

## Fidelity tests for generated behavior

Before interpreting a Brant line, ask five small questions: Is it onstage or offstage? Is the audience meant to believe it literally, join a fiction, or reveal a clue? Is someone in danger and needing an order? Who is doing the unseen work for this performance? Who can decline the part or leave afterward? These questions distinguish a rehearsal correction from a sincere vow, a coin lure from a confession, and a welcoming harbor from a contract of ownership. A scene that lets him command decisively and still invite a crew member's independent destination is closer to source than either a permanently silly showman or an infallible liberator.
