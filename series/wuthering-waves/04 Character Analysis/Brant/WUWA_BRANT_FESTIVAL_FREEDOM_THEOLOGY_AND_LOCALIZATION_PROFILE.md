# Carnival freedom: a position that survives an unanswered divine question

Brant's argument at `flow#/4384/2/34–37` is stronger and narrower than “he is an atheist” or “he dislikes all religion.” In the Chinese anchor, he first states the troupe's concrete purpose: an outstanding Carnival performance that returns the festival to celebration (`Main_Linaxita_2_3_39_55`). He then asks whether a real god would want controlled lives and a joyful festival turned into a ruler's instrument (`_56`). He proposes winning the Laurel and obtaining a miracle as a test of the punitive claim; if punishment were actually divine, he says they should break the divine shackles (`_57`). Finally, whether divinity exists or not, he wants Ragunna's people to decide their own lives and future on equal terms (`_58`). The logical sequence is a conditional challenge to *authority claimed in the divine name*, with a freedom principle that does not wait for a settled metaphysical answer.

This is not an optional line chosen only on one Rover branch. The raw `ShowTalk` action has seven `TalkSequence` units. Earlier units permit inquiry into three clues before rejoining; items 34–37 are all in the twenty-one-item final unit, and all four are `WhoId: 1462` with `PlayVoice: true`. The preceding Montelli speaker proposes that a Laurel miracle might enable communication with the Sentinel (T30–33). Brant replies with the troupe's distinct festival aim. The following Montelli response names the family's interest in an open, nonmonopolized market (T38) and requests Rover's help (T39). This is a local coalition against control, not proof that Brant's freedom ethic and Montelli commercial incentives are interchangeable.

The four text witnesses agree on the final practical commitment but not every step of the argument. Chinese and Korean `Main_Linaxita_2_3_39_57` explicitly connect the Laurel/miracle test to exposing a false “divine punishment” claim, then entertain the possibility that such punishment *is* divine. English compresses the contest condition into “if we win the Laurel” and retains the second branch. Japanese takes a materially different path: if evil people go unpunished, the idea of divine punishment fails, and people must overturn the present order in which a god lets evil go free. That is an anti-theodicy formulation, not the same Laurel-triggered empirical test. The prior `_56` and following `_58` still frame the speech as a challenge to control and an insistence on freedom, but a Japanese quotation or performance analysis must not be silently back-translated into the Chinese causal chain.

The childhood source gives the speech a personal stake without supplying a theorem. At `flow#/4381/2/23–27`, Brant recounts being put on a pilgrimage ship after questioning an unseen Sentinel, being raised by other Fools who shared scarce supplies, watching them die, and building harbor from a wreck. The source supports anger at a coercive order and loyalty to people who made survival possible. It does not prove he has direct knowledge that the Sentinel is absent, that every believer supported exile, or that winning the Laurel would in fact settle theology. The Carnival speech is both a moral case and a plan; its proposed miracle remains conditional at this point in the story.

For a reconstructed scene, Brant can challenge a priest or patron who invokes divinity to deny another person's choice while still listening to evidence about a particular belief. If a supposed divine command were verified, his stated moral commitment would not simply disappear; neither would he automatically possess the power to overthrow a god. A crossover may use the principle that people should chart their own lives, but should not force the exact Carnival coalition onto a different city, make a commercial ally speak for the troupe, or treat the JA causal fork as an error in the Chinese character.
