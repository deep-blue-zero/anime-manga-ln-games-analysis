## 1. The volume’s governing problem

Volume 12 places two claims beside one another that should not be confused: a person’s feelings can matter profoundly, and that importance does not give the person authority to force someone else to acknowledge, reciprocate or physically enact them. Yoko insists that romantic desire is the uniquely real form of attachment. She reads a fictional sister story, Sumika’s distress, Nene’s history and Kanoko’s fear through that rule, then intervenes to manufacture the reactions she regards as proof. Kanoko’s repeated refusals expose what the rule costs when its supposed truth is imposed on another person. Yoko is not merely an observer whose interpretation may be mistaken. She creates the coercive circumstances that she then cites as evidence. [V12/57/i011–030; V12/58/i038–055; V12/59/i061–073.]

The alternative does not emerge as a painless victory of correct vocabulary. Nene has to show that her newly recovered friendship with Sumika can guide a difficult present action rather than remain a consoling description. Sumika has to distinguish wanting Kanoko from demanding compensation for her own care. Kanoko has to begin separating the wish that Hime’s kiss should not change anything from the claim that intimate acts with an unloved person cannot matter. These are different people making different revisions. Nene’s refusal to return to Yoko, Sumika’s rejection of bodily repayment, and Kanoko’s decision to speak about an earlier confession cannot be compressed into a single lesson that everyone has now learned. [V12/58/i032–036; V12/59/i067–076; V12/60/i094–120; V12/61/i126–140.]

The most important change in the Sumika–Kanoko relationship is not an established conversion to mutual romantic love. Kanoko says that she needs the person who listens to her and fears losing that person because she gives nothing equivalent in return. Sumika recognizes that she has not understood why Kanoko accepted dating. The pair begins to make the support arrangement discussable again. Their roles as girlfriends and sisters become available languages for care rather than sufficient evidence that their feelings already match. The ensuing conversation gives Sumika new information, including Kanoko’s account of Hime’s kiss and the opening statement about a middle-school confession. It does not provide a complete history or Hime’s own interpretation of that history. [V12/60/i107–120; V12/61/i123–140.]

This repair must retain its counterevidence. Sumika refuses to hurt Kanoko as a way of collecting a debt and stops her hotel confrontation when Kanoko asks. Later, however, Sumika holds Kanoko’s hand against an explicit request to let go, interpreting release as another abandonment. Kanoko subsequently chooses to continue and places her hand over Sumika’s; that uptake is real, but it is later than the refused request. The volume therefore supports a locally accepted renewal of listening, not a certificate that every method used to obtain disclosure was consensual or that all future boundaries will be honored. The difference between remaining available and making someone remain is still consequential. [V12/59/i065–066; V12/60/i110–120; V12/61/i135–140.]

Formally, the book repeatedly separates a selected picture from the event around it and a final sentence from the argument that produced it. The hotel photograph travels farther than the facts; a story about fictional sisters is made to stand in for actual people’s feelings; an interrupted coercive demand leaves an unshown interval; a later report of “nothing” cannot erase the visible threats and restraint. The ending reverses the direction of inquiry. Instead of forcing Kanoko to say what someone else has decided is true, it opens the possibility of hearing an account that Kanoko has not yet supplied. That opening remains incomplete at the freeze. [V12/57/i006–017, i026–029; V12/58/i053–055; V12/60/i085, i097; V12/61/i138–140.]

## 3. Sequential, form-sensitive close reading

### 3.1 Shift 57: making a story into an instrument

#### 3.1.1 A limited agreement does not disappear when a setting is suggestive

Kanoko’s hotel presence begins under an explicitly limited self-account: she has come to talk. She also anticipates Sumika’s displeasure and intends concealment. These facts matter together. The secrecy is a real feature of Kanoko’s choice; it does not establish that she accepted whatever Yoko might subsequently do. When the photograph reaches her and Yoko proposes using it to provoke jealousy, Kanoko refuses. She also declines alcohol by saying she is a high-school student. The scene therefore supplies several distinct boundaries before physical danger becomes overt: discussion rather than intimacy, no deliberate jealousy display, and no drinking. An account that calls her simply compliant would miss the refusals the chapter actually gives her. [V12/57/i005–010.]

Yoko’s hospitality helps maintain access while the purpose of the encounter shifts. She points Kanoko toward refreshments, handles the bag and offers to explain the problem by example. Kanoko recognizes that Yoko has been useful and listened to her, yet finds her less approachable than Sumika. This is a perceptual difference within Kanoko’s own account, not proof that she already understands the coming danger. Nor does declining alcohol establish anything about intoxication beyond that refusal: no intoxication is depicted or needed to explain the subsequent coercion. [i007–010.]

The bag and phone initially appear as ordinary logistical objects. Their later use changes their significance. At the first handling of the bag, the analyst should not give Kanoko knowledge that the phone has been removed. The later discovery, the incoming caller display and Yoko’s explanation establish that specific information. Retrospectively, the bag handling becomes consequential, but the scene-level knowledge boundary remains staged rather than instantaneous. [i008–009, i026–027.]

#### 3.1.2 Olivia and Erna: a represented story and a selected reading

Yoko finds the café’s script and retells the fourth chapter of *乙女の心臓*. The depicted material concerns Erna’s romantic attachment to Olivia, Erna’s contemplated departure through marriage, and Olivia’s distressed but sisterly response. Erna’s prospective husband is connected to her earlier life and described within that embedded story. None of these panels depicts Kanoko marrying, Sumika ending an actual pact, or a newly completed café performance. The story is an object being interpreted in the hotel. [i011–017.]

Yoko’s crucial move is to treat grief at a sister’s departure as evidence of romance that the older sister refuses to acknowledge. She calls the outcome tragic in a way that makes the sister category responsible for suppressing the true feeling. Kanoko disputes the idea that the younger sister’s conduct is merely a test. This disagreement preserves a distinction the reading needs: the represented story contains loss and attachment; Yoko supplies a theory of what those feelings must really mean. Strong feeling does not independently settle the category that she assigns to it. [i013–017.]

The inference is attractive to Yoko because it makes inaction incriminating. An older sister who does not claim a romantic partner becomes someone who has failed to admit her real desire. The same premise can later classify Sumika’s past restraint as inadequate love and present anger as proof of love. Under that arrangement, contradictory outcomes are both absorbed into Yoko’s theory. The text does not have to refute every possible romantic reading of Olivia to show the defect in applying that reading as compulsory knowledge about living people. Kanoko’s objection already demonstrates that the interpretation is not shared. [i016–023.]

The embedded story is also not analytically disposable because Yoko misuses it. It gives the characters a vocabulary for separation, sisterhood and choosing what another person wants. The question is what authority that vocabulary has. It can illuminate a possible resemblance without determining Sumika’s past feeling, Nene’s present needs or Kanoko’s consent. The hotel discussion crosses that line when literary analogy becomes a warrant to produce an answer by pressure. [i017–025, i028–030.]

#### 3.1.3 The earlier jealousy project becomes Kanoko’s knowledge

Yoko tells Kanoko that Nene loved Sumika and that her own earlier relationship with Nene was directed toward provoking Sumika. That information reaches Kanoko now. The reader’s prior access to Nene’s account does not mean Kanoko entered the hotel with an equivalent understanding. Kanoko’s response links the new explanation to what she had heard of Sumika’s distress: the old rupture can now appear as purposeful interference rather than the unfortunate by-product of two people falling in love. [i018–022.]

Yoko’s present self-account does not replace Nene’s changing account of participation, later love and injury. Nene’s initial agreement to the jealousy strategy is relevant, and V10–V11 already established it. It does not authorize all later conduct or settle what Nene eventually wanted. Yoko describes a willingness to return Nene had Sumika come to claim her. The language exposes how readily she makes one person into a device for eliciting another’s response. It is evidence of Yoko’s stated rationale, not a conclusion that Nene could simply be transferred between two claimants. [i019–023; inherited WY10-C09/C10, WY11-C07/C08.]

Kanoko objects that Sumika was hurt and that dating is being treated like a game. Yoko answers by elevating romantic desire over other forms of attachment. Her position is not merely that romance can be valuable. She treats it as uniquely authentic, making sisterly affection and restraint suspect alternatives. That claim is contradicted by neither a single platitude nor an immediately successful argument. The rest of the volume tests it through Nene’s valued friendship and Sumika’s refusal to exact intimate repayment. Those later acts have their own evidence; they should not be presented as knowledge Kanoko already possesses in this exchange. [i022–025; V12/59/i068–071; V12/60/i110–120.]

#### 3.1.4 The phone makes concern visible without making it consensual

When Yoko displays Kanoko’s phone with Sumika calling, the private arrangement becomes an actionable information asymmetry. Kanoko has not authorized the custody or the forwarding. Yoko says she has also sent Sumika the photograph; she then answers the call on speaker and controls its end. Sumika asks where Kanoko is, urges escape and says she will come. Yoko recasts this concern as a successful romantic provocation. The caller’s actual words and Yoko’s interpretation must remain separate. [i026–029.]

The scene does not show an abstract failure of communication. It shows one person controlling another’s communication channel. Kanoko’s own refusal to send the photograph has already been overridden. Sumika’s attempt to obtain facts reaches Kanoko only in a form Yoko manages. The same image can therefore be evidence of Kanoko’s location, a deliberately misleading implication about her wishes, and a tool for summoning a reaction. Its visual truth does not certify the story Yoko attaches to it. [i006–007, i026–029.]

Kanoko says that she no longer wants to consult Yoko and tries to leave. Yoko instead announces that Kanoko must now recognize the importance of love. The chapter ends by moving from an argument about others’ feelings to an attempt to dictate Kanoko’s own response. No eventual agreement or completed intimate act is supplied at this cutoff. What is established is a narrowing of Kanoko’s practical options and Yoko’s refusal to accept the stated end of the consultation. [i024, i029–030.]

### 3.2 Shift 58: a refused answer and a concrete response

#### 3.2.1 Nene receives information rather than fulfilling an inferred role

The chapter cuts back to the café’s tea-cleanup moment. Nene receives the photograph and shows it to Sumika. This is the first direct establishment of her place in the communication route. It resolves the V11 recipient uncertainty at a later source boundary; it does not make the V11 report of an unseen addressee wrong. Nene is initially unsure why Yoko sent the image. Her receipt of it does not give her full knowledge of Kanoko’s reason for going to the hotel or the acts occurring there. [V12/58/i032–034.]

Nene recognizes a possible repetition of the old jealousy strategy. Sumika’s response prioritizes Kanoko’s immediate situation rather than the meaning of Yoko’s game. The distinction is practical: they need a location and a way to reach her, not a complete theory of Yoko’s motive before acting. Nene offers a probable hotel based on her own prior experience and agrees to help. Probability should not be rewritten as a previously verified address, but uncertainty does not render the initiative empty. The later arrival tests the route. [i034–037, i056–057.]

This is a substantive continuation of Nene’s V11 repair. She does not use the information to reclaim Yoko, punish the new couple or test Sumika’s former affection. She makes her knowledge useful to the present problem, acknowledges her own earlier failure to stop the pattern and accompanies the response. Her intervention can be imperfect without being merely another form of rivalry. The precise forecast adjudication belongs in §14; the important immediate observation is the difference between recalling a harmful strategy and choosing to reproduce it. [i034–036; V12/59/i067–076.]

#### 3.2.2 Coercion seeks an exact proposition, not merely a disclosure

Inside the room, Yoko threatens and physically coerces Kanoko while treating the encounter as instruction about romantic importance. Kanoko repeatedly asks her to stop and seeks to leave. Yoko does not treat these as sufficient decisions. Instead, she requires Kanoko to say the proposition Yoko has selected. The coercion includes restraint and interference with clothing despite refusal. Those depicted acts are enough to establish a serious violation without filling the unshown interval with a completed sexual act. [i038–047.]

The distinction between asking someone what she feels and forcing her to endorse a sentence is central. Kanoko tries an alternative response: she apologizes for meeting Yoko while dating Sumika and offers to treat the relationship seriously. Yoko rejects that answer because it is not the required one. The demanded agreement is therefore not a neutral request for clarity. Its exact content serves Yoko’s doctrine, and the threatened continuation supplies the cost of refusing it. [i043–047.]

Yoko invokes Kanoko’s earlier assent to the hotel and her earlier minimization of dating to justify proceeding. The sequence explicitly supplies contrary evidence: Kanoko agreed to talk, rejected the jealousy picture, tried to end the consultation and now objects to the acts themselves. An initial choice can be influenced and still be a choice; it can also be narrower than another person later claims. Nothing in the source permits the analyst to turn venue assent into continuing bodily permission. [V12/57/i005–010, i024–030; V12/58/i040–047.]

The language of instruction also shifts responsibility away from the actor. Yoko presents herself as making Kanoko understand what is already true, as though the pressure were a transparent medium rather than an action requiring justification. But fear produced by coercion cannot establish that Kanoko endorses Yoko’s theory of romance. At most, it supplies evidence of what Kanoko is experiencing under the imposed conditions. The continuing refusals are not noise to be discarded so that a deeper truth can be extracted. They are source evidence in their own right. [i040–048, i053–055.]

#### 3.2.3 Kanoko’s defense is a theory about Hime as well as about herself

The declaration at i048 that acts with an unloved person do not matter belongs to **Kanoko**, not Yoko. Its placement matters because Yoko then responds to it and searches for the stake it protects. Kanoko is visibly distressed while insisting on insignificance. The discrepancy does not license the analyst to replace her entire self-account with a diagnosis. It does show that the proposition cannot be treated as an uncomplicated description of how she feels in the moment. [i048–049.]

The subsequent references to Hime and Mitsuki explain why the proposition is so consequential. If a kiss from someone one does not romantically love is unimportant, Kanoko can preserve the hope that Hime’s acceptance of Mitsuki has not changed the relation she herself depends on. If the kiss must be precious, Hime’s response becomes harder for Kanoko to fit into her account. This is an emotionally motivated interpretive rule, not direct access to Hime’s feelings. The remembered images belong to Kanoko’s present thought and the already disclosed history; they are not a new kiss or a newly voiced Hime romantic answer. [i049–052.]

Yoko identifies that vulnerability and changes the demanded sentence to a claim about Hime’s precious kiss having been taken. This does not establish that Yoko has independently learned Hime’s actual interpretation. It shows her finding a proposition Kanoko is unwilling to say and making its utterance the condition of stopping. The content may resemble a possible ethical criticism of an earlier act, but the way Yoko obtains it is still coercive. An apparently defensible sentence cannot retroactively authorize the means used to compel it. [i052–055.]

The volume later offers a different way to distinguish the questions. Sumika can say that her own kiss matters to her without requiring Kanoko to give it the same meaning, and can ask Kanoko not to erase Hime’s event without claiming authority over Hime’s feelings. That later conversation is not a simple confirmation of Yoko’s universal rule. It changes the grammatical and relational terms: from a compulsory statement about what everyone must value to a person-specific account that can coexist with uncertainty. [V12/61/i126–134.]

#### 3.2.4 The omitted interval remains omitted

The hotel sequence reaches a threat, tears and a closed door. Later, i085 briefly returns to Kanoko beginning to open her mouth under the demand. Neither passage supplies the complete dictated answer or a continuous account of every intervening act. When Sumika arrives, Kanoko is in the lobby, withdrawn, while Yoko boasts that she has taken something precious. That boast is evidence of Yoko’s presentation of the event. It is not independent proof that an unshown kiss or completed sexual act occurred. [V12/58/i053–057; V12/60/i085.]

The later i097 report must be kept equally bounded. Kanoko says that the bodily or kissing acts she thinks Sumika imagines did not occur. The source has nevertheless already shown threats, restraint, disregarded refusals and interference with clothing. Preserving her report does not require calling all of that “nothing”; acknowledging the observed coercion does not require inventing a more extensive unshown assault. The responsible account holds both the visible violation and the limit on what can be asserted about the gap. [V12/58/i038–055; V12/60/i097–099.]

Sumika’s punch is an actual new act at the chapter ending, not merely an imagined threat. It follows Yoko’s boast and Kanoko’s visible state, but it does not reveal the missing facts. The formal cutoff gives the intervention force while postponing what it will accomplish. The next chapter must be read to distinguish protection, retaliation, information-seeking and the risk of further escalation. [V12/58/i056–058; V12/59/i060–066.]

### 3.3 Shift 59: rescue does not settle the meaning of the rescue

#### 3.3.1 A reaction can be real without validating the person who provoked it

Yoko falls after the punch. Sumika demands to know what happened to Kanoko, while Yoko presents the anger as the very reaction she wanted. Her response converts the cost of being struck into evidence that the intervention succeeded. This is an especially clear instance of the theory’s self-protective structure: if Sumika does not come, her love is insufficient; if she comes in anger, the jealousy experiment was necessary. Neither alternative leaves much room for Sumika’s stated purpose or Kanoko’s actual situation to count on their own terms. [V12/59/i060–064.]

Sumika does explicitly say that she loves Kanoko. The statement is not merely an interpretation of her expression. Yet it does not follow that Yoko was entitled to create danger in order to hear it, or that the motive for the intervention is exhausted by competitive possession. Sumika’s repeated question concerns what was done to Kanoko. Yoko’s interpretation concerns the emotional meaning of Sumika’s arrival. The two questions can coexist without being interchangeable. [i061–064.]

The analysis should not solve Yoko’s reduction by making Sumika’s conduct faultless. Sumika uses physical violence, threatens further retaliation and remains at risk of escalating the encounter. The protective purpose and the harm of the act belong in the same record. Nor should a general moral assessment replace the source sequence: there is an actual punch, a fall, hotel personnel including a uniformed attendant, and a later interruption. The narrative gives enough concrete action to distinguish them without inventing an arrest, prosecution or final legal disposition. [i058–066.]

#### 3.3.2 Kanoko’s request changes the intervention

The decisive limit comes from Kanoko. She asks Sumika to stop, and Sumika does. This is a specific instance of her request obtaining uptake. Its importance is heightened by the immediately preceding sequence in which Yoko repeatedly refused to stop. The contrast is stronger than a generic claim that one person cares and the other does not: the same kind of practical signal—do not continue—receives different responses in observable circumstances. [i065–066; contrast V12/58/i040–047.]

That local difference is not an unlimited character rule. Sumika’s later refusal to release Kanoko’s hand will prevent the volume from supporting “Sumika always respects an explicit stop.” For now, however, the interruption materially redirects the scene away from further retaliatory action and toward Kanoko. The later counterexample should not erase this successful uptake any more than this success should pre-approve the later restraint. Both belong to a time-indexed account. [i065–066; V12/61/i135–140.]

Yoko tells the surrounding adults that this was a quarrel and asks that it not become a major affair. The source shows this as her speech in the encounter, not a validated assessment of all the events in the room. The uniformed attendant is not identified as police in these pages. Later, Sumika reports suggesting that they go to the police and Kanoko shaking her head; no police visit or interview is shown, and Sumika still lacks a full account. A procedural-looking setting must not be mistaken for comprehensive shared knowledge or a finding that the coercion was harmless. [i064–066, i074–075.]

#### 3.3.3 Nene refuses the old hierarchy of romantic value

Outside, Nene confronts Yoko about the provocation. Yoko credits her with passing the photograph along, thereby trying to include the assistance within the experiment’s success. Nene has indeed transmitted the information, but what she intended and what Yoko wanted are different. An action can be exploited by another person without becoming the action that person wished its agent to perform. Nene’s subsequent refusal makes the difference visible. [i067–070.]

Nene tells Yoko that she has confessed, apologized and received a proper answer from Sumika. Yoko interprets the nonromantic answer as evidence that Nene is insufficiently valued and presents renewed romance with herself as a way to fill the resulting absence. Nene refuses. The refusal does not require that her old love never existed or that the breakup is painless. It establishes that returning to Yoko is not the course she now chooses, and that friendship with Sumika is not merely a consolation prize she must replace. [i068–071.]

Nene’s account of acceptance includes the earlier sister relationship, the rejected love and her own conduct. The point is not that Sumika has offered an indistinguishable substitute for romance. It is that Nene can remain personally important without receiving the desired romantic answer. This directly opposes Yoko’s attempt to rank all nonromantic attachment as deficient. Nene supplies a present decision under pressure, which is stronger evidence of the revised model than repeating the V11 tea scene’s ending-language would have been. [i069–071; inherited WY11-C17/C18.]

The exchange also preserves Nene’s agency. Her refusal is not authored by Sumika telling her whom she may date. It occurs in Sumika’s absence from this particular conversation, with Yoko offering an available alternative. Nene’s choice can therefore be read as an enacted distinction between valuing a past relationship and restoring it. There is no new Nene–Yoko reconciliation or renewed agreement after the refusal. [i067–073.]

#### 3.3.4 The hypothesis about Yoko remains a hypothesis

Nene asks whether Yoko herself loves Sumika and points to her persistent fixation. The question is intelligible in context: Yoko has repeatedly organized relations around obtaining Sumika’s response. But a coherent hypothesis is not the same as an established identity of feeling. Yoko explicitly denies it and describes hostility or irritation instead. The reading must retain both the observation that motivates Nene’s question and the denial that prevents an unqualified hidden-love conclusion. [i072–073.]

Several interpretations remain possible within this boundary. Yoko may misunderstand or refuse to name a romantic investment; she may resent a person who defeats her preferred account of desire; she may value the control involved in making another person react. The available scene supports the persistence and intensity of her attention more securely than one exhaustive explanation. It is unnecessary to decide the hidden category in order to identify the coercion or criticize the claim that romantic feeling justifies it. [i018–025, i061–073.]

The same caution that applies to Yoko’s diagnosis of Olivia and Sumika must apply to the analyst’s diagnosis of Yoko. Treating strong agitation as conclusive proof of love would reproduce the inferential shortcut the volume has made conspicuous. Nene’s challenge is a consequential act in the conversation, but it is not narrator-certified access to Yoko’s interiority. [i016–023, i072–073.]

#### 3.3.5 Assistance continues after the confrontation ends

Sumika does not yet know the complete hotel sequence. She sees Kanoko’s withdrawal, hears little explanation and learns that Kanoko does not want to go home. She proposes her own home as somewhere to settle. Nene returns toward the café, offers continued help and tells Kanoko that she is not alone. The assistance is distributed: a probable location, accompaniment, a direct confrontation, practical shelter and availability are different contributions. No single dramatic arrival exhausts the work of responding. [i074–080.]

Kanoko’s assent to staying at Sumika’s home is specific. It establishes a desired immediate destination, not consent to intimacy or proof that the relationship problem has been repaired. Sumika notices the potential ambiguity of inviting her girlfriend to an empty home and adjusts her wording. Kanoko’s own statement that she wants to stay matters; so does the continued lack of a full account of what happened. The scene moves toward safety and quiet while maintaining incomplete knowledge. [i078–080.]

The bath sequence represents distress and isolation after the public encounter. Kanoko’s repeated washing and tears are observable actions; they do not establish a clinical diagnosis, a uniquely specified bodily injury or a complete explanation of every thought. The reader has access to more of the room sequence than Sumika does, but not to a comprehensive medical or psychological assessment. The analysis should preserve that asymmetry rather than convert visible suffering into unsupported certainty. [i081–082.]

### 3.4 Shift 60: care mistaken for a debt

#### 3.4.1 Practical help gives the night a structure without solving it

The chapter’s opening returns briefly to Kanoko under Yoko’s demanded sentence. This is retrospective presentation of the same hotel event, not a second assault or a completed answer newly supplied in full. It links the aftermath to an unresolved interpretive pressure: what must Kanoko admit, and what does admitting it imply for Hime? The subsequent domestic sequence does not simply leave that pressure behind because the location changes. [V12/60/i085–086.]

Sumika offers food, asks about appetite, gives Kanoko time and arranges sleeping space. The small choices are not ornamental. They restore some opportunities to state a preference after a sequence in which objections were ignored. Kanoko later participates in clearing up, and Sumika supplies a spare toothbrush and separate bedding. Receiving assistance is not depicted as requiring total passivity. Nor is the shared household activity a new workplace-training experiment of the kind adjudicated in V05; it belongs to this particular night’s care. [i087–093.]

At i089 a brief hand movement and separation interrupt the approach, followed by Kanoko’s attention to the signed photograph in Sumika’s room. The page should not be inflated into another punch or a clearly narrated repetition of the hotel restraint. Its formal importance lies in the contrast between immediate difficulty around contact and an enduring sign that Kanoko has been valued. The photograph is a personal object with an earlier history, not new proof that its photographed smile was originally romantic or that Kanoko must now return Sumika’s feeling. [i089; inherited WY9-C01/C03.]

Sumika privately searches for sexual-violence consultation information. This is evidence that she takes the possibility of harm seriously and lacks sufficient knowledge, not evidence that a consultation occurred or that a professional assessed Kanoko. It also helps explain why her subsequent relief is conditional on the report she receives. The search and the report belong to different information states; the analyst should not erase the earlier concern once the later conversation becomes more intimate. [i092, i096–099.]

#### 3.4.2 “Nothing happened” has a speaker and a scope

Kanoko asks why Sumika continues dating when she receives nothing in return. Sumika says that loving Kanoko and being able to help her matter; she does not make repayment a condition of care. Yet Kanoko hears a relationship organized by asymmetry and worries about its durability. The disagreement is not simply whether Sumika has been generous. It concerns what generosity obliges the recipient to do and whether the absence of equivalent feeling makes continued closeness impossible. [i094–096.]

Kanoko then says that Yoko did not do the bodily or kissing acts that she thinks Sumika is imagining. She insists that the report is true. The record must identify **Kanoko as the speaker** and preserve the qualification supplied by her examples. A literal universal paraphrase—nothing harmful happened—would conflict with the earlier depicted threats and physical coercion. Conversely, the reader should not assume that her minimization proves an unshown completed sexual act. The source supports a violated boundary and an incomplete aftermath account, not unlimited inference in either direction. [i097–099; V12/58/i038–055.]

Sumika’s relief is also evidence of her knowledge, not a retroactive change in the source. She has not viewed the hotel panels available to the reader. Her response can therefore be sincere while remaining based on an incomplete report. The reader’s task is not to condemn her for failing to possess uncommunicated information, but to avoid treating her relief as independent corroboration that all coercion was absent. [i097–099.]

This distinction matters for the rest of the chapter. The coercive encounter has not ended conceptually for Kanoko. She is still trying to decide whether intimate acts must be important, whether lack of romantic love makes them inconsequential, and what either answer does to her interpretation of Hime. A safe location does not automatically supply a different explanatory rule. [i098–106, i126–132.]

#### 3.4.3 Kanoko initiates an act; Sumika does not owe its continuation

Kanoko initiates a kiss without depicted advance assent from Sumika. Sumika is startled and disengages. Kanoko identifies it as her first and asks whether it makes Sumika happy and whether it is important. This is not simply an unambiguous romantic confession expressed physically. Its questions connect the act to Kanoko’s unresolved theory of value and to the fear that she owes a girlfriend something. The later apology for forcing the kiss confirms that its manner is not to be redescribed as pre-agreed just because Sumika later values it. [i100–103; V12/61/i126–128.]

Kanoko attempts to extend the girlfriend-like exchange despite Sumika’s requests to stop. Sumika does not accept that the dating agreement requires her to take whatever is offered in this state. She distinguishes the happiness of being asked to date from a demand for further intimate acts. This gives the recipient of the initiated contact a real boundary, not merely the role of validating Kanoko’s experiment. [i104–106.]

The contrast with Yoko is important but must not become a flattening equation. Kanoko’s conduct is harmful in disregarding Sumika’s immediate response; her distressed and compensatory motive is not the same as Yoko’s deliberate coercive lesson. Different purposes and power conditions do not eliminate the need to record the refused act in each case. A source-grounded comparison asks who initiates, who objects, what happens after the objection and what interpretation is later offered. It does not need to declare all unwanted contact identical to preserve the limits that were crossed. [V12/58/i040–047; V12/60/i100–115.]

#### 3.4.4 The adviser becomes Sumika’s knowledge

Kanoko finally tells Sumika that she consulted Yoko when Sumika stopped listening, and that the dating proposal was intended to restore the former kind and available Sumika. This is new disclosure to Sumika. Earlier reader knowledge, Nene’s partial advice and Yoko’s private confidence did not amount to Sumika’s informed participation in the plan. The disclosure changes the meaning of the dating arrangement for its recipient without proving that every feeling within it was false. [i107–109.]

Kanoko names her conduct as betrayal, emphasizes that she disregarded warnings and proposes intimate repayment to prevent the relationship from ending. The self-indictment is evidence of her current interpretation and fear. It is not an exhaustive allocation of moral responsibility for the hotel coercion. Going to the hotel, concealing the visit and accepting advice are acts she can regret; none supplies Yoko with permission to ignore subsequent refusals. The reading must not allow Kanoko’s guilt to become a substitute for the actual consent sequence. [i107–113; V12/57/i007, i024–030; V12/58/i040–047.]

Sumika’s physical urgency in questioning her also remains part of the scene. She grasps Kanoko while trying to obtain an explanation, rather than receiving a leisurely, fully prepared confession. The information gain does not require pretending that every exchange takes place under perfectly calm or equal conditions. What changes the direction of the encounter is her refusal of the proposed repayment, not the disappearance of all forceful conduct. [i109–115.]

#### 3.4.5 A refusal to collect a debt makes another explanation possible

Kanoko’s proposal becomes more extreme: if she has hurt Sumika, Sumika should hurt her in return or take what a lover would want. Sumika rejects the premise. She does not accept harm as compensation or intimate access as the price of remaining together. Her forceful embrace and protest insist that wanting Kanoko’s happiness is not equivalent to wanting an opportunity to injure or possess her. The initial embrace itself is not separately preceded by a new consent negotiation; its later acceptance and consoling effect should be stated as subsequent uptake rather than universal permission. [i110–115.]

The passage’s explanatory force lies in what the refusal makes speakable. Kanoko says she fears that a relationship in which she gives nothing will break and that Sumika will leave. Her wish to date was therefore connected to preserving proximity and the confidant relationship, not merely to conducting an abstract experiment about romance. Sumika recalls having withdrawn listening and recognizes that she never understood Kanoko’s reason for dating. The two accounts now meet at a more specific problem than whether one is a good girlfriend. [i116–118.]

This does not make the dating proposal wholly innocent or wholly manipulative. It establishes its mixed and changing significance: Kanoko used a role to try to change Sumika’s attitude, withheld the advice behind it, valued Sumika’s care, and feared losing the person who supplied it. Sumika entered with acknowledged romantic feeling, knew of Hime’s place in Kanoko’s heart, but did not understand the concealed procedure. The new disclosure supplies enough information for negotiation, not proof that either previously knew all of this. [i107–118; inherited WY10-C19–C22, WY11-C01/C10/C19–C21.]

The invitation to talk at i119 uses the elder-sister relationship that Yoko had disparaged as inferior or false. Here the role does not suppress the existence of romantic feeling; it gives Sumika and Kanoko a recognizable way to restore a valued practice. Their apologies and comfort establish a local repair of availability. They do not establish that Kanoko now romantically loves Sumika, that no further request will be difficult, or that the old Hime-related history has already been heard. [i119–120.]

### 3.5 Shift 61: particular significance, imperfect listening, and a history not yet told

#### 3.5.1 Role language can offer something that ordinary argument could not

Sumika brings hot milk in the café’s stylized register, then comments on the incongruity of doing the work at a sleepover. Kanoko offers to carry the tray and the contribution is received. These ordinary acts prevent the renewed listening from existing only as a dramatic declaration. They also complicate the idea that real care must arrive without role or performance. The borrowed vocabulary offers familiarity; the practical act provides warmth; the return to ordinary speech acknowledges the performance without voiding it. [V12/61/i123–125.]

This is a different use of fiction from Yoko’s hotel lesson. Sumika does not initially ask Kanoko to infer a compulsory romantic truth from the language of the salon. She uses a shared register to make a place for conversation. The distinction is functional, not a rule that café language is always safe: earlier volumes supplied coercive uses of the same institution, and the present chapter will include a refused release. The claim is that a performed role can carry real assistance in this concrete exchange. [i123–125, i135–140; contrast V12/57/i011–023.]

#### 3.5.2 The same kiss has two participants, not one compulsory meaning

Kanoko apologizes specifically for forcing the kiss. Sumika says it was her first too and that she was surprised but happy. Neither statement should cancel the other. The apology identifies a problem in the act’s manner; the later personal valuation identifies what the event means to Sumika. Retrospective happiness is not an advance agreement, and acknowledgment of the unrequested act does not require denying that a participant may later value it. [i126–128.]

Kanoko asks what makes a kiss important when it is physically only a particular act. The question continues the hotel argument but now occurs with a person who can give her own answer. Sumika notices that Kanoko seemed to be forcing herself and asks whether the act was truly as inconsequential as she says. The inquiry differs from Yoko’s required sentence insofar as it seeks a more adequate account rather than simply treating fear as proof. That distinction should not be exaggerated into a claim that all later questioning remains free of pressure. [i127–128, i135–137.]

The volume’s strongest alternative to Yoko’s hierarchy emerges when Sumika qualifies the importance of the kiss as importance **to her**. It was with Kanoko, it was a shared first, and she does not want to deny the event simply because its circumstances were confused. The statement does not require Kanoko to have the same romantic response. It also does not assert that every first kiss has one universal value. The point is a personal relation to this event that can be described without making it an absolute rule for everyone else. [i132–134.]

#### 3.5.3 Hime’s event reaches another listener, not a new narrator of Hime’s mind

Kanoko tells Sumika that Hime was kissed by Mitsuki, while admitting uncertainty about some details and timing. Sumika’s response indicates that this is new information for her. The disclosure therefore updates a particular knowledge state: knowing of the confession or the later dating difficulties did not already entail knowing about the kiss. The distinction preserved in earlier volumes remains useful rather than bureaucratic. It explains why the present conversation supplies a new basis for Sumika’s response. [i129–130.]

Kanoko’s theory of the event is explicit. If a kiss is precious and someone takes it, Hime should reject that person. Because Hime has not simply rejected Mitsuki, Kanoko feels compelled to believe that the kiss was unimportant and that Hime remains unchanged. This is an attempt to keep several desired propositions together. It does not supply Hime’s actual explanation, and it does not revoke Hime’s previously explicit nonromantic answer. Hime may value Mitsuki, preserve limits and interpret the kiss in ways Kanoko’s either/or rule does not accommodate. The inspected V12 scene does not give Hime a new speech to settle those possibilities. [i129–134; inherited WY7-C12, WY8-C04/C11.]

Sumika asks Kanoko not to make Hime’s event disappear. That is not the same as authoritatively assigning Hime romantic feelings. It is a demand to recognize that something happened and that its significance cannot be settled merely by what Kanoko needs the event not to mean. Even here, the analyst must keep the source of each account visible: Hime’s earlier disclosed experience, Kanoko’s current interpretation and Sumika’s response are distinct. The conversation does not make Sumika an omniscient arbiter of the earlier pair. [i132–134.]

#### 3.5.4 Refusing abandonment can become refusing release

Kanoko tries to end the discussion and deal with the matter alone. Sumika takes her hand and refuses to let the conversation close that way. At i137 Kanoko explicitly asks to be released, and Sumika explicitly declines. Sumika explains that withdrawing now would repeat the earlier failure that left Kanoko to carry her worries alone. Her motive is intelligible and tied to a real prior error; the immediate boundary is nevertheless refused. [i135–137.]

The physical and rhetorical double meaning of “not letting go” is therefore essential. It can mean not abandoning someone emotionally. In this scene it also means keeping a literal hold after a request to release it. Reading only the metaphor would remove the act that gives the metaphor its force. Reading only the physical restraint would miss the history that makes Kanoko’s later response intelligible. A balanced account preserves both without declaring that the benign intention authorizes the method. [i136–140.]

Kanoko calls Sumika forceful, then says that being heard may allow something in her relation to Sumika and Hime to change. She asks to tell an older story. Her later hand placed over Sumika’s supplies affirmative uptake, not merely the absence of resistance. The sequence thus contains an actual movement from a refused request to a chosen continuation. That movement is evidence of local acceptance; it is not a retroactive deletion of the refused request or proof that the same method would be acceptable in another scene. [i138–140.]

This is why the volume cannot be summarized as a complete transition from coercion to consent. The hotel coercion and Sumika’s handhold differ substantially in purpose, severity and subsequent response. They are not interchangeable acts. Yet the shared problem—whether a stated refusal can be overridden because someone claims to know what the other person needs—remains available for scrutiny. The later scene belongs to the repair and also qualifies the repair’s maturity. [V12/58/i040–055; V12/61/i135–140.]

#### 3.5.5 The ending supplies a report, not the missing history

Kanoko begins by saying that in middle school she made it as though her confession to Hime had never happened. A remembered image of Hime accompanies the statement. This is new and consequential evidence: Kanoko now reports an earlier confession and a later act of treating it as absent. It revises the current account of what may underlie her insistence on an unchanged relation. It does **not** yet show the words of the confession, Hime’s response, the procedure of “erasure,” or what Hime now understands. [i139–140.]

The distinction matters especially because the project has carefully preserved Hime’s incomplete knowledge. A report that a confession occurred is not a license to give present Hime comprehensive knowledge of Kanoko’s current romantic attachment, the abolition project, the Yoko consultation, the dating arrangement or the hotel night. The earlier claim “Hime has not been shown receiving the current full account” can remain valid while the history becomes more complicated. The appropriate operation is to open a specific historical question, not to reverse every information record at once. [i140; inherited information boundaries in V09–V11.]

The last mainline image is therefore a threshold. The night’s conversation has reached a place where a previously excluded account can begin. The source does not take the reader through that account before the volume ends. Neither the affectionate cover nor the following half-chapter supplies the missing answer. The frozen endpoint is **V12/61/i140**, with an unfinished present disclosure and an only partially introduced past. [i140–145.]

### 6.2 Need, love, and entitlement

Kanoko’s statement that she needs Sumika is significant positive attachment. It is not an interchangeable synonym for returning Sumika’s romantic love. Yoko repeatedly treats needing a response, jealousy and fear of loss as entitlement to produce the desired reaction. Kanoko often retains polite phrasing while objecting. That form does not make her refusal hypothetical.
