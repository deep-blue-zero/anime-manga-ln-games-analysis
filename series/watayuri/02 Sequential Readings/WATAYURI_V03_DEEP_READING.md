## 1. The volume’s governing problem

Volume 3 asks what happens when a person’s secure place is made out of another person’s attention. Kanoko has found in Hime something more specific than a popular girl to admire: someone who recognized her hostility to compulsory sociability, exposed the difference between pleasant presentation and private feeling, and offered a relationship in which the presentation could rest. The later school flashback makes that special access a real part of their friendship’s formation, not simply Kanoko’s invention. But the present narrative shows her trying to preserve that place by preventing Hime’s other attachment from acquiring greater recognition. The origin of the claim explains its force; it does not establish that Hime has promised never to value anyone else. [V03/14/i080–085; V03/15/i095–098, i111–116; V03/16/i147–156.]

Sumika also thinks in terms of a place that must be protected. To her, Liebe is sustained by genuine good relations beneath its fictional sisterhood. Fragmentary recollections connect the introduction of romance with a previous loss. She consequently treats Kanoko’s attachment not only as a feeling to understand but as a prospective threat to a shared environment. Her inference that Kanoko’s interest is romantic has strong support in the pages the reader sees. Her further claim that preventing damage requires Kanoko to abandon Hime is a different proposition, carrying a different evidentiary burden. Understanding someone’s desire does not automatically confer authority to decide its permissible future. [V03/14/i061–070, i086–088; V03/15/i089, i112–116.]

The Blumen contest turns those differences into actionable choices. It is simultaneously an immersive school event, a customer-facing popularity mechanism, a form of recognition for a sister pair, and a route to a limited rule-making privilege. Hime discovers that she can enjoy Mitsuki’s recognition even after losing the lead in her own ambition. Kanoko discovers that supporting Hime’s expressed preference may strengthen the relationship she wants to prevent. Sumika can help Kanoko intervene in the contest while intending to stop Kanoko’s wider project. Their public cooperation is therefore real, but its shared purpose is narrow and unstable. It is neither proof of private trust nor the formation of a new sister pact. [V03/12/i005–010; V03/15/i091–110.]

The volume does not settle this conflict. Its latest present-day scene ends with Kanoko rejecting Sumika’s demand that she give up Hime. The mainline then moves backward to the school friendship’s origin and closes there. This arrangement increases the reader’s understanding of the refusal without showing what Kanoko, Sumika, or Hime does next. A retrospective explanation is not a present-day repair; the affectionate image on the final page must not be used to manufacture one. [V03/16/i117–119, i153–156.]

## 3. Sequential, form-sensitive close reading

### 3.1 Shift 12: a contest makes private preference operational

#### School fiction, work procedure, and the authority of a title

The opening idealizes the school and its seasonal occasion before returning to staff instruction. That transition matters: the pleasant school environment is a represented fiction which the café must make usable, not evidence that the staff have entered another literal school. The explanation then moves from atmosphere to procedure: eligible dessert orders generate guest voting opportunities, while the fictional student body supplies an allotment for the five staff participants. A staff member’s ninety votes are not ninety actual visitors; the imagined institution is translated into a real mechanism for influencing the result. [WY3-O01; V03/12/i001–007.]

The leading candidate receives the Blumen title, and the privilege of establishing a rule supplies a further reason to care about winning. At this point the various motives are not yet reconciled. Hime wants to be chosen; Kanoko thinks the honor fits Hime and wants to support her; Mitsuki expects sisterly support while retaining a separate view of who best suits the role. The method should therefore resist treating every stated vote as a transparent measurement of affection. A person may vote for an ideal representative, a partner, a strategic alternative, or herself, and those reasons may later diverge. [V03/12/i008–010.]

This is not merely an external contest interrupting intimate relations. Its symbolic reward attaches individual success to a pair, and its rule privilege may alter the conditions under which those relations are performed. The very structure that makes the event intelligible as a school celebration also makes it available for private projects. The source does not yet establish how far that privilege could actually be exercised, or whether management would accept a rule abolishing a foundational feature. Those implementation questions must remain distinct from a character’s later proposal. [V03/12/i008; V03/15/i114.]

#### Kanoko’s support is sincere but selectively organized

Kanoko begins by enjoying the workplace because it lets her be near Hime and help her. Her attention to the explanation is partly compensatory: she thinks Hime finds this kind of material difficult, so she will understand it for both of them. There is real practical care here, but it is organized around one relationship. When Hime recommends ordinary thanks, greetings, and wider conversation, Kanoko does not simply need a missing social script. She has difficulty accepting why those other interactions should matter in the way they matter to Hime. [WY3-O02; V03/12/i005–006, i011–014.]

The phone-return exchange shows that formally polite wording can still be pragmatically unclear. Kanoko’s initial response leaves Sumika uncertain whether she is declining the object or postponing the handover. Hime’s correction addresses an actual interpersonal difficulty without providing access to the deeper reason Kanoko is guarded. The scene thus establishes a problem in communication, but not a global absence of intelligence, feeling, or initiative. Later scenes show her acting forcefully when her own priority becomes salient. [V03/12/i011–014.]

An especially important focalization boundary occurs when the page imagines Hime’s ambitious response to the contest. The subsequent wording identifies this as what **Kanoko thinks Hime must be thinking**. It is evidence of Kanoko’s model of Hime, not an independently narrated second window into Hime’s mind. Her familiarity is substantial, but its habitual accuracy may itself make a new response harder for her to recognize. The later discovery that Hime can enjoy Mitsuki’s success challenges this model. [V03/12/i019; V03/15/i095–097.]

#### Sumika manufactures a diagnostic scene

Sumika’s flirtatious approach to Hime is legible to guests as role-play. Kanoko’s intervention makes the same scene diagnostic for a second audience: Sumika herself. The wrist interception, severe attention, and inability to treat the exchange as harmless performance give Sumika evidence about Kanoko’s investment. The end of the chapter explicitly describes earlier conduct as probing, so it is unnecessary either to treat the flirtation as Sumika’s own romantic confession or to invent an entirely benign motive for it. [WY3-O03; V03/12/i020–024, i032.]

This does not make Kanoko’s reaction a simple detector of romantic truth. At the moment of intervention, discomfort with an intrusive flirtation, protectiveness, exclusion anxiety, and jealousy remain possible contributors. The chapter’s subsequent private scene gives the reader more specific evidence. Sumika’s method also has a cost: she creates a situation likely to unsettle someone rather than asking for a voluntary account. The resulting information is not equivalent to trust freely offered. [V03/12/i020–032.]

On i026 Kanoko privately infers that the phone may have exposed her feelings. The lettering is interior thought, not a disclosure to Hime or a confession by Sumika. Hime’s visible uncertainty reinforces the distribution: the reader learns Kanoko’s fear, while Hime does not thereby acquire it. Sumika’s proposal that the newcomers read together can both help their preparation and create the circumstances for further observation. The text establishes the practical help; the later surveillance prevents the help from being treated as proof that no other purpose is operating. [V03/12/i025–029, i032.]

#### A borrowed sentence becomes a private act

While Hime sleeps, Kanoko takes photographs and describes the resulting lift in her own spirits. The collection now has an evidenced use: it can restore her mood. That observation is more precise than saying the photos are simply proof of obsession, and it is not a claim that every image was obtained by the same method. [WY3-O04; V03/12/i029–030.]

The novel then supplies romantic language which Kanoko identifies as fitting the two of them. The quoted fictional passage concerns a concealed love and the limited contact of two locks of hair. Kanoko brings strands of their hair together beside the sleeping Hime. The close-up supports intimate, deliberate enactment of the passage; it does not require the stronger assertion of an unambiguous kiss. The key evidence is the conjunction of her internal application, the explicitly romantic term **恋心**, and the depicted action. It substantially advances the romantic interpretation beyond a large photograph count or an isolated blush. [V03/12/i031.]

Yet three levels remain different. The novel’s speaker voices a fictional sentiment; Kanoko privately appropriates it; Hime, asleep, offers no corresponding classification or contemporaneous participation. Kanoko’s conviction that the passage suits “us” is not a declaration by both girls. Sumika sees the scene and treats it as confirmation, but her new certainty does not mean she knows the friendship’s complete history. The chapter closes by increasing evidence while keeping its distribution unequal. [V03/12/i031–032.]

### 3.2 Shift 13: the mistake of treating a performed identity as an empty one

#### Failure to recognize Sumika is socially conditioned

Kanoko’s alarm at Sumika’s ordinary presentation exposes how little attention she has given a coworker outside the salon role. Hime recognizes Sumika and has to supply the connection. The shift in hairstyle, glasses, clothing, and manner is substantial, but the story does not warrant a medical explanation for Kanoko’s misrecognition. It is more usefully connected to the narrowness of her prior observation and the difference between a role identity and the person maintaining it. [WY3-O05; V03/13/i033–040.]

Kanoko consequently recasts Sumika as a frightening or unserious gyaru rather than the quiet person she expected. Hime’s attempt to defend her partly reproduces the category being used against Sumika. The scene does not ask the analyst to adopt that judgment. It prepares a correction in which ordinary presentation, actual reading, and competent work turn out to coexist. Kanoko’s problem is not that every first impression is false; it is that she treats a visible contrast as sufficient explanation of the whole person. [V03/13/i037–045.]

#### Labor exists outside what a novice happens to notice

Nene’s kitchen appearance broadens the visible workplace. Dessert service depends on stock and thawing decisions, not only elegant delivery in the salon. Nene identifies a practical constraint and an alternative if the anticipated demand increases. The introduction is modest but important: the customer-facing fiction is sustained by work not all equally visible to Hime or Kanoko. Nene’s name, **寧々**, is admitted here without importing a surname or later biography. [WY3-O06; V03/13/i041.]

Kanoko initially sees Sumika sitting with a book while others move around her. Nene supplies a different context: Sumika’s arrival and departure pattern, attention to the role, and actual responsibilities are not captured by that selected moment. Nene also challenges the dismissive use of the gyaru label. This is a correction of evidence selection as much as a defense of a coworker. It should not be inflated into a quantified finding that Sumika does more work than everyone else; the volume has not measured workloads. [V03/13/i044–046, i052–054.]

The handling of alcohol orders further distinguishes role grades and assigned tasks from surface availability. For this reading, what matters is the depicted division of service, not a legal inference about age or licensing. The edition’s manual later elaborates operational details in its own paratextual lane. Neither the salon’s fictional seniority nor a visually mature presentation independently establishes someone’s ordinary age. [V03/13/i054; V03/extra/i165.]

#### The apparent contradiction in reading is resolved by a scope distinction

Sumika’s statement that she does not read in the salon sounds, to Kanoko and initially Hime, as though her bookishness is an empty prop. Her subsequent discussion of a novel therefore risks being classified as invented expertise. Kanoko’s check of the book gives her independent evidence that the account is genuine. Sumika then explains the missing distinction: in the salon she opens books she has already finished, so she can sustain the image without becoming absorbed in new material. [WY3-O07; V03/13/i047–058.]

This is an unusually clean demonstration of the analytical method’s warning that performed does not mean fake. The outward sign is deliberately arranged. The underlying interest is also real. Not reading new pages at work may help preserve attention to work rather than reveal indolence. The same object can thus function as personal interest, role prop, conversation resource, and an attention-management device. Those functions are compatible rather than mutually exclusive. [V03/13/i055–057.]

The book discussion supports a bounded everyday preference: Sumika reads and can enjoy discussing stories, including their less solemn adaptations. It does not license a comprehensive taste profile or an imported identification of a real-world title which the inspected lettering has not independently established. Her shift into enthusiastic ordinary conversation is useful speech evidence precisely because it differs from the ceremonious salon persona without contradicting her actual interest. [V03/13/i048–051, i055–058.]

#### A corrected model is not complete trust

At i058 Sumika supplies the ordinary surname **知花**, giving the current cast subject a verified ordinary identity, **Chibana Sumika**, alongside the café’s **Tachibana Sumika**. This is an alias update to **WY1-CAST-SUMIKA**, not a new character record. Kanoko’s continued use of the café address keeps their relationship asymmetrical even outside the role. The correction supplies a name she can use; it does not compel intimacy. [V03/13/i058–059.]

Sumika says she wants to become friends. The statement is evidence of an offered explanation, and the practical kindness supporting it is real. But subsequent focalization will reveal a protective surveillance project as well. It would be as premature to declare the friendship offer wholly fraudulent as to accept it as an exhaustive account of her motive. Kanoko can revise “nothing but a frightening, unserious gyaru” without granting unrestricted trust. [WY3-O08; V03/13/i059–060; V03/14/i069–070.]

Hime also makes a small but concrete adjustment: she will read the café material herself, recognizing that others have been doing work for them. Kanoko can help, but help is not made a substitute for all of Hime’s participation. The evidence supports willingness and a changed account of responsibility. It does not yet meet the inherited forecast’s stronger requirement of a comparable unfamiliar task in which candidly naming a limitation produces a demonstrated practical improvement. [V03/13/i060; WY1-PR04.]

### 3.3 Shift 14: an accurate observation becomes an uncertain policy

#### Sumika values the reality underneath the fiction

The move into Sumika’s focalization changes the reader’s relation to her competence. Earlier chapters showed useful interventions and questionable intrusions from outside. Now the source articulates a positive commitment: Liebe depends on coworkers genuinely getting along, not merely on a setting that says they do. She notices Mitsuki’s expanded emotional range and Hime’s progress. Her protection of the café is therefore not adequately described as protecting a profitable illusion while disliking all actual closeness. [WY3-O09; V03/14/i061–070.]

Her understanding remains partial. Calling Hime an innocent good girl records one observer’s model, not a definitive psychological assessment overriding Hime’s well-established impression management. Likewise, the thought that Kanoko is introducing romance into the café is stronger than an objective narratorial statement that romance will destroy it. The source gives Sumika interior access to her concern without removing the need to evaluate its inference. [V03/14/i065–070.]

The current sisters illustrate why the distinction matters. Mitsuki can praise Hime’s improved work while withholding a judgment that she is the most suitable Blumen representative. Hime wants more than abstract task approval from her particular elder sister. Kanoko’s reassurance initially fits her usual support role, but the emotionally consequential exchange takes place between Hime and Mitsuki. Their values in the contest are not already identical simply because the friendship has been repaired. [V03/14/i067–076.]

#### A sister’s support is not just another endorsement

Hime’s appeal to Mitsuki makes the relational significance of a vote visible. Her question is not exhausted by the mechanics of gaining ninety votes. She wants her sister to consider her worthy. Mitsuki’s blush and incomplete response indicate that this plea reaches a more exposed register than ordinary standards talk. Her practical clarity does not translate automatically into a clear explanation of her personal response. [WY3-O10; V03/14/i073–076.]

Sumika then describes a possible victory as recognition for the sister pair. What sounds like reassurance that Kanoko’s support for Hime will be fulfilled indirectly can instead threaten Kanoko’s understanding of her own place. The analyst should not assume she already hears the pair reward as Hime’s happiness. That difference becomes explicit in the next chapter, when she rejects precisely that equivalence. [V03/14/i077–079; V03/15/i103.]

#### Defining the institution does not settle the private relationship

Kanoko asks what sisterhood actually means. Sumika distinguishes the fictional mutual-support bond from a romantic relationship. This is important source terminology: the role neither requires the workers to be lovers nor makes every private relation enacted through it nonromantic. A classification of the script cannot by itself classify the participants’ feelings. Conversely, Kanoko’s romantic investment does not make the public sister system an actual network of lovers. [WY3-O11; V03/14/i080–082.]

Kanoko’s response shows that her interest in the rules is not neutral research. She is not attracted to the available sister arrangement simply because it would give her a role with someone. The identity of the partner is decisive. When Sumika describes Hime and Mitsuki as friends well suited to the pact, Kanoko answers that she alone is Hime’s friend. At this stage that is an exclusive claim by Kanoko, not a verified census of Hime’s current relationships. The final flashback will give it a historical basis without making it permanently true. [V03/14/i082–083; V03/16/i153–156.]

Sumika warns that possessiveness could make Hime dislike her. The warning may identify a real risk, but it is not a report of Hime’s judgment. Kanoko cries, and the conversation collapses when she turns toward Hime. The emotional impact is visible; deliberate use of tears as a tactic is not established. Sumika’s frustration at being unable to continue does not grant the analyst evidence that the distress is feigned. [V03/14/i083–085.]

#### A memory supplies a reason, not an exhaustive history

The following recollection juxtaposes a once-populated salon scene with absence and discarded cross necklaces. A partially shown figure’s remark about interfering in another person’s romance returns within Sumika’s thought. The arrangement strongly supports a connection between her current alarm and an earlier relational loss at Liebe. It does not identify every participant, reconstruct the entire sequence, or settle who caused what. [WY3-O12; V03/14/i086–088.]

The visible operation is subtraction: persons disappear from a repeated configuration, objects remain, and Sumika confronts the damaged social space. That is stronger evidence for experienced loss than for a universal law about romance. Her resolve not to let the place be broken again is psychologically intelligible even if the policy she derives from it is too broad. A later account could strengthen, qualify, or overturn her causal interpretation without erasing the fear actually represented here. [V03/14/i087–088; V03/15/i089.]

### 3.4 Shift 15: a coalition succeeds publicly before its private bargain fails

#### Interim numbers do not mean one thing to everyone

The midpoint standings show Mitsuki at **1,210**, Sumika at **1,103**, Kanoko at **501**, and Hime at **488** guest votes. These are **interim figures**, not final results. Hime’s surprise at being last does not establish that her performance has failed in every sense. The staff explain that the attractive sister interaction can direct recognition toward the elder figure who best fits the event’s ideal. Hime contributes to a success that is not returned to her in the form she initially wanted. [WY3-O13; V03/15/i091–094.]

This complicates individual measures of worth. Guest votes are neither a complete measure of service competence nor a direct measure of who is loved by coworkers. The contest’s fictional criteria and pair presentation affect where praise goes. Hime can still want to win, accept the actual standings, and begin to value the result differently. None of those attitudes requires a permanent abandonment of her desire for admiration. [V03/15/i092–096.]

#### Hime’s pleasure is not the pleasure Kanoko expected her to have

Kanoko privately asks whether Hime is really giving up. Hime answers in terms of Yano: a person who was once bad at performing is now doing it and being recognized. Hime is happy for her. This is the critical private-closeness cue missing from the V02 adjudication. It is not simply a public scripted embrace which Kanoko might reasonably treat as work. Hime herself gives a particular, appreciative account of Mitsuki’s development. [WY3-O14; V03/15/i095–097.]

Kanoko responds by recruiting Sumika to win instead. Her subsequent explanation identifies the reason: a Mitsuki victory would make Hime and Mitsuki Blumen sisters. When Sumika suggests that this is effectively Hime winning too, Kanoko explicitly rejects the equivalence. The shift distinguishes support for Hime’s original ambition from support for Hime’s present preference. Concern about work failure or an allegedly unrepaired hatred is no longer an adequate explanation of the whole response. [V03/15/i098, i103–105.]

This meets the original condition of **WY1-PR03** without requiring a diagnosis or the stronger claim that Kanoko never cares about Hime’s wishes. Her earlier assistance remains genuine. The new evidence shows a boundary at which preserving exclusive access takes priority over helping Hime obtain the form of happiness she has just described. [V03/15/i095–105.]

Hime also tells Mitsuki that her success would feel like her own. Mitsuki’s embarrassed correction does not clearly explain what the declaration means to her. Their connection is visibly warmer, and the contrast between standards talk and exposed attachment persists. However, the scene does not explicitly settle whether their understandings are friendly, in-role, romantic, or some unarticulated mixture. It supports **WY2-PR05** more directly than **WY2-PR01**. [WY3-O15; V03/15/i099–102.]

#### Real strategic cooperation can rest on incompatible final purposes

Kanoko offers her ninety votes to Sumika and asks for a changed outcome. Sumika considers how staff votes might alter the standings, including possible support from Mitsuki and Mai. Her calculation of a four-person, 360-vote bloc is a **plan dependent on assumptions**, not a completed ballot count or a final victory. This distinction matters especially because the narrative ends before a result. [WY3-O16; V03/15/i103–104.]

Sumika agrees on the condition that Kanoko hear a request of her own. The content is not fully specified before the public intervention. That makes the exchange different from consent to the later demand to relinquish Hime. Kanoko has asked for tactical help; she has not demonstrably agreed that Sumika may determine her attachments. Sumika, meanwhile, has not agreed in advance to every rule Kanoko might wish her to make. [V03/15/i104–105, i111–116.]

On the salon floor Sumika presents Kanoko’s choice as a brave, long-felt support for a worthy senior. The account smooths over the actual motive and recent timing. Guests respond warmly, and Hime accepts the apparent growth in Kanoko’s ability to choose and speak for herself. The public scene is therefore successful on one level: it produces a persuasive, cooperative configuration. But Hime’s approving interpretation depends on not knowing that Kanoko’s aim is to prevent the sister outcome Hime has chosen to support. [V03/15/i106–110.]

The scene also prevents a reductive description of Kanoko as unable to act or speak. She can make a request, redirect votes, enter a bargain, and perform a statement in front of guests. Her inhibition varies with stakes, interlocutor, and the degree to which another person supplies a usable script. Practical agency is present even when the use of that agency becomes controlling. [V03/15/i098–110.]

#### The two protective projects become explicit

At the restaurant afterward, Sumika briefly considers not making the demand she had intended. Kanoko has cooperated, the immediate competition may be manageable, and perhaps merely loving Hime need not become destructive. This hesitation is important counterevidence to the claim that Sumika mechanically opposes all affection between women or has only one unchanging motive. The source shows a conditional reconsideration before it closes. [WY3-O17; V03/15/i111–113.]

Kanoko then asks Sumika to use the Blumen rule privilege to abolish sisterhood. She predicts that Hime will return to her once that structure is removed. This is a proposal, not an enacted rule, and it rests on Kanoko’s causal model: the new institutional bond is what has made Hime change. The preceding scenes give the reader reason to doubt that abolishing the label would abolish Hime’s particular regard for Mitsuki. Kanoko is trying to act on a real institutional mechanism, but her desired emotional result is not guaranteed by the mechanism. [V03/15/i114.]

Sumika interprets the request as identifying and attacking the café’s relational core. She produces a phone image of Kanoko leaning over the sleeping Hime, names romantic liking and jealousy, and tells Kanoko to give up Hime. The displayed image is an evidentiary instrument in the confrontation. Its possession is established; a precise capture procedure need not be invented. It is also a new boundary issue, distinct from finding images on Kanoko’s misplaced phone. [WY3-O18; V03/15/i115–116.]

The conflict is not symmetrical in every action. Kanoko seeks to abolish a role system in order to redirect another person’s attachment; Sumika seeks to forbid an attachment in order to preserve that system. Kanoko has proposed the rule but not obtained authority or enacted it. Sumika has already probed, observed, publicly redirected the situation, and now made a direct demand. Both understand themselves as protecting something precious; neither has secured Hime’s participation in deciding what that protection should mean. [V03/15/i103–116.]

### 3.5 Shift 16: why “the only friend” is a history, not just a slogan

#### The present breaks before the past explains it

The chapter begins by repeating the confrontation’s decisive configuration. Kanoko classifies Sumika as an obstacle and says she will not give up. Sumika tries to continue, but no completed explanation, revised bargain, apology, or new agreement follows before the narrative changes time. The latest present-day endpoint is i118. The title image then places Kanoko alone on a school stairway, opening the history of the relationship whose surrender has just been demanded. [V03/16/i117–119.]

That ordering gives the flashback an argumentative function without making it a verdict. The reader is asked to understand what Sumika’s demand threatens for Kanoko. Understanding is not the same as accepting her right to dismantle Hime’s other bond. Similarly, the failure of the present conversation does not mean Sumika’s concerns about concrete controlling behavior were entirely invented. [V03/16/i117–156.]

#### Ordinary good intentions can leave someone without a voice

Kanoko’s earlier classroom is not introduced as a group of obvious villains. Her narration describes people who are friendly, sociable, and happy together—and whom she dislikes. The mismatch matters. Her withdrawal is not simply evidence that everyone has rejected her; she has actively found a place outside the collective social space. The roof-stair area is where she can eat and read without interference. [WY3-O19; V03/16/i120–125.]

Hime initially belongs, in Kanoko’s classification, to the same pleasant “everyone.” Seeing her on the stairs makes Kanoko fear that her private refuge will be reported or taken away. Hime instead continues visiting. This could be read as unwelcome attention rather than kindness, and Kanoko initially does read it that way. The scene does not allow the eventual friendship to erase the earlier intrusion as experienced by its future participant. [V03/16/i123–129.]

The class’s congratulatory video for a teacher’s marriage exposes a structural difficulty. Kanoko has not understood or participated in the project as others have; the classmates ask her to make its sign, assuming that a task will complete the distribution of work. They give her a design and emphasize that she has not yet done anything. There is practical reasoning in the request, but little inquiry into whether she can, wants to, or knows how to undertake it. She accepts despite inward resistance. [WY3-O20; V03/16/i126–131.]

Hime’s early response is blunt: others cannot know a disliked condition that is never stated, and silence lets it be treated as absent. That observation identifies a real communication problem. It is not sufficient proof that voicing a refusal would have been socially easy or costless. Kanoko’s difficulty includes the expectation that the group’s good purpose makes resistance illegitimate. Her failure to say no and the classmates’ failure to ask a useful question contribute differently to the eventual rupture. [V03/16/i132–134.]

#### The sign is not simply an unwanted task

Kanoko puts work into the sign, including decorative detail. This visible investment complicates the claim that she merely refuses all cooperation. The classmates then shorten the work under time pressure and explain that the group scene will be filmed on the roof. What they treat as a practical solution reaches the place Kanoko has treated as her own. Her anger gathers around work, compulsory inclusion, and threatened loss of refuge rather than one isolated insult. [V03/16/i135–137.]

She breaks the sign and kicks the damaged materials. The source depicts an actual destructive act; it should not be euphemized into a purely inward refusal or an accidental misunderstanding. But the sequence also supplies the context in which the object has become a material concentration of her frustrated agency. Explaining that sequence does not justify damaging the group’s work. [WY3-O21; V03/16/i138–140.]

Hime sees the aftermath and says nothing immediately. When the classmates later show Kanoko the broken sign, they initially treat her as someone whose effort has been wronged, not as the culprit. This is decisive counterevidence to a simplified story in which malicious classmates corner an innocent girl. Kanoko could remain silent and retain their sympathy. Instead, after noticing Hime’s silence and recalling their earlier exchange, she admits that she broke it because she disliked making it. Her action moves from concealed resistance to public accountability, however socially awkwardly expressed. [V03/16/i139–142.]

#### Hime protects Kanoko by overriding the truth she has chosen to tell

Hime interrupts with an alibi: the sign was intact while she was with Kanoko, after which they left together. The reader has seen why this cannot be accepted as an accurate account. The false alibi works because Hime’s ordinary credibility exceeds Kanoko’s clumsy confession. Hime also redirects the class away from identifying a culprit and toward replacing the object. That is an actual interpersonal and practical intervention, not merely an attractive speech. [WY3-O22; V03/16/i143–145.]

Its benefits and costs must both remain visible. Kanoko avoids immediate blame; the classmates move toward repairing the shared project; Hime has protected someone she believes has been burdened. But Kanoko’s self-disclosure is publicly cancelled, and the other students act on false information. Hime’s aside tells Kanoko to be quiet until she can explain privately. A caring intervention can therefore make room for one person while withdrawing her control over what has just been said. [V03/16/i143–146.]

This newly supplied antecedent belongs to the developmental history of Hime’s protective problem-solving. It is not a new present-day recurrence after the V02 repair, and must not be scored as confirmation of **WY2-PR04**, whose concern was what she would do under a later threat despite that correction. A later-published flashback can strengthen a character pattern without constituting a prospective behavioral test. [Predecessor WY2-PR04; V03/16/i143–146.]

#### “Good girl” and “good façade” are not the same self-model

In private Hime says Kanoko is bad at maintaining a façade. Kanoko answers with an accusation: people who force themselves to get along, and displace someone’s private place while acting good, are intolerable. Her outburst finally expresses the resistance that polite participation concealed. Hime treats this not as an impossible revelation but as evidence of the dislikes she has been trying to get Kanoko to name. [WY3-O23; V03/16/i147–150.]

Hime distinguishes her own project from silent self-denial. She does not claim that she meekly endures everyone for their benefit. She deliberately uses cuteness and performance to make people seek her; she wants to be liked rather than become merely the compliant good girl. Her speech is self-presentation too, and its bravado should not be treated as a complete account of motive. Nevertheless, its contrast with the classroom persona is directly available. Kanoko now encounters a person whose performance is active rather than merely submissive, and whose impatience can coexist with help. [V03/16/i150–152.]

The scene also contains actual reciprocity. Hime says she has not wanted to disclose her acting after an earlier exposure, yet she has told Kanoko. Kanoko recognizes that Hime assisted her despite that reluctance. Hime then describes Kanoko as resembling the person who once revealed what she thought, but frames Kanoko as inept at façades rather than simply malicious. This is Hime’s interpretation at an earlier time. It is neither a new present-day accusation against Mitsuki nor a substitute for V02’s fuller account of their childhood rupture. [V03/16/i153.]

#### The shared secret creates a real specialness—and a later danger of treating it as property

Hime calls Kanoko a friend and asks to rest her façade when they are together. Kanoko says she will not hate her. Those are reciprocal acts of acknowledgment. The relationship’s later significance is not built only out of one person imagining a bond that never existed. There was a deliberately distinguished private space, created partly by Hime’s own disclosure and invitation. [V03/16/i153–154.]

Kanoko’s attention then narrows to Hime’s face, hair, closeness, and overwhelming cuteness. The close-ups are not merely an objective inventory of attractive features; the thoughts and reactions locate the attention in Kanoko. Together with the earlier romantic passage and present refusal to relinquish Hime, this supports a romantic reading of her attachment with much greater confidence than was possible at V02. It still supplies no corresponding romantic account from Hime. [WY3-O24; V03/16/i155–156; V03/12/i031.]

The final narration names Kanoko as Hime’s one special friend from that day. It is strongest as a statement of the friendship’s origin and Kanoko’s understanding of its privileged space. It must not be converted into an enforceable promise that Hime can never make another friend, nor read as erasing the present evidence of Hime’s joy in Mitsuki. The volume’s structure deliberately places those two truths together: the special place was real, and its preservation is now being pursued in a way that may disregard the other participant’s changed wishes. [V03/15/i095–102; V03/16/i153–156.]

### The asymmetry inside the parallel

Kanoko and Sumika both protect a valued place, but “both are controlling” is too coarse to preserve their actual acts. Kanoko has advanced a proposal aimed at a system; she lacks demonstrated authority to enact it and has not secured Hime’s assent. Sumika has already exercised considerable interpersonal influence and now tells Kanoko what feeling or attachment must end. Each also has conduct outside this description: Kanoko checks a mistaken judgment and has shown practical care; Sumika performs useful work and briefly reconsiders intervention. The scope of criticism belongs to the act and its conditions, not a total character label. [AG04/AG07–AG10.]

The earlier Hime offers a third configuration. She protects someone from a group by managing the account the group receives, even against that person’s confession. Yet she also opens a private conversation in which refusal and dislike can become speakable. A later model must therefore distinguish **creating room for a person’s actual view** from **deciding in advance which expression of that view will count**. The same character can do both in one episode. [AG11–AG14.]

No V03 present-day rupture is marked repaired. A successful salon scene and a warm school memory are not evidence of such repair.

## 11. Written Japanese speech, address, and classification

**Canonical destination:** `03 Longitudinal Ledgers/WATAYURI_JAPANESE_SPEECH_ADDRESS_AND_REGISTER_LEDGER.md`. The excerpts are short and used only where the form is analytically material. Paraphrases elsewhere do not pretend to be official translation. Manga lettering supports wording, address and represented interruption; it does not establish timbre, pitch, breath or voice-actor delivery.

### WY3-JP01 — A polite form need not communicate a clear choice

Kanoko’s **「いいです」** in the phone-return exchange leaves the practical intention uncertain until the surrounding conversation resolves it. The point is contextual ambiguity, not a stable claim that the expression is intrinsically rude or that Kanoko lacks politeness. Her guarded answer and Hime’s intervention show a difference between producing a conventional form and making one’s intended response recoverable to the other person. [V03/12/i011–014; O02.]

### WY3-JP02 — Thought, projected thought, and spoken acknowledgment

At i019 the imagined ambitious Hime is explicitly framed as Kanoko’s reconstruction of what Hime would think. At i026 Kanoko’s realization about the possible phone discovery remains interior. At i031 her thought selects a passage from **『乙女の心臓』** as fitting the two of them. These modes matter more than a generic transcript’s list of words: attributing the lines to audible conversation would create false disclosures and erase the very asymmetry driving the scenes. [V03/12/i019, i026, i031; O02–O04.]

### WY3-JP03 — Romantic language is available before a mutual romantic declaration

The novel passage uses **「恋心」**. Kanoko privately applies it; later Sumika explicitly categorizes her liking as **「恋愛として」** and associates it with jealousy. These are convergent but not identical evidence routes: private self-application through fiction, another person’s direct classification, and Kanoko’s refusal to abandon Hime. The warranted current claim is strong evidence of romantic attachment, not an invented direct statement by Kanoko in precisely Sumika’s wording, a sexual-identity self-label, or reciprocal romance. [V03/12/i031; V03/15/i115–116; V03/16/i118, i155–156.]

### WY3-JP04 — The scope of “not reading”

Sumika’s clarification **「サロンではね」** restricts the earlier statement to a setting. The reference to books already finished distinguishes an actual private reading habit from the use of a book during work. That small restriction dissolves an apparent contradiction which Kanoko had treated as evidence of insincerity. A character language model should therefore record scope correction and contextual qualification, not a stable habit of lying about books. [V03/13/i055–057; O07.]

### WY3-JP05 — Ordinary and café names

Sumika supplies **知花純加** and contrasts the ordinary surname with **橘**. Kanoko’s continued café address is not an error for the analyst to silently normalize: it marks the relationship’s current distance and her incomplete movement out of the work frame. Hime and Mitsuki’s surname distinctions similarly remain source-conditioned; “Yano” in Hime’s appreciative account invokes a particular history rather than an interchangeable label. [V03/13/i058–059; V03/15/i096.]

### WY3-JP06 — Defining a script versus describing feelings

Sumika’s **「恋愛関係とは違うよ」** explains the sister system. Its grammatical scope is the institutional relation under discussion. It does not demonstrate that private romance is impossible, that no participant feels it, or that romantic feeling is already a mutually recognized relation. This distinction is central to why a bond can become emotionally consequential without the script naming it as romance. [V03/14/i080–082; O11.]

### WY3-JP07 — Exclusivity moves from assertion to request

Kanoko’s **「ひめちゃんの友だちは私だけです」** presents exclusive friendship as fact; the later request to abolish sisterhood attempts to preserve or restore that claimed arrangement. Her politer sentence structure does not make the content less absolute. Conversely, her direct refusal to give up Hime is stronger evidence of persistence than the assumption that a quiet person will always submit to a more fluent coworker. [V03/14/i083; V03/15/i114; V03/16/i118.]

### WY3-JP08 — Goodness, façade, and speech about dislike

The school sequence differentiates **「いい子」** from **「ソトヅラ」** and **「演技」**. Kanoko first groups Hime with socially acceptable good people; Hime then distinguishes active impression management from silently enduring what others demand. Her warning that unstated dislikes may be treated as absent identifies a communicative problem, but does not itself abolish the cost of stating them. Both girls can appear compliant while holding sharp private objections; the difference lies partly in whether that performance feels chosen and useful. [V03/16/i132–134, i147–152.]

### WY3-JP09 — Private trust permits another register

Hime’s request to rest the façade when they are together creates a relationship-conditioned speech space. The bluntness preceding it is not simply the discovery that she has no kindness. It coexists with the voluntary disclosure, practical rescue, and invitation to friendship. Kanoko’s more forceful objections also become possible in this private space. A future speech model must condition candid, sharp, playful and polished registers on audience and relationship, rather than choosing one as the only “real voice.” [V03/16/i147–154; O23.]

### WY3-JP10 — Encouragement through a knowingly performed image

In the supplement, Hime links the photograph to Kanoko’s spirits while openly boasting about her own cuteness. Kanoko’s closing distinction treats Hime’s kindness as real even where the cuteness is deliberately performed. This is not evidence of a wholly separate false and true vocabulary: the playful boast itself can do relational work. [V03/S16.5/i160–161; O25.]

These observations support recurring patterns, not a complete idiolect for every character. Sumika’s formal salon language and casual literary enthusiasm now have multiple contexts; Kanoko’s condition-dependent directness is substantially clearer. Nene’s small sample supports blunt workplace correction, not a mature speech model.
