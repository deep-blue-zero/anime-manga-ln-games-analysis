## 1. The volume’s governing problem

Volume 11 places two ways of dealing with another person’s feelings beside one another. Nene finally tells Sumika what she wanted, what she did to obtain it, and what she has regretted. Sumika answers from her own present position. The answer does not give Nene the romance she once sought, but it permits an acknowledgment of the relationship they actually shared. Kanoko, meanwhile, tries to make Sumika happy by discovering what a girlfriend is supposed to provide. Her search increasingly passes through Yoko, who offers not merely advice but a way to interpret every response Sumika makes. One movement allows another person to answer; the other tries to produce a response whose meaning has already been supplied. [V11/55/i105–124; V11/56/i131–156.]

The opposition is not simply honesty versus conscious deception. Kanoko genuinely wants Sumika to stop suffering and wants their valued connection to work again. Sumika genuinely enjoys dating her and makes several real efforts not to force an unwanted act or inconvenience. Nene’s confession includes self-accusation that may understate her own hurt. Yoko begins from some propositions that the pages support: Sumika does love Kanoko, jealousy has influenced Sumika’s conduct, and the couple’s desires are unequal. The consequential question is what each person makes those truths authorize. They do not establish that Kanoko owes physical intimacy, that Sumika’s restraint is secretly a request for its opposite, or that Yoko can speak for what Sumika needs. [V11/52/i017–028; V11/53/i060–066; V11/55/i097–101; V11/56/i133–155.]

Nene’s expanded retrospective account also complicates the idea that the old café relationship was destroyed by an outside romance alone. Nene accepted Yoko’s jealousy strategy, watched Sumika’s distress as possible evidence that it was succeeding, and eventually loved Yoko herself. Those are different stages, not a choice between “all calculated” and “all sincere.” Nene’s later acknowledgment of her own harmful choices is important because it restores agency to the person previously remembered chiefly as injured. It is incomplete if converted into a verdict that Yoko did no harm or Nene was never hurt. The present tea conversation becomes reparative when Sumika can add what the apparent abandonment meant to her, rather than merely endorse Nene’s entire self-indictment. [V11/53/i037–066; V11/55/i107–123.]

The café’s performed sisterhood is not exposed as meaningless by the failure of romantic reciprocity. Nene and Sumika remember learning the work, making the salon convincing, and being important to one another. Their discontinued pact retains a history, a taste, and an embodied familiarity. Yet those things do not obligate Sumika to become Nene’s lover or to restore the original formal pair. Nene’s final correction—that she chose Liebe, with Sumika included—resists another reduction of a whole shared life to a competition between romantic candidates. [V11/55/i091–104, i120–124.]

The last chapter develops a more dangerous reduction. Yoko repeatedly explains restraint, disappointment, permission, and anger as expressions of the same underlying desire. Kanoko can then interpret the absence of a demand as a reason to offer more, and can treat refusing an alternative venue as proof that jealousy should not matter. The hotel entry is an actual decision made under those influences; it is neither an absence of all agency nor permission for every possible subsequent act. The final image shows Yoko transmitting a photograph of Kanoko inside the room and asking an unseen recipient to alert Sumika. It establishes manufactured informational asymmetry and an announced emergency, not a depicted assault, rescue, confrontation, or breakup. [V11/56/i139–156.]

## 3. Sequential, form-sensitive close reading

### 3.1 Shift 52 — A dating agreement with different immediate meanings

#### 3.1.1 Delight, secrecy, and the public couple’s performance

Sumika’s morning begins with ordinary preparation transformed by a particular attachment. Makeup, humming, and attention to Kanoko’s signed photograph locate happiness in a private routine before the café’s role machinery is active. The photograph is not a new declaration by Kanoko: it is an existing object newly lived with by Sumika. Her greeting at work similarly singles Kanoko out before broadening to Hime. The sequence gives stronger evidence of Sumika’s delighted investment than a generic claim that she has become cheerful. It also retains the difference between what Sumika feels privately and what an observer can infer from a greeting that can still be made socially general. [V11/52/i001, i005.]

Kanoko’s first urgent priority is different. She brings Sumika aside and insists that the dating arrangement remain unknown, especially to Hime. Her continued love for Hime is not a newly uncovered secret from Sumika; the couple already knows that asymmetry. What is missing is a mutually worked-through account of what preserving it requires. Sumika hears the request within an important new relationship and offers reassurance. Kanoko treats secrecy as a necessary protection for her pre-existing place beside Hime. These meanings overlap enough to produce agreement while remaining different enough to generate further trouble. Privacy should not be equated with deceit automatically, but this particular privacy request matters because it keeps Hime outside an arrangement partly organized around her. [V11/52/i006–007.]

The salon scene then produces convincing intimacy out of a fictional account of a sleepover. It would be an evidentiary error to turn that story into an actual overnight visit. Sumika can use the role to tease Kanoko, make closeness legible to guests, and enjoy an interaction that now has additional private meaning. Kanoko’s embarrassment can serve the performance while concealing its private cause. Their public success consequently proves neither equal ease nor shared understanding. The manga stages the pair as readable to the audience precisely while giving the reader information that the audience lacks. [V11/52/i008–009.]

This is not merely another example of “real feelings leaking through a mask.” Several mechanisms coexist. The invented sleepover is deliberate fiction. Sumika’s pleasure in playing intimacy is personally grounded. Kanoko’s denial and embarrassment can be used as role material whether or not she likes the private implications. The same outward scene has a fictional proposition, a real performance task, and an unequal affective investment. A useful cumulative performance record must keep all three rather than classify the whole exchange as either fake or authentic. [V11/52/i006–009; PER01–PER03 below.]

Hime and Mitsuki continue their café service and in-role assistance nearby. Their participation supplies ordinary continuity after the earlier return and contact negotiations; it is not a new romantic development. Yoko also appears in an apron doing kitchen assistance and asking where to take refuse, with Hime supplying practical guidance. This establishes actual work participation in V11, beyond V09’s visitor status. It does not, by itself, document a formal permanent re-employment decision or its terms. Kanoko’s encounter with Yoko, who calls her by the ordinary surname Mamiya, supplies a small familiarity cue whose significance becomes clearer only later in the volume. The surname cannot give all nearby workers knowledge of the advisory relationship. [V11/52/i009–012.]

#### 3.1.2 Hime’s reassurance is kind but does not meet Kanoko’s hidden wish

Hime revisits the earlier school conversation and apologizes for pressing Kanoko about Mitsuki. Hime’s correction is specific: Kanoko need not be forced into getting along, and difficulty with Mitsuki does not mean Hime will leave Kanoko. This gives Kanoko a real alternative to compliance. Hime is not abandoning her, demanding that she abandon another friendship, or announcing an informed response to Kanoko’s old abolition project. She still does not know that project or Kanoko’s romantic stake. The scene is consequential precisely because benevolent reassurance can be accurate within Hime’s knowledge and painful within Kanoko’s. [V11/52/i013–014.]

Kanoko later wishes Hime had said she would leave. That thought should not be flattened into the claim that Kanoko no longer wants Hime. It suggests the difficulty of living with a secure positive bond that neither meets the desired romantic condition nor releases her from wanting it. It also complicates the earlier assumption that restoring Hime’s friendship and Sumika’s support would restore Kanoko’s own emotional equilibrium. Her imagined solution is not delivering the relief she expected. The source supplies that contradiction; it does not yet provide a final account of whether her attachment to Sumika is changing romantically. [V11/52/i015–016, i027–028.]

Kanoko declines Hime’s invitation to leave together because she has plans with Sumika. That is a concrete choice, not only internal distress. Sumika offers another opportunity to talk but does not extract an account when Kanoko cannot supply one. The scene thus already contains a better listening procedure than the harsh refusal preceding the dating proposal. However, the return of listening does not resolve the unspoken problem. Sumika can make space without possessing the answer, and Kanoko can choose time with her without knowing how to describe what she wants from it. [V11/52/i014–018.]

#### 3.1.3 Rehearsal becomes a test of what “girlfriend” permits

At Sumika’s home the girls actually rehearse. The script’s separation language and missed cue produce an ordinary work task inside a charged private situation. This is more than V09’s visit whose complete rehearsal was unshown: V11 depicts practice itself. Yet Sumika and Kanoko initially describe the visit differently. Sumika takes pleasure in a girlfriend’s visit; Kanoko emphasizes the rehearsal obligation. Neither description alone cancels the other. The task is real, and the personal meaning is unequal. [V11/52/i017–018.]

When Sumika approaches, Kanoko startles. The first explanation is that Sumika is reaching for her phone. The analysis must not replace that explanation with a completed kiss or assume Kanoko’s anticipation accurately identifies the intended act. Sumika subsequently says that she will not do something Kanoko dislikes. This is a stated constraint, not proof that all relevant preferences have already been communicated. The chapter immediately tests it when Sumika asks about a kiss in minimizing language. [V11/52/i019–022.]

Kanoko’s visible response stops Sumika before the kiss occurs. Sumika abruptly checks herself, apologizes, and rejects her own suggestion that kissing is a light matter. The emphatic self-directed gesture at i023 is Sumika’s interruption of herself, not another character striking her. This matters to agency analysis: the consequential act is a request followed by perception, withdrawal, and apology. The scene does not require Kanoko to complete an eloquent refusal before Sumika recognizes that continuing would be wrong. It supplies a concrete improvement over treating a relationship label as sufficient bodily authorization. [V11/52/i022–024.]

Kanoko then says a kiss would not bother her and that Sumika may do it if she wants. Those words must remain in the record. Erasing them because Kanoko looks unhappy would lose part of the chapter’s argument about offered permission and the meaning of an offer. Treating them as proof of equal desire would lose the other part. Kanoko has begun to understand girlfriend behavior as something she can supply to restore or reward Sumika’s happiness. Sumika does not take the offered kiss; she holds Kanoko and says that she wants her to smile. The refusal to convert the opening into a kiss is an observable choice, not merely a benevolent motive attributed from elsewhere. [V11/52/i024–026.]

Sumika’s subsequent explanation names several things that already make her happy: speaking her love, being Kanoko’s girlfriend, and being close. Kanoko asks whether there is something else Sumika wants to do. The mismatch is not simply that Sumika has no desires and Kanoko is mistaken. Sumika has just expressed a desire, withdrawn it, and chosen a different response. Nor is it simply that Kanoko is willing to do anything. Her questions, startle, silence, and later unease show an unsettled response to the arrangement. The missing element is a shared account of why an offered act would matter to each of them, not a sufficiently long list of permitted acts. [V11/52/i021–028.]

#### 3.1.4 The unsolved feeling after the apparent solution

The chapter ends in Kanoko’s interior accounting. Hime remains her friend, Sumika has become kind and available again, and dating should therefore have put things back in order. But Kanoko cannot feel glad. The final difficulty is not supplied as a narrator’s diagnosis of secret reciprocal love. It is a failure of Kanoko’s instrumental explanation to account for her own experience. She has acted to obtain a state and now finds that living in it feels different from predicting its usefulness. [V11/52/i027–028.]

This is the first clear test of WY10-PR01. The dating title has not aligned expectations. Sumika’s personally meaningful happiness, Kanoko’s restoration project, a bodily proposal, its withdrawal, and Kanoko’s attempt to offer what might make Sumika happy all affect actual choices. The evidence is more than a blush or an awkward pause. It establishes neither a fraudulent non-relationship nor reciprocal romance; it establishes an existing relationship whose meaning is still under negotiation and partly withheld. [V11/52/i017–028; §14.]

### 3.2 Shift 53 — Nene’s “mistake” and the limits of a self-indictment

#### 3.2.1 The present happy Sumika reopens the earlier question

Sumika’s present happiness produces a second observer’s problem. She apologizes to Nene for trying to regulate whom Nene may date and recognizes the impropriety of her earlier interventions. Nene notices the change and connects it to Kanoko. At this point Nene has grounds to recognize Sumika’s love, not complete knowledge of the couple’s actual agreement, its secrecy, or its advisory origin. The later partner-name correction in Shift 54 would make little sense if that knowledge were silently assigned here. [V11/53/i032–036; V11/54/i078–081.]

Nene sees an expression of happiness she could not obtain from Sumika in their own earlier situation. That present comparison opens the retrospective account. The chapter title’s first-person possessive matters: this is Nene’s effort to understand her own error. It is not a neutral tribunal presenting everyone’s complete motives. The reader can take her newly depicted choices seriously without accepting her self-blame as an exhaustive allocation of responsibility. [V11/53/i035–040, i060–066.]

#### 3.2.2 Yoko’s initial theory makes distress look like progress

The earlier offer is now expanded. Yoko proposes that dating Nene will provoke Sumika’s jealousy and overcome the assumption that Sumika is heterosexual. Nene raises the possibility that she may actually fall in love with Yoko. Yoko answers with an approving interest in the kind of girl Nene is. The offer gives Nene both a strategy and a personally gratifying relation through which to carry it out. The account does not make Nene an uninformed participant in the jealousy project, but neither does initial knowledge establish informed agreement about every future feeling or commitment. [V11/53/i037–040.]

Yoko’s theory gains apparent confirmation through workplace interactions. She asks Nene for instruction, the two become close in front of Sumika, and Sumika reacts. Yoko identifies the reaction as jealousy; Nene is pleased by what she takes it to mean. The important epistemic transition is from observing distress to believing the distress demonstrates romantic progress. The source supports Sumika’s possessive or threatened response more securely than Yoko’s claim that the response proves the particular romance Nene wants. A fear of losing a valued sister and friend remains a different explanation. [V11/53/i041–044.]

Nene then asks whether Sumika cannot get along with Yoko. On the surface this resembles a reasonable request for coexistence. Inside the narrated sequence, Nene also wants the conflict to reveal that Sumika cares in the desired way. A reader should not turn every earlier conciliatory word into a calculated lie, but here the retrospective account specifically identifies the pleasure in provoking a reaction. Her public or interpersonal reason and her hoped-for diagnostic result are not identical. [V11/53/i043–046.]

The first kiss with Yoko further alters the situation. Nene’s experience is personally significant, not merely an abstract step in a plan aimed at somebody else. Yoko’s reminder that Nene is her girlfriend gives the immediate interaction its own claim on attention. The narrative therefore does not support the simple chronology “Nene used Yoko until she abruptly became sincere.” Instrumental purpose and actual attraction can overlap before Nene clearly recognizes their changing balance. [V11/53/i045–047.]

#### 3.2.3 An experiment in jealousy neglects the person being tested

The increasing couple presentation and Nene’s attention to Sumika’s gaze make the workplace an audience for a private test. Nene later asks for a schedule change, including a Friday shift, while hoping Sumika will act before losing her. The schedule has real labor consequences; its emotional meaning is not reducible to a neutral staffing preference. At the same time, Nene’s desired inference is not shared with Sumika. Sumika encounters changes in a valued relation without being told the actual question to which Nene wants an answer. [V11/53/i047–051.]

Sumika’s reaction is angry and hurt. She understands Nene as dismissing the months of sisterhood that mattered to her. Her hostile permission to do whatever lovers do is spoken rejection of the situation, not a depiction of all the acts named. The quarrel helps explain her later general prohibition against workplace romance: she experiences love as the force that has made an important person disregard their bond. This explanation is historically substantive, but it remains partial. Nene’s concealed scheme and Sumika’s own way of demanding continuity also shape the injury. [V11/53/i049–051.]

Yoko next sharpens the choice between performed sisterhood and lovers’ desire. Her language treats bodily wanting as the distinguishing proof that the lover relation is real, while the café bond is categorized as play. Nene begins to see that what she wants and what Sumika values are not identical. Nevertheless, the binary Yoko supplies is not the only available account. The work, care, and later tea conversation demonstrate that a performed institutional relation can carry genuine attachment without becoming a romance. Physical desire cannot be used as the universal criterion that disqualifies that attachment. [V11/53/i052–056; V11/55/i091–104.]

Nene imagines a rule-changing victory and two acceptable outcomes. Sumika might stop the change and choose her; failing that, Nene could become Yoko’s sister and lover. What this imagined success tree omits is Sumika’s withdrawal as a friend. Nene can picture two romantic directions while failing to anticipate damage to a third kind of relation she also values. This is a precise limitation in her decision model, not proof that she never cared about Sumika. Indeed, the later distress depends on the relation having mattered. [V11/53/i055–060.]

#### 3.2.4 Genuine love for Yoko does not cancel the earlier strategy

When Sumika does not pursue Nene in the desired way, Nene turns more fully toward Yoko. She asks for affection and comes to love Yoko herself. The source explicitly supports that change. It also shows how relief from guilt and the wish to be desired can become part of the attraction. Nene’s account of Yoko as someone who gave her the escape she wanted has explanatory force without establishing that Yoko’s interests were identical or that all the terms had become mutually clear. [V11/53/i058–063.]

Nene’s counterfactual wish that she had confessed and been rejected earlier is not an additional childhood or early-workplace event. No such rejection occurred in the original scene. It is her later judgment that an unwanted answer could have been less damaging than an indirect method designed to prevent having to hear it. The present volume will eventually give her a real answer, but the analysis must preserve the difference between a wished-for past alternative and a newly enacted conversation. [V11/53/i060–061; V11/55/i107–123.]

The departure account also becomes more specific. Nene reports that Yoko’s side work was discovered at her main job, leading to departure from Liebe. The account is source testimony, not an independently investigated employment rule. Nene expects the relationship to continue outside the café. Yoko treats the end of their sister play and its earlier enjoyment as a reason not to continue in the same way. This recalls V10’s divergence over seriousness: stated liking and enjoyment do not necessarily give Nene the stable commitment she seeks. [V11/53/i062–065; WY10-C07–C11.]

#### 3.2.5 “I hurt Sumika” is important, but it is not the whole truth

Nene concludes that the central story was not simply that she had been hurt; she had hurt Sumika and their friendship. This revises a passive self-image and connects her present wish to support Sumika’s love with her own history. It does not warrant deleting the evidence that Nene was also hurt, that Yoko proposed and reinforced the jealousy strategy, or that Sumika made harmful interventions. Responsibility is not a single slot that becomes unavailable to everyone else once Nene occupies it. [V11/53/i060–066.]

The strongest reading preserves the constructive and risky aspects of the self-account. Nene can now identify choices she made and harms she had not faced. That makes a meaningful apology possible. Yet she may also find it easier to accuse herself completely than to tolerate a history with several real loves, several forms of injury, and no simple innocent position. The subsequent conversation is important because Sumika is allowed to answer that account. The manga does not leave Nene alone with a final monologue that the analyst must mistake for settled mutual understanding. [V11/53/i065–066; V11/55/i107–124.]

**Prospective checkpoint after Shift 53.** Sumika’s acknowledged love and present kindness are established; Nene’s actual knowledge of the new dating arrangement remains incomplete. The expanded old history establishes Nene’s active jealousy strategy, subsequent genuine attachment to Yoko, and regret. It does not establish Sumika’s past romantic reciprocity, an already completed Nene–Sumika repair, or a future safe outcome for Kanoko. [V11/53/i032–066.]


### 3.3 Shift 54 — Advice before the adviser knows the couple

#### 3.3.1 Nene’s first assumption is wrong

Kanoko asks Nene about girls dating girls. Nene initially imagines that the other girl is Hime and that Sumika has been disappointed. That mistaken internal identification is not incidental: it shapes the stakes under which Nene first hears the questions. Her earlier awareness that Sumika loves Kanoko did not establish that Kanoko had agreed to date Sumika. The scene makes advice an information-dependent activity rather than a delivery of context-free wisdom by an experienced adult. [V11/54/i069–072.]

Nene’s initial answer usefully refuses exoticism. Two girls’ dating need not consist of a wholly different set of ordinary activities from other couples’ dating. Kanoko, however, is not asking only for a description of possible dates. She reports that her feelings have not changed since the relationship began and seeks examples of behavior that would make it feel like dating. “Normal couple” activities become possible substitutes for an internal or mutually articulated change she cannot find. The stated problem belongs to Kanoko’s own experience; it is not an analyst inferring nonreciprocity solely from her face. [V11/54/i072–075.]

When Nene asks what Kanoko wants, Kanoko worries that having wants would be selfish. Her partner is happy while she feels that she gives nothing. She wants to make that person happy and consequently seeks an act she can provide. This is not adequately described as indifference to Sumika. It is a genuine concern organized through an exchange model: receiving care creates a deficit that a sufficiently appropriate girlfriend act might repay. The risk is that Kanoko’s own comfort becomes a technical obstacle to balancing the exchange, rather than part of the relationship’s subject matter. [V11/54/i073–077.]

Nene counters that acts alone do not define the relation, that kissing is not compulsory, and that the participants’ feelings matter. Those distinctions are valuable, but her reassurance about mutual love cannot be treated as a finding that this couple has it. She is still operating with incomplete information, and the reader knows that Kanoko’s problem concerns precisely the mismatch between an accepted status and unequal feelings. A sound analysis can preserve the principle while marking its uncertain application. [V11/54/i076–079.]

#### 3.3.2 Naming Sumika changes the consultation

Kanoko finally asks what would make **Sumika** happy. The proper name corrects Nene’s imagined partner and changes her understanding of the entire conversation. This is a direct demonstration of why named-subject attribution matters: “her girlfriend” was not specific enough for the adviser’s actual knowledge. Nene has now learned the dating identity, while the disclosure still does not give her Yoko’s role in producing it. Kanoko asks for secrecy, and Sumika’s arrival does not distribute the preceding words to her. [V11/54/i078–082.]

The Saturday arrangement is concrete: meeting around Kichijoji and then going to Sumika’s home for practice. It is a planned date, not an already completed outing. Nene’s reassurance that Kanoko’s presence already makes Sumika happy is strongly supported by Sumika’s behavior, but it does not settle Kanoko’s unresolved feeling of having nothing to give. The consultation obtains useful constraints against forced couple behavior without supplying the desired formula. That incomplete result helps explain why a later adviser who claims to know the hidden answer can become attractive. [V11/54/i080–083; V11/56/i138–147.]

#### 3.3.3 A work arrangement makes the old pair’s conversation possible

The narrative shifts to festival preparation. Nene and Mai discuss practical food and staffing arrangements; Yoko’s availability is mentioned in that operational context. Sumika then arrives at the closed café, unexpectedly free on the day Nene understood to be reserved for her date. The cancellation has not yet been fully explained. The chapter’s sequencing lets Nene recognize the discrepancy before the reader receives Sumika’s account of the call. [V11/54/i084–086; V11/55/i097–098.]

Mai leaves to obtain supplies and asks Nene and Sumika to remain. The absence is operationally plausible, and the later supplement makes her wish to give the old pair time together explicit. It is not a scene in which Mai forces a confession or supplies the content of the conversation in advance. She creates an opportunity. Nene and Sumika decide what to do with it, and the actual outcome depends on choices and disclosures Mai is not shown witnessing. [V11/54/i087–088; S56.5/i160–161.]

Sumika’s invitation to tea recalls the old elder-sister relation without formally reconstituting it. The two are alone in a familiar work space but temporarily able to occupy it as guests. This change of position matters. In the usual salon performance, their attention is partly organized around customers and the fiction. In the closed salon, the setting can prompt memories and ordinary conversation without an audience requiring a predetermined affectionate result. The familiar institution becomes a resource for an unscripted answer. [V11/54/i088; V11/55/i091–104.]

### 3.4 Shift 55 — An answer that does not give Nene the romance

#### 3.4.1 Shared work is a real history, not a lesser substitute

The empty salon allows Nene to notice what it feels like from the customer’s side. The old pair remembers the difficulty of making the role and service fit together: the uniform and setting had been researched, Nene’s cool presentation took work, and Sumika’s role helped the interaction become legible. Their exchange is not only nostalgia for a romantic possibility that failed. It is recollection of making something together, learning practical habits, and recognizing each other’s contribution. [V11/55/i091–094.]

The source supplies relative duration in the conversation, including the café’s two-year history and a much shorter Hime period. These are character-level chronological statements, not a basis for inventing an external calendar year or exact elapsed days between every earlier volume. What matters locally is that the old pair has a sustained shared history while the newer ensemble has changed it. The work history cannot be erased merely because its original sister pact ended. [V11/55/i092–095.]

Nene congratulates Sumika, and Sumika confirms the new relationship while requesting privacy. Sumika again apologizes for her earlier anti-romance stance. Nene’s support is an actual present act; it does not prove that her own earlier feeling has vanished. The chapter will show both the wish to support Sumika and a brief contrary temptation. Keeping both prevents a sentimental account of effortless self-sacrifice and a cynical account in which every supportive word is false. [V11/55/i094–096, i114–119.]

#### 3.4.2 The canceled date demonstrates restraint and a mistaken explanation

The cancellation is first recounted from Sumika’s side. Kanoko reports an urgent matter but cannot explain it, asks to postpone the date, and responds to Sumika’s disappointment by offering to follow the original plan after all. Sumika retracts the pressure, calls the protest a joke, and permits rescheduling rather than continuing to insist. She says she does not want to constrain Kanoko. These are actual choices after a moment of disappointment, not evidence that the canceled date meant nothing to her. [V11/55/i097–098.]

Sumika suspects the errand may concern Hime. That is her explanation, not what Kanoko disclosed. The last chapter will identify the actual meeting with Yoko. The reader must therefore distinguish Sumika’s generosity under a guessed explanation from fully informed approval of a hotel visit with a concealed adviser. It would be equally wrong to say she prohibited that visit: she does not yet know about it. [V11/55/i098; V11/56/i138–156.]

Sumika explicitly tells Nene that Kanoko still loves Hime. This disclosure corrects any model in which Sumika’s happiness depends on believing that Kanoko has already reciprocated. She knows that the asymmetry persists and values being able to speak and occupy the girlfriend position. At the same time, her restraint and satisfaction do not prove that she has no further wishes. She says that asking for more would be too greedy. The distinction is between having a wish and insisting that Kanoko satisfy it, not between desire and total absence of desire. [V11/55/i099–101.]

Nene sees a contrast with the Sumika who once demanded that romance be stopped. She wants the new couple to succeed and recognizes that her own earlier advice has helped make this change possible. Yet the improvement is not a permanent behavioral certificate. It is a current arrangement that Sumika is choosing to sustain, with costs and limits still incompletely negotiated. Yoko will later reinterpret precisely this restraint as proof of hidden pressure; the reader has stronger direct evidence that Sumika also intends to avoid making Kanoko uncomfortable. [V11/55/i098–102; V11/56/i144–147.]

#### 3.4.3 The tea preserves a bond without restoring its institutional status

Nene notices the aroma of their former sister tea, 雪の結晶, and asks for more. The sensory preference is directly evidenced in ordinary conversation; it does not require inventing a universal favorite drink. The tea’s role is more specific than a sentimental object. It is something the café can still make after the formal pair is discontinued, and something whose appeal Nene can acknowledge without reinstating a romantic claim. [V11/55/i103–104.]

Sumika continues to value the time when they were sisters. Nene initially answers by reminding her that the pact is over and Kanoko is now in that position. The exchange exposes two different meanings of continuity. Formal status has changed; the personal history remains precious. If Nene treats only the present status as real, she risks repeating Yoko’s dismissal of sisterhood as temporary play. If Sumika treats the history as an obligation to restore the old pair, she would deny the changes that have occurred. The ensuing conversation is strongest because it need not choose either extreme. [V11/55/i103–107, i120–124.]

#### 3.4.4 Nene speaks instead of testing; Sumika answers instead of being decoded

Nene asks about Sumika’s orientation using the old premise that Sumika had been assumed heterosexual. Sumika refuses the demand for a settled categorical self-label: she loves Kanoko and does not see why she must first resolve a taxonomy of herself. The source supports a particular present love and an explicit resistance to categorization. It does not authorize an analyst to replace her words with a permanent lesbian, bisexual, or other identity assignment. Nene’s own previously established self-understanding remains a separate subject’s evidence. [V11/55/i105–106.]

Nene then discloses that she loved Sumika and dated Yoko to make Sumika turn toward her. This is new information reaching Sumika, not merely something the reader can retrieve from the preceding chapter. The distinction between reader knowledge and recipient knowledge is the chapter’s causal center. An account that says “Sumika already knew how Nene felt” would eliminate the very event through which the old injury becomes discussable. [V11/55/i107–109.]

The disclosure initially destabilizes rather than soothes. Sumika asks why Nene broke their sister relation; Nene reproaches the absence of the response she wanted. Nene’s statement that Sumika did not stop her must be read against the many earlier objections the manga actually shows. It concerns failure to stop her **in the desired relational sense**—by choosing or holding on to her as she hoped—not a literal claim that Sumika had voiced no prohibition. Preserving that distinction avoids allowing a distressed self-account to erase contrary scenes. [V11/55/i109–113; V11/53/i049–051.]

Sumika says the departure had felt like being discarded. She asks why Nene did not tell her directly. Crucially, Sumika does **not** claim that she would certainly have reciprocated in the past. She says she does not know how she would have answered then, but would have been happy to be told. That is a narrower and more humane correction to Yoko’s jealousy theory: disclosure could have given Sumika a real question to answer, whereas distress was not a reliable substitute for her answer. The counterfactual remains uncertain. [V11/55/i112–113.]

#### 3.4.5 Rejecting a tempting repetition is part of the repair

The new causal account threatens to revive Sumika’s belief that love destroys valuable relations. If Nene’s love rather than Yoko alone caused the rupture, Sumika wonders whether romance itself was the problem after all. For an instant Nene imagines that a renewed anti-romance stance might undo Sumika’s new relationship and return the old sister bond. The source depicts the temptation and its rejection. It is not a plan Nene executes or an invisible motive that cancels all subsequent support. [V11/55/i114–117.]

Nene instead distinguishes her selfish manipulation and avoidance from the existence of love. She accepts responsibility for provoking jealousy, hiding the actual request, and failing to face what Sumika might say. This does not make every harm exclusively Nene’s, but it gives her apology concrete acts rather than a vague verdict that she is a bad person. She is no longer asking Sumika’s distress to do the work of an affirmative answer. [V11/55/i116–119.]

The apology also permits Sumika to test whether their former relationship mattered to Nene. Her embrace receives an answer that the jealousy strategy had obscured: the bond was precious, even while Nene wanted a different kind of relation. Physical comfort here belongs to this scene’s actual uptake. It is not a general lesson that an embrace resolves every refusal or authorizes contact for every other pair. [V11/55/i119–123.]

#### 3.4.6 A local ending to love and an altered continuing friendship

Sumika thanks Nene for loving her but says she loves someone else now and cannot return that love. Nene accepts the answer and thanks Sumika for ending her love. The combination is stronger than the earlier unresolved departure from Yoko: an actual recipient hears the old declaration, gives a definite present answer, and receives an acknowledgment of that answer. It is still bounded. Nene’s phrase supplies her experienced sense of closure; no narrator guarantees that every future ache, memory, or ambivalence has disappeared forever. [V11/55/i120–123.]

Their resumed teasing supplies immediate behavioral follow-through. Sumika tries to interpret Nene’s choice to remain at the café as choosing her over Yoko. Nene corrects the simplification: she chose Liebe, and Sumika is included in that choice. This is not a repudiation of Sumika’s importance. It is an account spacious enough to include work, place, friendship, and other people without making them merely evidence of a won romantic competition. [V11/55/i124.]

The same page qualifies the apparent ease. Sumika jokes about a friend’s kiss, and Nene cautions her about the lack of tact in such teasing. The repaired relation can contain correction; it does not become one in which every joke is harmless. Nene nevertheless voices support for the new couple. The closing phone notification then reports a photograph from Yoko. At this point the image itself and its recipient’s response are not supplied. The intrusion into the scene’s closure sets up the last chapter without undoing the repair that actually occurred. [V11/55/i124.]

**Prospective checkpoint after Shift 55.** Nene has disclosed the old love and jealousy scheme to Sumika, received a nonreciprocal present answer, and participated in a local repair. Sumika has not declared past reciprocal love. Kanoko’s dating identity and continued love for Hime are now known to Nene, but the canceled date’s actual destination and Yoko’s advice remain outside Sumika’s shown knowledge. The photograph notification is new suspense, not yet an observed rescue or a verified received account of danger. [V11/55/i097–124.]


### 3.5 Shift 56 — “For her sake” becomes somebody else’s procedure

#### 3.5.1 The adviser is identified without rewriting V10

The last chapter returns to Kanoko’s original explanation of the problem. She loves Hime and needs Sumika’s valuable listening and help. Preparing for the scheduled date, she cannot find a clothing choice that would make a meaningful difference because Sumika praises her so readily. An object—the crumpled contact slip—then opens the earlier consultation history. This does not depict a new first contact occurring after the tea scene. It supplies what the reader had not known about the advice preceding the V10 dating proposal. [V11/56/i127–134.]

Yoko is now directly identified as the adviser. The narrative shows the earlier call, the face-to-face discussion, her diagnosis of Sumika’s jealousy, and her proposed remedy: Kanoko should date Sumika so that Sumika’s feelings will be untangled and the former kindness restored. Kanoko explicitly says that she does not like Sumika in that romantic sense. Yoko reframes the issue as whether Kanoko wants to help someone who is suffering. The move is consequential because it turns a difference in desire into an apparent opportunity to perform care. [V11/56/i131–134.]

The scene confirms part of Kanoko’s earlier motive and the continuity of the Goeidō contact route, satisfying the relevant original forecast under its allowance for a later explanation. It does not make Yoko’s identity retrospectively visible in V10. The V10 anonymous-caller freeze remains correct for that source boundary. V11 owns the newly supplied identity and instructions. It likewise does not show Sumika hearing those instructions or agreeing that dating should function as a cure administered through another person’s advice. [V11/56/i132–137; WY10-C21; WY9-PR01.]

The replay of the proposal also adds Kanoko’s small painful response to Sumika’s sincere declaration. The feeling is real evidence of disturbance, but it is not uniquely named. Pity, guilt, attachment, desire to restore stability, and an emerging feeling she does not understand are not yet fully separated. The source instead establishes a narrower contradiction: she obtains the restored listening she wanted and cannot simply experience the result as an uncomplicated success. This strengthens the opening chapter’s finding without converting uncertainty into a hidden romantic confession. [V11/56/i135–137; V11/52/i027–028.]

#### 3.5.2 A new consultation converts restraint into a problem requiring intervention

A current message from Gotō Yōko asks how the girlfriend arrangement is going. Kanoko calls again. Yoko presents herself as an available matchmaker, and Kanoko asks how to make Sumika happy. This is an actual renewed initiative, not merely continued possession of the old contact information. The source thus gives both historical explanation and present repetition of Kanoko’s use of the advisory route. [V11/56/i138–140.]

Yoko introduces a time constraint: she says she will travel soon and not return until the following month. The trip is a reported plan, not independently depicted travel. In the immediate conversation it makes waiting seem costly. Kanoko has a date with Sumika, but Yoko urges a meeting now and tells her to make sure Sumika accepts the other errand first. The later cancellation therefore cannot be described as Sumika being bypassed in every respect; she is asked about rescheduling. What is withheld is the material identity, purpose, and eventual venue of the replacement meeting. [V11/56/i140–142.]

The cancellation call has already been shown from Sumika’s side. Its replay and explanation do not create a second canceled date. Kanoko now treats Sumika’s willingness to release the date as a reason to doubt whether she is wanted. The same response Sumika offered to avoid constraining her becomes evidence that something is missing. This is an especially clear example of a good intention failing to communicate itself: Sumika can genuinely restrain a demand, and Kanoko can genuinely feel disappointed by the restraint. Neither fact establishes Yoko’s claim to know the correct remedy. [V11/55/i097–098; V11/56/i142–144.]

Yoko recasts the restraint as concealed desire. Sumika supposedly wants ordinary lovers’ activities but is frightened of losing Kanoko, and may still hate romance. Kanoko’s recollections of the earlier kiss request and withdrawal can then be fitted to that account. The inference has a plausible starting point—Sumika did want a kiss—but overreaches when it makes the withdrawal merely an obstacle to be circumvented. The reader has directly seen Sumika stop, apologize, and decline Kanoko’s subsequent offer. Her actual decision must not be replaced by the adviser’s explanation of what she “really” wants. [V11/56/i143–147; V11/52/i021–026.]

Yoko also affirms that Kanoko need not abandon Hime and that the two girls agreed to date. Both assertions draw on real aspects of the arrangement. The illegitimate expansion is from those facts to the suggestion that there is therefore no significant problem in the next proposed intervention. Consent to dating does not resolve the meanings of kissing, secrecy, a changed venue, or the use of photographs. A correct partial diagnosis is not authority over all the decisions that follow. [V11/56/i146–148.]

#### 3.5.3 Relocation, the choker, and the narrowing of an objection

Yoko proposes changing places so she can explain how Kanoko might help Sumika. The exterior and reception establish a hotel. While Kanoko waits, Yoko retrieves what she says Nene left on their previous visit: a choker. She asks Kanoko to return it. Kanoko objects that Yoko should do that herself; Yoko says Nene has rejected further meetings and permits Kanoko to dispose of it if returning it is impossible. These are a displayed object, attributed history, a request, and a proposed alternative. The source does not show an actual return to Nene or disposal. It also does not show a visit back to Café Liebe at this point. [V11/56/i148–150.]

The object joins the new consultation to an earlier relationship whose private circumstances Kanoko does not fully know. It is not just an ominous prop added by the analyst. Kanoko has now been asked to carry a material trace between Yoko and Nene while trying to solve her own relation with Sumika. This places her in another intermediary position. The source does not establish that Nene authorized Yoko to involve her, nor that Kanoko understands the complete significance of the previous hotel encounter. [V11/56/i149–151; V10 §§3.2–3.3.]

When Yoko produces the room card and proposes continuing upstairs, Kanoko asks for another place. Her objection is actual and should not disappear from a later account of entering willingly. Yoko initially treats it as unnecessary reserve, then invokes what Sumika would think about the concealed meeting. The terms shift from Kanoko’s own preference about a place to the supposed jealousy of a third person. A reader who looks only at the final assent would miss how the earlier objection is recoded. [V11/56/i151–153.]

Kanoko realizes that Sumika might be angry about the secret contact. Yoko says that is jealousy and suggests that Kanoko is being well managed. Kanoko tries to distinguish an angry response from jealousy; Yoko collapses the distinction again. She then imagines the person Kanoko loves alone with someone else and predicts the possessive desire not to lose that person. The manga places recalled Hime-related imagery inside Kanoko’s response. This is persuasion working through a personally salient example, not a neutral general lesson whose truth the narrator has certified. [V11/56/i152–155.]

#### 3.5.4 The final assent is specific, influenced, and dangerous to overread

Kanoko insists that jealousy changes nothing and is not important. When Yoko again offers to go elsewhere, Kanoko says the present place is fine. The sequence supplies real assent to the venue, but not a simple history in which the venue was freely preferred from the beginning and every material issue was known. Yoko’s framing has made insisting on another place appear to concede the very possessiveness Kanoko wants to deny. Kanoko can therefore act to preserve a self-description while discounting her earlier discomfort. [V11/56/i153–156.]

This is not a reason to deny Kanoko all decision-making capacity. She asks questions, objects, tries to distinguish concepts, and changes her expressed choice. It is a reason to analyze the available choices together with the meanings that have been attached to them. Yoko’s offers of alternatives remain in the source, but so does the pressure that makes one alternative feel like a defeat. The resulting permission concerns continuing the conversation in this room. It cannot authorize an unshown physical act, image distribution, or any future outcome merely because Kanoko crossed the threshold. [V11/56/i151–156.]

Inside, Kanoko reassures herself that nothing bad is happening and that she need not tell Sumika. Yoko holds the phone, says she is only making contact, and sends a photograph of Kanoko in the hotel room with messages asking the recipient to tell Sumika that her younger sister is in trouble. No request for Kanoko’s permission to make or transmit this particular image is depicted. Yoko thus combines encouraging secrecy from Sumika with creating a route by which Sumika may be alerted. That combination is observable; its entire future purpose is not. [V11/56/i156.]

The earlier notification at i124 and this outgoing photograph are strongly linked by the chapter arrangement. However, the notification does not name the phone’s owner, and the outgoing screen does not name the recipient. ### 9.1 Two productive interruptions and one displaced objection

Sumika’s interruption of the kiss request and Nene’s interruption of her own opportunistic thought are different kinds of restraint. The first prevents an unwanted or insufficiently welcome physical act from being pursued. The second prevents a new disclosure from being used to destroy another relationship for personal gain. Neither requires pretending the original desire never existed. Both become meaningful because a choice changes after a perceived problem. [AG04/AG16.]

Kanoko’s hotel objection follows another path. It is not directly met as an account of her own preference; it is reinterpreted through what Sumika would supposedly feel and what Kanoko should be willing to say about jealousy. Kanoko eventually changes the choice, but the source preserves the mechanism by which the objection was displaced. This is why a record of the final “yes” alone would be incomplete, while a record saying no choice was made would also be false. [AG22–AG23.]

### 9.2 Repair is evaluated by reception and follow-through

Nene’s apology becomes a local repair through Sumika’s own account, an explicit present answer, accepted comfort and renewed ordinary talk. The earlier conversation with Yoko had not supplied the same convergence. Yet even this stronger repair has a time boundary: the text does not certify all future conduct. Nene’s later warning about Sumika’s teasing is useful evidence that continued friendship need not mean abolishing the possibility of correction. [AG14–AG18.]

The opening Sumika–Kanoko conversation also contains a real correction, but its repair is narrower. Sumika does not kiss Kanoko, apologizes and offers closeness without that demand. Kanoko’s underlying unease nevertheless continues and becomes a reason to consult elsewhere. An honored local limit can therefore coexist with an unaddressed relational problem. The source does not require choosing between “Sumika did nothing good” and “the relationship is now safe and clear in every respect.” [AG03–AG05/AG19–AG24.]

**Sumika’s restraint is real, even though the couple remains unsettled.** The opening request contains an inappropriate minimization, but the subsequent withdrawal and refusal to take the offered kiss are actual choices. Kanoko’s unease does not retroactively turn them into secret coercive demands. Conversely, one respected cue does not certify every future act. [C03/C10/C21.]

Those facts complicate both “she secretly already loves Sumika” and “she feels nothing and merely lies.” Her earlier denial and current unchanged-feeling report remain central; her future development remains open. [C01/C04/C19–C20.]

**Nene’s agency does not cancel her hurt.** The new account establishes knowingly manipulative choices and genuine subsequent love. It also preserves Yoko’s participation and the mismatch that injured Nene. The most self-accusing speech must remain attributed rather than become the analyst’s sole moral verdict. [C07–C08/C14–C17.]

**The tea repair is not merely consolation because romance fails.** It changes shared knowledge, obtains an actual answer, acknowledges specific wrongdoing, and permits renewed ordinary interaction. Its value is not conditional on the old pair becoming lovers. Nor does Nene’s gratitude for an ending prove permanent emotional immunity. [C12/C15–C18.]

**Yoko’s advice is not harmless simply because some of it is accurate or alternatives are offered.** She correctly identifies relevant desire and secures scheduling assent, but treats restraint as a problem, redefines an actual venue objection, and sends a selected image while Kanoko plans secrecy. These are observable manipulative mechanisms. Their presence does not supply a complete future intention or an unshown physical act. [C19–C24.]

**The endpoint’s danger must remain distinct from a supplied outcome.** The message, photograph, location, concealment and recent persuasion warrant concern. They do not establish who reads the message, what anyone does next, or what subsequently happens in the room. A source-grounded account must be able to stop at an alarming incomplete state.
