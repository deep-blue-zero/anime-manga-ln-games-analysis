## 1. The volume’s governing problem

Volume 14 asks whether a love that cannot be returned in the same form can nevertheless be received, valued, and protected. Its answer is not that Kanoko has finally discovered romantic love for Sumika. Kanoko continues to love Hime, distinguishes that feeling from her attachment to Sumika, and does not obtain an agreement to resume dating. The change is that she can stop treating Sumika’s love as either a debt to repay with girlfriend-like behavior or a problem whose disappearance would restore the old arrangement. She wants this particular person to remain able to feel, speak, and be happy. Sumika, in turn, begins to accept that receiving this care need not be identical to obtaining the romantic response she wants. [V14/67/i013–028; V14/69/i079–098; V14/71/i135–156.]

The movement requires the failure of an attractive account of separation. Sumika initially wants to end her love beautifully, occupying the position of a generous elder sister who releases the younger one. That explanation protects a real right—her right not to keep dating under conditions she cannot bear—but it also hides jealousy, shame, and the fear that acknowledging these feelings will disqualify her from being Kanoko’s sister at all. The staged reading gives that hidden difficulty a public form. Sumika can help Kanoko recover a missed cue, yet cannot complete the emotional goodbye without crying. Her professional ability and private distress are simultaneous, not mutually exclusive assessments of the same person. [V14/67/i022–024; V14/68/i053–061; V14/69/i072–086.]

Kanoko also has to revise her method. Her first proposal still makes her own compliance the presumed solution to Sumika’s unhappiness. Sumika refuses both the convenient dating arrangement and the offered bodily substitute. Later, Kanoko distinguishes valuing the love from becoming a girlfriend, and asks Sumika not to injure herself on Kanoko’s behalf. That is not a demand that Sumika remain romantically available forever. Its stated horizon is that feeling may continue until it genuinely changes, rather than being forcibly ended to satisfy a script. The new sister relation is therefore not simply the old confidant arrangement restored intact. It includes Sumika’s disclosed personal stake and Kanoko’s chosen responsibility to care about it. [V14/67/i014–021; V14/69/i080–096.]

Yoko provides the volume’s most consequential counterposition. She treats romantic and physical motives as the truth beneath supposedly different attachments, then presents the reactions she has provoked as confirmation. Mai confronts both the harm and that interpretive certainty. The subsequent memory of Yoko’s unnamed senior gives her certainty a possible personal history; it does not turn it into a universal law or excuse what she did to Kanoko. Nene can acknowledge that Yoko once helped her while still supporting the present restriction and blocking contact. Explanation, remembered benefit, accountability, and continued access remain separable. [V14/70/i103–126.]

The ending makes these distinctions ordinary enough to live with, without claiming that they have become effortless. The party contains awkward care, food, jokes, embarrassment, intrusive contact, confidentiality, and a newly specific declaration of personal importance. Kanoko promises Hime a future disclosure but does not make it. Sumika is allowed to keep an unreturned love, but not to treat every intimate act as available. The later photograph board gives that arrangement a durable image: the old signed portrait remains, and a new shared photograph joins it. The object that once helped Sumika imagine a privileged place can now record a place Kanoko has actually given her, without proving that the two feel the same kind of love. [V14/71/i129–156; compare the preserved V13/66.5 photograph history.]

## 3. Chapter-by-chapter close reading

### 3.1 Shift 67 — 姉であるために: an elder sister’s beautiful ending

#### 3.1.1 Obtaining a conversation is already a choice

The opening realizes the decision that V13 left prospective. Kanoko asks for time alone with Sumika before the reading, and Mai makes room for it. This is not a second rooftop discussion, an imagined reconciliation, or an automatic continuation of the old confidant arrangement. Kanoko has selected an audience and a practical opportunity. Sumika initially frames the need to meet through unfinished rehearsal, but the private conversation soon exceeds that professional rationale. The surrounding preparations matter: the café cannot suspend its collective obligations indefinitely, yet those obligations do not prevent a short, consequential private exchange. [V14/67/i001, i005–006.]

Sumika first expects anger about what she told Hime. Her apology concerns the disclosure of dating and separation, not a confession that she has disclosed every part of Kanoko’s secret. Kanoko’s answer preserves the difference: Hime knows there was a concealed relationship, but is not shown to know whom Kanoko loves. The scene thus begins with a repair attempt about a real breach whose scope is limited. Neither Sumika’s embarrassment nor Kanoko’s willingness to proceed should be flattened into complete forgiveness of all earlier pressure. What changes is that the conversation can move to its intended subject. [i006–008.]

The two are still tempted to make Hime the organizing problem. Sumika believes a confession need not end the friendship, while Kanoko draws on the rooftop experience to renew her commitment to Hime. Yet Kanoko separates that future from the present meeting. She will tell Hime someday; she is not announcing that it has already happened, naming a date, or requesting Sumika to arrange it immediately. The distinction is analytically important because much of the prior conflict concerned treating another person’s next disclosure as the solution to one’s own pain. Here Kanoko insists that there is also a Sumika–Kanoko question to answer. [i009–012.]

#### 3.1.2 The first proposed solution still makes compliance do too much

Kanoko asks to date again. She has accepted movement toward Hime and wants to return what Sumika has given her, so the proposal can appear to her as a way of repairing both problems at once. Sumika hears a different arrangement: she would occupy a convenient or fallback position while Kanoko’s desired romantic future remains elsewhere. Her questions about what happens after Hime accepts or refuses are not merely jealous obstruction. They expose how little of the proposed relationship’s future the two have actually agreed. A sincere intention to make Sumika happy does not settle what Sumika can bear. [i013–017.]

Sumika’s refusal is specific. It is a refusal to resume this dating arrangement, not a renewed demand that Kanoko’s love for Hime become forbidden. That difference should survive the scene’s harshness. Sumika is entitled not to be a girlfriend on terms that distress her. The passage does not authorize the analyst to turn her particular refusal into a universal rule that every asymmetrical relationship must end, or that Kanoko’s wish is wholly empty. Kanoko’s concern is real; her initial method assumes that availability and repayment will be enough. The inadequacy lies in the fit between the offered act and the person receiving it. [i014–017.]

Kanoko’s inward recognition that the proposal is unfair is also a source-level self-assessment, not an excuse to replace her with an abstract “selfish girl.” The source shows an individual trying to use a familiar solution—give what another wants, keep the relationship—and confronting the fact that the solution misdescribes the other person’s desire. She does not yet know an adequate alternative. This temporary lack of a method helps explain why the chapter ends with continued thought rather than either a successful reunion or a final dismissal of Sumika’s value. [i017, i025–028.]

#### 3.1.3 A withheld kiss is an action, not a lack of evidence

Sumika tests the proposal through the possibility of bodily intimacy. Kanoko answers in terms of what Sumika wants. The subsequent approach makes the difference between that answer and relaxed, personally desired participation visible. Kanoko closes her eyes tensely; Sumika holds her face, then releases her and does not complete the kiss. The analytically decisive event is not simply that a kiss fails to occur. Sumika recognizes a form of compliance that would make the act feel forced and chooses not to take it. [i018–021.]

This restraint should not sanitize the entire procedure. Sumika has created a pressured test during an already difficult negotiation. Kanoko is trying to preserve the relationship, and the provocative question makes that stake bodily immediate. The release is a consequential correction within the scene, not proof that the approach was a perfectly neutral inquiry. Conversely, describing only the pressure would erase the refusal that distinguishes Sumika’s actual behavior from Yoko’s later-criticized insistence that available bodily acts reveal the truth of love. Sumika does not treat Kanoko’s conditional verbal compliance as an unlimited entitlement. [i018–021; compare V14/70/i104–113.]

The episode also sets up a pattern the volume will complicate rather than mechanically repeat. Kanoko later initiates a kiss during the reading, and later still offers kisses within the renewed sister arrangement. Each has its own initiator, context, response, and evidentiary status. The initial withheld kiss cannot become advance permission for the later initiated one; the later positive personal meaning cannot retroactively erase the initial restraint. Nor does Sumika’s continuing attraction mean that any one of these moments settles every future act. [i021; V14/68/i060–062; V14/69/i097–098.]

#### 3.1.4 The beautiful ending is a proposed self-image

Sumika now describes a love that could not have reached its desired outcome and presents sisterhood as the form through which she can continue helping Kanoko. Her request that the love be allowed a beautiful ending carries generosity, pride, and a wish for control over how she is seen. She wants to be the person who can step aside without burdening the younger sister. The performance of that position is not identical to café fiction: this is a private account of actual feelings. Nevertheless, it is a composed account, selecting which feelings will be made available to its recipient. [i022–024.]

At this point in the sequential reading, it would be premature to say that every word is a conscious lie. Sumika is trying to inhabit the role she describes, and the relationship really is no longer a mutually accepted dating arrangement. Later in this same volume, she explicitly calls the beautiful-ending explanation a lie and supplies the jealousy and self-blame it omitted. The correct retrospective update is therefore attributable: the later confession revises the completeness of this earlier self-presentation. It does not make the right to end dating disappear or invalidate every act of care that preceded it. [i022–025; V14/69/i084–086.]

Kanoko’s apology closes the immediate exchange, but not her inquiry. When Hime asks how things went, Kanoko cannot give a confident settled answer. Hime’s invitation to speak later, only within what Kanoko can disclose, leaves an available audience without converting the invitation into a demand for the hidden love target. Kanoko’s remaining dissatisfaction is not simply refusal to accept “no” to dating. It is a question about what she wants from Sumika that the dating proposal failed to express. The rest of the volume will answer that question more precisely than the first conversation could. [i025–028.]

### 3.2 Shift 68 — 妹であるために: work, attention, and an unplanned intervention

#### 3.2.1 Kanoko’s attention expands beyond what Sumika does for her

The title’s younger-sister emphasis does not reduce Kanoko to passive dependence. Her memory of Sumika’s earlier kindness is followed by an explicit distinction: she likes Sumika, but the feeling is not the romantic one directed at Hime. The volume does not conceal that distinction merely to make a later kiss appear to announce an uncomplicated romantic conversion. Kanoko is searching for a way to return care under unequal feelings. [i029–033.]

The event preparations give that search a broader object. Photographs, signatures, curtains, collective timing, and the staff’s playful recitation turn Liebe into a shared undertaking. Sumika’s importance is not exhausted by listening to Kanoko’s troubles. She helps make this group and this setting work. Kanoko watches her participate in that work and recognizes the place Sumika has been protecting. The recognition matters because it gives Kanoko something to value that is neither romantic repayment nor a service purchased for Hime-related problems. She can care about Sumika’s commitments and join them. [i031–038.]

This is not a declaration that Kanoko has become independent of Hime in every domain. Her current thought remains strongly relationship-conditioned. What changes is the range of purposes capable of organizing her attention. The source gives an actual task in which she wants to contribute to Sumika’s valued environment. Later party dialogue will make the personal preference still more explicit: Kanoko would attend because Sumika is there, even without Hime. The work scene supplies an earlier, less verbal instance of the same widening, without making it a complete developmental endpoint. [i035–038; V14/71/i134–135.]

#### 3.2.2 The play becomes actual, but its characters remain fictional

The reserved audience arrives and the staged reading begins. The long-prepared story of Olivia and Erna now has a real performance, with Mai’s narration and the staff’s roles distributed across the presentation. Erna’s marriage-related departure and the elder sister’s refusal to classify her attachment as romance are spoken inside that fiction. Their resemblance to the staff’s private situation is important, but resemblance does not collapse their levels of reality. A line voiced by Kanoko as Erna is not automatically a new confession to Sumika; a line voiced by Sumika as Olivia is not a complete report of her current motives. [i039–044.]

Kanoko actively interprets the resemblance. She sees an elder sister apparently acting against the younger one’s immediate wishes for the younger one’s sake, and connects that to Sumika’s decision. This is a meaningful reading by Kanoko, but it is not the manga narrator certifying that Sumika’s breakup was wholly selfless or that the script has provided the correct outcome for both women. The next chapter exposes precisely what that account omits. The fictional story supplies a language through which Kanoko can notice her relation; it cannot decide the relation for her. [i047–052; V14/69/i072–086.]

The backstage conversation involving Haruko and Nene provides a second way of seeing the event. Haruko’s comparison to a student presentation and Nene’s reminder of the performers’ actual youth complicate the café’s accomplished surface. The framing does not justify treating these comments as lines delivered to the audience during the performance. They are a distinct backstage insert in the sequence. Their function is to allow competence and the need for support to coexist: being a convincing elder sister in a fictional school does not mean being an adult who can bear every private demand without assistance. [i045–046.]

#### 3.2.3 A missed cue reveals both capability and dependence

Kanoko’s growing appreciation becomes a practical interruption. While reconsidering Sumika’s earlier care, she loses her place. The missed cue is not evidence that she never learned the role or that her public capacity has collapsed. It is a specific attentional lapse with an identifiable context. Sumika points her back to the script and helps restore the exchange without abandoning the public fiction. The audience receives an effective performance; Kanoko receives concrete help. Both descriptions are true, but they identify different recipients and different kinds of benefit. [i053–055.]

This scene is a particularly strong instance of the project’s distinction between performance and falsity. Sumika’s in-character recovery is not “fake help” because it is performed. It works partly because it is performed: the role supplies a socially legible way to cover the interruption and continue. Nor does it certify a fully repaired private relationship. The missed cue is addressed; the breakup question is not. The volume’s later emotional collapse should not be used to remove this earlier evidence of Sumika’s skill, attentiveness, and ability to protect a colleague’s contribution. [i053–056.]

Kanoko’s inward affirmation of liking Sumika remains nonromantic in the terms she has supplied. It becomes more particular rather than changing category by implication. She notices someone who works, covers mistakes, protects a place, and worries about others while neglecting her own needs. This specificity is the basis for a personal relationship that cannot be reduced to “another person who will listen about Hime.” It does not yet tell Kanoko how to make that recognition available to Sumika. [i045–055.]

#### 3.2.4 Sumika’s tears interrupt both script and private pose

As the goodbye becomes imminent, Sumika cries. The role that previously helped her conceal a practical error no longer contains the private stakes of the scene. Kanoko’s spoken farewell as Erna makes the end of Sumika’s own love feel actual; Sumika’s later account will state this link. At the moment of the performance, the reader sees tears, disrupted delivery, and concern from those nearby. The cause should be confirmed through the later account rather than inferred solely from an expressive face. [i056–058; V14/69/i072–073.]

The darkness at i059 changes visibility as well as atmosphere. The pages do not require an invented agent for the lighting change. What matters to the subsequent information state is that the audience’s access to the final exchange is interrupted. Kanoko approaches and initiates a kiss. The sequence establishes the act and Sumika’s surprise; it supplies no depicted advance request or affirmative advance assent. A later account of why the kiss mattered should not replace that act-level record. [i059–062.]

Kanoko’s intervention is not adequately described as either a conventional love confession or a meaningless stunt. Her surrounding thought concerns Sumika’s self-neglect and her own newly clarified wish to respond. The later school supplement explicitly distinguishes this kiss from the romantic liking directed at Hime. At the same time, the difference in category does not erase its personal significance to Sumika. The scene’s force lies partly in refusing the assumption that an intimate gesture must either have a single romantic meaning or no meaningful relation to the people involved. [i060–062; S71.5/i158–161.]

The act also cannot solve the chapter by itself. Sumika still needs to say what the beautiful-ending account concealed, and Kanoko still needs to separate caring for that love from offering girlfriend-like repayment. The kiss interrupts a failed goodbye and makes the private question impossible to close with professional composure. It is a turning point, not a substitute for the dialogue that follows. [V14/69/i079–098.]

### 3.3 Shift 69 — 姉妹であるために: making unequal care reciprocal

#### 3.3.1 The audience’s successful event is not everyone’s knowledge

The customers’ departure supplies evidence that the reading was received as a successful and emotionally affecting event. They discuss Sumika’s tears and the lighting interruption through the fictional school story. This is not proof that all guests witnessed the kiss or understood the breakup. The reader has access to private motives that the audience’s comments do not establish. Indeed, the different reception is one of the scene’s achievements: work can succeed publicly while its participants are undergoing a private crisis that is only partly visible. [i064–066.]

Kanoko then changes a concrete plan. Rather than leave with Hime, she stays to speak with Sumika. Hime accepts the change and notices that Kanoko looks more resolved. Her response is not a romantic surrender of Kanoko to a rival; it is an actual allowance for another valued relationship to claim time. Hime continues to offer a future hearing without requiring all the information now. The earlier rooftop discussion gains behavioral follow-through, although Kanoko’s secret and Hime’s own sensitivity to distance remain. [i067–068.]

#### 3.3.2 Nene makes the repaired friendship usable

Nene finds Sumika withdrawn from the others and ashamed of the unfinished emotional business. Her offer is practical and particular: she can stay, listen, and be relied upon. This matters in the longitudinal account of their repaired relation. Nene does not need to recover a romantic position or the old sister pact in order to be important to Sumika now. Nor is she merely a neutral adviser with no history. Her earlier experience gives force to her warning against leaving the other person with a composed, incomplete account. [i069–076.]

Nene initially lacks the latest relationship information. She refers to Kanoko as Sumika’s girlfriend and learns that they have broken up. This small correction should remain visible because it prevents the analyst from treating every coworker as already briefed on the entire private sequence. Nene’s subsequent advice is informed by Sumika’s new disclosure, not by an implausible omniscient understanding from the start. [i071–073.]

Sumika now supplies the cause of her tears: the fictional farewell made her love’s ending feel final. She fears that another conversation will expose more grief and spoil the beautiful separation. Nene’s response does not require Sumika to resume dating. It requires her not to make hidden pain someone else’s guessing problem. Kanoko cannot know what would make Sumika happy if Sumika never says it. Nene’s remembered regret gives this advice a personal cost; she is recommending a procedure different from the jealousy experiment and silent withdrawal that damaged her own earlier relationship. [i072–076; preserved V11 tea-scene findings.]

The advice is successful in a bounded sense. Sumika gets up and goes to the conversation. It does not make Nene the author of everything that follows, remove Sumika’s agency, or certify Nene’s permanent freedom from her own regrets. What it demonstrates is that the accepted repair has become a usable route for care rather than only a concluding statement in an earlier volume. [i076–079.]

#### 3.3.3 Kanoko’s new proposal is not resumed dating

At the stairs, Sumika first reiterates that the breakup stands. Kanoko does not answer by producing a new romantic confession or accepting Sumika as a substitute for Hime. She says that making Sumika cry cannot be the right way to settle things, then explains that giving value back to Sumika’s love need not mean dating. The first part is Kanoko’s contestable judgment: pain alone cannot prove every breakup wrong. The second is the substantive contribution that changes the available options. She is no longer asking Sumika to accept the same dating bargain with a kinder label. [i079–082.]

Kanoko asks Sumika not to make loving her a reason to damage or restrict herself. She wants the feeling to be allowed to continue until it changes in its own time. The request differs from both compulsory romance and compulsory emotional extinction. It acknowledges Sumika as a person with a feeling that Kanoko cannot return but can still care about. It also makes Kanoko’s own attachment speakable: Sumika is not disposable once she stops functioning as the uncomplicated listener Kanoko previously wanted. [i081–083.]

This is the strongest evidence for the inherited V13 forecast, because Kanoko’s reasoned contribution materially alters the conversation. The result is not simply that she repeats “I want you to stay.” She introduces a distinction Sumika’s beautiful-ending account could not accommodate: love may be valued without being converted into dating, and sisterhood may include the lover’s actual pain rather than require it to vanish. The argument is still local and reciprocal. It does not bind Sumika to remain available regardless of future cost. [i080–096.]

#### 3.3.4 Sumika’s confession changes the information, not the past acts

Sumika responds by making her own less flattering motives explicit. She names jealousy of Hime, the harsh refusal of Kanoko’s consultation, the happiness of dating, and the failure to notice how vulnerable Kanoko remained to Yoko’s intervention. She fears that these failures disqualify her from being a sister. Her earlier beautiful-ending explanation is now explicitly described as a lie. The scene does not merely replace one abstract lesson with another; it supplies facts about Sumika’s self-understanding that Kanoko previously lacked. [i084–086.]

The self-indictment must not be treated as an exhaustive causal judgment. Sumika’s refusal and absorption in the new relationship contributed to an unsafe support situation, but Yoko chose her own deceptive and coercive acts. Sumika’s claim that she failed to protect Kanoko does not mean she knowingly watched the hotel harm or caused every later decision. Likewise, acknowledging jealousy does not make all earlier kindness covert manipulation. The source shows those motives and acts coexisting. The revision concerns completeness and accountability, not a reversal from “good person” to “bad person.” [i084–089; preserved V11–V12 source account.]

Kanoko’s answer is equally specific. She calls what Yoko did terrible and rejects the rule Yoko tried to impose about romance and bodily contact. Yet she says that something precious was not taken from her, because Sumika’s later care mattered. This is Kanoko’s assessment of what survived, not a finding that no harm occurred, that she has recovered completely, or that arriving later was equivalent to preventing the assault. Sumika initially hears protection in the prevention sense and objects that she was too late. Kanoko makes a different form of protection intelligible: helping her retain a relation and a value that Yoko’s interpretation had tried to destroy. [i087–089.]

This answer has consequences for the volume’s ethical structure without becoming a general therapeutic formula. Kanoko is not required to interpret the event through maximal damage in order for the harm to count. Nor may the analyst use her chosen meaning to minimize the depicted violations. The source permits a person to say that she was hurt and that something important remains hers. The two statements are not contradictory. [i087–090.]

#### 3.3.5 Mutual protection does not require identical love

Kanoko now wants to protect Sumika’s love as well as receive its kindness. The offered hand and accepted embrace give that statement a bodily form different from both the earlier pressured approach and the unannounced stage kiss. The sequence depicts mutual participation in this particular comfort. It does not grant unlimited permission for every future act. [i090–093.]

Sumika’s history of resentment toward romance becomes part of the explanation. She has feared that her own selfish love would reproduce the damage she associates with Nene and Yoko, or corrupt the love she admired in Kanoko. Kanoko refuses that total condemnation. She can cherish the fact that Sumika loves her without pretending to love Sumika in the same way. This does not make the relation symmetrical; it makes its asymmetry less dependent on concealment. Sumika can acknowledge continuing unrequited love and attempt sisterhood within it. Kanoko can acknowledge continuing love for Hime and still make a serious personal commitment to Sumika’s well-being. [i091–096.]

The humor at the end is not disposable. Kanoko again suggests kisses as something she can give; Sumika refuses, Kanoko apologizes and withdraws the offer, and Sumika’s own wavering exposes the continuing attraction. No further kiss occurs. The scene prevents the renewed agreement from becoming a frictionless principle in which every intimate wish has an obvious answer. Desire remains, kindness remains, and particular acts still require their own negotiation. The pair can laugh and hesitate without resolving the relationship by erasing either person’s feeling. [i097–098.]

### 3.4 Shift 70 — “後藤葉子”: an interpretation confronted by other people

#### 3.4.1 Mai changes the terms of access

Yoko arrives expecting Nene and encounters Mai instead. Mai admits that the invitation was arranged through Nene to prevent avoidance. The deception should be recorded as a chosen method, not concealed behind the good purpose of protecting staff. It is also not equivalent to Yoko’s coercion of Kanoko: the people, power, physical circumstances, and available responses differ. A responsible account can identify the method without flattening every misleading invitation into the same act. [i101–103.]

Mai has the hotel photograph/messages and says that she has heard Kanoko’s account. That is an attributed information route. It gives Mai more evidence than she had when she earlier resisted immediate exclusion of a former employee, but it does not turn her into a witness to every omitted interval. Her accusation names the harm to Nene, Kanoko, and Sumika, including what she describes as an attempted sexual assault of a minor. This is a character’s explicit assessment of the conduct. The analysis can preserve it without importing an external legal conclusion or inventing a completed official process. [i103–106.]

Yoko’s answer minimizes and reframes. She points to the absence of completed intercourse and presents her intervention as teaching Kanoko to refuse or understand desire. The preserved V12 refusals and control of communication prevent that account from replacing the acts actually shown. A claim that one was helping someone learn does not make overriding her stated refusal disappear. The fact that Mai now challenges Yoko also does not retrospectively make every earlier suspicion fully evidenced at the moment it was first voiced. The new confrontation has its own information state. [i104–107; accepted V12 O/AG/INF records.]

#### 3.4.2 A result manufactured by intervention is not neutral proof

Yoko describes the reactions she wanted: recognition of romance’s importance, jealousy, and distress that exposes what people supposedly really desire. The account fits a recurring mechanism already visible in the hotel sequence. She selects a reaction, helps produce it, and treats it as confirmation of an interpretive rule. But the present reader has just seen Kanoko and Sumika articulate a relation that resists that rule. Their changed sisterhood is not evidence that Yoko’s procedure was necessary, justified, or successful on its own terms. [i106–113; V14/69/i087–096.]

Mai’s counterargument is not that bodily or romantic desire is unreal. It is that people can seek different things and still create a meaningful relation through mutual understanding. Dating words cannot do that work automatically, and neither can sister words. When Yoko points to the fact that Sumika and Kanoko became lovers, Mai supplies the update: they have ended dating and are trying to understand one another more deeply as sisters. The point is not a return to a pure relation from which romance has been purged. Sumika’s love remains part of the arrangement. What changes is the refusal to make matching romance the only possible measure of the bond. [i110–113.]

Mai also offers a hypothesis about Yoko: perhaps she originally wanted the sort of nonromantic sister relation she now denies. The hypothesis uses Yoko’s known attachment to the fictional novel and her hostility to the possibility of such bonds. At the time Mai speaks, it remains an interpretation offered by Mai. The later memory gives it additional relevance, but does not authorize treating every detail of Yoko’s inner history as already known by the manager. [i107–111, i119–124.]

#### 3.4.3 A managerial boundary is not remorse or an investigation

Mai forbids further approach to the girls and states why she is not pursuing police involvement at this point: she is respecting Kanoko’s wishes. She also warns that further conduct will prompt a report concerning the incident. The source establishes the warning and its stated rationale. It does not depict a report, interview, arrest, adjudication, or newly identified police witness to the earlier hotel scene. V12’s receiving correction remains necessary; the uniformed attendant there does not become police because the institution now discusses that option. [i114–116.]

Yoko agrees not to continue, but her account rests on futility, boredom, or incomprehension rather than a clear acknowledgment of harm and remorse. Future compliance remains untested. Mai’s later uncertainty about whether Yoko will return is not a procedural failure in the analysis; it is the evidence’s limit. The café has now expressed a boundary and a possible consequence. The source has not shown the boundary’s long-term enforcement. [i115–117.]

Nene’s response preserves a difficult distinction. Mai asks whether Nene disliked the response **to Yoko**, not simply whether Nene hates Yoko. Nene supports the boundary while admitting that the very aspects she now judges inadequate once helped her. That acknowledgment does not reinstate the romance or cancel the present assessment. It keeps the past from being reduced to a relation in which nothing of value ever happened. The subsequent blocking screen gives Nene’s current access decision a concrete form. Mixed memory and practical separation can coexist. [i118, i125–126.]

#### 3.4.4 The senior memory explains a pressure point without completing a biography

Away from the café, an unrelated call of “senpai” triggers Yoko’s memory. The unnamed senior shares books with her, and the library scene gives the younger Yoko an enthusiastic creative activity: writing sister stories, repeatedly, including a story about the senior. This is new evidence of a particular attachment and imagined relational form. It makes Mai’s question more than an arbitrary accusation, but it does not by itself establish an exclusive nonromantic orientation or a complete theory of Yoko’s later conduct. [i119–121.]

The senior initiates a more intimate embrace. Yoko is startled and begins to qualify or interrupt; the senior asks not to be rejected or hated. Yoko then gives reassurance and says that their feelings are the same. The sequence is important because the reassuring answer follows the senior’s fear rather than a fully displayed mutual clarification of what each person wanted. The analyst should not infer that the feelings were actually identical merely because Yoko says so. Equally, the scene does not supply enough interior explanation to state with certainty that Yoko felt no romantic attraction at all, or that this one interaction caused every later coercive act. [i121–123.]

No unshown sexual history should be added. The memory depicts closeness, a plea, and an answer. It does not depict a completed sexual act or provide a full chronology of the relationship afterward. Its relevance is the pressure on a desired bond: the fear that refusing a particular form of intimacy might mean rejecting the person or losing the relationship. That problem resonates with the present Kanoko–Sumika discussions, while their choices and degrees of coercion remain different. [i122–123; compare V14/67/i018–021 and V14/69/i080–098.]

The current phone screen names the contact only as the senior and shows an adult-and-child profile image with partial recent-contact wording. It does not establish the child’s parentage, marital status, a complete current relationship, or a newly sent answer by Yoko. Yoko’s renewed insistence that there is no relationship apart from romance remains her conclusion. The volume places it beside evidence of other possibilities, rather than endorsing it as the final truth. [i124.]

The chapter closes by shifting from Yoko’s isolated certainty to Nene and Mai going toward the group. Nene’s block and Mai’s warning against reducing everything to romance are small present acts, not merely philosophical statements. Their ordinary movement toward the party gives the alternative a social setting: relationships do not have to be pure, painless, or identical in form to remain worth participating in. [i125–126.]

### 3.5 Shift 71 — 後夜祭のたわむれ: living with the new arrangement

#### 3.5.1 Celebration is work’s aftermath, not a universal cure

Mai’s formal toast celebrates the successful reading program. The group’s jokes about her elaborate framing, the offered drinks and sandwiches, and the optional entertainment make the scene a release from the performance’s strict demands. Six core café workers are depicted at the gathering. Haruko’s absence from the shown party should not be converted into an explanation of her private schedule or feelings. The room is a party setting with entertainment facilities; no need arises to invent a particular worker’s home. [i127–130.]

Sumika is uncomfortable with the conspicuously kind attention. Mitsuki’s blunt explanation—that they are encouraging her after rejection—makes the group’s partial model audible. It is not a full collective understanding of the private renegotiation. The coworkers can know that Sumika has been hurt and wish to help without knowing every promise, kiss, or secret. Hime’s drink suggestion and Nene’s food offer are actual ordinary care, not automatic proof that they have grasped the entire relation. [i129–132.]

The distinction matters for character reconstruction. Sumika’s ability to receive grand declarations is not the only issue. She is also embarrassed by a party that seems organized around her vulnerability. Kanoko responds by sitting beside her, insisting that this need not be treated as pity. The new support relation has to survive other people’s labels and the awkwardness of being visibly cared for. [i131–133.]

#### 3.5.2 Being there for Sumika is not merely liking the café

Kanoko covers Sumika’s hand out of the group’s immediate attention and says that she will return to Hime’s side later. The touch is initiated without a depicted advance request, but no refusal is shown in this episode. Its covert staging separates the dyad’s private reassurance from the group’s conversation. It should not be described as the same event as Sumika’s earlier refusal to release Kanoko’s hand, nor treated as proof that either person has acquired unlimited permission for touch. [i133–134.]

The group assumes Kanoko attends gatherings chiefly because Hime brings her. Kanoko corrects that account directly to Sumika: she would have come even without Hime because Sumika is here. Sumika initially translates the statement into the safer idea that Kanoko enjoys Liebe. Kanoko’s dissatisfied response indicates that the generalization loses something important. She has named a person, not only an environment. Her subsequent request for potatoes and their slightly awkward small talk keep the scene from becoming a perfectly delivered confession of a new category. The personal value is clear before the category is. [i134–136.]

This is a meaningful development without requiring the analyst to call it romantic. Kanoko’s earlier selective attachment to Hime has not vanished. She still intends to return to Hime’s side, and she later promises Hime an eventual disclosure. What has changed is that Sumika now supplies an independent reason to attend, stay, and speak. The relationship is neither an interchangeable service nor a covert replacement of Hime as the beloved. [i133–136, i147–151.]

#### 3.5.3 Honest desire still has act-specific limits

Sumika’s remark about Kanoko eating and being thin produces another immediate self-correction. She remembers that bodily commentary may be uncomfortable and recognizes that her own attraction changes how such words can be heard. Kanoko does not respond by declaring every possible bodily comment or act acceptable. She invites Sumika to stop concealing her feelings in this particular exchange, accepting that the love includes embarrassing desire. The permission is communicative and contextual; it is not an unrestricted sexual or contact authorization. [i137–140.]

The awkward photograph request continues the difference between shared feeling and a shared image. Mai asks the pair to pose while they are visibly embarrassed. The resulting picture can record real mutual warmth even though they have just struggled to find words and do not claim equal romance. Its eventual place on the board is meaningful because the photograph has a newly grounded relational history, not because a smiling pose can independently prove everything about the people pictured. [i140, i154–156.]

The Hime–Mitsuki comic interruption should not be used to certify the group’s perfect boundary maturity. Hime notices whisky-bonbon labeling and attributes Mitsuki’s behavior to intoxication; Mai’s denial is played against the packaging and the ongoing behavior. Mitsuki handles Hime’s face and tries to feed her while using sister language. Hime is startled and objects to how strange the behavior is. This is intrusive, uncomfortable comedy, not a new consensual romantic agreement. The source supplies no measured dose or clinical assessment, and none is needed. [i141–142.]

Individual reinspection is consequential here: i141–142 does **not** contain a clear new Hime command such as an explicit request to stop opening her mouth or release her. Her exclamations, question about the chocolate, and address to Mitsuki establish alarm, but the exact trigger of WY8-PR01 remains unclean. It would be wrong to invent a sharper line in order to score the forecast. It would be equally wrong to call the forecast’s untriggered state evidence that Mitsuki has become universally respectful. The related conduct qualifies the general picture; the conditional test remains separate. By i146 Mitsuki is asleep on Hime’s lap, and Hime thanks Kanoko for practical assistance. No omitted clean boundary negotiation is inserted between those states. [i141–146.]

Kanoko’s small pinch of Sumika’s hand likewise remains an act with a response: Sumika registers pain. Kanoko wants Sumika not to keep sacrificing her own desires whenever Hime is involved. That personal demand is not an unequivocal request to control Hime’s or Mitsuki’s attachment, so it does not automatically satisfy WY4-PR01. The moment adds pressure to the supposedly settled arrangement without making it collapse. Protecting one another’s love is a chosen aspiration, not proof that every method of encouragement is already harmless. [i142–143.]

#### 3.5.4 Hime can wait without having no feelings about secrecy

Hime asks how matters with Sumika stand. Kanoko says that the two will work things out together, while acknowledging that she cannot give all the details. Hime responds by allowing the secret. Her own experience has taught her that romantic matters can be difficult to say. Yet her interior reaction also registers loneliness at hidden parts of Kanoko’s life. The two sides of the response belong together: respecting a boundary is compatible with wanting more access and feeling its absence. [i147–148.]

Kanoko then promises to tell Hime the long-hidden matter someday and asks her to wait. Hime agrees. The source establishes a commitment to future disclosure, not the disclosure itself. It supplies no new identified romantic confession, answer, or knowledge of the old abolition proposal. The promise should not be treated as a covert way of moving the Hime–Kanoko forecast past its actual trigger. [i149–150.]

Kanoko does make a different declaration now. She tells Hime that, for the first time, she has a special important person besides Hime. The surrounding exchange and Sumika’s overhearing make the referent clear. Hime is pleased and relieved; Sumika is surprised by the value being named. This is not simply the analyst inferring affection from a blush. Kanoko has made a personal claim in words and directs a smile toward Sumika. The source can establish new specialness without deciding that it has become romantic. [i150–153.]

#### 3.5.5 The photograph board is a later state, not a whole-series ending

Mai calls the group for a photograph and distinguishes private memories for the backstage space from merchandise. The small joke about a digital camera and printing reinforces that images have uses, audiences, and material afterlives. A staff picture need not function like the customer-facing signed photographs that previously structured Sumika’s longing. It can belong to a collectively lived occasion while retaining private meanings for particular participants. [i154–155.]

Sumika’s concluding narration refuses an easy label: the relation exceeds a simple senior–junior bond and does not fit uncomplicated romance. She describes continuing to face romantic and other feelings and protecting each other’s love as their form of sisterhood. This is a source-grounded self-account supported by the conversation and party, not a license to assign an external diagnostic identity or predict permanent equilibrium. Kanoko has not returned Sumika’s romantic love in the same terms. Sumika has not stopped loving her. Neither condition cancels the real care that the new arrangement makes possible. [i155–156.]

The final page places Sumika later in school uniform before the photographs. The old signed Kanoko portrait is intact, and a new shared smiling image joins it. The exact interval after the party is not supplied. The material contrast with the earlier stored photograph matters: keeping an image no longer has to mean holding onto an imagined exclusive future or trying to hide an unbearable feeling. It can preserve a relationship whose value has been spoken and whose inequality is acknowledged. The mainline freezes here, at **i156**, not at the party’s earlier dialogue. [i156; preserved V13 Shift 66.5.]

This is a substantial local closure of the Sumika–Kanoko movement. It is not proof that the entire manga has ended, that Kanoko’s future confession to Hime has occurred, that Yoko will comply indefinitely, or that all outstanding forecasts have received tests. The strength of the ending lies in specifying what can now continue, not in pretending that every future problem has been solved.

## 4. Separately bounded short, production testimony, and edition matter

### 4.1 Shift 71.5 — Kanoko overhears an explanation that does not quite fit

The short begins with Kanoko overhearing schoolgirls discuss kissing. The girls are not being shown receiving Kanoko’s confession about the stage event, and their words are not Hime’s account of her own kiss with Mitsuki. They debate sensory enjoyment, liking, and the communication of love in ordinary peer conversation. Kanoko listens without entering the exchange. That audience distinction is necessary: the reader gains access to Kanoko’s reflection, while the speakers do not thereby learn her history. [S71.5/i158–159.]

The discussion prompts Kanoko to consider the kiss she gave Sumika. She distinguishes wanting to please Sumika from the romantic liking she feels for Hime. The short therefore resists a simple retrospective recoding of the stage kiss as a secretly mutual romantic confession. But it also prevents “not romantic” from becoming “only an interchangeable senior.” Kanoko tries the senior–junior explanation and finds that it does not capture the whole feeling either. Her uncertainty concerns the adequacy of a category, not the absence of a person-specific attachment. [i160.]

At the café’s Halloween presentation, Sumika plays a wolf and threatens a playful trick if the girls do not give sweets. Hime and Kanoko recognize the costume routine. Kanoko’s teasing suggestion about a kiss embarrasses Sumika and makes the new bond playful rather than purely solemn. The final private formulation is a wish for this awkward, earnest, lovable person to be happy. That wish supplies positive content to Kanoko’s liking without requiring an analyst-imposed romantic label. [i160–161.]

Chronologically, the reflection presupposes the stage kiss and the relation that follows it. The package does not settle its exact position relative to the mainline’s later photograph-board page. It should therefore remain a separately bounded post-kiss/Halloween episode rather than automatically advancing the mainline high-water mark. It also does not grant general permission for future kisses: a teasing sentence and a remembered act are not a permanent contact contract.

### 4.2 Afterword — an arc’s construction is not the whole work’s ending

The afterword identifies a closing point for the long Sumika–Kanoko movement and discusses difficulties built into that movement. It describes creative choices involving Sumika’s protection of Kanoko’s love, Yoko’s return, and the experience of being the person Kanoko loves rather than the other way around. This is useful production testimony because it identifies problems the creator reports deliberately constructing. It is not a substitute for the actual scenes or an instruction to erase counterevidence to a preferred interpretation. [i162–163.]

The testimony can support a bounded statement that this volume is designed to close a particular arc. It cannot establish that Kanoko’s love for Hime has been resolved, that every continuing character problem has ended, or that V14 is the completed manga. The final story page’s new arrangement and future-disclosure promise remain the narrative controls. Likewise, the creator’s account of the production interruption and health-related absence is an afterword self-report, not an independently researched medical or publication history. No additional diagnosis or current-status claim is made here. [i162–163.]

A particularly useful methodological distinction follows. An author may explain why a character had to face difficult circumstances; the analysis must still distinguish the character’s choices, other people’s harmful acts, and the narrative arrangement that places them together. The afterword’s construction account does not make Yoko’s coercion necessary or justified within the characters’ moral world. It supplies another witness level, not a retrospective permission slip.

### 4.3 The event-seating manual — six sessions are reported, not six repetitions shown

The institutional manual describes how special events differ from ordinary café service: reservations, timed entry, food orders, the performance itself, and closing/payment tasks are organized differently. It uses a twenty-person session as an example of reservation grouping and reports two sessions per day across three days, totaling six sessions. These are edition-level setting statements. They extend the operational picture without making six complete performances independently depicted in the mainline. [i165.]

This distinction prevents a consequential chronology error. The mainline closely presents one performance containing Kanoko’s missed cue, Sumika’s tears, and the lighting interruption, then shows a completed program and later celebration. The manual does not tell us that every session reproduced the same private crisis or that the intimate intervention happened six times. Nor does it supply a complete calendar for every private conversation. The six-session statement should be routed to the institutional/performance record with its witness class preserved, not used to multiply narrative events.

The manual also adds a useful account of labor. Public atmosphere depends on work that the guests may barely notice: managing entry, handling orders within a fixed schedule, completing the reading, and resetting afterward. Sumika’s scene-management, Kanoko’s cue recovery, Nene’s backstage attention, and Mai’s organization belong within that collective operation. The café is neither only an allegorical stage nor merely a source of romantic misunderstanding; it is a workplace whose routines make recurring cooperation possible.

### 4.4 Edition illustrations and the Halloween bonus

The underlying cover, back cover, spine, catalogue, and creator material remain edition framing. The flower-bed cover does not establish a literal event in the story. Its soft, private presentation can be discussed as visual framing, but it is not evidence that the character is in a particular relationship or emotional state at the mainline endpoint. [Cover; i164–167.]

The electronic-edition bonus at i168 presents Mitsuki in an Alice costume and Hime as a rabbit. Background commentators express surprise at Mitsuki’s Alice choice. Mitsuki calls Hime a cute rabbit; Hime praises Mitsuki’s Alice costume and asks Mitsuki to chase her properly. Mitsuki then embraces Hime, while the commentators remark that the participants seem happy. No Kanoko photographing is shown. The costume-and-pursuit joke does not establish reciprocal Hime–Mitsuki romance, generalized permission for future contact, or a fixed post-coda chronology. [i168.]

### 4.5 What remains outside this source operation

The V10 special-edition booklet is a distinct inventoried object and has not been opened. No later numbered volume or magazine chapter was retrieved to resolve a V14 uncertainty. The admitted afterword and colophon can identify what this edition says about its production and earlier serialization, but they do not establish the current external publication ceiling. Reaching the last object in the old inventory is therefore an **inventory-bounded reading milestone**, not a claim of complete series analysis or completed synthesis.

### 7.1 The limits of “acting naturally”

The volume does not solve its conflicts by stripping away every role. The café’s fiction helps Sumika cover a cue, lets the group work, and later supplies a language for continuing care. What fails is the assumption that successful performance guarantees complete self-understanding or permits one person to decide the other’s meaning. Sumika can be sincerely kind as an elder sister while hiding how much she wants to be loved. Kanoko can sincerely play Erna while finding that the script does not settle what she owes Sumika. Yoko can interpret a fictional story intelligently while using it coercively as a rule for actual people. [PER04–PER11.]

A useful current model therefore asks who knows that a performance is occurring, what it enables, and what it conceals. Publicly preserving the fictional school is not the same as privately maintaining a beautiful-ending story after it has become misleading. One supports a shared work; the other withholds information necessary to a shared decision. The distinction depends on audience and consequence rather than a universal opposition between genuine and performed selves.
