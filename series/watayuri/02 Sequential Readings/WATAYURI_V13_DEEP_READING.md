## 1. The volume’s governing problem

Volume 13 connects two attempts to decide a relationship’s future without exposing the decision fully to the other person. In middle school, Kanoko writes a love letter to Hime, withholds her name, learns how Hime intends to answer an unidentified girl, and destroys the letter before identifying herself. In the present, Sumika decides that continuing to date Kanoko will sustain an intolerable stalemate, then tells Kanoko that their romantic arrangement should end and that she should confess to Hime. Neither action is adequately described by its stated protective purpose alone. Kanoko wants to preserve a real and valuable friendship; Sumika wants Kanoko to stop suffering and also has limits of her own. Yet both try to organize what another person will encounter next. The volume gradually makes room for a different act: not preserving an arrangement at any cost or prescribing its successor, but insisting that the people involved actually speak to each other. [V13/62/i017–034; V13/64/i080–092; V13/66/i145–158.]

The recovered confession is especially important because the source does **not** reveal a familiar scene in which Hime knowingly rejected Kanoko and both agreed to forget it. The letter is anonymous. Hime discusses the problem with Kanoko as her trusted friend, not as the identified person asking for romance. Kanoko then supplies an invented explanation for the absent writer and proposes that the two remain friends without falling in love. Hime’s later recollection confirms that this earlier promise-language mattered to her, but it does not establish that she knew who wrote the letter or understood the private sacrifice Kanoko attached to the arrangement. A shared memory can therefore coexist with radically unequal knowledge of what is being remembered. [V13/62/i021–031; V13/65/i109–112.]

Kanoko’s historical solution is not simply to deny that she loves Hime. She preserves a hierarchy in which being the special friend is secure so long as Hime never develops a different special attachment. Hime’s earlier preference for spending time with Kanoko over dealing with boys’ confessions makes that solution emotionally credible. Mitsuki later exposes its weakness without needing to become Hime’s mutually acknowledged lover: she confesses, receives a refusal, and nevertheless remains important. Kanoko sees someone else retaining a valued place after taking the risk she erased. The question becomes not merely whether confession succeeds, but what Kanoko sacrificed by treating possible romantic rejection as inevitable loss of every form of specialness. The source does not answer the counterfactual of how Hime would have responded to Kanoko’s identified confession. It shows why that unanswered question now hurts. [V13/62/i011–016, i025–034.]

The park outing offers a genuine alternative to unequal giving. Kanoko listens to Sumika’s account of the conversation with Nene and gives an interpretation that helps her. Sumika, in turn, tries to free Kanoko’s gratitude from an obligation to repay care with equivalent service or romantic feeling. The day is not exposed as a sham when Sumika later ends the dating arrangement. Its comfort, ordinary pleasures, and reciprocal listening are real. What the later turn overturns is Kanoko’s inference that a good day means both women have moved forward in the same way. The final chapter of the mainline’s first half and the separately packaged cleanup short give the reader information Kanoko lacks: Sumika’s feelings make continued sleepovers and kisses difficult for her to bear. Her desire to help and her need for distance cannot responsibly be reduced to either pure altruism or pure selfishness. [V13/63/i039–062; V13/64/i081–090; S66.5/i160–162.]

Sumika has the right to stop dating. The analytical problem is not that she fails to obtain permission to withdraw from romance. It is that she joins her own withdrawal to a confident account of what Kanoko needs, continues after Kanoko asks her to stop saying painful things, and predicts a favorable relational outcome from a confession to Hime. The school-play script makes this departure sound like the correct act of an elder sister: let the younger leave so that she can begin again. But a script cannot guarantee Hime’s answer, and the person supposedly being released does not experience the announcement as liberation. Kanoko leaves in anger. The difference between ending one’s own participation and directing another person’s future remains essential. [V13/64/i083–092.]

Hime’s later intervention also has mixed methods, but a different informational structure. Hime recognizes that something is wrong without knowing the hotel events, the anonymous letter’s author, or Kanoko’s romantic attachment to her. She first offers time and space, then becomes more insistent as Kanoko’s reassurances fail to fit her visible distress. Hime’s concern is not omniscient kindness: she eventually admits that she also wanted Kanoko’s attention and feared being left behind. That acknowledgment makes Hime a participant with needs, rather than merely the object of two other people’s care. It does not make her need for attention romantic, nor give her a right to every secret. [V13/65/i093–122; V13/66/i125–149.]

The rooftop ending is a partial, affirmative change. Kanoko accepts that even doing nothing does not keep relationships motionless. Hime affirms that wanting to stay together can become a reason to discuss change rather than forbid it. Kanoko does not then confess her love. She identifies another unfinished relationship: Sumika’s. She wants to speak properly with Sumika instead of treating the breakup as an outcome that Sumika alone may explain on both their behalf. This does not revoke Sumika’s freedom to end dating. It establishes Kanoko’s own wish for an answer and for a relationship whose meaning cannot be settled by either a helpful role or a unilateral theory of her welfare. The next conversation and the scheduled reading event remain beyond V13. [V13/66/i149–158.]

## 3. Sequential, form-sensitive close reading

### 3.1 Shift 62: the confession that Hime cannot knowingly answer

#### 3.1.1 A genuine place beside Hime precedes the demand that nothing change

The chapter begins inside the historical account announced at V12’s endpoint. Kanoko identifies the school period in which she met Hime, then describes the difference between Hime’s agreeable presentation and her more forceful private personality. To Kanoko, the façade is not simply evidence that Hime is afraid of everyone. It is a means by which Hime pursues the life she wants. Kanoko admires the person who can expose that mechanism without ceasing to be kind. Sumika’s admission that she does not fully understand the façade matters: a listener can receive the account without pretending to possess Hime’s entire psychology. The framing preserves Kanoko as the principal interpreter of the recollection, even as the depicted scenes allow the reader to examine the limits of her conclusions. [V13/62/i001, i005–006.]

The first developed school episode gives their specialness a public dimension. Hime includes Kanoko in an invitation with classmates. When that inclusion is understood as looking after a lonely person, Hime resists the charitable explanation. Later, alone with Kanoko, she repeats the point: she regards Kanoko as a friend, and would like their closeness to be visible rather than confined to private moments. The move from surnames with respectful address to Hime-chan and Kanoko becomes an enacted adjustment in the relation, not a label the analyst supplies afterward. [V13/62/i007–010.]

This is important counterevidence to a retrospective account in which Kanoko invented every sign of being chosen. Hime does choose her, protects the relation from a patronizing explanation, and asks for a change that makes it more ordinary and mutual. Yet the content of the choice is friendship. Neither the refusal of pity nor the change of address contains a promise that Hime will never want another important relationship. The later exclusive interpretation grows out of a real gift of recognition; it is not identical to the terms of that gift.

The rooftop setting contributes to this distinction. It is a place where private speech can revise a public account, but it is not a space outside social consequence. What Hime says there changes how Kanoko understands herself among classmates. In the last chapter, the same kind of threshold will again permit a new formulation of togetherness. The recurrence is not simply nostalgic decoration. V13 tests whether the place that once made the relationship feel secure can support change rather than only preserve the earlier arrangement. [V13/62/i009–010; V13/66/i150–155.]

#### 3.1.2 Hime’s preference is particular, but Kanoko converts it into a protective rule

The next school passage links attraction to everyday access. Kanoko’s attention to Hime becomes more openly romantic to the reader, while Hime continues to deal with confessions from boys. Kanoko initially has difficulty naming why those encounters disturb her, then explains that a boyfriend would reduce their time together. Hime replies from her existing position: she does not intend to date these confessors, has not understood falling in love, and still separates her future wealth aspiration from present romantic feeling. Kanoko outwardly aligns herself with that account while privately already loving Hime. [V13/62/i011–016.]

The asymmetry is not merely that one knows a secret. The two apparent statements of romantic noninvolvement have different relations to their speakers’ experience. Hime’s is presented as her account of herself at that time. Kanoko’s protects a feeling she is not ready to disclose. Their conversational agreement therefore cannot establish that both freely endorse a permanent absence of love. It begins with an apparent similarity that conceals a decisive difference.

Hime’s preference for going home with Kanoko rather than giving more time to boys’ confessions further strengthens Kanoko’s confidence. The preference is not imaginary or automatically manipulative. Hime enjoys this friend and places their time together above an obligation she does not want. But choosing Kanoko over unwanted confessions is not the same comparison as choosing Kanoko over a beloved romantic partner. The latter situation has not occurred. Kanoko’s strategy will depend on keeping it from becoming conceivable. [V13/62/i014–016.]

A strong reading therefore preserves both warmth and overextension. Hime is not guilty of promising a romance simply because she is affectionate. Kanoko is not irrational for taking their closeness seriously. The source shows how a person can correctly perceive that she matters and still build an insecure theory about the conditions under which she will continue to matter. The danger lies in turning a presently favorable comparison into a rule that the other person must never change.

#### 3.1.3 An anonymous letter creates disclosure without identification

Kanoko writes a love letter that names the effects Hime has had on her life: brightness, ease, laughter, and a larger-feeling school world. Its romantic purpose is not inferred from a single blush. It is an actual communicative act, delivered to Hime’s shoe locker. However, Kanoko withholds her name and proposes an after-school meeting at which she could identify herself. The structure preserves a final retreat before the relation must be openly reclassified. [V13/62/i017–020.]

Anonymity has a precise informational consequence. Hime can receive the proposition that a girl loves her without knowing that her trusted friend is that girl. When she asks Kanoko how to respond, she is not staging a knowingly cruel rejection of Kanoko under cover of uncertainty. The pages do not establish that recognition. Hime’s account of unfamiliarity with a girl’s confession, her concern about rejection and possible social complications, and her statement that she does not expect to fall in love all reach the person whose hopes they endanger. They do so through a friendship consultation rather than an identified confession exchange. [V13/62/i021–024.]

This scene answers V12’s open question about the old confession, but in a way that changes the question’s premise. There is no complete direct answer to “Kanoko, will you be my lover?” because Kanoko never supplies that identified request. Hime does give a discouraging prospective answer about the anonymous writer. Kanoko receives it as a forecast of losing her special position. The reader should not collapse those facts into either “Hime never said anything rejecting” or “Hime knowingly rejected Kanoko and then denied it.” Both formulations lose the actual route by which the information moved.

Hime’s concern about social consequences also remains attributed. It can show defensive reputation management without establishing hostility toward every same-sex relationship or a stable orientation label. Conversely, the friendship setting does not make every word harmless. Kanoko hears a possible future for herself that she had not truly imagined while writing the letter. Her distress is supported even though Hime lacks the information needed to understand its cause.

#### 3.1.4 Erasure is an action with a victim of ignorance, not literal forgetting

At the meeting place, Hime waits while Kanoko hesitates out of view. Kanoko is frightened not only of hearing no, but of becoming one of the indistinguishable people whose affection Hime rejects. The special friend can presently stand beside Hime while those other people are discussed; the identified suitor might, in Kanoko’s model, join their category. This is the relational cost she seeks to avoid. [V13/62/i025–028.]

Kanoko eventually enters as the friend rather than as the letter’s author. She urges Hime to stop waiting merely to reject someone, destroys the letter, and invents an explanation for the absent writer’s failure to appear. The supposed girl, she suggests, mistook excitement between girls for love and then recovered from that mistake. The explanation is especially revealing because it offers Hime a harmless account of the disappearance while disowning the very feeling Kanoko continues to have. The private love is not extinguished; its public trace is removed and given a false interpretation. [V13/62/i029–031.]

The torn letter should therefore be described materially and epistemically. It is destroyed. Hime does not receive its author’s identity. Kanoko’s explanation is not independent testimony about another person. Nor does the destruction establish that Hime forgets the incident: later in the volume Hime recalls the letter and promise-language. What is erased is the possibility of treating the encounter, at that moment, as Kanoko’s identified romantic initiative.

Kanoko then proposes that they remain friends forever without either falling in love. The scene does not show a full affirmative Hime reply specifying the same terms and meaning. Later Hime calls the earlier exchange a promise Kanoko proposed, which gives the reader more evidence about Hime’s retention of it. That later evidence must be added rather than anticipated. It still does not make the agreement a fully informed endorsement of Kanoko’s hidden reasoning or a binding prohibition on all future attachment. [V13/62/i031; V13/65/i109–110.]

#### 3.1.5 Mitsuki changes the meaning of the sacrifice without answering the counterfactual

The return to the present explains why Kanoko can no longer leave the old arrangement undisturbed. Mitsuki declared her love, was refused, and nevertheless stayed special to Hime. Kanoko does not need evidence that Hime reciprocates Mitsuki romantically for this to destabilize her strategy. What hurts is that another person appears to have survived the risk she treated as fatal. The fear that confession would turn her into “everybody” is no longer the only visible model of what can happen. [V13/62/i032–034.]

Kanoko’s questions consequently concern lost possibilities as well as jealousy. Was erasing the confession a mistake? Why should someone who spoke receive access she denied herself? Her language about Hime’s kiss has a possessive force, but the analysis must retain that force as Kanoko’s framing rather than endorse a right she had acquired over Hime’s first kiss. Hime’s bodily choices and Mitsuki’s conduct remain separately assessable under their own scenes and permissions. Kanoko’s pain explains the stakes of the comparison without settling ownership or blame.

Sumika’s immediate function is to hear this account. The chapter does not make her solve the counterfactual, nor does it give Hime access to the conversation. The new historical knowledge belongs to the reader and to Sumika through Kanoko’s disclosure. Its first present effect is closeness and comfort; its later effect on Sumika’s decisions will be shown separately. A consequential explanation has been completed, but not the relationship problem it explains.

### 3.2 Shift 63: a good day that does not mean the same thing to both people

#### 3.2.1 Care includes ordinary choices and the possibility of contributing

The following morning places Kanoko in a quieter state than the hotel aftermath or the previous night’s disclosure. Sumika offers sleep and breakfast, while Kanoko remembers the comfort of being near her and wants to help. These small acts matter because they widen Kanoko’s represented agency beyond refusing danger or confessing a secret. She can choose to get up, participate in a household task, accept an outing, and respond to ordinary teasing. None of this needs to be promoted into proof of complete recovery or newly reciprocal romantic love. [V13/63/i036–041.]

The proposed date remains a date in the women’s current arrangement. Calling the outing meaningless because Kanoko has not declared equivalent love would flatten an actual shared activity. Sumika’s playful alternatives and Kanoko’s willingness to go where Sumika likes show an effort to make their time together pleasant, but they also preserve the asymmetry: Kanoko is still highly alert to what she has received and what she can give back. Her gratitude does not automatically distinguish freely chosen participation from a perceived need to repay.

The bakery supplies unusually useful low-stakes evidence. Sumika has concrete food choices and practical habits; Kanoko has trouble identifying a particular preference. Sumika’s offer to deal with the queue while Kanoko chooses is a logistical accommodation. Kanoko initially hears a possible rebuke in her own slowness and apologizes, while Sumika clarifies. Sumika’s payment is also given a personally expressive explanation: she wants to look good in the date. This does not become a bill Kanoko must later settle with affection. [V13/63/i042–046.]

These details support reconstruction more responsibly than a generalized declaration that Sumika is always generous or Kanoko has no preferences. The source supplies choices in one setting. It also shows how a person under relational strain can treat even benign assistance as a sign that she is failing to contribute properly. The repair consists partly in making the assistance’s meaning explicit before that inference hardens.

#### 3.2.2 Being cared for and feeling indebted are not the same experience

At the park, Sumika recognizes Kanoko’s preoccupation with repayment and asks her to rest and attend to herself. The request is not simply “stop thinking about Hime” in a new disguise. It concerns the pressure Kanoko places on herself to make every received kindness equivalent. Her tears then connect the ordinary day to the more difficult material: Yoko’s intervention, the disclosure about Hime, and the relief of having been heard without being discarded. [V13/63/i047–051.]

Sumika asks about handholding rather than treating visible distress as automatic permission for any contact. That question is valuable as a distinct local procedure, even though it cannot erase the preceding volume’s refused release or certify the later breakup conversation. The source allows the same person to be more careful in one encounter and more forceful in another. A model that turns her into either a uniformly safe listener or an invariably coercive helper cannot describe the actual sequence.

Kanoko’s statement that she is glad Sumika was beside her yesterday and today is a positive evaluation of Sumika herself. It is not only a request that Sumika manipulate Hime. Yet Kanoko’s next concern is again how to return the benefit. The chapter therefore does not present a single reassuring sentence as the end of the debt model. It gives that model a practical alternative: Kanoko can hear something Sumika wants to say, not because an account must be balanced but because Sumika also needs a listener.

#### 3.2.3 Kanoko becomes useful without performing a romantic repayment

Sumika asks for a turn speaking and recounts Nene’s earlier love and the recent conversation about it. She quickly clarifies the historical nature of the confession, preventing the wording from being mistaken for a new present romantic proposal. Her difficulty is not simply whether Nene forgives her. She wonders whether claiming a restored friendship is convenient self-interpretation after failing to understand Nene’s feelings. The repaired relation from V11 remains real, but Sumika is still assessing her responsibility within it. [V13/63/i052–056.]

Kanoko’s answer neither supplies omniscience about Nene nor competes to be the best interpreter of her. She offers a distinction: different desired forms of relationship do not make one person automatically at fault, and continuing together can matter without making the past painless. Her uncertainty is part of the quality of the answer. She does not pretend that hearing Sumika’s account gives her every absent person’s interior state.

Sumika finds this helpful. The scene is a concrete instance of Kanoko contributing to the relationship without providing the romantic or physical equivalence she previously thought she owed. The contribution is not hypothetical future potential. Sumika asks, Kanoko answers, and Sumika reports relief. The limited outcome matters more than a broad claim that Kanoko is now healed or an accomplished counselor. [V13/63/i055–057.]

The subsequent ordinary exchange with children over a flying toy gives Sumika’s explanation of care a low-stakes demonstration. Help need not be part of a private debt ledger. Sumika can assist because she wants to, and she can be happy when a valued person is happy. Kanoko can recognize that logic without having identical feelings. The example does not prove that nobody has selfish motives; it shows that calculating a return is not necessary for an act to be intelligible. [V13/63/i058–060.]

#### 3.2.4 The closing object prevents a false shared-progress conclusion

Kanoko’s account of the day is hopeful: she feels a little further forward and extends that hope to Sumika. The qualification matters. She is interpreting Sumika, not reporting access to Sumika’s unspoken decision. The chapter then leaves Kanoko’s point of view and shows Sumika at home taking down the signed photograph. [V13/63/i061–062.]

This act must not be exaggerated. The photograph is **not torn**. The separately packaged short shows the intact print placed in a drawer. Removal from display is nevertheless consequential. The image whose smile had earlier organized Sumika’s desire is being moved out of its ordinary visible place. The act can support a reading of attempted emotional containment or preparation for a changed relation; it does not establish hatred, final disposal, or success in ceasing to love. [S66.5/i160–162.]

The contrast is therefore more exact than “the happy date was false.” Kanoko accurately experienced comfort, and Sumika genuinely gave and received care. What Kanoko could not see was the personal cost of continuing the present arrangement. V13 preserves the good day while refusing to let one participant’s hopeful summary determine the other’s future. The volume’s problem is not the absence of sincerity. It is that sincere experiences can lead toward different choices.

### 3.3 Shift 64: recognizing harm, then prescribing a way forward

#### 3.3.1 The café contains different kinds of care and different information audiences

The next workplace movement begins with apparently workable ordinary relations. Sumika greets Kanoko brightly. Hime notices that Kanoko seems better but remains worried, and makes an opening for conversation. Kanoko does not give Hime the account she gave Sumika. Elsewhere, Hime and Mitsuki’s discussion of schoolwork supplies ordinary cooperative continuity; it is not a new confession, a contact-limit episode, or evidence that either knows the hotel history. The ensemble is functioning without sharing a single understanding of what has happened. [V13/64/i064–066.]

Mai and Nene then arrange a private conversation with Kanoko. Mai apologizes for the work-related pretext and explains that the setting is meant to prevent others hearing. The pretext thus creates privacy rather than demonstrating that every backstage person was already informed. Nene is identified as Mai’s information route about Saturday. Sumika’s involvement in the crisis, Nene’s relay, Mai’s present briefing, and Hime’s continuing uncertainty must remain separately located. [V13/64/i067–068.]

Mai offers Kanoko a choice about timing: the conversation need not happen now if it is difficult. Kanoko elects to speak. This is an observable procedural difference from simply using managerial authority to obtain a complete report. It does not make the pretext unproblematic in every possible respect, but the apology and timing option are part of the scene’s actual construction. The manager’s need to know does not erase the employee’s immediate condition.

#### 3.3.2 Mai refuses to make “nothing happened” the measure of harm

Kanoko’s account minimizes what was done to her and indicates that she does not want more made of the incident. Mai recognizes the limits of what the known account can establish, then objects to the inference that this means Kanoko was not harmed. Threatening someone into saying something they did not want to say is itself consequential. Mai names Kanoko as the person harmed and offers future help, while saying she will not press further against Kanoko’s wishes. [V13/64/i069–071.]

The exact attribution is essential. Mai’s phrasing about the absence of an established bodily act does not become an omniscient narrator’s denial of the coercion depicted in V12. Kanoko’s own minimizing language remains part of her attempt to understand the incident. The previous reading already distinguishes shown violence, an omitted interval, Yoko’s claims, and Kanoko’s scoped later report. V13 adds a workplace listener who refuses to make the most restrictive possible definition of harm the price of receiving support.

The scene also does not create a police report or legal finding. The accepted V12 correction remains effective: hotel personnel were not identified as police, and the proposed police visit was not shown to occur. Here Mai’s available help and her acknowledgment of injury are actual speech and management conduct, not a completed official process. A further request for Kanoko’s understanding begins near the end of the exchange, but its full content and implementation are not supplied in the displayed continuation. The analyst should leave that unshown content open rather than invent a completed sanction or notification. [V13/64/i071–072.]

Kanoko’s subsequent reflection shows why the distinction matters. She considers whether something was taken from her and remembers both the frightening statements she was made to confront and Sumika’s reception of her distress. The question is not answered by a simple physical inventory. Nor does her renewed appreciation of Sumika establish that Sumika will remain able to occupy the same role indefinitely. The next movement will expose the difference between needing a person and agreeing on the form in which that person can continue to help.

#### 3.3.3 Public sisterhood can reassure truthfully without settling the private arrangement

The salon scene uses the coming reading event to show actual knowledge of Kanoko’s effort. Hime reports progress with the script; Kanoko remains less confident; Sumika’s older-sister speech reassures her that her work has been seen. Guests receive an appealing sister performance, while Kanoko receives something personally meaningful. The sincerity of that reassurance does not require the guests to know about the night’s disclosure or the park conversation. [V13/64/i073–075.]

Kanoko’s interior response is restorative. She imagines returning to the position in which she stays Hime’s friend, bears one-sided love, and has Sumika beside her. The source does not present this only as an external analyst’s reconstruction: she actively organizes the new sense of safety around that arrangement. It is therefore important to preserve the temporal order. The reassurance and Kanoko’s confidence occur **before** Sumika says she wants to stop dating. The later decision does not retroactively make every preceding kind line deceptive, but it shows that Kanoko has mistaken present kindness for a guarantee of unchanged terms. [V13/64/i076–079.]

Their walk begins with shared script language and a practical acknowledgment of Kanoko’s memorization. This is genuine preparation for an event still ahead. It also establishes the vocabulary Sumika will soon use outside rehearsal. When a fictional older sister releases a younger one, the line can function as a reading exercise, as an interpretation of their private relation, and as an attempt to persuade. Those are different acts even when the words repeat.

#### 3.3.4 Sumika’s own limit is real; her account of Kanoko’s necessary future remains contestable

Sumika announces that their lover arrangement should end. She does not present the reason as cessation of love. Kanoko still loves Hime, and Sumika argues that dating someone else cannot resolve the jealousy Kanoko is trying to hide. She imagines a future of Hime and Mitsuki growing closer while Kanoko suffers and Sumika helps conceal that suffering. Continuing the current arrangement would, in her view, maintain the problem rather than address it. [V13/64/i080–082.]

There is real evidence behind this concern. Kanoko has repeatedly described wanting Hime to remain unchanged and has used the dating arrangement partly to recover Sumika’s former support. Sumika now knows much more about that purpose than she did at V10’s proposal. The problem is not that she fabricates every premise. It is that an explanation with substantial support can still outrun what one person is entitled to decide for another.

Two decisions should therefore be separated. Sumika can decide that she cannot continue as Kanoko’s girlfriend. That withdrawal does not require Kanoko’s agreement, and Kanoko’s anger cannot obligate her to remain. But Sumika also tells Kanoko what she must do about Hime, predicts that the relationship will survive, and presents the end of dating as the route to Kanoko’s liberation. Those propositions require more than Sumika’s right to set her own limit. They concern Kanoko’s disclosure, Hime’s answer, and the future meaning of two relationships. [V13/64/i083–090.]

#### 3.3.5 The stop request is not honored, even though the desired outcome is framed as freedom

Kanoko explicitly asks Sumika to stop and not say these things. The exchange does not pause at that request. Sumika continues to explain that Kanoko cannot keep avoiding Hime, that their dating has not solved the underlying problem, and that the hotel events have made the incompleteness especially visible. Kanoko says she had finally believed everything could return to normal and experiences Sumika’s argument as breaking that restoration. [V13/64/i083–086.]

The reader should not convert the request into a mere ornamental sign of embarrassment. It is directly relevant to the new forecast preserved at V12: what does Sumika do when Kanoko asks to halt a painful Hime-related exchange? Here there is a distinct request and continued pressure. Yet the rationale differs from the preceding handhold. Sumika is not now insisting that Kanoko remain physically with her as the only alternative to abandonment. She believes she must send Kanoko out of the sustaining but stagnant arrangement. The practical direction changes from holding close to breaking away; the tendency to define Kanoko’s needed next step persists. Section 14 records this as a qualified revision of the original mechanism, not an unqualified prediction success.

Sumika’s continued love is explicitly reaffirmed. She embraces Kanoko and urges her to tell Hime. The embrace should not be treated as proof that the preceding verbal boundary has become irrelevant. Nor does the declaration that this is done because of love certify the wisdom of the method. The source lets affection, self-limitation, pressure, and hope occupy the same scene. They must remain distinguishable if the analysis is to explain why Kanoko can be cared for and hurt at once. [V13/64/i087–089.]

Sumika’s reassurance about Hime also has a knowledge limit. She can reasonably think the present relation differs from middle school. She cannot guarantee that Hime will respond without a painful consequence, and she does not possess Hime’s full account of the old letter. Her prediction is an intervention by an invested participant, not the manga’s advance certification of the next volume’s outcome.

#### 3.3.6 The quoted script does not turn departure into mutual agreement

The older-sister lines about leaving and beginning again give Sumika’s decision a practiced form. Naming Erna explicitly marks the quotation. The fictional relation can make her act legible and can help her sustain it; it cannot prove that Kanoko wants the same lesson. Kanoko’s answer is anger, not a performed grateful release. She calls Sumika foolish, pulls away, and leaves. Sumika does not prevent the departure. [V13/64/i090–092.]

This ending is neither a settled new friendship nor a dissolved workplace sister pact. The dating arrangement has been explicitly withdrawn from by Sumika. The other relational forms remain unsettled, and Kanoko’s response shows that she has not accepted Sumika’s explanation on her own behalf. Their spatial separation renders the cost visible without identifying a single morally pure person. Sumika’s resolve and Kanoko’s pain are both present; neither establishes the content of the conversation they have not yet managed to have.

### 3.4 Shift 65: Hime learns a relationship fact without learning the hidden romantic object

#### 3.4.1 Concern appears first as attention, not a successful diagnosis

The school scenes position Hime near Kanoko while Kanoko is withdrawn. Hime’s lunch and after-school questions are ordinary choices made in response to an unusual friend, not a sudden acquisition of Sumika’s information. She asks whether Kanoko is all right, notices that yesterday’s reassurance has not produced visible ease, and offers a break from work for an outing. [V13/65/i093–098.]

Kanoko’s refusal of that outing is analytically useful. She wants to go to the café. If Sumika were merely a replaceable device for managing Hime’s attention, choosing Hime’s company away from work should automatically solve the problem. It does not. The workplace and the person there now matter in their own right. This does not establish that Kanoko has fallen in love with Sumika; it establishes that her attachment to the relation cannot be exhausted by one instrumental description.

At work, Sumika’s bright greeting and Kanoko’s response fail to fit together smoothly. Hime can see a difficulty without knowing its cause. Her decision to speak with Sumika follows an observed relational discrepancy, not a supernatural insight into a secret couple. Practical time pressure and continuing service remain present around the exchange, so the café does not stop functioning merely because its private relations are strained. [V13/65/i099–104.]

#### 3.4.2 A friend’s importance does not automatically authorize disclosure

Hime asks for a private conversation and explains that Kanoko is important to her. Sumika initially maintains the information boundary: if Kanoko has not told Hime, she will not disclose the private matter in her place. Hime’s concern does not by itself make that refusal disloyal. A relation can be genuinely close while containing material that one participant cannot yet share. [V13/65/i105–108.]

Hime’s reaction nevertheless matters. She feels that a private part of Kanoko’s life is inaccessible to her and available to someone else. The scene does not require the analyst to convert this into romantic jealousy. It is enough that Hime is accustomed to being central to this friend and now fears losing access. The motive becomes more explicit in the next chapter, where she admits wanting Kanoko to look toward her.

Her own memory then complicates any claim that a good friend must always disclose immediately. Hime remembers that she did not initially tell Kanoko about Mitsuki’s confession and kiss because she expected Kanoko to dislike the information. She did not necessarily think of herself as maliciously hiding; she judged what the other person could bear or would want. That comparison makes her current exclusion less simple. She knows from her own conduct that withheld information can coexist with care. It does not tell her whether Kanoko’s present withholding rests on the same cause. [V13/65/i109.]

#### 3.4.3 Hime’s remembered promise is new evidence of retention, not recovered knowledge of authorship

Hime remembers the damaged letter and Kanoko’s proposal that neither should fall for anyone. She calls the old exchange a promise and wonders whether it prevents Kanoko talking about a present romance. This is a major addition to the information map. The early historical scene withheld a full affirmative Hime reply; the present scene establishes that she retained the exchange and believes it can influence Kanoko. [V13/65/i109–110.]

However, the new evidence does not fill every earlier gap. Hime is not shown identifying Kanoko as the anonymous writer. Her hypothesis is that Kanoko may now like someone and feel constrained by the old words. She does not arrive at the true target of Kanoko’s affection. The old promise is consequently shared as a remembered conversation but not as an equally understood confession-management arrangement. A responsible retrospective account must preserve both facts.

Sumika’s abrupt correction redirects the inquiry. The relevant love, she says, is hers for Kanoko. She identifies her own feelings and reveals the dating arrangement and its ending, while withholding Kanoko’s love for Hime. The disclosure solves one mystery for Hime and keeps another intact. The scene is not a complete briefing disguised by a few omitted details; the omitted target is the condition that would most radically change Hime’s interpretation of her own role. [V13/65/i111–115.]

#### 3.4.4 Sumika’s explanation is candid, selective, and still unsettled

Sumika says that she accepted the relation in which Kanoko did not return her love and that she ended it because being a girlfriend was not helping in the way she thought. Her explanation acknowledges both desire and failure. Yet it is not an exhaustive account of her decision. The photo storage and cleanup short show private endurance limits that this account does not simply lay before Hime. The source supports multiple contributing motives without requiring a hidden master motive that renders all spoken concern false. [V13/65/i113–118; S66.5/i160–162.]

Her self-condemnation is also attributed. She calls the relationship selfish and compares it with the kind of romance she previously feared. That is evidence of how she judges herself after the breakup, not a demand that the analyst erase the park’s real reciprocal listening, the withdrawal of the earlier kiss request, or her refusal of bodily repayment. The problem is not that every caring act was unreal. It is that a caring arrangement can become unworkable under unequal feeling and incomplete agreement about its purpose.

When Hime asks why she ended the relationship, Sumika becomes defensive. Hime clarifies that she is not attacking her, and Sumika apologizes for taking the question that way. This is a small but concrete local correction of conversational pressure. It contrasts with the preceding street exchange, in which Kanoko’s request to stop did not produce a pause. Different recipients and different perceived threats elicit different regulatory responses. [V13/65/i116–120.]

Sumika asks Hime to remain attentive to Kanoko as her friend and leaves. Hime now knows a dating history she did not know before, but cannot determine Kanoko’s wishes from it. She had assumed Kanoko’s rejection of romance meant no romance could become personally relevant to her. Dating a person she did not love creates a new interpretive problem: Sumika must have been important enough for Kanoko to agree, but in what way? The final panels preserve Hime’s inquiry rather than turn that reasoning into an answer already obtained. [V13/65/i121–122.]

### 3.5 Shift 66: returning to the stairs without returning to the old arrangement

#### 3.5.1 Hime first makes room for refusal, then becomes less willing to accept reassurance

The chapter reuses the breakup’s language as Kanoko’s memory. This is not another announcement by Sumika. At school, Kanoko is still preoccupied, and Hime offers rooftop lunch and an after-school outing. Kanoko declines because she wants to be alone. Hime initially accepts that limit and returns to an ordinary classroom activity. This actual uptake should remain visible when assessing her more insistent behavior later. [V13/66/i123–129.]

Kanoko’s interiority explains the difficulty without making Hime’s guess correct. The problem is precisely that Hime is implicated in everything Kanoko wants to discuss. Kanoko cannot readily tell her about Sumika without approaching her own concealed love. She also imagines that Hime’s conversation with Sumika may have included an appeal to remain friendly. That imagined appeal is not the recorded content of Hime’s conversation. The reader has seen Hime ask and receive partial information, not instruct Sumika to keep dating or offer a predetermined solution. [V13/66/i128–129; V13/65/i105–120.]

The shift from an initially respected refusal to a renewed approach at the shoe lockers matters. Hime has not simply ignored every no from the beginning. But neither does an earlier respectful pause entitle her to later obtain disclosure by any means. The source depicts an escalation under continuing uncertainty, not a universally correct communication technique.

#### 3.5.2 Hime’s fear becomes part of the conversation

At the lockers, Hime again asks to speak. Kanoko invokes preparation for the next day’s reading event and says nothing is wrong. Hime catches her wrist to stop her leaving and explains that she is worried and wants to help. The touch is an initiating act, not a previously negotiated permission. The analysis should distinguish it from the stronger violence elsewhere in the series without making it disappear because the eventual conversation is useful. [V13/66/i130–132.]

Hime names episodes in which Kanoko’s private state escaped her: the anonymous letter incident, the Blumen period, and the effects of Mitsuki’s confession. She has noticed a pattern. What she lacks is the hidden proposition that would connect it. Kanoko knows the connection and therefore experiences Hime’s approach to the pattern as especially dangerous. The same words can be a concerned question for Hime and a threat of exposure for Kanoko. [V13/66/i133–136.]

Their conflicting theories of friendship become explicit. Kanoko wants to preserve the relation by insisting that nothing has changed. Hime fears that accepting this reassurance while seeing distress will allow the relation to drift away. Neither wants separation; each associates prevention with a different procedure. Kanoko’s statement is protective withholding, while Hime’s question is an attempt to bring a concealed change into discussion. The motive of staying together does not guarantee agreement on how much can be said now.

#### 3.5.3 An incomplete disclosure produces a moment of disproportionate alarm

Hime finally says she knows the matter involves romance and has spoken to Sumika. Kanoko reacts forcefully, gripping Hime’s shoulders and demanding to know what she heard. The reaction is not just evidence of ordinary embarrassment over a breakup. To Kanoko, the dangerous possibility is that Hime has been told who she loves and who wrote the letter. Hime, who does not know those facts, has not anticipated the scale of the alarm she triggers. [V13/66/i137–139.]

This is an especially clear scene for distinguishing first-order information from belief about someone else’s knowledge. Hime possesses the fact of Sumika’s love, their dating, and the breakup. Kanoko temporarily believes Hime may possess more. That second-order fear organizes her bodily and verbal response before the actual disclosure scope is clarified. The scene cannot be analyzed accurately by saying simply that “the secret comes out.” The secret whose exposure Kanoko fears remains unspoken.

Hime turns the moment into movement. She offers to explain in another place and runs, asking Kanoko to follow her this time. This is playful staging and also a way of obtaining the private encounter Kanoko had refused. Kanoko does follow. Both the strategic maneuver and the later willing participation belong in the record. A successful outcome does not make the maneuver wholly nonstrategic; the maneuver does not make every subsequent response unreal. [V13/66/i140–144.]

#### 3.5.4 The staircase clarifies what Hime knows, not everything Kanoko feels

On the stairs, Hime gives the actual scope of Sumika’s disclosure: Sumika loves Kanoko, they dated under unequal feelings, and Sumika ended it. Kanoko’s relief reveals what had been at stake. Hime still does not know Kanoko loves her. The moment also creates a distinct grievance: Kanoko had wanted the dating arrangement kept secret, and Sumika has disclosed it. Sumika protected the Hime-directed love while exposing the existence and ending of their own relation. These two confidentiality questions must not be collapsed into either complete betrayal or complete secrecy preservation. [V13/66/i144–145.]

Hime then acknowledges her own contribution to the pursuit. She feared Kanoko was moving away and wanted her to look toward her. This is a more reciprocal account of concern than an exclusively benevolent description in which Hime has no stake in the result. It can be both caring and self-involving. The acknowledgment does not establish romantic jealousy; the content she names is the fear of losing a friend’s attention and continued presence. [V13/66/i146–148.]

Kanoko answers that she is always looking toward Hime, while continuing to conceal the romantic meaning of that attention. She explains that romance entering their surroundings threatens to change or break the relation. Hime now receives a more explicit statement of the anti-change fear. She still does not receive the old abolition plan, the private exclusive-friend assertion in its original setting, or the anonymous letter’s authorship. This materially improves the information state without automatically satisfying an inherited forecast whose trigger was disclosure of that particular older project.

#### 3.5.5 “Staying together” changes from a freeze to a task

Kanoko recognizes inwardly that remaining motionless does not keep the world from changing. Her outward formulation is consequently less absolute than the old proposal that neither should love anyone: even with things she cannot say and a possible future failure, she wants to remain with Hime. Hime identifies a shared desire not to part and treats it as a reason they might work through difficulties. The agreement is about continuing the relation, not about abolishing all future attachment or treating secrecy as permanently harmless. [V13/66/i149–150.]

The opening of the rooftop door and offered hand place this change inside a familiar composition. Hime again occupies the bright threshold, but now the familiar gesture arrives after a negotiation about withheld information and mutual fear. Kanoko’s remembered Sumika advice enters the sequence, yet the action is not simply obedience to an instruction to confess. She says she will move toward Hime and takes the possibility of further speech seriously. She does not supply the romantic confession at this endpoint. [V13/66/i151–155.]

The distinction matters because the visual resolution could easily tempt a stronger conclusion than the dialogue supports. Brightness, joined movement, and the title’s familiar staircase can register renewed possibility without announcing mutual romance. Likewise, Kanoko’s less rigid view of change need not mean she no longer loves Hime or no longer feels jealous. A developmental revision is most convincing when it preserves the continuing problem it has made more discussable.

#### 3.5.6 Kanoko chooses an unfinished conversation with Sumika

The final rooftop exchange redirects attention from Hime as the hidden romantic object to Sumika as a person whose decision Kanoko wants to understand. Kanoko apologizes for withholding the dating history, says she cannot yet tell everything, and divides the difficulty into a part helped by talking with Hime and another part that belongs with Sumika. Hime accepts that her intervention has been useful without obtaining a complete account. [V13/66/i156.]

Kanoko says she had thought dating would please Sumika and did not expect Sumika to end it. Her anger has not disappeared. Hime supplies the explanation she heard—that Sumika did not want to hold Kanoko back—but Kanoko does not regard that explanation as sufficient. She wants to speak properly with Sumika rather than allow the arrangement to end on that account alone. [V13/66/i157–158.]

This is positive evidence that Sumika’s place matters to Kanoko, not merely that a useful service has been withdrawn. It is still not an equivalent romantic declaration. Kanoko may be seeking the restoration of a confidant, an answer to a perceived abandonment, a revised form of closeness, or some combination she has not yet articulated. The source gives a chosen next action—talk to Sumika—not the completed answer to what that action will reveal.

The volume therefore ends with a commitment to encounter another person’s response. It does not end with an actual new Sumika conversation, a rescinded breakup, a completed festival reading, or Hime learning Kanoko’s love. The difference between intending dialogue and conducting it is the final prospective boundary. The next source unit must test what happens when the newly claimed agency meets Sumika’s own limits and wishes; V13 does not supply that outcome in advance.

## 4. Separate short, edition matter, and production testimony

### 4.1 Shift 66.5: removing an image from display is not destroying it

The cleanup short resumes Sumika’s action after the park outing. At i160 she removes the signed photograph of Kanoko from its displayed position and places the **intact** photograph in a drawer. The sound and hand movement at the earlier i062 coda do not depict a torn print. This matters especially in a volume that really does show a letter being torn: the two objects must not be assigned the same fate because both are associated with painful love. [S66.5/i160; compare V13/62/i029–030 and V13/63/i062.]

Storage can still be an emotionally consequential act. The photograph’s earlier visibility organized a recurrent encounter with Kanoko’s image. Putting it away changes that arrangement without proving that Sumika wants every trace of Kanoko gone. The rest of the short makes a simple “love has ended” interpretation particularly implausible. Her cleaning removes the immediate signs of shared domestic time, while the bedding still carries a reminder of Kanoko’s presence that she finds difficult to resist. The analysis can describe that lingering attachment without reproducing erotic detail or inventing another encounter.

The ordinary sequence is carefully material: household cleanup, bath-related tidying, bedding and laundry decisions, putting away sleeping arrangements, and preparing the room for its usual occupant. These acts give a concrete form to trying to regain a manageable private space. They also provide everyday behavioral evidence for Sumika that is not reducible to her café persona. Her competence at restoring the room does not imply equivalent ease in restoring emotional distance. [S66.5/i160–162.]

### 4.2 The short supplies a private limit that qualifies the spoken breakup account

Sumika explicitly plans to tell Kanoko the next day that they should stop dating. She says she cannot bear further sleepovers and kisses in the present arrangement. The placement is consequently specific: **after the park day, before the next-day breakup**, not after the rooftop ending. The short adds access to Sumika’s private state before a choice already shown in the mainline. It does not reverse the choice afterward. [S66.5/i161–162.]

This evidence qualifies, rather than simply refutes, her account to Kanoko and Hime. She can genuinely think that Kanoko’s unaddressed love is keeping her in pain and genuinely find their own unequal intimacy painful to endure. The source does not establish that every caring explanation is a lie masking the second motive. It does establish that a model of purely selfless liberation is incomplete. ### 6.2 Specialness has at least three different meanings here

For historical Hime, special friendship includes chosen company, public recognition, and freedom to speak without the usual presentation. For historical Kanoko, it increasingly means remaining outside the category of rejected suitors and protected from a future romantic rival. For present Sumika, being special to Kanoko includes the hope that care, closeness, and dating might become personally reciprocated. The same vocabulary of importance cannot be used as though it defines one shared status. [REL01–REL08; i007–034, i047–057.]

The park gives Kanoko an actual way to make Sumika feel helped. It does not follow that this supplies everything Sumika wants from dating. Conversely, Sumika’s unmet romantic wish does not make Kanoko’s contribution worthless. The analysis must preserve the intermediate category that the series repeatedly makes difficult to see: a relation can be meaningful and reciprocal in some forms of care while unequal in romantic desire. V13’s ending asks how to speak about that relation, not whether it can be erased by choosing a simpler label.

The historical comparison with Mitsuki is likewise narrower than a rivalry ranking. Kanoko is disturbed because Mitsuki retained importance after a refusal, not because V13 proves Mitsuki has won Hime’s romantic love. Hime’s established nonromantic answer remains intact. The comparison revises Kanoko’s assumptions about risk and survival, not the actual classification of the HM bond. [REL05; inherited WY7-C12, WY8-C11.]

### 6.3 Ending dating does not settle every other relationship form

The clearest status change is Sumika’s explicit withdrawal from dating. The public sister relation, workplace cooperation, confidant availability, and possibility of personal friendship are not each separately renegotiated at the street scene. Calling it a complete dissolution of every bond would overstate what is shown; calling it merely a proposal without force would understate Sumika’s stated decision. The adequate exit description is: dating has been ended by Sumika, Kanoko disputes the explanation and wants to talk, and the future form of their connection is not jointly settled. [REL10/REL11/REL20.]

Kanoko’s later wish to speak must not be treated as authority to override that withdrawal. She can seek understanding, name what Sumika means to her, and ask for a different arrangement. Sumika can still have limits. The unresolved issue is precisely how these separate agencies will meet. An analysis that predicts romance as the necessary reward for good care would reproduce the debt logic the park and V12 challenge.

### 9.1 The right to withdraw and the wish to direct are not the same claim

A crucial restraint on the analysis is that Sumika is not required to keep dating because Kanoko finds the breakup painful. Her personal limits do not need to be justified by a successful theory of Kanoko’s future. The problem is that she gives the limit together with a prescription: disclose to Hime, trust the relationship will survive, and interpret departure through a liberating script. The first proposition concerns Sumika’s participation; the others concern Kanoko’s next act and Hime’s response. The latter remain contestable even if the former is final. [AG12–AG15.]

Kanoko’s insistence at the ending has a corresponding limit. A request for a proper conversation can restore her role as an interpreter of her own experience. It cannot make Sumika owe a romantic relationship. The next volume may supply a conversation, refusal, renegotiation, or a different development; V13 establishes only the wish to ask rather than treating Sumika’s account as the last word on both people.

### 9.2 A later useful conversation does not retrospectively authorize its every method

Hime and Kanoko reach a constructive exchange through a sequence that contains initial respectful distance, renewed insistence, wrist contact, an alarmed shoulder grip, and a playful pursuit. The final offered hand and chosen speech should be preserved, but not used to erase those earlier acts. The appropriate analysis is sequential: request, response, pressure, clarification, and uptake occur at different moments. [AG19–AG23.]

The same principle governs the earlier night, the park, and the breakup. Sumika can ask carefully about contact in one scene and continue after a spoken stop in another. This is evidence for a conditional regulatory model, not grounds for declaring every episode either equivalent to the hotel coercion or immune to criticism because it is less severe. Differences of force, stakes, available exit, and subsequent response are part of the evidence.

The letter and photograph provide a controlled contrast. Kanoko destroys the letter to prevent an identified romantic encounter, then speaks a substitute account of its author.
