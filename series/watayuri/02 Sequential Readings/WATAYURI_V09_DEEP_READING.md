## 1. The volume’s governing problem

Volume 9 makes the wish to help inseparable from the wish to matter. Sumika does not cease to be a helpful person when her interest in Kanoko becomes more personally demanding. She still teaches, listens, improvises effective public scenes, offers hospitality, and notices situations Kanoko would find painful. The difficulty is that she increasingly wants those acts to establish a particular position: to be the person who understands Kanoko best, receives her special expression, and can give her a happiness that Hime does not. The care is not exposed as entirely counterfeit. Its coexistence with a competitive wish becomes impossible to ignore. [V09/42/i017–022; V09/43/i025–028, i037–045; V09/44/i063–074; V09/45/i085–097.]

The photograph is the volume’s most precise instrument for this distinction. It preserves a smile but not, by itself, the relationship to which that smile was addressed. Sumika can own a print, take the photograph, appear beside Kanoko, invent an attractive story about the scene, and request an affectionate inscription. None makes her the original object of Kanoko’s attention. Conversely, Kanoko’s gratitude and occasional smiles toward Sumika are real: the amused response at i038 directly prevents the claim that she never smiles at her. What Sumika wants is not the mere presence of a pleasant expression. It is a particular meaning and a preferred place within the expression. [V09/42/i018–020; V09/43/i027–028, i038; V09/44/i064–074.]

Nene’s interpretation makes this problem speakable before Sumika can accept its category. The subsequent dream, bodily reaction, jealousy, and private reconsideration give the romantic reading independent support beyond Nene’s suggestion. Nevertheless, the volume does not supply a simple progression from denial to a settled self-label. Sumika shifts from insisting that her feeling is only sisterly to resolving that she will not make it romantic. She recognizes a competitive motive in a supposedly helpful invitation, yet answers that recognition by renewing the ideal of a selfless elder sister and withdrawing a kind of consultation. Insight, shame, restraint, and concealment operate together. [V09/44/i049–074; V09/45/i075–097.]

Goeidō’s return then exposes the cost of making the earlier café rupture into a complete explanation of every present problem. Sumika’s warning has a real history behind it; the returning visitor’s charm cannot erase that history. But Nene’s stated wish to leave the relationship closed is followed by a private message asking to meet. Goeidō’s claim that she wants to apologize is not yet an apology received by Sumika. Kanoko’s hope that such an apology might repair Sumika’s attitude toward romance is her own theory, formed without knowing Sumika’s new personal stake. The volume brings these incomplete accounts into contact and stops before any proposed meeting resolves them. [V09/46/i111–134.]

**Central distinction:** the right to have a feeling, the ability to describe it, the wish to help, the authority to intervene, and the ability to remain someone’s confidant are different questions. V09 neither condemns all protective action as possessiveness nor grants a protective person automatic authority over other people’s relationships. Its strongest evidence lies in the specific points where a person acts on one question while believing she has settled another. [V09/45/i091–097; V09/46/i122–134.]

## 3. Sequential, form-sensitive close reading

### 3.1 Shift 42: producing a sister image does not determine its private meaning

#### 3.1.1 Public fluency, practical schedules, and a newcomer’s incomplete briefing

The media interview opens with a small but useful asymmetry. Hime’s practiced pleasantness does not supply the fictional school’s motto on demand; Mitsuki’s cue repairs the public exchange. The incident is neither a return to complete incompetence nor evidence that Hime’s successful persona is effortless. A jointly sustained presentation can survive because one participant supplies the information the other lacks. Hime’s later concern about recognition at school also distinguishes enjoying public admiration from wanting every real-world audience to know where she works. Mai’s assurance concerns what will be published, not an unlimited guarantee of anonymity. [V09/42/i001, i005–006.]

The post-interview day is not ordinary opening time. Recovery has made a four-person shift with one person off possible, alongside meetings and media work. Sumika’s wish to visit as a guest even on a day off is a concrete sign that Liebe is more than an obligation to her; Mai’s instruction to rest is a counterweight to treating management as interested only in extracting more labor. Neither detail proves the workplace harmless or cancels the earlier recovery concealment. The volume is showing a current arrangement with genuine attractions and practical limits. [V09/42/i006, i010–011.]

Nene introduces **Shinooka Haruko / 篠岡春子** as another kitchen worker. Haruko’s uncertainty concerns the establishment’s fictional social structure. It does not establish inability to do kitchen work. The distinction matters because the other employees readily treat role knowledge as obvious once they have internalized it. Her questions make the institution’s conceptual incompleteness visible without requiring a new external theory of it. Mai’s tolerance of partial understanding for a kitchen role also shows that different jobs require different degrees of participation in the public fiction. [V09/42/i011–015; native i012.]

#### 3.1.2 The demonstration and the business-hours limit

Sumika demonstrates sisterhood by placing Kanoko in an intimate-looking configuration. The large floral presentation offers the new worker—and the reader—a persuasive image before anyone has agreed on the full meaning of the relation. Kanoko’s embarrassed response and request to reserve such behavior for business hours are therefore essential evidence, not noise around an otherwise definitive pair image. The boundary is contextual: it does not say that all touch is forbidden, that the pact is unwanted, or that every in-hours act is authorized. [V09/42/i013–015.]

The same limit returns when Sumika resumes the language of a dependable elder sister. This recurrence shows Kanoko actively distinguishing the usefulness of the relationship from the setting in which its ceremonial intimacy should be performed. She can appreciate a confidant while resisting a certain presentation of that bond off duty. Sumika’s surprise is equally important. An established role gives her a familiar repertoire, but it does not ensure that the other person welcomes that repertoire in every private setting. [V09/42/i020–022.]

#### 3.1.3 The smile, the gift, and the missing address

The proposed album promotion turns work-trip memories and new interior photographs into sellable objects. For Kanoko, it also makes Hime photographs more naturally accessible. Her gratitude to Sumika is explicit and contextually justified. Yet Sumika wants the photographed expression reproduced for her, then notices Hime’s gift and wonders whether a gift of her own might secure such a response. The thought connects material exchange, attention and personal recognition without proving that her earlier help was merely a transaction. [V09/42/i015–020.]

Kanoko’s answer is generous in one sense and disappointing in another: Sumika has already done enough through help with Hime’s return and the birthday letter. It is an acknowledgment of real benefit, but not an answer to the unspoken question about being preferred. The very success of the supporter role can leave Sumika unsatisfied because its successful outcome is still organized around Hime. The important asymmetry is not “one cares and the other feels nothing.” They value the same relationship for different immediate reasons. [V09/42/i018–021.]

The closing group pose and Sumika’s interior vow join protecting the salon, its companions and her younger sister. Those commitments are positively established. The chapter has not yet made a romantic self-classification explicit. It has, however, shown a new pressure inside the claim that being helpful is enough: Sumika wants the relationship to return a particular kind of attention, not only evidence that her assistance worked. [V09/42/i019–022.]

### 3.2 Shift 43: being indispensable is not the same as making the desired future possible

#### 3.2.1 Signatures and the private use of a public role

The promotion is now actually operating. Kanoko receives a request for her own autograph and initially compares its plainness unfavorably with Sumika’s practiced flourish. Sumika’s response recognizes different staff styles rather than making Kanoko imitate one universal performer. This is small-scale mentoring with a concrete object and an immediate social result. It also adds a low-stakes domain to Kanoko’s characterization: she can be embarrassed by attention to herself while still performing the requested work. [V09/43/i023–027.]

Sumika then wants an inscription on her own copy, with an affectionate elder-sister address. When Kanoko begins to repeat the earlier restriction, Sumika invokes the fact that they are currently in business hours. The line exploits the literal form of the boundary while leaving its purpose and desired audience unresolved. It need not be read as a calculated campaign to override Kanoko, but it cannot be treated as proof that the private request is wanted simply because its timing fits the words of an earlier condition. The scene distinguishes compliance with a time phrase from a fully shared understanding of what the phrase was protecting. [V09/43/i027–028.]

#### 3.2.2 Hime’s three-person happiness and Kanoko’s unsuccessful self-reassurance

Hime’s pleasure in admiration is not hidden from the reader. The renewed praise of her hairstyle makes her happy; the pleasing performance and the private enjoyment support one another. Mitsuki’s affectionate praise and hair contact prompt Kanoko to reassure herself that Hime reacts similarly to praise from anyone. That is a character’s explanatory maneuver, not a conclusive account of Hime’s feelings. Kanoko’s own attempted compliment also shows the distance between what she wants to say and what she can comfortably deliver. [V09/43/i029–033.]

Mitsuki acknowledges Kanoko’s important friendship and particular knowledge of Hime. Hime says she values Kanoko as much as her elder sister and likes the three-person arrangement. Her whispered emphasis gives Kanoko good reason to believe this is a sincere preference. Nevertheless, receiving assurance of nonexclusive importance does not satisfy the desire to be uniquely important. Kanoko has achieved an outcome she said she wanted, yet still experiences Mitsuki as a rival. The mismatch is evidence that the stated arrangement and the deeper emotional requirement were never identical. [V09/43/i033–036.]

This is a strong continuation of the nonexclusive-friendship problem, but it is **not** confirmation of WY3-PR02. Hime has not been told the actual abolition proposal or exclusive-friend claim. Her sincere preference cannot be retrospectively converted into an informed response to a disclosure that did not occur. The scene deepens the question that forecast preserves; it does not meet its original test. [V09/43/i034–035; original WY3-PR02.]

#### 3.2.3 A confidant who can hear what Hime cannot

Away from work, Kanoko admits that she sometimes hates Mitsuki despite the improved arrangement and cannot say this to Hime. Sumika’s value as a listener is therefore relationally specific. She receives the feeling that would threaten Kanoko’s preferred image before its primary object. This is a real expansion of Kanoko’s support network, not only dependency in a new costume. It also creates a circuit in which the listener is repeatedly asked to absorb distress about someone else’s centrality. [V09/43/i036–040.]

Sumika momentarily adopts the elegant elder-sister register, then jokes about being an ordinary schoolgirl in a fast-food restaurant. Kanoko smiles at her. This small exchange is significant counterevidence: there are actual responsive pleasures between them that are not merely fantasies in Sumika’s head. The later interpretation must not claim that every Kanoko smile belongs to Hime. Rather, Sumika attaches special importance to one particular smile and increasingly reads its address as a measure of her own position. [V09/43/i038; native-page recheck.]

Kanoko recognizes that Sumika has changed from the earlier demand that she give Hime up. Sumika now calls herself an ally of the status quo. The phrase seems modest, but Kanoko’s next questions expose how much it asks: how can the status quo be preserved if Hime herself changes? Rejecting Mitsuki now is not a promise that Hime will never experience romantic love. Kanoko’s fear is organized around that future possibility, not solely around an existing rival’s current conduct. [V09/43/i039–041.]

#### 3.2.4 The maid fantasy defines the desired condition, not the future

Kanoko’s account of Hime’s wealthy-marriage ambition produces an initially surprising distinction. A husband is not necessarily the intolerable competitor. A formal marriage without romantic attachment could, in her imagined future, leave her place intact; she pictures herself remaining nearby as a maid. The central condition is that Hime not give romantic love to someone else. This is evidence about Kanoko’s desired arrangement and confidence in Hime’s old ambition, not a prediction of a marriage, a service occupation, or permanent emotional incapacity. [V09/43/i041–043.]

The disclosure also moves Hime’s private aspiration to another person under a request for secrecy. It establishes Kanoko’s act and Sumika’s access to the information. It does not establish that Hime authorized this retelling. The secrecy request can coexist with a real need for advice; that combination is exactly why confidentiality and permission must be tracked separately. [V09/43/i041–043.]

Kanoko asks whether Sumika will help. Sumika qualifies the situation by noting that no concrete action is presently planned, yet continues the promise of support. The scene is closer to the trigger of WY4-PR01 than the analogous earlier consultations, but it has not yet supplied a clear limit on securing the desired relational outcome. The later home exchange must be read before adjudicating the test. At this point, Sumika is promising availability while privately recognizing that the arrangement still will not produce the smile she wants. [V09/43/i044–045.]

Kanoko’s diary scene closes the chapter on unease rather than satisfaction. The practice of recording Hime-centered experience persists; the question of whether this arrangement is acceptable remains active. The volume has not portrayed a completed transition from exclusivity to effortless plural attachment. It has shown a person articulating the condition that makes plural attachment tolerable to her—and beginning to doubt whether that condition can be secured. [V09/43/i046.]

### 3.3 Shift 44: the interpretation arrives before the acceptable self-description

#### 3.3.1 Nene challenges both the question and the questioner

Sumika’s opening question to Nene risks treating unrequited same-sex love as something to which a category of people simply becomes accustomed. Nene objects to the premise and its tactlessness. Her correction is not a denial that she has relevant experience; it is a refusal to let that experience be converted into a generic type. Sumika’s exaggerated affectionate thanks and Nene’s further rebuke keep their own familiar but asymmetrical interaction visible. Nene can provide useful guidance without welcoming every form of familiarity. [V09/44/i049–051.]

The consultation turns when Sumika describes Kanoko’s preoccupation with Hime, then says that she herself would not make Kanoko so sad. Nene does not treat this as transparent evidence of disinterested concern. She asks what the comparison means and suggests that the consultation sounds like Sumika’s own romantic problem. The inference is grounded in Sumika’s repeated allocation of attention and her implicit claim to be a better alternative, not in one isolated blush. [V09/44/i052–056.]

Nene’s hypothetical Hime–Kanoko couple is an interpretive test, not a depicted future or actual relationship. It asks whether Sumika could support the outcome supposedly required by her ally position. Sumika’s insistence that she does not like women and is unlike Nene is evidence of a self-description under pressure. It does not authorize a diagnosis, a permanent orientation label, or the conclusion that Nene has complete access to her interior life. Nene herself qualifies the reading and acknowledges possible error. [V09/44/i055–058.]

That qualification matters to the evidentiary architecture. The romantic interpretation becomes stronger through the remainder of the chapter and the following one; it does not become true simply because an experienced character pronounced it. Nene initiates a question which Sumika’s own attention, jealousy, dream and private reflection subsequently make difficult to dismiss. [V09/44/i059–074; V09/45/i075–097.]

#### 3.3.2 A useful performance produces an unmanageable reaction

Sumika’s defensive distinction is not just between romance and friendship. It is between an ideal of protective sisterly love and a category of romance she associates with selfishness and damage. Her older account of Nene and Goeidō supplies the feared example. The memory image here adds no independently new café history; it shows the present explanatory use of already significant material. [V09/44/i059–060.]

Hime’s observation that Sumika especially calls Kanoko cute is independent interpersonal feedback. It does not settle the category, but it complicates Sumika’s explanation that nothing distinctive is happening. The shift to actual customer work then prevents the chapter from becoming only introspection. Kanoko needs help explaining a photograph; Sumika invents an effective forest anecdote and sustains the sister image for the guests. She remains professionally capable while her private understanding is destabilized. [V09/44/i061–067.]

The close performance gives Sumika an affective response she cannot comfortably absorb. Kanoko’s visible expression is not, by itself, a confession; the important new evidence is the sequence of Sumika’s reaction, interruption, retreat and continued thought. The story separates the successful scene received by customers from the bodily disturbance and self-questioning carried backstage. [V09/44/i065–069.]

#### 3.3.3 The smile’s production history changes the observer’s claim

The subsequent photograph-production recollection explains the chapter’s opening image. Hime prompted the desired smile when Sumika could not, and Sumika took the picture while Hime maintained that response. This is a disclosed production context for the image, not an additional current photo session invented from a repeated composition. The remembered arrangement changes Sumika’s interpretation of what she had hoped the photograph meant. [V09/44/i047, i070–073.]

The move from heartbeat to pain is formally consequential. Sumika first reacts to an apparently intimate expression within their public performance, then sees the special smile addressed to Hime. Her interior language makes the loss possessive: the smile is not hers. This does not establish a legitimate ownership claim over Kanoko’s feeling. It establishes that she has begun wanting something that the neutral helper account cannot describe. [V09/44/i066–074.]

A restrained conclusion is stronger than either extreme. “She has always only wanted to control Kanoko” would discard the actual care and pleasure already depicted. “She only wants Kanoko to be happy” would discard the particular pain of seeing Hime supply that happiness. The evidence supports a developing romantic and competitive investment alongside real protectiveness. Sumika’s distress at her own feeling also shows that recognizing an impulse is not the same as endorsing or acting on it. [V09/44/i071–074.]

### 3.4 Shift 45: the useful invitation and the withheld explanation

#### 3.4.1 The dream is exposure to a wish, not evidence of reciprocity

The opening scene appears to offer exactly the direct recognition Sumika wants: Kanoko declares affection toward her and asks for a response. The awakening and subsequent explicit reference to the dream identify its status. None of the imagined Kanoko speech establishes what the real Kanoko knows, wants or permits. It does, however, provide strong evidence about the question that is occupying Sumika, especially when paired with her later confusion over the real visitor’s reference to her feelings. [V09/45/i075–079, i089–090.]

The title’s retrospective formulation, “芽生えていたから,” places emphasis on something that had already begun developing. The chapter does not need Nene to manufacture the feeling from nothing. Nene’s words make it available to attention and language; Sumika’s attempt to blame the suggestion is itself part of the defensive account. The source still withholds an uncomplicated, accepted self-label, and the analysis should retain that distinction. [V09/45/i077–079; V09/42/i018–022.]

The meeting exposes a practical cost of this absorption: Sumika has not been listening carefully. Hime asks for clarification and accurately restates the proposed event, whereas Sumika privately admits distraction. This is a local reversal of their usual distribution of role fluency, not a claim that Sumika has lost general competence. It also places the private conflict inside a workplace that still has real preparation to complete. [V09/45/i078–081.]

#### 3.4.2 A fictional elder sister lets go; actual rehearsal remains unperformed

The late-September school-festival event will include a small staged reading adapted from **乙女の心臓**. The described episode concerns strongly bonded Blumen sisters and a marriage proposal for the younger sister; the **elder sister ultimately chooses to let her go**. This is the scripted plot explicitly given on i081, not a source-confirmed future for the actual performers. [V09/45/i080–081; native i081.]

The choice of Sumika and Kanoko as leads follows their Blumen pairing. Kanoko’s difficulty is embarrassment about rehearsing in the salon, not a demonstrated inability to read or perform. Hime offers practice at her home, then includes Mitsuki in the three-person arrangement. Kanoko initially responds positively and then withdraws, invoking the difficulty of aligning days off and removing all three from the shift. The practical obstacle is real within the discussion; her continuing jealousy makes it insufficient as a complete account of her reaction. [V09/45/i081–084.]

Sumika’s alternative—two leads practicing at her home—is operationally sensible. Later self-reflection also identifies a wish to show that she understands Kanoko better than Hime does. These facts should not be forced into a choice between innocent logistics and pure manipulation. The invitation can solve a real scheduling problem and serve an unacknowledged competitive need. The more demanding interpretation preserves both, then asks what the invitee actually believes she accepted. [V09/45/i083–086, i092–094.]

No completed rehearsal or festival performance is shown in this volume. The visit occurs, but Kanoko explains that practice was not her immediate reason for coming. A plan approved by the manager and an actual visit are distinct from the work outcome that originally justified the plan. [V09/45/i085–091.]

#### 3.4.3 Hospitality cannot supply the missing romantic category

Kanoko initiates the timing of the visit, and Sumika offers permission not to come rather than simply compelling attendance. At home, she gives a cold drink and tries to make her guest comfortable. These are concrete acts of hospitality. They complicate any model in which the invitation’s private desire makes every component coercive. [V09/45/i085–088.]

The ambiguity of Kanoko’s reference to her feelings briefly allows Sumika’s dream to intrude on interpretation. Kanoko then specifies that she wants to discuss Hime and can speak about this only to Sumika. Sumika’s immediate hope or alarm and subsequent shame are not a reciprocal confession interrupted by bad timing. They are the difference between the meaning she momentarily wants and the meaning the speaker actually supplies. [V09/45/i089–091.]

This is also a cost of confidentiality. Sumika is valuable precisely because Kanoko cannot take the same confession of jealousy to Hime. Yet Sumika now has a private stake that makes endlessly hearing the confession painful. The volume does not say that a confidant owes unlimited emotional availability. It shows that changing the terms without disclosing the reason can make the withdrawal difficult for the other person to understand. [V09/45/i090–097; V09/46/i132–134.]

#### 3.4.4 A boundary can be real and still be delivered through fear and concealment

Sumika proposes some distance from Hime. Kanoko asks whether the supposedly helpful invitation was intended to separate them. Sumika’s outward denial is followed by inward doubt and then a sharper recognition: she wanted to demonstrate superior understanding and experience a kind of victory. Kanoko apologizes for suspecting the supportive person she trusts, while the reader has just been shown why the suspicion was not wholly groundless. This asymmetry is more precise than calling the entire conversation either honest reconciliation or calculated deception. [V09/45/i092–095.]

Sumika says she will not interfere with Kanoko’s love or make her cry. She then insists that Kanoko maintain a good relationship with Mitsuki because Hime values them both, warning that repeated hostility could cost Hime’s regard. The statement sets a real limit on the ally role: support cannot simply mean eliminating the other valued relationship. Its persuasive means nevertheless appeals to Kanoko’s feared rejection, and its delivery is shaped by Sumika’s own shame. The scene strongly supports the need to distinguish kinds of help, without automatically satisfying the narrower forecast trigger; §14 retains that distinction rather than scoring the thematic resemblance as a confirmed prediction. [V09/45/i095–097; WY4-PR01 below.]

The final request to stop consultations like this one changes an established channel of support. Its scope should not be inflated into “I will never help you again,” nor softened into an inconsequential suggestion. Kanoko had described this as the topic she could tell only Sumika; that particular availability is now withdrawn. Sumika’s interior resolve to protect a precious younger sister and not make the feeling romantic is not transmitted as a full explanation. The following day, Kanoko is still trying to understand what changed. [V09/45/i097–099.]

The chapter ends with Goeidō’s arrival. This is not merely an unrelated cliffhanger attached to a completed private problem. The returning person is central to Sumika’s theory that romance destroys the café, precisely when Sumika is using that theory to interpret and restrain herself. The narrative has brought back the apparently settled example before the present conflict can be closed. [V09/45/i100; V09/46/i101–134.]

### 3.5 Shift 46: the past returns as several people’s unfinished present

#### 3.5.1 A graduate’s visit is not employee reinstatement

Mai introduces **Goeidō Yōko / 五影堂葉子** through the café’s graduate fiction. The written contact slip later supplies **Gotō Yōko / 後藤葉子** as an ordinary-name witness for the same person. This is an alias addition to the existing historical subject, not a second character. The actual operational state is a former staff member visiting as a customer. Neither the old visitor pass nor the graduate title reinstates her employment or old sister pact. [V09/45/i100; V09/46/i103–104, i121–122.]

Mai initially handles the visit, and Nene stays in the kitchen. Nene’s startled response is visible, but her statement that she is all right and her continued work must also be retained. The scene establishes management of a potentially difficult encounter, not proof that Nene experiences no difficulty. Goeidō’s inspection of the tea menu then lets her infer that Sumika remains while Nene is absent from the displayed salon roster. It does not give her knowledge that Nene still works in the kitchen. [V09/46/i105–108.]

Goeidō’s choice of Sumika’s recommended tea after Mai suggests her own makes the old relationship relevant through an ordinary customer decision. Her magazine-based explanation presents the visit as casual nostalgia. Later she gives Kanoko a more specific apology purpose. The difference should be recorded as changed presentation and an unverified account of motive, not automatically as proof that one statement is false and the other exhaustively true. The café is a setting in which socially acceptable explanations can also regulate who receives sensitive information. [V09/46/i106–109, i122–123.]

Sumika’s absence is established. Her later suspicion that Goeidō deliberately chose it is not. The analysis cannot convert a suspicious coincidence into verified scheduling knowledge or a premeditated plan without another source route. [V09/46/i108, i126–127.]

#### 3.5.2 Identity knowledge reaches Kanoko later than it reached the reader

Kanoko now recognizes that Nene was the former Saionji of Sumika’s account and confirms the equivalence directly. The earlier volumes had supplied this identity to the reader, but that did not make it knowledge shared by every character. This is a consequential update: Kanoko can now ask the person described as injured rather than relying only on Sumika’s protective history. [V09/46/i110–113.]

Nene says that she and Yōko have not been in contact since the departure, that there is nothing she wants to discuss, and that the relationship is over. Mai asks for controlled circulation of the background rather than immediate retelling to everyone. These are actual verbal boundaries and information-management decisions. Neither should be discarded because later evidence complicates them. At this moment, Kanoko has reason to think reopening the relationship is unwanted. [V09/46/i111–113.]

Kanoko’s next reaction reveals the limits of judging history from the present visitor’s appearance. Goeidō does not look as bad as the destructive account led her to expect. This is evidence of Kanoko’s impression, not counterproof to the reported harm. The pleasant customer, the former romantic partner, and the person Sumika resents can all be the same individual. A readable current smile is not a complete moral biography. [V09/46/i114–117.]

#### 3.5.3 Nene’s private initiative changes the current account, not every earlier truth

The reader sees Nene checking her phone in distress. Mitsuki notices her expression but is not shown reading the private content. The subsequent display of messages identifies an actual initiative from Nene: she says that she is still at Liebe, now in the kitchen, and wants to meet. Goeidō’s spoken wish to meet in return follows. The source therefore moves beyond Kanoko’s assumption that nothing remains to be discussed. It does not yet depict a transmitted reply, an arranged time, or a completed reunion. [V09/46/i118–119, i125–126.]

There is a real tension between Nene’s earlier statement and this later action. Several explanations remain possible: she changed her mind, concealed a wish, discovered that the visit affected her more than expected, or wants a meeting for reasons not equivalent to restarting the romance. The pages establish the shift more securely than any one explanation. “It was always a lie” and “she is simply over it” both overstate what is known. Nor does present desire authorize Sumika, Kanoko, or the analyst to decide what Nene must do next. [V09/46/i112–113, i118–119, i125–126.]

The sequence is particularly important for the older protective model. Nene is not merely the passive object of other people’s theories about her harm. Her present decision supplies another source of agency. That agency can coexist with vulnerability; the fact that she initiates contact does not prove that a future encounter would be safe, wanted in every respect, or free of conflicting motives. [V09/46/i125–126; compare the accepted V04 account of Nene’s chosen earlier relationship.]

#### 3.5.4 An apology offered through an intermediary has not yet repaired anything

At the departure exchange, Goeidō recognizes Kanoko’s sister connection with Sumika and asks her to pass along contact information. She says that she came to apologize, acknowledges doing something hurtful and then leaving, and asks to explain the earlier event. She also appeals to the idea that love cannot simply be stopped. These are consequential statements, but their epistemic level remains reported intention and self-explanation. Sumika has not heard the explanation directly or accepted an apology. [V09/46/i121–123.]

Kanoko’s hesitation is not ignorance of the resentment. She tells Goeidō that Sumika is still affected and that an apology may not change that. The visitor’s acknowledgment of this obstacle makes the exchange more persuasive without independently proving sincerity. Her manner can be read as genuine regret, strategic recruitment of an intermediary, or some combination. The present witness supports caution and competing readings rather than a settled diagnosis of manipulation or exoneration. [V09/46/i122–124.]

Kanoko then constructs a hopeful causal chain: if the earlier conflict can be cleared up, perhaps Sumika’s dislike of romance and her recent strange behavior can change. This is not an absurd thought given the explanation Sumika has repeatedly offered. It is incomplete because Kanoko does not know about Sumika’s jealousy, dream, or private reconsideration of the invitation. A partial but coherent model makes the outside apology appear capable of doing work it may not be able to do. [V09/46/i124–125; V09/45/i093–097.]

#### 3.5.5 The protective explanation closes the conversation before the object is resolved

At the next-day briefing, Sumika protests that she was not informed and would have come even on her day off. Mai explains the graduate/customer treatment and refuses to ban someone merely for having visited. The manager’s decision is not forgiveness on behalf of everyone, a judgment that no prior harm occurred, or proof that future access will be unrestricted. It is a current distinction between a difficult history and sufficient grounds for a particular customer sanction. [V09/46/i126–129.]

Kanoko’s report that Goeidō wanted to meet Sumika triggers an angry response. Sumika’s alarm has a real historical basis, yet its force also makes it harder for Kanoko to supply information. The momentary shock after her outburst does not by itself complete an apology. Mai offers to handle contact, rejects an escalating demand for private details, and stresses distance from a person who is no longer an employee. These are actual limits on intervention, not a completed settlement of the underlying conflict. [V09/46/i129–131.]

Sumika rejects the apology account as attractive words used to manage people and says that Goeidō’s conduct invariably produces trouble. The history gives this suspicion weight; the total formulation nevertheless outruns what this volume independently establishes about the present purpose. Kanoko now hears the warning through her own recent experience of the withdrawn consultation. She interprets Sumika as becoming strange whenever romance is discussed. The reader has grounds to see why this explanation is both partly responsive and incomplete. [V09/46/i132–133.]

Mai stops the discussion because opening time is approaching and offers further time if needed. That pause is not consent to any unilateral meeting, delivery of the slip, or private intervention. The final sequence isolates Kanoko with the contact paper and depicts it being crumpled in her hand. It does not show a sent message, the paper being thrown away, or a settled refusal to use the information. **The endpoint is an unresolved choice under an incomplete explanation, not the first completed act of the next volume.** [V09/46/i133–134; native i134.]

## 4. Separately bounded components

### 4.1 Shift 46.5: asking what a sister is reveals that there is no single shared answer

Haruko’s question is not answered by the institutional definition alone. Mai can explain the source fiction, the connection between senior and junior, and mutual support. Haruko still wants to know what the participants themselves mean. The kitchen worker’s incomplete familiarity therefore performs useful analytical work: it distinguishes reciting the concept from understanding how different people inhabit it. Nene’s earlier irritation at a work-time question also preserves the practical setting; the question is not asked in a world without tasks to complete. [V09/S46.5/i136–137.]

Mitsuki describes mutual support, development and admiration, explicitly finding in Hime capacities she feels she lacks. Haruko’s follow-up about Hime’s reciprocal respect immediately exposes the inadequacy of assuming a straightforward hierarchy. Hime’s response and Mitsuki’s annoyance do not cancel the bond, but they show that the same formal pair can contain different descriptions of its asymmetry. Neither a senior label nor a reverent public manner establishes private obedience. [V09/S46.5/i138–139.]

Kanoko emphasizes a person who helps her. She rejects the marriage comparison, while Sumika describes a close relationship containing much of what lovers have but with romance removed. The comparison is evidence of Sumika’s current normative distinction, not an objective definition that settles the actual feelings involved. Her hope to meet the other person’s wishes and her pleasure at being useful continue the mainline’s central problem: usefulness is a real relational value, but it can become the acceptable vocabulary for a desire she cannot otherwise admit. [V09/S46.5/i139–142.]

Nene’s closing interior observation suggests that Sumika’s feeling for Kanoko may differ from what she felt toward Nene. This is another character interpretation, valuable because of its comparative basis but not omniscient testimony. It does not independently date the first onset of Sumika’s feeling, identify every feature of the former sisterhood, or establish Kanoko’s reciprocity. [V09/S46.5/i142–143.]

**Chronological restriction.** The short requires Haruko’s presence and the established current sister pairs. Its precise place within that workplace period is not fixed by its position after i134. Friendly explanation here is not proof that the interrupted Goeidō discussion has been repaired, that consultations have resumed, or that any contact slip has been used. The short adds separately bounded evidence to speech, performance and relationship interpretation; it does not advance the mainline endpoint.

### 4.2 Afterword, publication credits, and the staff-departure manual

The two-page afterword comments on production, the volume’s focus, and the included material. Those comments are creator paratext, not hidden character testimony. The original-publication credits separately identify the numbered serial chapters, the half-chapter and the anthology one-shot. This distinction is important because a visually continuous EPUB can otherwise encourage a false assumption that all following manga belongs to the same story. [V09/paratext/i144–146.]

The operational-manual extra explains how departed salon staff are represented within the school fiction and distinguishes that public absence from actual employment. Its Nene example is particularly relevant to Goeidō’s inference from the menu. It also describes the graduate framing used for Goeidō and a fictional school-year adjustment. These details clarify institutional presentation; they do not change anyone’s real age or establish that every guest knows the backstage truth. The manual’s account is kept as an edition witness alongside, rather than substituted for, the mainline’s demonstrated information states. [V09/extra/i165; mainline i104–113.]

The cover and underlying cover place Sumika and Kanoko in an elegant, physically supportive composition. They supply framing for the volume’s focus, not evidence of a separately dated embrace or mutually acknowledged romance. The back-cover presentation juxtaposes ordinary and performed appearances without making either a complete psychological truth. Catalogue and publication-design matter are not mined for uninspected narrative claims. [V09/cover; V09/extra/i164, i166–167.]

### 4.3 The electronic-edition bonus belongs to the interview occasion

The i168 bonus contains a color illustration and an interview-day mini-scene. The interviewer asks to photograph customer service. Kanoko tugs Hime, who offers to take over; the interviewer recalls girls who could not do things alone at school and marvels that this interaction now forms part of their work. Kanoko privately plans to put Hime forward so that she can obtain good professionally taken photographs. This is Kanoko’s photo-seeking motive within the interview occasion, not a request to expose Hime without her façade or a Sumika explanation of persona leakage. The skit does not disclose Kanoko’s love to Hime or supply a post-ending intervention; its precise relation to every mainline panel is not independently fixed. [V09/bonus/i168; compare V09/42/i001–006.]

### 4.4 Separate one-shot: 運命の私, not another Watayuri chapter

**Witness status.** The one-shot runs from i147 to i162; i163 is its thank-you page. The credits identify an anthology source. Its two principal figures, **星奈** and **律**, are not assigned Watayuri subject IDs. The reading below is a bounded account of the separately packaged work, not evidence of Hime, Mitsuki, Kanoko or Sumika’s psychology. No cross-continuity identity or shared setting is supplied by the package. [V09/paratext/i146; V09/OS/i147–163.]

The story places private knowledge behind teasing apparent uncertainty. An eraser and a bandage carry the signs of a romantic charm; the focalizing girl knows more about the intended recipient than she initially lets the other person see. Astrology, lucky colors and a supposedly fated encounter provide a language through which attachment can be expressed while being made to look as though an external system authorized it. The narrative’s internal access makes this asymmetry visible; the physical objects alone would not prove every motive. [V09/OS/i147–157.]

Ordinary care and deliberate difficulty coexist. The exchange of a bitter drink for a more suitable one is useful attention to another person’s immediate comfort. The subsequent pretense not to be the relevant beloved creates distress rather than simply clarifying affection. The story therefore does not support the rule that a person who correctly notices another’s needs will necessarily communicate her own wishes generously. [V09/OS/i151–160.]

The focalizer wants to be loved as herself rather than as the result of fortune-telling. That is her interpretation of the distinction she needs; it does not prove that the other girl’s affection is actually empty of person-specific knowledge. Her physical gesture toward the bandaged finger follows an objection to removing the bandage. The intimate ending is not retroactive permission for that action. [V09/OS/i157–162.]

A limited formal comparison is possible: both works in this package distinguish an attractive relational vocabulary from the particular person’s unspoken demand. In the one-shot it is fate; in the mainline it is the reliable sister and ally. This is an **analyst’s cross-work comparison**, not a claim of shared continuity, a creator-endorsed master key, or a substitute for the mainline’s separate evidence. The one-shot supplies no future resolution to the contact paper, the Goeidō visit, or the Sumika–Kanoko consultation rupture.

### 13.4 Competing readings that remain worth testing
