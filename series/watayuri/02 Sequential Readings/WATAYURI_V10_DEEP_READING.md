## 1. The volume’s governing problem

Volume 10 makes the apparent success of a relationship proposal less reassuring than its vocabulary suggests. Nene asks Yoko whether their relationship was serious, receives an affirmative answer, and discovers that Yoko means something different by seriousness. Kanoko asks Sumika to date her, receives the confession she has been seeking, and then reports the result to someone whose advice Sumika does not know about. Neither sequence permits the analyst to infer equal feelings from agreement on a relationship word. The important questions are what each woman thinks the agreement accomplishes, what she knows about the other person’s purpose, and whether an answer actually addresses the question that prompted it. [V10/49/i075–087; V10/51/i143–154.]

Nene’s history gives these questions a particular emotional weight. Her lesbian self-understanding is not introduced as nothing but shame. She remembers pleasure in discovering her attraction, a wish to speak honestly about it, and repeated disappointment when a particular beloved does not return it. Café Liebe offers meaningful work, a shared fiction she values, and a sister whose reassurance helps her enter the salon. Her love for Sumika grows inside that genuinely sustaining arrangement. Yoko’s later offer is attractive partly because Yoko can recognize a problem that Sumika’s language of uncomplicated friendship does not recognize. That recognition is real; it does not make the relationship subsequently offered adequate to Nene’s changing wishes. [V10/47/i005–028; V10/48/i032–055.]

The present Nene–Yoko encounter does not reveal that Nene was an innocent stranger to every instrumental element in the old relationship. The fuller recollection shows that she initially accepted a proposal intended to make Sumika jealous. Nene then developed love for Yoko herself. Yoko’s renewed offer continues to invoke the earlier project, whereas Nene wants to know whether she herself has become the object of a serious relationship. Nene’s eventual departure is therefore a rejection of a particular arrangement after an attempted clarification, not proof that her earlier pleasure, choice, or love was unreal. It also does not vindicate every act of control Sumika performed in the name of protecting her. [V10/49/i083–090.]

Kanoko’s present difficulty is different but formally related. Hime continues to value her and tries to speak honestly; Mitsuki openly acknowledges needing Hime’s help; Sumika still wants to help but increasingly cannot tolerate how Kanoko seeks it. These changes threaten the stable arrangement Kanoko thought she could preserve. Her request to date Sumika explicitly aims to end Sumika’s hatred of romance. Sumika, meanwhile, experiences an opportunity to be loved by the person she loves. The two intentions are not automatically incompatible, but the volume has not established that both participants understand them or that Kanoko reciprocates Sumika’s romantic feeling. The final call sharpens that information problem without identifying its other speaker. [V10/50/i100–124; V10/51/i135–154.]

## 3. Sequential, form-sensitive close reading

### 3.1 Shift 47 — Nene’s particular desire and the attraction of a shared role

#### 3.1.1 An unanswered message is not yet a demonstrated lie

The volume opens in the present, with Sumika asking Nene whether Yoko has contacted her. Nene says no; Sumika asks to be told if a message arrives and says Nene must not meet Yoko. V09’s reader knows that Nene sent an outgoing request. That does not by itself make a statement about incoming contact false. The reply will be shown later. The scene establishes Sumika’s monitoring and prohibition, Nene’s participation in an incomplete exchange, and a difference between what the reader knows and what Sumika knows—not proof that every item of Nene’s answer is a lie. [V10/47/i001; V09/46 meeting-request record.]

The frame matters because the chapter then leaves this problem unresolved while reconstructing Nene’s earlier life. Sumika’s warning can initially look like a straightforward attempt to protect someone who has been hurt. The history does not abolish that concern, but it gives the person being protected desires and interpretive authority that the warning alone does not represent. Nene has a reason for wanting to ask her own questions. The reader should not pre-emptively grant Sumika the right answer merely because a later meeting might go badly. [i001, i005–028; later i054, i057.]

#### 3.1.2 Sexual self-knowledge is not a single story of shame

The explicit two-years-earlier heading places Nishidera Nene at eighteen, working in convenience retail. She can help a coworker with a practical checkout problem, yet the subsequent social arrangement leaves her isolated while others converse easily. The juxtaposition distinguishes being useful from feeling included. It is not evidence that she cannot work with others, and it does not assign a single cause to every instance of loneliness. [i005–007.]

Nene’s recollection moves further back to her first attraction to a girl. She names herself a lesbian and initially experiences that discovery as something special. The narrative then accumulates disappointing asymmetries: the girls she loves turn toward boys; a girl’s affectionate language of friendship does not mean what Nene hopes it means. Those recollections are Nene’s account of repeated experience. They support both her longing and her sensitivity to categorical exclusion, but not a universal proposition that a lesbian can never be loved or that every straight woman has deliberately misled her. [i008–010.]

The bar conversation makes her desire more precise. A setting in which she need not translate a female beloved into an imagined man offers meaningful relief. Yet the suggestion that she could simply find a girlfriend among other lesbians does not answer her actual problem. Nene wants the particular person she loves to love her, rather than regarding the availability of any suitable partner as interchangeable fulfillment. That distinction later explains why shared sexual identity with Yoko can be a powerful opening without guaranteeing a satisfactory relationship. Recognition of a category is not knowledge of a particular wish. [i010–011; later i051–055, i086.]

This is also an important limit on reconstruction. The chapter directly supports Nene’s self-applied sexual identity. It does not establish that every present preference is unchanged from adolescence, that she has only ever loved the people depicted here, or that she is incapable of moving beyond a past attachment. Her own historical narrative deserves weight without becoming a complete biography.

#### 3.1.3 Mai recruits for work and for an imaginative possibility

Mai’s school-uniform presentation enters a setting Nene does not initially associate with a café job. Nene’s objection to bar work at eighteen is answered by clarifying the kind of business. The ages and work expectations are source dialogue; this reading does not use them to make an independent legal claim. Mai’s appearance, café school rank and actual age remain different categories. The ordinary name Koshiba Mai is also not a new person distinct from the manager already known. [i012–014.]

The pitch combines practical employment with a fiction Nene recognizes: the girls’ school, sisterhood and yuri atmosphere. Her later return to an unsatisfying ordinary workplace makes the alternative attractive. The café is not merely bait for romance, nor is its imaginative appeal incidental to the labor arrangement. The source gives Nene both an employment decision and a chance to inhabit a social form she finds meaningful. Neither component should erase the other. [i014–016.]

Preopening practice is another useful boundary. A rented practice space, preparation and learning precede a functioning salon. The series’ elaborate fiction has to be made workable by people who can fail, learn and improvise. The institutional world is constructed, but construction is not synonymous with fraud. This becomes especially important when Nene’s real anxiety is relieved by Sumika’s ability to act inside it. [i016–024.]

#### 3.1.4 The person who misreads Nene can still become important to her

Nene’s first encounter with Chibana Sumika is not an unqualified moment of recognition. Sumika’s gyaru presentation surprises her; the casual handling of Nene’s lesbian identity and the accompanying claim of having no prejudice strike Nene as intrusive. Nene’s private reaction distinguishes the person she sees from the kind of elegant-school interaction she expected. The discomfort is evidenced, but the chapter does not ask the reader to decide that Sumika’s every subsequent kindness must therefore be false. [i017–018.]

Training develops their shared repertoire. Sumika can learn quickly, while Nene attaches significance to the cross and the tradition it represents. Mai modulates how explicit the love language should be: the café wants a playable relationship, not every possible declaration delivered without restraint. Nene’s reprimand about the object and Mai’s correction of speech show that the role has material and linguistic rules. Sumika’s competence emerges through adjustment to those rules rather than through an innate identity as the perfect sister. [i019–021.]

At opening, Nene is nervous. Sumika offers to turn a mistake into an improvised scene; the promise, closeness and practical readiness help Nene enter the salon. Its force is not diminished because the words and gestures can also belong to the role. The reassurance responds to a real need. What it does **not** establish is a promise of romantic reciprocity. The same episode can be true care for Sumika and the beginning of romantic hope for Nene. [i022–024.]

The following early-work sequence gives the café a history of collective effort. Three people solve problems; business develops; later staffing strain exposes the difference between Nene’s availability and Sumika’s school obligations. Mai’s interest in a fresh novice also has a narrative-commercial purpose. Sumika’s attachment to their existing sisterhood remains visible. Nene eventually identifies this period as the time she realized she loved Sumika. The recollection changes the reader’s account of the old pair without turning Sumika’s ignorance into deliberate exploitation. [i025–028.]

**Prospective checkpoint after Shift 47:** Nene’s earlier love for Sumika is now directly established, and her sexual self-label has a source-grounded history. Her present motive for contacting Yoko is not yet fully supplied by the chapter. Sumika’s current love for Kanoko must not be carried backward as proof that her earlier relationship with Nene was secretly reciprocal.

### 3.2 Shift 48 — Shared vocabulary, unequal possibility

#### 3.2.1 Yoko recognizes a real boundary

Yoko’s entry is given an ordinary name, a work-availability account and a salon identity. Gotō Yōko and Goeidō Yōko are one subject with context-dependent names. The upper-grade role does not supply an actual biographical age or an independent employment history beyond what she reports. [V10/48/i029–031.]

Her response to Sumika’s casual disclosure of Nene’s sexual identity is significant. Yoko identifies it as something not to discuss indiscriminately. For Nene, someone has recognized an uncomfortable distinction that Sumika has not handled well. This can be genuine, useful sensitivity even though later scenes reveal conduct that is difficult to trust. An analysis that calls all Yoko’s understanding counterfeit from the beginning would erase why Nene finds it compelling and what the correction actually accomplishes. [i032–033.]

Nene reassures Sumika that she is not upset. Her spoken accommodation should not erase the previously shown irritation or the private romantic problem. The chapter repeatedly lets Nene protect a workable interaction at the cost of leaving its most important asymmetry unspoken. That is a behavior in this relationship, not a proof that she is incapable of direct speech: she will ask explicit questions of Yoko later. [i033–035; V10/49/i075–086.]

#### 3.2.2 Being outside a shared reference is not identical to being outside love

Yoko knows the fictional source vocabulary and can design a deliberately provocative role from it. Nene and Mai recognize what she is doing; Sumika experiences a form of exclusion. The playful upper-sister characterization and the real interpersonal situation begin to resemble one another, but the resemblance must not serve as evidence that Yoko’s role has already enacted a real seduction or betrayal. At this point the character design is a consciously discussed performance proposal. Its later resonance gains force precisely because the text first establishes it as something the workers can knowingly play. [i036–040.]

Sumika pulls Nene away despite Nene’s protest. That is an actual interaction, unlike the proposed fiction. Sumika’s jealousy, insecurity about reference fluency, protective dislike and desire for familiar access need not be collapsed into one motive. Nor does feeling excluded give her automatic authority to physically redirect Nene. The source preserves a protest even inside the episode’s comedy. [i038–039.]

Yoko then demonstrates fast practical and theatrical adaptation. Nene’s increased responsibility for explaining things to her alters the old pair’s time together. A salon expression of wanting more time with Nene affects Nene personally, but it still does not give Nene an actual romantic commitment from Sumika. The labor distribution, public role and private expectation all change at once, at different levels. [i040–044.]

#### 3.2.3 An inclusive sentence can close a particular hope

The private quarrel is not adequately described as Sumika simply revealing anti-lesbian prejudice. Nene asks whether Sumika’s dislike of Yoko is about her being a lesbian. Sumika denies that explanation and objects that Nene herself puts a distance between them by making sexual orientation decisive. Sumika’s language presents friendship as something that should not depend on that difference. But for Nene, who wants more than the friendship Sumika is describing, the same language marks the boundary she cannot cross. [i045–050.]

The scene therefore contains at least three propositions: Sumika does not want Nene to withdraw from friendship because of sexual difference; Nene wants her particular love to become possible; and Nene hears Sumika’s confidence in their nonromantic relationship as the end of that possibility. These propositions can coexist without one person’s account being the entire truth. Sumika is not shown promising romance and then rescinding it. Nene is not merely misunderstanding an otherwise secretly reciprocal declaration. She is responding to a real mismatch while using her history to explain its pain. [i046–050.]

Nene’s interior formulation that she does not hate her sexuality is especially important. The injury is not reducible to self-disgust. She experiences repeated exclusion from the very starting point of the relationship she wants. That account can be emotionally accurate for her without becoming a general theory of everyone else’s desires. Her vulnerability is specific enough that Yoko can respond to it with a particular kind of understanding. [i049–053.]

#### 3.2.4 The request to be heard precedes the offer to date

Yoko approaches about a uniform problem, notices Nene’s distress and stays. Nene explicitly asks whether she may discuss a romantic problem; Yoko invokes the difficulty of explaining their experiences to people who do not share them. This is an actual permission-seeking exchange about conversation. It should not be inflated into permission for all later interventions. [i051–053.]

Nene’s wish to stop repeating painful attraction to straight women gives Yoko’s dating offer an intelligible appeal. Yet the complete terms are not supplied at once. The narrative will later return to this offer and disclose the proposal to make Sumika jealous. At the initial presentation, shared identity, understanding and companionship are foregrounded. The later addition changes the reader’s explanation of Nene’s participation without pretending that the missing clause was available earlier. [i053–055; V10/49/i083–085.]

At i054 the present resumes: Yoko has actually replied, and Nene asks when they can meet. Nene’s private aim is now more specific than the V09 ending allowed. She wants an intelligible end to the old relationship. That motive does not exclude temptation, nostalgia or a changing wish during the encounter. It does, however, rule out treating the meeting request as already sufficient evidence of a simple intention to resume romance. [i054; later i057–072.]

**Prospective checkpoint after Shift 48:** the old love triangle has a newly visible information structure. Nene knows her love for Sumika; Sumika has not been shown receiving it; Yoko receives Nene’s romantic problem and offers a response. In the present, a reply and arrangement now exist, but their outcome remains open.

### 3.3 Shift 49 — A pleasurable answer to the wrong question

#### 3.3.1 The reunion is real; its purpose is not yet shared

Unlike the uncompleted contact route at V09’s ending, this chapter depicts the actual Nene–Yoko meeting. Nene wants to ask whether Yoko was serious and why the relationship could end as it did. Yoko’s late-arrival message and startling affectionate greeting reintroduce a familiar dynamic: Nene registers an inconvenience or discomfort while also feeling the pull of a person she wanted to remain beside. The greeting should not be embellished into a specifically depicted kiss when the image does not establish one. [V10/49/i057–060.]

Yoko names the outing a date and directs its sequence toward a photographic exhibition and dinner. Nene repeatedly distinguishes having come to talk from having come simply to enjoy renewed couplehood. Yoko assures her that there is time, challenges her to speak immediately, turns the challenge into a joke, and again redirects the encounter. The result is not total silence but a sequence in which Nene’s central question repeatedly loses priority. [i061–072.]

The pleasures are not fake merely because the direction is uneven. Nene remembers enjoyable previous dates and recognizes that she became serious about Yoko. Yoko notices things Nene might enjoy and supplies affectionate attention. Precisely because these acts answer some of Nene’s desires, they can displace the answer to another desire: a shared understanding of the relationship. The analytic claim is about incompatible purposes and control of timing, not proof that every pleasant gesture was emotionally empty. [i063–072.]

#### 3.3.2 Recognition and display have different audiences

At the exhibition, the gender of the photographer’s lover and the gender of the photographer complicate an assumption in the conversation. Yoko’s familiarity with the work contrasts with Nene’s developing understanding. This is a small epistemic episode, not sufficient evidence for a general accusation about Nene’s beliefs. More important to the surrounding encounter is the difference between enjoying photographs, enjoying an artist and imagining a relationship through an image. [i064–065.]

The restaurant’s couple benefit makes recognition public. Yoko asks that two women receive it; the server’s initial surprise is followed by accommodation. Yoko openly enjoys that surprise and asks whether Nene dislikes being known as a lesbian. Nene’s response distinguishes surprise at the situation from a simple wish to conceal her identity. Yoko’s apparent willingness to name them as a couple can feel like the recognition Nene has lacked, while Yoko also enjoys the social performance itself. Those are not identical motives. [i068–071.]

The scene should not be converted into either a broad portrait of institutional discrimination or proof of universal acceptance. It is one depicted exchange. Its literary function is to make a couple label available before the couple has actually clarified what renewed couplehood means. External recognition arrives more readily than internal agreement. [i068–072.]

#### 3.3.3 Intimacy does not retroactively complete the conversation

Nene drinks, later appears unsteady, and insists that Yoko not leave before they have talked. The next transition has Nene waking clothed in a hotel room, disoriented about how they got there. Yoko gives an explanation: Nene could not readily go home and did not want Yoko to leave because she still wanted to talk. Nene partly recalls the earlier situation. The distinction between a shown state, Yoko’s report and Nene’s fragmentary recollection matters. The source does not supply a continuous record of every decision during the transition. [i071–075.]

Yoko again suggests postponement, now until tomorrow. Nene finally asks what the relationship had been and says she was serious. Yoko responds with an offer of renewed relationship and intimacy, asking whether Nene wants the reason for the breakup or a lover starting now. The alternatives are not equivalent answers to the original question. They change the decision Nene is being asked to make: from assessing the previous relationship to accepting a present opportunity. [i075–079.]

The transition into the next morning implies renewed sexual intimacy. It does not demonstrate that both now understand commitment in the same way, that every possible act has been agreed to, or that anything sexual occurred while Nene was asleep. Nor should Nene’s participation be erased simply to make the scene a schematic account of powerlessness. Her wish for Yoko, her stated confusion and the delayed conversation coexist. The source supports an assessment of compromised clarity and uneven control over the encounter without supplying the facts needed for a categorical legal determination. [i073–081.]

Nene’s later refusal of a shared shower is also locally meaningful: it is a specific preference Yoko does not force past. That local non-escalation cannot retroactively settle the clarity of the broader encounter. Conversely, concern about the broader encounter should not cause the analyst to invent a violation of a particular refusal that is not depicted. [i081.]

#### 3.3.4 The withheld clause changes whose project the old relationship served

When Nene asks whether the restart is serious, Yoko says yes—and anticipates the enjoyment of Sumika finding out. The return to the old offer then supplies its concealed clause: dating Yoko was expected to make Sumika jealous and turn her toward Nene. Nene had initially entered an arrangement with that strategic element. The retrospective addition therefore revises a possible simple contrast between a wholly sincere, uninformed Nene and a wholly instrumental Yoko. It does not eliminate the difference that developed later. [i081–085.]

Nene says that, from partway through, she became serious about Yoko herself. Yoko responds that she too is serious: she likes Nene and seriously enjoys romance. The disagreement is no longer merely whether a feeling exists. It concerns what the feeling commits the relationship to and whether the old third-person project still defines it. Yoko’s words allow genuine liking and enjoyment while failing to provide the relationship Nene is now asking for. Calling Yoko’s statement nothing but an absence of feeling would be less precise than the source. [i085–086.]

The revelation also limits a retrospective absolution of Nene. She was not always a passive object in a scheme directed at Sumika; she once accepted using a relationship to affect Sumika’s attention. Her later love for Yoko does not make that initial purpose disappear. At the same time, agreement to an earlier casual or strategic arrangement does not permanently bind her to it after her wishes change. The source gives her the right to clarify and leave without requiring a morally immaculate past. [i083–088.]

#### 3.3.5 Leaving is an action, not a completed emotional cure

Nene decides not to meet Yoko again, dresses and leaves. Her formulation that Yoko gives her what she wants but nothing beyond it identifies the problem as something subtler than simple deprivation. Yoko can satisfy an immediate desire while leaving Nene alone with the larger meaning she hoped would accompany it. This is Nene’s interpretation of the relationship after the exchange, supported by the sequence but not a diagnosis of Yoko’s entire personality. [i086–088.]

Yoko lets her go with an easy acknowledgment of the enjoyable time. There is no mutual accounting of harm, no demonstrated remorse that resolves Nene’s questions, and no constraint preventing the depicted departure. An actual boundary and exit have occurred. Their future reliability remains untested. [i087–088.]

At work, Sumika questions Nene’s absence and its burden on Haruko, then suspects the meeting. Nene initially says it is not Sumika’s business. Sumika eventually apologizes; Nene cries and apologizes too. The exchange is relationally significant without being a full disclosure of the old love for Sumika or the jealousy arrangement. The reader should not supply the missing scope of either apology. Nene’s tears after an enacted departure also resist the idea that a correct decision necessarily feels like relief. [i089–090.]

**Prospective checkpoint after Shift 49:** the reunion occurred, its former strategic basis was disclosed to the reader, Nene described her changed love, and she chose a new boundary. Sumika’s earlier warning gains some factual support about the risk, but not unrestricted authority over Nene’s choices. Kanoko and Sumika do not automatically inherit the reader’s new knowledge of the historical arrangement.

### 3.4 Shift 50 — The person who needs help and the person she needs help from

#### 3.4.1 Rehearsal has become a real missing activity

Kanoko overhears Nene saying she will not meet Yoko again and warning Sumika against interference. For the reader, this follows a substantial account of the meeting. For Kanoko, it is a partial overheard exchange, and its significance is tied to Sumika’s apparent preoccupation with Nene. Kanoko cannot know the entire cause of that preoccupation from the exchange she hears. [V10/50/i093–095.]

The public rehearsal also gives the old practical promise a measurable absence. Hime and Mitsuki work with their scripts, make character-name mistakes, correct themselves and offer one another help. Hime invites Kanoko to practice with them. Kanoko says she practices with Sumika, but privately acknowledges that Sumika has not practiced with her even once. This is more than a generalized complaint that Sumika feels distant. A specific promised or anticipated shared activity has not materialized. [i095–099.]

The rehearsal is not the completed festival. Scripted anxiety about sisters separating belongs to a fiction the performers are learning, even though it now resonates with their lives. Mitsuki’s use of Hime’s ordinary name where a character name is required, followed by correction and Hime’s comparable difficulty, makes performance visibly a learned collaboration. Mistakes do not by themselves announce private relational collapse. [i095–098.]

Kanoko’s answer about practice nevertheless hides a real difficulty. It preserves an outward picture of her own sister pair and avoids explaining the failure in front of Hime. That function is supported by the contrast between speech and thought; a unique conscious motive for every word is not. Hime’s offer remains genuinely helpful even when Kanoko cannot use it without confronting the relationship it would place her beside. [i098–103.]

#### 3.4.2 Another person’s improvement can be the source of distress

Mitsuki admits that Hime is better at this kind of performance and that she has to work harder. Hime responds with assistance and affection. This is a concrete development from the earlier problem of wanting to be the person who helps while struggling to accept help. Here Mitsuki’s acknowledged limitation is not merely an analyst’s inference from a mistake; it is part of the exchange. [i100–102; preserved V05/V06 care findings.]

Mitsuki’s wish to respect Hime and remain special beside her is then qualified as not the romantic sense of specialness. The qualification does not make her love vanish. It identifies an attempt to keep the workable relation within the terms Hime has actually accepted. Nor does Hime’s embrace reverse her nonromantic answer. This is a positive collaboration under unequal feelings, not a newly mutual romance. [i101–103.]

Kanoko can intellectually repeat that Hime is straight and Mitsuki naïve while remaining distressed by what she sees. The discomfort is not reducible to Hime’s poor work performance: the pair’s cooperation is precisely what is going well. Her familiar explanation no longer neutralizes their importance to one another. The source therefore deepens, rather than first discovers, the earlier account of jealousy at credible private closeness. The prior forecast’s already recorded support is not counted a second time as a new prediction result. [i102–105; WY1-PR03’s preserved earlier disposition.]

#### 3.4.3 Respecting a stated boundary can leave an unspoken need unmet

Kanoko says she feels unwell, asks Hime to leave with Mitsuki, and tells her not to open the changing space. Hime respects those instructions. Kanoko privately wants the pair out of sight while outwardly reassuring Hime that she is fine. This does not make Hime’s departure evidence of indifference. It shows the difficulty of receiving care when the recipient’s spoken request conceals what is hurting her. [i104–105.]

Sumika finds Kanoko withdrawn and offers an opportunity to talk. Kanoko remembers the earlier limit on jealousy consultation and chooses to leave. Sumika does not physically prevent that departure. The source gives a real offered opportunity and a real reason Kanoko cannot readily trust its availability, not a simple absence of concern on one side. [i106–108.]

At home, the fictional younger sister’s departure becomes a text through which Kanoko reads her own situation. Her irritation at how easily the script speaks of love is itself informative. It does not transform rehearsal dialogue into a decision to dissolve the actual sister pact. The page can convey identification with a story without depicting that story’s outcome in the present. [i108–109.]

#### 3.4.4 Hime asks a question Kanoko does not want opened

The next school exchange begins with care: Hime checks Kanoko’s condition and says Kanoko is important to her. Kanoko privately takes comfort in what she wants to remain unchanged. She imagines enduring Mitsuki’s inclusion as a price for keeping Hime’s regard. That is not an agreement Hime has made to keep her emotional world permanently static. [i110–111.]

Hime then identifies the previous illness excuse as a lie and asks whether Kanoko is avoiding Mitsuki. She does not treat all lying as a sufficient reason to condemn Kanoko; she wants to discuss the underlying problem. This is a useful instance of Hime applying her own distinction between a pleasing presentation and a particular truth that matters. It is not the original reputational-threat forecast’s test, because no new threat to another person’s reputation supplies that specified situation. [i112–115.]

Hime says she has thought about the importance of speaking sincerely and wants Kanoko to understand what happened with Mitsuki’s confession. Kanoko’s interior response asks Hime not to think so deeply about love or other people’s confessions. She wants Hime to remain the smiling person who has not fallen in love. This is Kanoko’s protective and self-protective model of Hime, not the narrator’s declaration that Hime must never change or cannot understand anyone else’s love. [i114–115.]

When Hime raises the possibility that Kanoko is avoiding Mitsuki because Mitsuki loves someone of the same sex, Kanoko denies treating that as strange. She instead questions whether the feeling is serious, recasting it as admiration, light language or fashion. The distinction matters. Kanoko’s response is not adequately captured by labeling her simply hostile to same-sex romance; she is trying to make this particular rival attachment less consequential. The source does not require that every spoken argument perfectly disclose its speaker’s motive. [i116–117.]

#### 3.4.5 The kiss becomes Kanoko’s knowledge now

Hime replies that Mitsuki kissed her. This is a new disclosure to Kanoko of an earlier event, not a new kiss, and it should not be backdated into Kanoko’s earlier confrontation with Mitsuki. Hime also says she does not think Mitsuki would lie in that way and understands the confession as serious. The kiss and her knowledge of Mitsuki’s character are evidence in **Hime’s explanation**. They are not a general rule that physical contact proves sincerity, establishes consent or requires romantic reciprocation. [i118.]

Kanoko cuts off further explanation with apparent agreement. The close framing then reveals distress that the smooth verbal closure does not communicate. Hime thanks her for listening and proposes returning to class; Kanoko has not told Hime that she herself loves her. The conversation changes one crucial information state while leaving another intact. Hime knows more clearly that Kanoko is troubled; she still lacks the account that would make the reaction fully intelligible. [i119–120.]

This is not confirmation of the old disclosure forecast about Kanoko’s abolition or exclusive-friend project. The new information runs in the opposite direction: Hime tells Kanoko about Hime’s own experience. Conflating those disclosures would lose exactly the knowledge asymmetry the longitudinal method exists to preserve. [WY3-PR02; i113–120.]

#### 3.4.6 A real listener limit becomes a harmful refusal

At the café, Hime suggests rest and Mai offers Kanoko a break under a fully staffed shift. The practical option exists. Kanoko instead seeks Sumika urgently, asking when they can talk, whether she can visit and whether the work shift is over. Her repeated appeal is not abstract interest in advice; it is a search for the person who had promised to hear her. [i120–122.]

Sumika initially responds but changes course when Kanoko says Hime is behaving strangely because of Mitsuki. Sumika invokes the prior consultation restriction. Kanoko answers by invoking the prior promise of help. Both references have actual history behind them. What they do not have is a shared account of what support now means, especially after Sumika’s private feelings have changed. [i121–123.]

Sumika’s outburst brings Nene and Kanoko into one accusation: each follows her own love without attending to the concern of people around her. It culminates in a broad refusal to hear more about Hime. A confidant can legitimately have limits; that principle does not make this particular delivery considerate, adequately explained or proportionate to the person in front of her. Sumika’s fear, jealousy, fatigue and disappointment are causally relevant without becoming automatic justification. [i123–124.]

Kanoko’s narrated collapse responds to both Hime and Sumika seeming different from the people she expected. Her appeal for anyone to help marks vulnerability. It does not by itself establish that the next helper will be trustworthy, that she has accepted every possible intervention, or that all responsibility for a later choice belongs to the people who hurt her first. The volume ends this chapter at the point where an unavailable familiar support route could make another route attractive. [i124.]

**Prospective checkpoint after Shift 50:** Kanoko now knows about the kiss; Hime still does not know Kanoko’s romantic account. Sumika has made a real and injurious restriction more explicit. No specific plan to control Hime’s or Mitsuki’s attachment has been stated in the help request, and no actual sister-pact dissolution follows the script’s rhetoric.

### 3.5 Shift 51 — A confession that does not say the same thing twice

#### 3.5.1 Sumika recognizes jealousy before she can own its implications

Sumika first places Nene’s meeting and Kanoko’s distress under the same explanation: neither listens to her, and romance keeps destroying their relations. That is her interpretation, not the volume’s final causal verdict. It leaves out her own role in painful communication and the difference between protecting another person and requiring that person to accept one’s advice. [V10/51/i125.]

The following workplace coldness makes their conflict visible without supplying guests with its meaning. Sumika wants to help, resents Kanoko’s continuing behavior, and fears she has become disliked. Kanoko pulls Hime away; Sumika’s attention stays on the pair. Her impulse that Hime should become angry at Kanoko might appear to offer a solution to her own jealousy, but she immediately recognizes that it would hurt Kanoko. The harmful wish is a thought she checks, not an enacted plan to arrange rejection. [i127–132.]

Her private identification of jealousy is more explicit than the prior defensive account. Still, naming one emotion is not yet the same as openly acknowledging a romantic relationship. She rehearses the sister script alone, struggles with its threatened separation and thinks of herself as an unreliable elder sister. The role remains a language of responsibility and self-reproach, not merely a disguise she can discard once her desire is recognized. [i132–134.]

The historical Nene sequence and this scene consequently illuminate each other without making them identical. Nene once heard Sumika’s confident friendship language as a limit on love. Sumika now tries to use sisterly obligation to organize a feeling that exceeds that role’s reassuring explanation. Later self-knowledge is a development, not evidence that the earlier Sumika knowingly offered Nene a romance she never intended to honor.

#### 3.5.2 Public permission does not settle private meaning

Guests notice that the Blumen sisters no longer play together as they did. Kanoko approaches Sumika’s reading place, closes the book and asks to touch her hand. The request is not a blanket grant of permission for every subsequent form of contact, but it is a specific initiative rather than a purely inferred wish. Sumika answers through the older-sister role, draws her close and reassures her that she can ask when lonely. The audience reads the scene as desirable sisterly intimacy. [i135–138.]

Sumika’s response is also personally affecting. Afterward, she wants to know what the approach meant. Kanoko says Hime had suggested getting along with Sumika. Sumika immediately cautions herself not to become overjoyed, interpreting the gesture as something that might originate with Hime rather than a special preference for herself. Her physical response and private acknowledgment that the hand contact pleased her remain visible alongside that defensive interpretation. [i138–140.]

This sequence supplies more than a blush. It contains a personally salient initiative, immediate desire to read it specially and a retreat from that reading. It is therefore a bounded test of the inherited instability forecast, assessed in section 14. It does not establish that Kanoko’s behavior was wholly spontaneous or wholly insincere. The conversation supplies a reason for Sumika’s caution, not complete access to Kanoko’s purpose. [WY9-PR02; i135–140.]

The later phone disclosure also must not be projected back into every individual gesture as though the source had printed a detailed instruction list. Kanoko says events went as she was told; the advice itself, its timing and its exact scope remain unshown. The analyst can identify a concealed preparatory influence without pretending to possess its complete script. [i153–154.]

#### 3.5.3 Kanoko challenges the explanation, not merely the refusal

After work, Kanoko waits on the stairs and asks to discuss Sumika herself. Sumika expects another attempt to reopen the Hime topic, but Kanoko asks whether Sumika truly hates romance. The question targets the reason Sumika has given for withholding help. Sumika resists, declaring that she is trying to accept Kanoko’s love despite her own distaste. Kanoko replies that the way she is being addressed is frightening and proposes a different explanation: Sumika is jealous because she loves Kanoko. [i141–144.]

Sumika first retreats into sisterly love, friendship and the senior–junior bond. Those are not fabricated relationships; they are insufficient as a complete description of her present response. Kanoko’s insistence makes the difference explicit rather than allowing an analyst to infer romance solely from appearance. Yet Kanoko is also directing an emotionally consequential conversation toward an outcome she wants. Correctly recognizing another person’s desire does not automatically make the way one uses that recognition fair. [i145–147.]

Kanoko takes Sumika’s hand and asks whether the contact and earlier workplace episode made her heart race. When Sumika continues the nonromantic explanation, Kanoko tightens her grip; Sumika says it hurts. The source’s small pain utterance must not disappear into an account of purely tender mutual contact. The exchange contains closeness, pressure, denial and recognition at once. A later embrace does not retrospectively erase the earlier painful grip. [i146–147.]

#### 3.5.4 The proposed relationship has an expressed purpose

Kanoko asks Sumika to date her and become her lover so that Sumika’s hatred of romance can end. She calls the proposal a confession and a request for herself. The wording makes a concrete relational offer, but it does not contain a corresponding unambiguous statement that Kanoko romantically loves Sumika. Her stated objective concerns changing Sumika’s relation to romance. Whether her feelings toward Sumika are also changing remains a question, not something the word confession can answer alone. [i148–149.]

The asymmetry is sharpened by the appeal that Sumika should hear the request if she values Kanoko. It connects the relationship decision to the proof of care Sumika has struggled to provide. Sumika’s hope that she is being confessed to is understandable; the scene does not therefore certify that she has understood the complete project behind the request. Her question about whether Kanoko is really willing to choose her receives assent, and she finally tells Kanoko that she loves her. This is an explicit personal declaration, not merely café speech or a hypothetical reading of a facial reaction. [i149–151.]

Kanoko’s request and Sumika’s declaration are two different speech acts. One proposes a status for an articulated purpose; the other states the feeling that makes accepting the status desirable. Neither should be silently rewritten to say what the other says. At the end of their encounter, the dating arrangement is supported more strongly than reciprocal romantic love or a fully shared account of why they have entered it. [i148–153.]

#### 3.5.5 The final call withholds an identity, not the existence of advice

At home, Sumika thinks of herself as Kanoko’s girlfriend and wonders whether calling would be welcome. Her pleasure is explicit and the event is not another dream. The cutting then shows Kanoko on an actual call, saying that they have started dating, thanking the recipient, reporting that everything went as she was told, and agreeing to keep the matter from Sumika. These are concrete communications, not a merely imagined future. [i152–154.]

The other speaker is not shown, named, or quoted on these pages; the phone does not display a recoverable name. Goeidō is a strong contextual candidate because V09 supplied her contact route, Kanoko’s theory about changing Sumika’s attitude, and a history of advice involving induced jealousy. The chapter title’s reference to a senior reinforces the possibility. Those connections support an **inference**, not a direct identification. This reading therefore uses **unidentified interlocutor/adviser** for the actual record and retains Goeidō as a named hypothesis. V11 evidence cannot be imported to settle it. [V09/46/i124–134; V10/49/i083–085; V10/51/i126, i153–154.]

The unshown identity does not make the final scene empty of evidence. Kanoko represents the successful proposal as having followed advice and accepts a secrecy condition directed at Sumika. What remains unknown is who supplied the advice, exactly what was advised, when it was given and how fully Kanoko’s conduct was planned. The ending creates a new asymmetry between the person delighted to be a girlfriend and the person privately reporting the arrangement’s success. [i152–154.]

This is the volume’s strongest formal parallel with Nene and Yoko: getting a wanted answer or an accepted relationship label can leave the relationship’s purpose unshared. It is not proof that Kanoko has no affection for Sumika, that Sumika’s joy is foolish, or that the new pair will repeat the earlier pair’s entire history. Those would be forecasts disguised as conclusions. V10 ends with a real new arrangement whose meaning requires further evidence.

**Nene’s agency is not a reason to dismiss the conditions under which she acts.** She initiates contact and once accepted a strategic relation; she is also emotionally invested, has difficulty obtaining a focused discussion and passes through an incompletely depicted intoxicated transition.
