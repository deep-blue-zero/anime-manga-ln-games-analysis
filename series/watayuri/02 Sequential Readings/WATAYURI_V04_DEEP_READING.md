## 1. The volume’s governing problem

Volume 4 changes the question from whether Kanoko’s love should be allowed to continue to whether it can be acknowledged without being made into an immediate claim on Hime. Sumika initially treats knowing that the feeling exists as a reason to stop it. Kanoko initially treats keeping it unspoken as the only way to preserve her special position. The eventual alternative is neither a successful confession to Hime nor a new romantic attachment replacing the old one. It is another person who can hear the love, remember that it exists, and remain with the person who bears it. That is a substantial relational change, but it does not make Kanoko’s expectations of Hime fully accurate or settle what Hime herself wants. [V04/17/i027–034; V04/19/i079–086; V04/20/i088–108, i114–121.]

The volume earns this alternative by testing two accounts of protection. Sumika’s recollection explains why a romance inside the café feels dangerous to her: a former sister chose someone else, the new pair displaced the earlier bond, and the later departure left the salon changed. Yet Nene, the person Sumika remembers principally as someone who was hurt, insists on her own authorship of that relationship. Being injured does not retrospectively mean that the choice was never hers. Her intervention separates warning, assistance, and control more sharply than Sumika’s general prohibition can. Sumika subsequently changes not just her stated opinion but several observable acts: she apologizes, allows refusal, releases a hand when asked, listens to an account she had not possessed, and offers support without promising to determine the romantic outcome. [V04/17/i009–027; V04/18/i052–057; V04/19/i068–085; V04/20/i104–109.]

The Blumen result gives this change an institutional form. Sumika wins the actual contest; Kanoko’s ballot contributes to that victory for reasons that remain partly antagonistic to Hime and Mitsuki’s pair recognition. The victory therefore precedes, and cannot itself certify, private reconciliation. Only after a further negotiation does the former tactical alliance become an accepted sister pact. The rule finally announced is not the earlier proposed ban on dissolving sisterhood. It emphasizes getting along and remaining together. That shift matters, although a hope that nobody will be missing next year still carries Sumika’s unresolved fear of loss. A declaration of solidarity is not proof that every future departure, conflict, or refusal has already been accommodated. [V04/19/i061–078; V04/20/i110–122.]

The summer-uniform chapter tests the same distinction on a smaller, more comic scale. Mitsuki likes something about her own presentation that Hime finds difficult to look at and difficult to explain. Others’ attempts to protect her can be heard as condemnation of her appearance. The practical repair comes through a design accommodation she likes, together with Hime’s effort to distinguish her own discomfort from a fault in Mitsuki. However, their last private exchange again reveals unequal comprehension of suggestive language. An improved working arrangement and a sincere correction do not produce complete emotional transparency. The volume closes with two bonds more workable than before, not with every participant understanding the same relationship in the same way. [V04/21/i131–152.]

## 3. Sequential, form-sensitive close reading

### 3.1 Shift 17: what a rule can preserve, and what it cannot decide

#### Concealment is an activity before it becomes a doctrine

The opening returns to Kanoko’s long-maintained relation to her own feeling. The phone image and school-associated objects connect present attachment to the origin just disclosed in V03. The important point is not that the volume now invents an entirely different Kanoko, but that it foregrounds the labor of keeping something present to oneself and absent from another person’s hearing. The later telephone scene completes this structure: Hime can offer real comfort while not knowing the exact thing that requires comfort. Kanoko writes a declaration and erases it. An unsent or erased statement is evidence of her formulation and inhibition, not of Hime’s knowledge. [V04/17/i001, i033–034.]

That distinction matters because both participants in the confrontation initially collapse feeling and future action. Sumika thinks that allowing the love to continue will endanger the café. Kanoko insists that because she will not confess or demand a romantic answer, there is nothing for Sumika to stop. Neither account is complete. The former predicts a necessity not yet established; the latter leaves out Kanoko’s already evidenced attempts to redirect the contest and remove a sister system Hime values. The legitimate right not to have one’s feelings commanded does not make every strategy pursued around those feelings irrelevant. The scene should preserve both propositions rather than select a wholly innocent and a wholly controlling party. [V03/15/i103–118; V04/17/i005–008, i027–032.]

Kanoko’s withdrawal from the bargain is itself an action. She says that the contest and sisterhood no longer matter to her in the terms Sumika has offered, and she will seek Hime by another route. Sumika responds with a proposed use of the winner’s authority: sister pacts should not be dissolvable. This proposal is an attempted structural cure for a remembered injury. It does not ask what each current participant wants from a particular bond; it attempts to make one kind of loss unavailable. The difference between preserving an institution and preserving its members’ choices is already built into the conflict before the past is narrated. [V04/17/i005–008.]

#### The remembered romance develops through ordinary work permissions

Sumika’s account identifies a former arrangement: she, Mai, and Saionji were present before Goeidō entered the salon. Goeidō’s glamorous senior role and popularity affect how the others encounter her, but those theatrical school years are not ordinary biographical ages. Sumika presents herself as susceptible to Goeidō’s charm while unable to read her motives securely. That admitted attraction and uncertainty make the account more particular than a neutral organizational history. She is an involved witness describing the loss of a bond she valued. [V04/17/i009–013.]

The approach to Saionji is staged through ostensibly practical requests. Instruction, order handling, and the need to keep the workplace fiction intact create reasons to stand near someone, guide them, or permit a further approach. Sumika notices that the requests exceed what she expects from Goeidō’s apparent competence. The source thus makes practical justification and interpersonal pursuit difficult to separate. A request can be useful, performative, and privately motivated at once; the fact that it has a work explanation does not settle the whole act. Equally, suspicion about ulterior attraction does not prove that every practical request is fraudulent. [V04/17/i010–016.]

Repeated small allowances matter more than a single decisive breach. The language and choreography of having little alternative normalize accommodation: one person is already close, a lesson is already under way, and objecting can appear to spoil either work or entertainment. The pattern has an asymmetry. Goeidō often controls the framing of the interaction, while Sumika is asked to react to the frame already established. The reader can observe that sequence without asserting a complete, consciously plotted seduction strategy beyond what the panels and Sumika’s account establish. [V04/17/i011–016.]

The audience adds another layer. Goeidō’s behavior creates an enjoyable spectacle for guests, and she privately treats that spectacle as a reason Sumika need not worry. Later, when Sumika encounters a kiss, the same general area of conduct is defended as a real love that she should not obstruct. The change of explanation is consequential: “only the show” minimizes her claim to intervene, while “their genuine love” also minimizes it. These explanations need not mean that the relationship was never sincere. They do mean that Sumika is repeatedly positioned as the person whose discomfort should yield to somebody else’s preferred account. [V04/17/i015–016, i022–023.]

#### A loss of sisterhood is not identical to a loss of every relation

The account distinguishes degrees of evidence. Sumika says she thinks the two were already lovers at an earlier point. That is an inference about timing. The later kiss is depicted as something she encounters. Saionji’s request to change sisters and the transfer of the cross establish a further, public-role consequence. Neither the earlier inference nor the witnessed intimacy should be used to erase the specific decision Saionji communicates when she chooses Goeidō as her sister. [V04/17/i017–024.]

Saionji also changes her schedule to overlap with Goeidō’s availability. This alters the practical distribution of companionship and work. The emotional loss is therefore not exhausted by jealousy over a label: Sumika has less access to the person, a changed shared routine, and a role object she is asked to relinquish. She tries to accept the new pair as suitable to the school ideal. Her attempt at assent matters; she does not describe herself as having opposed the relationship identically at every moment. Yet the success of the public pair does not restore her own private place. [V04/17/i018–025.]

Goeidō’s later departure is reported in connection with her main work. Sumika interprets the aftermath as evidence that Saionji alone had been serious. The account supports the departure and the hurt as represented, but it does not give unrestricted access to Goeidō’s interior motives. It also does not establish that the hurt person disappeared from all café employment. That distinction becomes decisive when V04 identifies Nene as the former Saionji. Leaving a salon identity, losing a sister pact, and leaving the workplace are separable states. [V04/17/i026–027; V04/18/i055–057.]

Sumika’s generalization consequently exceeds even the detailed story she tells. The story contains changing schedules, disputed interpretations of performance, a chosen sister replacement, a later departure, and her own attempt to make sense of the result. “Romance destroyed it” collects these into one explanation but does not demonstrate that every romance must repeat that combination. The account is strongest as an explanation of what Sumika fears and why; it is weaker as a universal causal rule governing Kanoko’s future. [V04/17/i009–027.]

#### Kanoko’s refusal protects something real while hiding its costs

Kanoko answers that the strangers’ story is not hers. This is a justified objection to substituting an analogy for knowledge of her particular situation. She also claims that Hime will not love anyone and that remaining friends can preserve their relationship indefinitely. Here the limits reverse: Sumika lacks grounds to determine Kanoko’s future, but Kanoko also lacks unlimited grounds to determine Hime’s. An observer’s repeated experience of another person’s refusals is not identical to a demonstrated permanent incapacity to choose. [V04/17/i028–032; V04/20/i088–095.]

The telephone ending keeps that uncertainty emotionally concrete. Hime notices that something is wrong and encourages Kanoko not simply to endure. Kanoko accepts the comfort while withholding the particular conflict and erasing the declaration. The friendship is therefore neither wholly false nor transparently understood. Hime’s concern is real, and her ignorance is real. Kanoko’s concealment preserves access to the person she wants to address, but it also prevents that person from answering the actual problem. The chapter ends with relief through contact, not with the problem resolved. [V04/17/i033–034.]

### 3.2 Shift 18: returning to a place, or returning to a position

#### The wish to go back becomes visible in current work

The announced staff-vote deadline gives the argument a practical limit. Kanoko cannot postpone every choice until the emotional situation becomes simpler. Her wish to return to the earlier relation with Hime now meets Hime’s expressed support for becoming Blumen sisters with Mitsuki. The crucial evidence is Hime’s own preference, not merely a rival’s conspicuous closeness. Kanoko wants a familiar position restored, but that position cannot be recovered simply by assuming that Hime’s later investment is unreal. [V04/18/i035–040.]

Kanoko’s conflict also affects service. Guests refer positively to the earlier Sumika–Kanoko scene, which was successful public cooperation but not an uncomplicated private bond. Kanoko can answer politely, yet the encounter does not resolve her discomfort. Sumika supplies a plausible explanation for her withdrawal and maintains the salon’s continuity. That help is genuine work even while Sumika is a cause of the private distress. The contradiction is not repaired by saying she is either only helpful or only coercive. A competent worker may protect the public scene while mismanaging the person whose behavior she is explaining. [V04/18/i041–043.]

Hime notices that something is wrong and approaches the problem through a friendship she understands. She wants Kanoko to connect with people beyond herself and thinks that naming the conflict will make correction possible. Her advice has a reasonable aim. However, she does not know what Sumika demanded, why Kanoko cannot easily disclose it, or how directly the concealed issue concerns Hime. Under these conditions, insistence can become pressure even when it is motivated by care. [V04/18/i044–048.]

#### The interrupted explanation must not become a completed disclosure

Kanoko begins to explain that the problem concerns Tachibana, but the full formulation of Sumika’s demand appears within Kanoko’s thought. Hime is not shown receiving the complete account. This is an important information boundary: Hime can know that a quarrel exists without knowing that it concerns Kanoko’s romantic attachment, the abolition proposal, or her exclusivity expectations. The reader has enough context to understand the tears more specifically than Hime does. [V04/18/i047–049.]

The exchange also shows that distress does not abolish responsibility. Kanoko calls Hime foolish and then promptly apologizes, recognizing that it was not Hime’s fault. Hime apologizes for pressing her. These apologies repair a particular interaction; they do not settle the hidden issue. The scene is useful evidence of the friendship’s capacity for correction without becoming proof that either person now possesses the other’s full account. [V04/18/i048–049.]

When Sumika subsequently reaches toward Kanoko, the contact is rejected. Kanoko asks to be left alone and repeats that she is doing nothing. Her refusal has a bodily as well as a verbal form. Sumika is now confronted with a person already crying because of the attempted prevention, not merely a hypothetical person who might cry after a future romance. That reversal is one of the volume’s central causal tests: an intervention justified by anticipated pain has produced present pain that the protector must account for. [V04/18/i050–051.]

#### Nene is not merely the example in Sumika’s story

The kitchen conversation changes the status of the past. Nene’s identification as the former Saionji is not a new character’s arrival; it joins the sparse kitchen subject already observed to the earlier salon identity. Sumika explicitly asks why Nene ignored her advice, chose Goeidō, and broke their sisterhood. Nene therefore receives a chance to speak about an event previously organized by Sumika’s explanation. The later account does not erase what Sumika saw, but it changes who is permitted to interpret the hurt. [V04/18/i052–055.]

Nene’s answer separates kindness from welcome assistance. The compact contrast in her description—kind, but troublesome—rejects the assumption that a caring intention makes intervention acceptable. She does not deny having been hurt. Instead, she insists that she fell in love by her own choice and that its painful possibilities belong to a life someone may still choose. The analysis need not elevate her view into a universal principle that every warning is wrong. In this particular dispute, it directly contests Sumika’s treatment of injury as retrospective proof that the chooser should have obeyed her. [V04/18/i056.]

This matters for Nene’s characterization as well as for Sumika’s development. A model based only on the earlier recollection would risk making Nene a credulous victim whose surviving function is to explain another character’s fear. Her current speech instead preserves her authorship, critical judgment, and ability to advise. Remaining in the kitchen also complicates the proposition that the earlier romance destroyed every connection to Liebe. The old sister role ended; a changed relation to the workplace and to Sumika continues. [V04/18/i055–057.]

Nene finally gives Sumika a more specific object of concern. Kanoko may be a person who could cry, not simply a person who could destroy the café. Stopping the love is not the only form kindness can take. This does not immediately cure Sumika’s fear—she still cannot easily watch a possible injury without acting—but it makes another action imaginable. The later apology, listening, and promise not to leave Kanoko alone should be read as a sequence of responses to this challenge, not as evidence that the initial prohibition was harmless all along. [V04/18/i056–058; V04/19/i068–085; V04/20/i104–109.]

#### An apology must alter what happens next

Sumika’s first attempt to speak with Kanoko meets Hime’s intervention. Hime now has evidence that Kanoko is distressed and that Sumika’s approach may intensify it. Her protective stance is therefore not simply irrational obstruction. But she still does not know the full issue and risks taking over a negotiation between two other people. The scene distributes partial competence and partial ignorance across all three participants. [V04/19/i067–074.]

Sumika eventually stops relying on a practical pretext and acknowledges that telling Kanoko to give up was wrong. She says that Kanoko may decline the conversation. This is a more specific correction than a vague promise to be nicer: it identifies a prior demand and withdraws the expectation that the injured person must immediately cooperate with the apology. Yet Hime hears the acknowledgment without receiving its complete object. The words make responsibility partially public while the romantic content remains private. [V04/19/i068–069.]

The exchange does not proceed in a clean line from apology to acceptance. Hime thinks parting without a real conversation is inadequate; Sumika again presses; Kanoko tells Hime to stop; Sumika apologizes for becoming heated. This unevenness matters. A person can recognize overreach and repeat a smaller version of it before changing the actual interaction. The source gives a local developmental sequence, not an instantaneous conversion into perfect respect for boundaries. [V04/19/i070–074.]

The decisive question is finally directed to Kanoko: what does she want to do? That is different from Hime deciding that the conversation must happen, deciding that it must not happen, or Sumika treating the desire to apologize as an entitlement to be heard. Kanoko’s later decision to wait outside gives the question an observable answer. She approaches the possibility of conversation under changed conditions rather than being forced to remain in the earlier encounter. [V04/19/i074–077.]

Sumika’s intervening thought about Hime’s ability to make Kanoko smile is also double-edged. It registers something she wants to understand and perhaps to offer, while recalling the smile once directed toward another person in the earlier triangle. The composition links her present attention to her history of exclusion. It does not, by itself, establish that Sumika has recognized a romantic love for Kanoko. The supported state is stronger than disinterested management but less specific than a completed romantic self-classification. [V04/19/i075.]

#### From mutual noninterference to an invitation to be known

Outside, Kanoko first asks for noninterference, not emotional intimacy. She specifies that Sumika should leave Hime and her attachment alone and explains the ballot in terms of the outcome she preferred to avoid. This is a coherent request for a boundary, though it does not resolve her own wish to regulate Hime’s other relationship. Sumika agrees to stop interfering and then discovers that she does not actually know what Kanoko wants. The distinction between recognizing the category “romantic love” and understanding the person living it becomes explicit. [V04/19/i076–079.]

Sumika proposes that Kanoko may be suppressing her own love. That suggestion is an interpretation, not an authoritative discovery of a hidden pathology. Its value is tested by what Kanoko subsequently says and does. Before that test, Kanoko asks Sumika to let go of her hand. Sumika releases it and qualifies her account: she may be wrong, and her own experience is limited. The scene ties a better interpretation to a better procedure. Making room for correction includes allowing the person to reject the contact and the explanation. [V04/19/i080–083.]

Kanoko’s assessment of Sumika then changes in a precise way. She does not suddenly stop noticing the loudness, forcefulness, teasing, or unfamiliar social style that irritated her. What matters is that Sumika had named the feeling as love and treated it as a real thing before trying to remove it. This was a damaging intervention, but it also contained a form of recognition Kanoko had not otherwise possessed. The later willingness to listen makes that recognition usable rather than merely threatening. [V04/19/i084–085.]

The order is important: Kanoko chooses to remain, a boundary is stated and respected, the other person admits not knowing, and only then does Kanoko risk explaining Hime. Her movement toward Sumika is not a reward automatically owed for apology. It is a limited choice supported by changed conduct and by her own need for someone who can understand the feeling. The chapter ends with a proposition that can now be examined—Hime does not fall in love with anyone—rather than with the analyst treating that proposition as already proven. [V04/19/i083–086.]

### 3.4 Shift 20: saying it to someone other than the beloved

#### Kanoko’s account distinguishes rejection from nonexistence

Kanoko explains that Hime makes others like her without choosing them in the same way. Her account uses the contrast between the widely appealing façade and the privately acknowledged friend. On this view, a confession would move Kanoko from an exceptional position into the undifferentiated class of people whose desire Hime declines. The fear is not simply that the answer might be no. It is that speaking might destroy the kind of recognition she already has. [V04/20/i088–092.]

The narrative supports the sincerity and importance of this model for Kanoko, but it does not certify every proposition within it. Hime has repeatedly shown selective concern and has formed a further valued bond with Mitsuki. Kanoko’s account can describe her experience of Hime’s refusals without proving a permanent incapacity for romantic choice or friendship with anyone else. Sumika’s questioning usefully prevents the account from becoming a narrator’s diagnosis. [V04/20/i088–096; inherited WY3-C11/C15.]

Kanoko also realizes that explaining the model discloses Hime’s façade. Her request that Sumika keep it secret introduces a second person’s privacy into the conversation. Sumika agrees, which makes further speech possible, but their new trust has been built partly through information about someone absent. The confidentiality promise is a real new boundary; it does not retrospectively authorize every prior intrusion into Kanoko’s phone, and it does not establish that Hime has consented to this particular account being shared. [V04/20/i092–093; inherited WY2-C11.]

Sumika remains troubled by the picture of a love that cannot safely be answered or even addressed to its object. She has withdrawn the demand to stop, but she has not ceased to think that the situation may hurt. That persistence is important counterevidence to a simplistic conversion story. The new ethical task is not to stop caring about possible pain. It is to decide how care may operate when its recipient understands the risk and retains a choice. [V04/20/i093–094.]

#### Correctly noticing another love does not authorize suppressing it

Kanoko redirects Sumika toward Mitsuki. She argues that Mitsuki’s attachment to Hime is genuine and that this, rather than her own concealed feeling, has changed the situation. Sumika initially reaches for the role explanation, then recalls earlier evidence and revises her interpretation. This is a consequential gain in her understanding, but it is not a fresh present-day confession by Mitsuki. A prior statement is reconsidered from a newly informed viewpoint. [V04/20/i095–096.]

Kanoko’s tactical suggestion also shows why the new relationship cannot be analyzed solely as escape from an overbearing protector. She asks Sumika to stop Mitsuki’s romance instead. Her desired noninterference is not yet a general principle she extends equally to the rival. In that moment, she seeks an arrangement under which both her own position and Sumika’s preferred stability could be preserved by regulating somebody else. The earlier wish to abolish sisterhood has not simply vanished into mature acceptance of Hime’s freedom. [V04/20/i095–100.]

Sumika does not enact that redirected prohibition in this volume. The reader instead sees her attempting to locate what she herself wants. Mai and Nene discuss her tendency to focus on others and hope that she will recognize her own wishes. Their exchange supplies another perspective on her behavior; it does not provide an authoritative romantic label for her. The question of her investment is made salient, not conclusively answered as one particular kind of love. [V04/20/i098–099.]

#### The failed resolution is Kanoko’s claim that perpetual silence is enough

Kanoko initially tries to conclude that the conversation has restored clarity. She will not give up, need not confess, and can go on preserving the old position. But the internal return of Hime’s school-period injunction—that what remains unsaid can be treated as absent—makes that solution emotionally unstable. The school-uniform figure is not Hime arriving at the current stairway. The scene brings an earlier relational lesson into Kanoko’s present thought, where it contests her claim that indefinite silence costs nothing. [V04/20/i100–103.]

This is not a revelation that Kanoko never understood the practical risks. It is the discovery that avoiding a feared answer and securing recognition of the feeling are different needs. She can protect her access to Hime while leaving the love without any acknowledged place. The ensuing tears give Sumika information that her earlier prohibition did not obtain: the painful object is not only a future rejection but the ongoing isolation of carrying something she cannot safely name to the person concerned. [V04/20/i101–108.]

Sumika’s embrace is not preceded by a shown request for permission. The analysis should not turn it into a perfectly consent-negotiated act simply because it becomes comforting. The more bounded evidence is that Kanoko is initially surprised, then accepts the support as Sumika changes what she offers. Earlier rejected contact and later accepted contact have different interactional histories. Neither supplies general permission for future touch. [V04/18/i050–051; V04/19/i083; V04/20/i104–109.]

The promise itself is sharply limited. Sumika cannot ensure that Hime will reciprocate, that the love will stop hurting, or that Kanoko will never cry. She offers not to leave Kanoko alone with it. In doing so, she relinquishes a claim to control the answer while taking responsibility for a form of companionship. Her apology concerns not merely harsh wording but having recognized the love without recognizing the person’s loneliness. The emerging repair consists of a different action and a different attainable promise. [V04/20/i104–109.]

#### The confession and ceremony are interwoven, not one public disclosure

The source moves to the next day’s public announcement and later inserts further portions of the preceding stairway conversation. Sumika’s victory speech acknowledges that Mitsuki led the guest vote and thanks the people whose different choices produced the total. She praises the others as members of one salon rather than defining the title as personal superiority. This is a performed address, but the private sequence gives it a specific emotional basis. It cannot be dismissed as empty simply because it is spoken before customers. [V04/20/i109–114.]

The intercut confession needs particularly careful attribution. The complete statement identifying Hime as the person Kanoko likes occurs in the school-uniform stairway scene at i115. The surrounding pages braid that earlier dialogue with the public ceremony. A free-standing repetition of liking is carried across the montage, but the sequence does **not** establish Hime hearing the full private declaration about herself. The visible café reactions belong to the public approach and sister proposal; they do not automatically transfer every remembered word into the room’s shared knowledge. [V04/20/i114–118.]

This construction gives the reader two understandings of the coming pact. The guests can see an emotionally persuasive sister scene. The reader also knows that the person to whom Kanoko has spoken is not the person she loves romantically. Sumika’s special role is to know and preserve the existence of that love. Her offer does not require Kanoko to pretend that the romantic object has changed. The new intimacy is therefore both genuine and categorically distinct from the desire that occasioned it. [V04/20/i115–120.]

The difference from a substitute-romance reading is substantial. Kanoko’s gratitude and accepted embrace establish a new positive relation with Sumika. They do not show her redirecting her romantic attachment, nor do they establish Sumika’s own reciprocally classified romance. The actual sister invitation is accepted with an explicit request for noninterference. A role that previously looked like a threat to Kanoko’s place can now provide a protected place for a feeling it does not itself fulfill. [V04/20/i117–120.]

#### The new sisterhood changes the ensemble without completing its knowledge

The public proposal has an audience, so it is not free of situational pressure. However, it differs from the earlier tactical alliance and from a purely coerced role: it follows a substantial private repair, an offer to remain with the feeling, and Kanoko’s willing articulation of a condition. Her first-name gratitude gives further evidence of changed address and trust. Those events justify recording a newly accepted pact, while leaving the exact extent of any prior agreement and its future reliability bounded. [V04/20/i118–120.]

Hime’s response is both affectionate and limited by what she knows. She is pleased that Kanoko has another friend and says she worried that Kanoko’s accommodation to her had restricted other connections. This is strong evidence against assuming that Hime’s own conception of their special friendship requires Kanoko’s isolation. It is not the informed response forecast by WY3-PR02, because she has not been shown receiving the actual abolition proposal or exclusive-friend claim. The observed attitude and the untriggered prediction must remain distinct. [V04/20/i120–121.]

Sumika’s announced rule then changes from preventing sister dissolution to maintaining good relations among the salon members and wanting nobody missing next year. The new wording broadens the concern from keeping a particular pair intact to supporting a group. It also leaves a productive tension. Remaining together is a wish grounded in the earlier loss, and a rule demanding continuity could still be experienced as pressure if someone later wants to leave. V04 provides no such future test. The present achievement is the abandonment of one coercively fixed solution in favor of an articulated collective aspiration, not proof that the problem of departure has been solved for all circumstances. [V04/17/i007–008; V04/20/i122.]

### 3.5 Shift 21: the difference between “I dislike looking” and “there is something wrong with you”

#### Ordinary presentation retains choices the conflict did not settle

The summer chapter does not abandon character analysis in favor of an unrelated costume joke. It moves the problem of interpreting another person’s preference into ordinary school styling, work clothing, and embarrassment. Hime advises Kanoko about presentation; Mai introduces the new café uniform; the workers respond differently to what is offered. The new Sumika–Kanoko relation is already visible in supportive praise and in Sumika’s readiness to help her junior handle the performance. Kanoko’s positive response to Hime remains just as visible. A new friendship has enlarged her available support without erasing the earlier attachment. [V04/21/i125–130, i136–138.]

Mitsuki is pleased by the new clothes. Other people’s reactions focus on their different fit and on how guests may interpret the presentation. The analysis should keep her own pleasure separate from the reactions that surround it. She is not initially shown choosing the uniform in order to provoke Hime, nor is her body an objective fault to be corrected. The comic staging gives the reader access to Hime’s uneasy gaze and comparison, but it also depicts Mitsuki’s particular wish to enjoy wearing the same attractive work clothes as the others. [V04/21/i130–135.]

Hime has trouble saying what is wrong because more than one motive is active. She is embarrassed by looking, conscious of comparison, concerned with guest attention, and unwilling to make Mitsuki feel ashamed. Invoking what customers might think allows her to avoid owning the personal component. That avoidance is not merely tact: it creates room for Mitsuki to interpret the concern as a judgment about her suitability or dignity as a sister. The problem is partly produced by speaking an observer’s preference as though it were an impersonal standard. [V04/21/i131–135, i144–148.]

#### Work competence and affection remain different dimensions

Mitsuki insists that she is still a salon worker and undertakes service rather than simply withdrawing from view. This is important agency evidence within a chapter that visually emphasizes appearance. The question is not only what others wish to see; it is whether she can continue the work she understands herself to be doing. Mai hesitates to force a change on someone who genuinely likes the outfit, while Hime recognizes the need to give a reason rather than merely insist. Their responses echo the earlier distinction between anticipating another’s discomfort and deciding on that person’s behalf. [V04/21/i139–140.]

Hime’s own intervention remains mixed. She positions herself to redirect attention and takes orders effectively, which Mitsuki notices and praises. This is a concrete continuation of Hime’s acquired service skill. It is not a new test of whether admitting a limitation improves an unfamiliar task: the task is familiar, and the causal variable in WY1-PR04 is not isolated. At the same time, the useful work gives Hime a different perspective on Mitsuki’s response. Mitsuki’s pleasure in sharing the uniform and the work is not reducible to the suggestiveness that Hime perceives. [V04/21/i141–143.]

The parallel sister scene is also practically specific. Kanoko acknowledges difficulty performing; Sumika offers to carry more of that interaction. This supports the reality of post-pact assistance. It does not prove equal competence, equal labor, or a mature independence from the senior. A protective role may create room to learn and may also consolidate reliance. At this endpoint, the observable result is that the new relation functions at work and that Kanoko can name a limitation to someone other than Hime. [V04/21/i136–138.]

#### A well-meant correction can be heard as shame

The proposed wrap supplies a quick remedy from the observers’ perspective, but it does not initially match Mitsuki’s preference. When Hime cannot deny the suggestion that she is uncomfortable with Mitsuki’s appearance being seen, Mitsuki accepts the covering in a way that is visibly deflated. She apologizes in terms of being a shameful or unsuitable sister. The source does not endorse that assessment. It shows a person translating others’ reactions into a judgment against herself. [V04/21/i144–147.]

Hime’s correction becomes more useful when she distinguishes what had been combined. Mitsuki is not disgraceful; Hime’s own response to seeing her does not prove that she is. The correction also acknowledges a desire to keep others from looking, which should not be polished into wholly disinterested care. Concern, embarrassment, and possessive attention can coexist. The value of the speech lies in locating the feeling more accurately, not in making every motive admirable. [V04/21/i148–149.]

Mitsuki’s relieved response shows that this distinction matters to her. It does not show that every practical concern has disappeared. The eventual vest is a better accommodation because she likes the resulting design, not because she has been made to accept the proposition that her unmodified presentation was shameful. Mai credits Hime with the suggestion and recognizes that a practical solution could have come earlier. The resolution therefore combines speech repair with alteration of the material arrangement. Neither alone was sufficient in the preceding exchange. [V04/21/i149–150.]

#### The closing misunderstanding is local, comic, and consequential

Backstage, Mitsuki asks why Hime first rejected the shame interpretation and then proposed additional clothing. Hime attempts to distinguish her own embarrassment and envy from a defect in Mitsuki. The wording of wanting Mitsuki’s physique briefly sounds more person-directed than Hime intends. Her clarification establishes the intended comparison; Mitsuki’s embarrassment and complaint about misleading language show that the words carry an implication Hime has not recognized. [V04/21/i151–152.]

This is direct evidence of unequal pragmatic comprehension. It is not enough, on its own, to settle which kind of romantic or friendly liking each has explicitly acknowledged. The previous repair has made honest questions possible, but direct phrasing does not guarantee a shared interpretation. Mitsuki can ask for clarity and Hime can give a sincere answer while the answer introduces another mismatch. The volume’s final joke thus preserves the larger series problem in a smaller form: saying something true is not the same achievement as knowing what another person will hear. [V04/21/i151–152; inherited WY2-C09.]

The chapter’s body-focused comedy should neither be ignored nor allowed to replace the participants’ agency with a catalogue of appearance. The relevant formal evidence is who looks, who is looked at, who gets to define the problem, whose preferences are heard, and how a workable arrangement is negotiated. Its endpoint is a chosen accommodation and a remaining linguistic misunderstanding. It establishes neither reciprocal romance nor a permanent answer to how care should handle embarrassment. [V04/21/i130–152.]

## 4. Separately bounded supplementary witnesses

### 4.1 Shift EX, i154–157: a promotional story about indirect care

The collaboration short is explicitly identified as work created for Yuriten 2018. An office employee complains about a superior’s way of treating her and encounters Café Liebe through the event. The school fiction supplies a kind of respite from the work situation she has been describing. Hime and Mitsuki’s performance demonstrates the appeal of an immersive setting without requiring the short to establish a new stage in their private relationship. The real-event information at i156 belongs to the promotional component, not to a chronology of the mainline characters. [V04/EX/i154–156.]

The final page, i157, is essential to the short’s meaning. The superior explains that the indirect recommendation was meant to give an overworked subordinate room to rest. What looked like unnecessarily roundabout work is reinterpreted as a route to relief. That miniature reversal can be compared with the mainline’s interest in care whose purpose is initially misread. It cannot certify that every mainline intervention is benevolent, because the promotional story supplies its own explanatory closure rather than the same unresolved conditions. [V04/EX/i157.]

This component is therefore admitted for **bounded promotional-form analysis**, not as an unqualified event after Shift 21. No mainline employee is assigned a new ordinary friendship or preference from the anonymous guest’s experience. Printed event dates and listed exhibitors are inspected paratext, not independently researched historical claims and not a dating device for the Blumen contest.

### 4.2 Shift 21.5, i158–161: the old pair remains in work objects

Nene and Mai encounter remaining tea associated with the former Tachibana–Saionji sister pair. The practical question is what to do with stock that no longer has its original menu function. They propose using it for staff consumption. The leftover object thus records a relation that has ended institutionally without becoming materially absent from the workplace. The tea is evidence of a former paired offering, not proof that the former pair is restored. [V04/21.5/i158–159.]

A remembered selection scene gives the object a more particular emotional history. Sumika enjoys the elder-sister position and asks Saionji to show her more cheerful face; reciprocal help is part of how the role is explained. Nene then prepares the tea in the current kitchen setting and encounters a troubled Sumika. The contrast reverses who appears to need support. Hime interprets Nene’s arrival as encouragement, while Nene’s own expression and memory remain quieter than an explicit declaration of motive. [V04/21.5/i159–161.]

This is meaningful additional evidence for Nene and Sumika, but it does not establish a forgotten romance between them, cancel Nene’s criticism, or undo the former sister dissolution. A person can retain memory, assist a former partner in a role, and refuse that person’s earlier control. The supplement makes that coexistence visible through ordinary tea preparation rather than another climactic argument. [V04/21.5/i158–161; V04/18/i055–057.]

The present-kitchen frame occurs after Nene’s Saionji role has ended; the tea-selection memory occurs during the earlier sisterhood. The exact placement of the current tea break relative to the Blumen reconciliation and the summer-uniform ending is **not fixed here**. The clothing and recalled object do not justify treating the whole short as a new day after i152. It adds a separately bounded present/past comparison without advancing the mainline high-water mark.

### 4.3 Afterword and edition framing

The afterword discusses the production choice between making Sumika and Kanoko sisters sooner or allowing their relationship to develop first, and retrospectively comments on the length of that route. This is useful evidence of the creator’s account of composition. It is not permission to import an eventual romantic outcome or treat every apparent foreshadowing image as a dated story event. The account also acknowledges the collaboration material; it does not collapse that material into the mainline. [V04/paratext/i162–163.]

The service-manual extra supplies an in-fiction explanation for the summer uniform and identifies the wrap’s original practical purpose. This is edition paratext that can clarify a role mechanism or object. It does not override what the chapter actually shows the participants understanding at the time. The underlying cover and bonus illustration offer affectionate paired compositions, including a Nene–Goeidō image, but cannot establish a later reunion, mutual present commitment, or the sincerity of every past act. [V04/paratext/i164–168.]

These differences are not reasons to ignore paratext. They specify what it can answer. The mainline establishes actions and represented thoughts; the half-chapter adds a bounded memory-and-work scene; the collaboration stages a promotional encounter; the afterword reports compositional deliberation; the illustrations frame the volume’s relationships without a reliable event time. Keeping those responsibilities separate preserves more evidence than treating all pages either as interchangeable canon or as irrelevant extras.

## 11. Written Japanese: who may say what, to whom

**Canonical destination:** `03 Longitudinal Ledgers/WATAYURI_JAPANESE_SPEECH_ADDRESS_AND_REGISTER_LEDGER.md`. The following observations concern visible Japanese wording and address. English renderings are analytical paraphrases, not quotations from a translation edition. No acoustic delivery is inferred.

### WY4-JP01 — Practical inevitability as a social framing

The recurrent sense of **しょうがない** around Goeidō’s approaches makes particular allowances appear practically unavoidable. Sumika’s remembered account also explains how a technically plausible work reason can make an objection seem unreasonable. The linguistic issue is not that the phrase is inherently manipulative. It is that, in this sequence, repeated circumstantial explanations accumulate while Sumika’s discomfort receives little space. Record the speaker or focalizer for each occurrence; do not give Goeidō every phrase in Sumika’s narration. [V04/17/i010–016, especially i012; O02.]

### WY4-JP02 — Naming love versus understanding the person

Sumika’s repeated use of romantic classification is initially more decisive than her knowledge of Kanoko’s wishes. Kanoko later values precisely the act of having her feeling recognized as **恋**, even though she rejects the demand attached to it. The word acquires different pragmatic functions: accusation, causal warning, recognition, and finally something the listener agrees to remember. A lexical match does not make those speech acts equivalent. [V04/17/i027–032; V04/19/i079–085; V04/20/i117; O04/O12/O16.]

### WY4-JP03 — Nene’s compact separation of motive and effect

Nene’s contrast **優しいけど迷惑** separates a caring disposition or intention from the experience of receiving the intervention.
