## 1. The volume’s governing problem

Volume 7 reaches the answer that Volume 6 withheld, then refuses to let that answer perform more work than it can. Hime tells Mitsuki that she likes her, but not romantically, and cannot return the love being offered. This establishes a shared distinction between their feelings. It does not make Mitsuki stop loving her, make Hime comfortable with continued romantic attention, or automatically restore their former friendship and working arrangement. The central movement is therefore not from misunderstanding to a painless truth. It is from several incomplete explanations of avoidance to an explicit answer whose consequences still have to be lived. [V07/35/i097–116; V07/36/i129–150.]

Hime’s departure has a double significance. It is an exercise of agency: a previous agreement to remain does not prevent her from saying that she now wishes to leave. It is also an attempted solution to a relationship she cannot keep unchanged. She initially offers the real, administratively adequate fact that Mai has recovered, while withholding the intimacy problem that makes continued participation distressing. Her absence then becomes actual, not merely threatened. Yet the others cannot replace her wishes with their own need to preserve Liebe. Their legitimate concerns about the work, the sister role and her unhappiness give them reasons to ask, not an automatic right to keep her. [V07/32/i005–024; V07/36/i121–128.]

Kanoko’s response substantially changes the ensemble. The separation she previously wanted has occurred, but it has not produced the happy Hime she expected. She asks Sumika to help recover the Hime she encountered at Liebe, thereby acknowledging value in a life that includes Mitsuki and the café. This is a real revision, not proof that jealousy has disappeared. Her first private confrontation with Mitsuki exposes the confession and a useful distinction between declaring a feeling and considering its recipient; it also becomes accusatory and physically harmful. Only later does she revise her account of Hime’s response, apologize for hitting Mitsuki and cooperate with her. Recognition, aggression, apology and changed conduct must remain separately visible. [V07/32/i015–024; V07/33/i030–054; V07/34/i065–084.]

Mitsuki learns that asking to be heard is not the whole of communication. Nagisa’s advice outside the café distinguishes the legitimacy of having a wish from the consequences of how that wish is expressed. Kanoko’s later correction distinguishes romantic rejection from the loss of an already important friendship. Mitsuki consequently seeks an actual answer rather than treating Hime’s earlier pleasant presentation as sufficient. But she brings another expectation into the final conversation: once the confession is ended, Hime should no longer need to leave. Hime identifies the missing step. A completed exchange does not instantly extinguish its emotional cause. [V07/34/i058–064, i076–084; V07/36/i132–147.]

The ending allows both characters to be distressed without converting their distress into the same desire. Hime’s tears do not reveal a secretly affirmative romantic answer. Mitsuki’s tears do not invalidate Hime’s refusal. Hime can care about Mitsuki, dislike having to hurt her, refuse romance, offer a small act of comfort and still be unable to promise ordinary proximity. Mitsuki can learn to ask and listen, genuinely accept the words at one level, and still desperately resist the departure they have not prevented. The final separation between staffroom and stairwell leaves each with a grief the other cannot wholly observe. [V07/36/i137–150.]

## 3. Sequential and form-sensitive close reading

### 3.1 Shift 32: leaving a role does not make its history disappear

#### 3.1.1 The statement of departure acquires an audience and consequences

The opening gives Hime’s announcement the reception that V06 withheld. The objections identify different things at risk. Sumika resists the disappearance of the shared salon arrangement. Nene points to the Shirasagi role and the relationships built around it. Mitsuki asks about their sisterhood and about the promise between them. These are related losses, but they are not a single undifferentiated demand. Hime answers as though the original temporary-work premise can resolve them all: Mai’s arm has recovered, the assistance is no longer needed and departure sooner rather than later should minimize damage. [V07/32/i001, i005–009.]

That explanation contains true facts. Its inadequacy is not that a healed arm is imaginary or that a temporary worker cannot leave. It is that the explanation does not reveal the reason for the changed answer after Hime had already agreed to remain. Her suggestion that what she contributed in the salon can be regarded as if it had not happened attempts to make the emotional and institutional remainder disappear. Nene’s and Sumika’s objections show why other people cannot simply experience the history as nonexistent. At the same time, their investment does not obligate Hime to continue indefinitely. The scene holds an asymmetry: she can choose not to keep supplying her labor and performance, but cannot choose that others never valued them. [i005–006.]

Mitsuki asks the corresponding relational question. Hime says that sisterhood ends with the work while ordinary friendship can remain. This presents separation of categories as a solution. Yet it is asserted before the pair has discussed what their ordinary friendship now permits or how either can tolerate it. V07 will later show Hime refusing the equally simple inverse claim that a rejected confession restores the old working friendship. At the opening, however, she uses a tidy distinction to close a difficult exchange rather than to negotiate its implications. [i007–008; compare V07/36/i142–147.]

Her denial that Yano is at fault should therefore be preserved as an utterance, not expanded into the narrator’s complete causal verdict. It can protect Mitsuki from public blame, protect Hime from exposing a private event, and express her belief that failing to reciprocate is not a wrongdoing to be assigned to someone. The chapter does not yet decide the exact combination. Later Hime focalization will establish more than the group hears here. [i008; V07/35/i097–116.]

#### 3.1.2 Mai leaves an opening without declaring the problem solved

Mai does not respond by reinstating the false injury premise. She acknowledges the request, asks for time, defers announcing the departure and leaves room for Hime to retract it if her mind changes. This is a materially different procedure from keeping a relevant fact hidden so that the girls would stay. It recognizes that Hime’s answer can change. It also preserves management’s hope of retaining her, so it should not be idealized as an absence of organizational interest. [V07/32/i009.]

The immediate result is not ordinary continuation. The narration explicitly states that Hime no longer came, and the subsequent pages show the café without her. A recipient’s request for time has not prevented the absence from becoming real. Accordingly, V06’s claim that no completed departure had yet been shown must be updated at V07: **ordinary attendance has actually ceased during this interval**. That does not establish a legally characterized employment termination, a completed birthday exception or irreversible separation from every participant. [i010–014.]

The workplace scenes make absence visible through continuing activity rather than a completely emptied stage. Mai serves; Kanoko does her work; Sumika notices the disrupted arrangement; Mitsuki loses track of a service prompt. The environment continues, but not neutrally. This matters for the interpretation of indispensable care. Hime is neither a disposable mask whose removal leaves nothing nor a sole support without whom the business literally cannot operate. Practical substitution and relational loss coexist. [i011–014.]

#### 3.1.3 Kanoko discovers that winning the separation was not getting Hime back

Kanoko’s first private reckoning directly revisits her earlier project. Hime and Mitsuki are now apart. By the old logic, this should restore the comfortable situation before Liebe. But the visible Hime, and Kanoko’s reaction to her, do not match that predicted benefit. The phone photographs and the retained star accessory carry the history of special attention into this disappointment. They no longer simply secure a place; they help her notice the difference between possession of a familiar relationship and the other person’s present happiness. [i015–016.]

Kanoko tries direct access before the later indirect intervention. She asks Hime at school why the decision changed so suddenly. Hime repeats the administratively convenient explanation. A school bell interrupts the opportunity, but interruption is not the whole reason the exchange fails: Hime continues to present no problem when Kanoko later uses the birthday arrangements as a messaging pretext and then offers help more openly. The offer meets a reassuring refusal rather than the real account. [i017–023.]

This does not establish that Kanoko now understands the confession, and it certainly does not establish knowledge of the kiss. What she understands is a discrepancy between Hime’s assurances and the person she sees. Her initial hypothesis about Mitsuki will be partly right, but the source first gives an attentional achievement, not complete causal knowledge. It also gives a limit on their special friendship: being the person who has often heard Hime’s private self does not guarantee access when Hime cannot bear to articulate the problem. [i022–024.]

The call to Sumika is consequently more than routine cooperation between in-role sisters. Kanoko voluntarily approaches someone outside the original Hime–Kanoko dyad because direct inquiry has failed. More importantly, she names the desired Hime as the one she saw **working at Liebe**, rather than simply the one who existed before Mitsuki intruded. That revised object of care is the chapter’s principal developmental change. It does not prove generalized altruism or the extinction of jealousy, but it gives her a reason to work for a setting she previously wanted to use against a rival bond. [i024; its expanded account at V07/33/i030–034.]

### 3.2 Shift 33: correct information can emerge through a harmful intervention

#### 3.2.1 A supportive pact is renegotiated, not automatically applied

The chapter returns to the planning call rather than pretending that the intervention emerged from a single agreed purpose. Kanoko wants something done about Yano. Sumika explicitly refuses to help stage a fight and questions what Kanoko is asking for, given her earlier wish to separate the pair. Kanoko admits that wish, then explains why Hime’s new state is not the outcome she wanted. Sumika is not asked merely to confirm an unchanged jealous plan. The conversation exposes and renegotiates the purpose of the help. [V07/33/i030–034.]

Kanoko invokes the shared café in terms shaped by Sumika’s own concern that nobody be missing. This does not mean that the earlier Blumen rule has acquired coercive legal force. It is being recalled as a desired form of belonging. Sumika’s pleased sister-language gives this convergence a role-mediated expression, while Kanoko resists making her reason simply the fact that they are sisters. The private concern precedes and exceeds performance. Their cooperation is strongest when its actual object is identified, not when the public label is used as a substitute for agreement. [i032–034.]

Mai and Sumika remain limited observers. Mai admits that she had thought the visit repaired the pair and did not understand the reversal. Kanoko’s observation helps reveal that limitation. Management’s willingness to organize a conversation is therefore not evidence of an omniscient scheme. It is a response to uncertainty, with risks created by assigning an emotionally involved person to investigate another emotionally involved person. [i034–035.]

#### 3.2.2 Privacy is deliberately created, then partially breached by escalation

The salon is repurposed after work for a private conversation. Sumika turns off the monitor and refuses to let the managerial interest in the situation justify watching it. The act is small but consequential: a space normally structured by being seen is temporarily made less observable. The later camera manual confirms the practical existence of the monitoring system, but the mainline act itself establishes the privacy decision. [i027–029; edition i165, separately classified.]

The privacy is not absolute. As the discussion grows louder and becomes physically dangerous, Sumika and Mai enter. That does not entitle the analysis to distribute every previous sentence to them. Kanoko hears a substantive confession account from Mitsuki; the others have evidence of the confrontation and at least some of its escalation. Exact knowledge of every preceding claim remains separate. Switching off the monitor is neither proof that nothing can be heard nor proof that the whole conversation was effectively public. [i036–054.]

#### 3.2.3 The unasked question is what Hime was expected to do

Mitsuki initially resists explaining herself. Kanoko reconstructs a sequence: the visit, the initial willingness to remain, the later avoidance and the changed work answer. Mitsuki identifies the confession as the available explanation and tells her about it. This is a genuine information transition. It is not yet Hime’s confirmation of the full cause, and the account does not give Kanoko the kiss. [i036–040.]

Kanoko then identifies a real gap in Mitsuki’s model. Mitsuki understands herself to have disclosed a feeling under the agreement to speak truthfully. She does not initially formulate the exchange as one requiring Hime to decide what response she can give. The insistence that she expected nothing is challenged by the recipient’s practical burden: after a confession, continuing in the same relationship may no longer feel like a neutral act. [i041–047.]

Kanoko’s diagnosis is not the same as a neutral solution. She treats Hime’s likely answer as already known, interprets the difficulty through prior rejected suitors and demands that Mitsuki let the confession be ended. This supplies a path toward clarity, but initially compresses a particular friendship into a general category of unwanted confession. The later chapter will correct that compression from within Kanoko’s own understanding. At this stage, she can be right that Hime’s response matters and wrong to presume that receiving a rejection is the only missing step. [i042–047; V07/34/i080–084.]

Mitsuki’s tears and refusal to let it end reveal why her declaration of no expectation cannot be treated as exhaustive self-knowledge. Not demanding an explicit romantic answer is compatible with hoping that closeness can continue, hoping that the confession will not be rejected and fearing that rejection will destroy an already precious place. The source does not require that her earlier statement be a calculated lie. It shows an expectation becoming visible under threat. [V07/33/i044–049.]

#### 3.2.4 The matching objects expose the rivalry rather than settling it

Mitsuki’s comparison with Kanoko shifts the argument from Hime’s answer to the right to stand beside her. Kanoko insists on the distinctness of her special friendship. Mitsuki then points to the matching birthday objects, showing the anklet and explaining the three-person design. This is new information for Kanoko, not the first time the reader knows that Hime chose gifts for both. The same objects that supported reciprocal-care repair in V06 now disturb an exclusivity assumption. [i048–051; V06/29/i077–079.]

The objects do not prove identical feelings. A shared motif across different items expresses Hime’s inclusion, not an equivalence between friendship and romance or a uniform degree of attachment. Nor does Mitsuki’s recognition of similarity establish that Kanoko has explicitly confessed her own romance to Mitsuki. Rivalry, selective attention and analogous pain are visible; the exact category of what Mitsuki knows about Kanoko must not outrun her words. [V07/33/i048–052.]

The discussion culminates in a blow. Kanoko hits Mitsuki; Sumika intervenes, restrains and removes her; Mai remains with Mitsuki. The later apology explicitly identifies the hit, confirming that this sequence cannot be reduced to a table being struck. The analysis need not invent a precise force, injury or diagnosis. It must nonetheless retain the concrete boundary violation. Concern for Hime and the usefulness of an earlier argument do not make violence an acceptable or successful communicative method. [i046, i052–054; V07/34/i083.]

The chapter therefore yields both an epistemic gain and a damaged interaction. Kanoko knows about the confession and the anklet. Mitsuki has been forced to consider Hime’s response and her own concealed expectations. But neither has yet heard Hime’s actual account, and the intervention itself requires repair. Calling this a successful mediation because it produces information would erase the harm by which the scene ends. [V07/33/i040–054.]

### 3.3 Shift 34: learning from a failed conversation without declaring it harmless

#### 3.3.1 An ordinary-school listener supplies a different frame

Mai apologizes for her supervisory failure after the confrontation and admits that she does not understand everything that happened. Mitsuki, meanwhile, begins to accept the proposition that ending her confession might make Hime return. That is a newly adopted explanation, not yet a proven causal mechanism. It will motivate useful action and later reveal its own limitation. [V07/34/i055–057.]

The school sequence prevents the café from monopolizing insight. Mitsuki’s classmates notice her preoccupation and offer to listen. The ponytailed classmate is named **Nagisa / 渚**; the short-haired companion is left unnamed in this record. Mitsuki describes the difficulty involving someone she likes at work without giving these listeners the complete private history. Their advice is correspondingly advice to a partial account, not an omniscient adjudication of the kiss, the gift dispute or Hime’s motives. [i058–064.]

Nagisa separates several propositions that the prior argument had joined. The recipient decides what answer to give. A confession expresses the speaker’s wish, but having such a wish is not by itself proof that confessing was wrong. The manner of acting and the failure to consider the recipient remain open to criticism. This moves Mitsuki away from treating rejection as a punishment she must undergo merely for loving and toward considering what another person needs from the exchange. The distinction matters precisely because Nagisa does not guarantee success. [i060–064.]

The classmates’ surprise when the loved person is identified as a girl is a local response, not a general statement about their society. It is not followed by the kind of rejection that would justify turning this scene into evidence of Mitsuki’s exclusion on that ground. Conversely, a supportive exchange with these particular peers does not prove that all her social contexts are safe or that her earlier difficulty was imaginary. The scene adds an available support route and a piece of corrective reasoning, not a completed social biography. [i061–064.]

#### 3.3.2 A demanded apology is not yet a chosen apology

Back at Liebe, Sumika tries to make Kanoko apologize. Kanoko refuses. Mitsuki does not want an easy apology that would erase the force of what Kanoko felt, but this is not a declaration that being hit was right. Sumika insists on their ability to work together and then acknowledges her own angry tone. The exchange contains three distinct positions: the need to repair conduct, the reluctance to perform a false reconciliation and the practical necessity of functioning in the shared workplace. None completely cancels the others. [i065–067.]

It would be especially misleading to score the later public scene as proof that a coerced apology worked. The apology has not occurred at this point. The subsequent change comes through another way of encountering one another. Keeping that order intact allows the volume to show why a procedure that simply demands the right words can fail, even when the underlying demand for accountability is legitimate. [i065–084.]

#### 3.3.3 The role becomes a means of inquiry, not just concealment

Mitsuki recognizes that Kanoko may understand parts of Hime’s life that she does not. After Mai supplies guests with the school-busyness explanation for Hime’s absence, Mitsuki initiates an in-role conversation asking about Hime from Kanoko’s perspective. She asks rather than simply declaring that the rival knows nothing. The salon gives the exchange a recognizable form and a reason to remain courteous, although it also makes refusing difficult under audience attention. Its enabling and pressuring functions coexist. [i068–075.]

Kanoko describes Hime’s school behavior and care for friends: help received, help returned, ordinary conversation and pleasure in spending time together at the salon. The content matters because it is not a claim that Hime values only Kanoko. It gives Mitsuki information that fits the letter’s earlier request to let Hime contribute. It also permits Kanoko to describe a valued Hime without reducing her to a secret possession or an object whose ordinary sociability is wholly false. [i071–074; compare V06/29/i069–072.]

The guests respond warmly to the exchange. Their pleasure confirms that the performance succeeds as a public scene, not that they have learned the private confession or judged the earlier violence. Mitsuki’s gratitude can nevertheless be sincere within it. The scene is a useful counterexample to both extremes: public performance is neither automatically empty nor an automatic certificate of private resolution. [V07/34/i074–075.]

#### 3.3.4 Kanoko corrects the premise she herself helped impose

The cleaning conversation supplies private uptake. Mitsuki says that she wanted to know Kanoko herself. This is different from treating her only as a source of information about Hime. She acknowledges Kanoko’s ability to give something to Hime and contrasts it with her own feeling that she only receives. The latter is an emotionally significant self-assessment, not an objective inventory. The earlier source clearly contains Mitsuki’s practical help, instruction and care. Her sense of inadequacy is evidence of how she values what she contributes under this threat. [i076–079; inherited WY5-C03–C05 and WY6-C06.]

Kanoko then revises the general rejected-suitor frame. Hime ordinarily refuses unwanted confessions readily. Her hesitation here, together with her repeated wish to be with Mitsuki and the matching gifts, indicates that Mitsuki already matters as a friend. The reluctance to answer need not mean that no significant bond existed. Kanoko distinguishes wanting to end a romantic confession from wanting to expel its speaker from one’s life. This is not proof that she now knows every aspect of Hime’s experience; it is a better account of evidence that her anger had initially flattened. [V07/34/i080–082.]

That correction is especially important because it relinquishes a part of Kanoko’s exclusivity claim. She tells her rival that the rival already has a special place. This does not erase the jealousy that made the anklet disclosure painful. It shows her choosing a statement that is useful to Hime’s desired world even though it limits her own privileged self-description. The revised act is stronger evidence of change than merely saying she wants Hime happy. [i080–084.]

The apology for hitting is then self-offered. Its timing follows the exchange rather than the earlier order to apologize. Mitsuki receives it with gratitude and asks why Kanoko is now affirming her importance. Kanoko’s answer preserves ambivalence: Mitsuki’s directness can disturb others, yet that same willingness to act is needed if they are to reach Hime. The emergent alliance is practical and conditional. It is not a new sister pact, an end to rivalry or a reciprocal romantic reclassification. [i083–084.]

The chapter’s title can thus be read through the sequence rather than as praise for failure itself. A failed intervention becomes material for more careful action. The hit does not become justified because another conversation follows. The improvement lies in the revised interpretation, the chosen apology and the ability to ask what another person knows before imposing a solution. [i055–084.]

### 3.4 Shift 35: the feared harm is not an absence of care

#### 3.4.1 Hime’s ordinary refusal provides a comparison, not a universal law

A boy asks Hime out, and she refuses dating rather than insulting him personally. They agree to remain ordinary friends. Her polished response is compatible with a clear negative answer; this is not a case where she cannot ever say no. The later use of the surname **Tanabe / 田辺** identifies the boy within this episode without supplying a wider biography. [V07/35/i085, i089.]

The subsequent school discussion shows why Hime does not experience such exchanges as costless. Other students have heard about the confession through another boy. They reassure her that she has not done wrong. Tanabe nevertheless avoids her, and Hime interprets this as another instance of a familiar pattern: people promise that things will remain normal, then do not behave normally. The witnessed event supports the local difference between promise and conduct. Her generalization about all such cases remains her account, not independently verified universal evidence. [i089–091.]

Her inner explanation restores the larger architecture of her self-presentation. She wants to be liked, to avoid a bad atmosphere and to keep disapproval away from the carefully maintained adorable persona. But the chapter does not let that motive exhaust her. What she fears in Mitsuki’s case is not simply losing another admirer. The later retrospective will show that the person whose pain she wants to avoid is someone she already values and does not want to deceive with a false romance. [i087–088, i097–116.]

The matching accessory on Hime’s bag also matters in this context. Her own participation in the design remains visible while the other two struggle over their items. It is not a token she casually distributed and forgot. The object belongs to a real attempt to include others, though inclusion did not settle what each hoped it meant. [i088; V07/33/i050–051.]

#### 3.4.2 Kanoko requests an answer without dictating which answer is good

The message fixes the postponed joint birthday celebration for **Sunday, July 30**. No absolute year is inferred from that weekday/date pairing. Kanoko candidly acknowledges that combining the event helps make sure Hime comes. Hime responds in terms of responsibility rather than spontaneous enthusiasm. The event therefore gives the later meeting an opportunity while also creating a social pressure; it is not an entirely neutral encounter chosen without any surrounding obligations. [i091–093.]

Kanoko then tells Hime that she knows about Mitsuki’s confession and obtained that knowledge from Mitsuki. This changes Hime’s information state. It does not reveal Kanoko’s old abolition proposal, her own romantic confession to Sumika or every word exchanged with Mitsuki. The scene should not be used to make Hime retrospectively informed about those different matters. [i093–095.]

Kanoko asks Hime to choose what she actually thinks is good and not escape through performance. The request does not dictate a romantic yes. It recognizes that a merely agreeable response would not answer the problem. Its force also comes from the specific friendship: Kanoko is one of the people who can identify Hime’s practiced evasion as such. Nevertheless, Hime’s subsequent inner struggle shows that identifying the mask does not by itself make removing it easy. [i094–096.]

#### 3.4.3 V07 supplies the interior account that V06 could not freeze

The chapter then revisits the sickroom encounter and its aftermath. These are not a second kiss, a second visit or new versions of the earlier events. They are later-presented evidence of Hime’s reception. The prior freeze correctly left her full explanation unestablished; V07 now changes the current account. [i097–115; V06/29/i085–088; V06/30/i125–126; V06/31/i129–151.]

Hime had expected that finding Mitsuki’s genuine liking would solve a problem. She endured hostility and jealousy to reach it. The discovery is instead of a different kind of liking from the one she wanted to receive. She explicitly understands her own feeling as nonromantic. This defeats the inference that her disturbance must be secretly affirmative attraction merely because it is intense, embodied or accompanied by tears. It also defeats the opposite overreach that a particular nonreciprocal relationship defines her lifelong orientation or capacity for every form of love. [V07/35/i097–102.]

She wants to apologize and reject the romance, but cannot say it to Yano. She also cannot immediately tell Kanoko. The failure of the familiar confidante route is not proof that the friendship was false. It identifies a problem Hime cannot yet represent comfortably even within the privileged relationship. Kanoko knows many of her ordinary private thoughts, but this is not an ordinary private complaint. [i099–104.]

At home, Hime attempts alternative explanations. Perhaps the contact was an awkward expression of friendship, perhaps a joke, perhaps it did not mean what she fears. These are defensive hypotheses generated by a character, not equally supported conclusions the analyst must retain after the fuller evidence. Mitsuki’s subsequent happiness and the recovery-concealment information make the romantic seriousness increasingly difficult to deny. Hime’s inference that Mitsuki helped keep her at the café belongs to this retrospective self-account; it is not a new publicly shared transcript of the full retention plan. [i103–107.]

This use of the recovery information is important for the inherited forecast. Hime thinks about it as evidence of Mitsuki’s investment. She still does not deliver the predicted explicit complaint about informed choice or trust. An analyst may reasonably examine the ethical problem of the concealment, but that examination cannot be substituted for a character response that has not occurred. The forecast’s earlier adverse evidence must remain visible. [i107; WY5-PR01 and §14.]

#### 3.4.4 The contemplated lover-role is not a relationship she accepts

Hime considers whether she could perform the role of a lover because she does like Mitsuki as a person. This is a possible strategy in her interior deliberation, not an agreement made to Mitsuki and not evidence of shared romantic desire. The very formulation distinguishes affection from willingness to enact the desired relationship. [i108–112.]

Some forms of closeness can be understood through the friendship she already recognizes. Others cannot. The recollection of the kiss and the sense of being looked at romantically make the strategy intolerable to her; the subsequent recoil and dropped tray belong to the earlier Sunday shift already shown in V06. A task error here is not evidence that all her acquired practical competence has vanished. Its narrative function is to expose a limit in her ability to maintain the reassuring presentation. [i109–112.]

The obstacle is also ethical in a character-specific sense. Hime says she cannot offer the love Mitsuki wants and cannot lie to Yano by pretending to be that lover. This is not a general renunciation of impression management. It is a boundary on what she can do to this particular person under these conditions. Her genuine care makes false reciprocation more difficult, not more legitimate. [i113–116.]

The chapter ends with the wish not to hurt Mitsuki. That wish explains why avoidance can feel protective to Hime while still leaving Mitsuki confused. Explanation is not proof of success: the absence has already harmed the person she is trying not to wound. Nor does it make every aspect of her retreat selfless. She also wants to avoid having to experience herself as the person who says the devastating words. The source supports both concern for Mitsuki and aversion to the painful interaction; it does not require choosing one as the only real motive. [i115–116; V07/32/i010–014.]

### 3.5 Shift 36: a clear answer cannot promise an unchanged world

#### 3.5.1 The manager creates a possible meeting rather than claiming to know its answer

Hime’s arrival is followed by a marked return to **a few days earlier**, when Mai met her at another café. The unfamiliar location matters: the discussion is not already inside the salon’s sister fiction, and Hime begins by guarding against a retention attempt. Mai distinguishes hearing a worker’s requests and concerns from simply insisting that she stay. She offers concrete procedures—mediation, arranging a discussion, responding to problems—rather than claiming that the healed arm has answered every question. [V07/36/i119–126.]

Hime initially anticipates and closes the available openings. She denies that there is a relevant interpersonal problem and asks Mai to look after the others after she leaves. Mai notices the practiced way she avoids contact, but does not identify the whole private cause. Her admission of uncertainty is important. The scene does not turn managerial experience into privileged knowledge of a teenager’s feelings or authorize the analysis to diagnose Hime through the manager’s impression. [i123–124.]

Mai then conveys a different employee request: Mitsuki wants an opportunity to talk with Hime about a problem. Mai says she has not been told its substance. The birthday provides a possible time, and Hime is asked to accept the conversation. Hime reflects on the fact that Mitsuki is prepared to face a painful answer and agrees. This is a real assent to a meeting, not assent to romance, touch or continued employment. [i125–128.]

The procedure is mixed rather than perfectly neutral. Mai wants the workplace to function; Kanoko has helped structure the birthday attendance; Hime feels responsibility for coming. Nevertheless, the source supplies an actual request and a displayed decision, not an unseen command. The improvement over the earlier recovery concealment lies in giving the current question a form in which Hime can hear it and answer. It does not retroactively authorize the concealment. [i121–128; inherited WY5-C06/C07.]

#### 3.5.2 An apology and a familiar façade do not yet constitute the reply

Back at the current arrival, the others leave to prepare, and Hime and Mitsuki speak privately in the staffroom. Mitsuki apologizes for confessing one-sidedly without considering Hime. This is an explicit acknowledgment of the way the disclosure was handled. It is not a separately worded apology specifically naming the kiss, and the reading must not supply one because that would make the repair more complete. [V07/36/i129.]

Hime answers with practiced reassurance: the matter is over, the work was temporary, there is no reason to assign blame. Mitsuki recognizes the register she uses toward an ordinary audience and invokes the promise to speak truthfully. The crucial contrast is not simply polite versus rude. It is between a socially acceptable answer that ends the occasion and an answer that makes the relevant feeling mutually knowable. Hime’s politeness is not false in every detail, but it avoids the requested content. [i130–133.]

Mitsuki’s insistence contains both progress and pressure. She says she is prepared to be rejected and asks Hime to put the answer into words. Her clenched hands, posture and interrupted composure show that this readiness is effortful rather than an absence of fear. At the same time, Hime is visibly distressed, prefers to leave the subject unspoken and is urged not to escape. The scene should not be turned into a universal lesson that a confessor is entitled to whatever answer, timing or bodily access she seeks. Here an agreed meeting becomes a difficult, pressured exchange whose concrete outcome must be assessed separately. [i132–140.]

Mitsuki also specifies what she likes rather than making only a demand. Her account includes Hime’s smile and appearance, kindness, companionship and help. Receiving Hime’s contribution remains part of the bond after the V06 letter; the new confession is not simply a regression to wanting an incapable dependent. Her description can be sincere even though it does not obligate Hime to feel the same way. [i134–135.]

#### 3.5.3 Hime’s boundary becomes shared, not merely visible to the reader

Hime says that since the confession, being touched and related conduct have become unpleasant for her, and that she cannot receive Mitsuki’s feeling as offered. This is more precise evidence than an isolated blush, a startled panel or the analyst’s interpretation of the tray incident. It is a direct, addressed statement about the current relation. It should be used to constrain future models of acceptable contact, without turning it into a global prohibition on every touch from every person for all time. [i137–138.]

She also says why she resists the words: she believes Mitsuki already knows the answer and that saying it will only hurt her. Mitsuki counters that she does not know unless it is spoken and asks to end the confession. Each is trying to remove a different uncertainty. Hime wants the foreseeable injury not to be enacted; Mitsuki wants the relationship not to remain organized by an answer she has never received. The fact that both aims are understandable does not make them jointly achievable without cost. [i138–140.]

At **i141**, Hime makes the distinction explicit to Mitsuki: she likes her, but her liking is not romantic, differs from Mitsuki’s and cannot answer that romantic feeling. The direct statement confirms what the preceding chapter’s interiority established for the reader. It is a negative romantic answer that retains positive personal attachment. It is not a confession of reciprocal romance, a total withdrawal of affection or a merely ambiguous smile. [i141.]

Mitsuki cries and collapses downward. The answer has been obtained; the feared hurt has occurred. The source does not frame those tears as a reason Hime should have offered a false affirmative answer. It shows why the truthful negative answer was difficult for a character whose familiar social practice is designed to avoid becoming a source of rejection. [i140–142.]

#### 3.5.4 The failed reset exposes the persistence of feeling

Mitsuki then proposes the hoped-for consequence: now the confession is over, they can go back, so Hime need not quit. Hime’s surprise reveals that this was not their shared understanding of the conversation’s purpose. She distinguishes the end of the exchange from the disappearance of love. If Mitsuki still loves her, ordinary proximity may repeatedly require her to refuse that desire. Hime does not believe a single answer can restore the earlier situation by itself. [i142–145.]

This is a substantive limit on Kanoko’s improved advice, not proof that the advice was worthless. Kanoko correctly recognized an important friendship and correctly opposed treating romantic rejection as evidence that it never existed. But a friendship’s prior reality does not guarantee that both can immediately inhabit it without the new asymmetry being felt. The final conversation tests the difference between identifying a bond and specifying sustainable conditions for continuing it. [V07/34/i080–084; V07/36/i143–145.]

Mitsuki says she can quickly get rid of the feeling and asks Hime to remain. Hime recognizes that assurance as a lie. The promise is born of desperation rather than established capacity. It is also a reversal in who sees through the protective presentation: the usually blunt person offers an impossible reassurance, while the practiced performer identifies what cannot be made true simply by being said. Their earlier debate about genuine feeling is not resolved by assigning honesty permanently to one character and falsehood to the other. [i146–147.]

Hime’s rebuke of Mitsuki’s excessive seriousness is similarly situated. From Hime’s immediate perspective, asking for the answer has added pain while leaving her planned departure unchanged. That does not establish a narratorial rule that it would have been better never to speak or that all clarification is cruel. It shows how she experiences the failure of the encounter to avoid the very harm she feared. The current evidence establishes a clarified romantic boundary, not an agreed evaluation of the conversation’s value. [i147.]

#### 3.5.5 Comfort remains possible without a promise to return

Mitsuki clings and lowers herself into Hime’s lap as she refuses the prospect of separation. Hime is also crying. A small comforting contact follows, and Hime suggests that Mitsuki go to the salon once she calms down, saying she will come later. This is not an affirmative response to the romantic confession. It is a limited act in the presence of another person’s distress, after a clear negative answer. The contact should not be used to retroactively authorize the kiss or erase the newly stated difficulty with romanticized touching. [i148–149.]

The exact scope of the promise matters. Hime has come for the birthday exception and the conversation. Saying she will go to the salon later does not retract her cessation of ordinary work. The volume does not show her putting on the uniform again, completing the party, accepting a future shift or concluding a workable friendship agreement. Those omissions are material because each could change a different state. [i149–150.]

The final page moves Hime outside the room. Mitsuki remains in tears; Hime crouches alone on the stairs and cries. The spatial division prevents either character from possessing the other’s whole experience. The reader sees that the person who refused is also hurt, but not that she wants the same romance. This is the ending’s strongest formal refusal of an easy equivalence: mutual suffering is not mutual romantic desire, and tenderness is not a guarantee that continuing as before is possible. [i150.]

## 4. Separate shorts and edition witnesses

### 4.1 Shift 36.1: supporting a person versus preparing for her defeat

The six-page short returns to Sumika and Kanoko during the unresolved confession/intervention period. Sumika knows that Mitsuki has confessed, while Kanoko focuses on how that development has affected Hime and on what should be done about Mitsuki. The birthday and its possible romantic consequences remain prospective within their discussion. This is not a conversation after the final i141 rejection or after Hime’s stairwell collapse. A placement near the earlier intervention is plausible; a precise hour or exact insertion between mainline panels is not established. [V07/S36.1/i152–157.]

Sumika initially anticipates the moment when Kanoko might be hurt and need the listener she promised to be. When she considers the possibility of Hime and Mitsuki becoming a couple, she realizes that she has been imagining Kanoko’s heartbreak rather than the range of Kanoko’s possible wishes and outcomes. The insight concerns the assumptions inside her support. She has not simply become an all-knowing, unconditionally benevolent confidante because she once promised to listen. [i153–155.]

Her sudden declaration that she supports Kanoko and will be there for her produces a useful question: what exactly is being supported? Sumika reaches for Kanoko’s happiness, while Kanoko points out that this is not the conversation they are having. The awkwardness is not evidence that Kanoko rejects the entire bond. It shows that the same supportive vocabulary can carry different immediate purposes for speaker and recipient. [i156–157.]

Sumika’s closing guilt is particularly important. Preparing to remain when someone cries can become an unexamined expectation that the person will cry. The short exposes that tendency without naming a complete romantic self-understanding for Sumika. A reading of emerging personal attachment is possible, but the direct evidence here is her self-surprise, anticipated role and unease about presuming Kanoko’s failure. No new reciprocal romance or explicit Sumika confession should be promoted from this episode. [i154–157.]

This is consequential supplementary characterization, not decorative comedy. It belongs in the relationship, information, performance and readiness homes with its temporal restriction. It also qualifies a simplistic reading of the mainline call as proof that the support relationship is now perfectly understood by both participants.

### 4.2 Shift 36.2: the manager’s work and her account of autonomy

The two-page explainer lists tasks that are easy to miss when attention remains on salon performance: reservation coordination, schedules, suppliers, cash/register and income management, event planning and care for the cast. Some are already visible in the mainline, while the direct-address presentation adds a broader account of the job. This supports an institutional model in which Mai’s contribution is more than recruitment trickery or appearing at emotionally convenient moments. [V07/S36.2/i158–159.]

The explainer also frames respect for staff initiative as a policy. That self-description must remain distinguishable from every instance of conduct. It cannot erase the earlier recovery concealment, prove compliance with legal requirements or certify that every managerial intervention gives adequate choice. Conversely, the earlier deception does not make all the practical tasks imaginary. A useful cumulative account holds observed competence, stated policy and actual boundary decisions in separate fields. [i158–159; inherited WY5-C06/C07/C16.]

Mai’s wish for an enjoyable break after things settle is prospective. No vacation, staff trip or post-ending continuation is established merely because she imagines one. The short also explicitly distinguishes **小柴舞 / Koshiba Mai**, her ordinary name, from **御子柴舞 / Mikoshiba Mai**, the café identity. This is an alias update to the existing character subject, not discovery of a second manager. [i158–159.]

### 4.3 Production afterword and character profiles

The afterword discusses the creator’s work during the period it identifies with COVID-era restrictions and remote arrangements. It belongs to production testimony. It is not used to date the fictional birthday year, establish public-health propositions or import later historical events into the manga’s chronology. The relevant analytical point is the separation between how the work was made and when its events occur. [V07/paratext/i160–161.]

The two character-introduction pages provide explicit name readings and role distinctions. In addition to the established Hime, Mitsuki, Kanoko and Sumika names, they confirm **小柴舞 / こしばまい** and **西寺寧々 / にしでらねね / Nishidera Nene**; Nene’s former salon role is **西園寺寧々 / Saionji Nene**. These are recoverable edition-witness facts. They update the same **WY1-CAST-MAI** and **WY3-CAST-NENE** records, with first explicit admission here, without rewriting the earlier files as though these ordinary surnames had already been verified there. [i162–163.]

The profiles also summarize the characters evaluatively. Such summaries can be recorded as edition framing but should not replace the more differentiated mainline account. A short statement connecting Hime’s façade to earlier hurt cannot overturn the already shown fact that deliberate presentation predates the rupture. A description of Mai as caring does not independently settle the permissibility of concealment. A profile’s romance language does not establish reciprocal romance that the chapter explicitly refuses. [i162–163; earlier WY2-C02, WY5-C07, WY6-C08.]

### 4.4 Camera manual, cover matter and bonus anecdote

The service-manual page explains the salon camera and the kitchen/backstage monitors, including their use in tracking floor conditions and coordinating service. It also gives an in-role answer for guests. This is useful supplementary institutional context for Sumika’s decision to turn off the monitor in Shift 33. It does not establish that private staffroom conversations are always recorded or that every worker sees every feed. [V07/edition/i165; V07/33/i029.]

The cover portrait, underlying cover, back-cover figures and promotional descriptions frame the volume’s emotional and relational concerns. They are not separately dated scenes. In particular, a group illustration cannot establish reconciliation after the stairwell ending. The crying Hime on the cover can be discussed as an anticipatory framing of the volume’s pain, but her exact feelings are grounded in the mainline, not inferred from the cover alone. [Cover; i164, i166–167.]

The electronic-edition bonus includes a brief narrated anecdote in which Mai reports working late, missing a train and being mistaken for a student by police, then correcting that impression. Its comic storytelling and staff reaction belong to the bonus witness. It is **not a dream**, not independent evidence of an exact age or a legal rule, and not a demonstrated gathering after the mainline ending. The surrounding illustration is explicitly labeled as an electronic-edition extra. [i168.]

### 9.1 Why the harm/repair sequence cannot be compressed

The MK sequence contains a private interrogation, a blow, an imposed apology opportunity that fails, public cooperation, private correction and a self-offered apology. Removing the blow makes the later repair too easy; treating the blow as the whole relation makes the correction impossible to see. The exact achievement is a new capacity to speak and work together around Hime without denying that the prior intervention failed. [AG06–AG11.]

The HM sequence likewise contains more than a binary choice between communication and avoidance. Hime chooses an absence, accepts a limited conversation, struggles to answer, gives a genuine no, and resists the inference that the no restores prior conditions. Mitsuki apologizes, asks, listens to the categorical answer and then shows how difficult it is to accept its practical implications. A single “reconciled” or “broken” field would lose the mechanism the volume makes visible. [AG01/AG15–AG19.]

### 9.2 Agency is not possession of a painless option

Hime’s right not to reciprocate does not provide a way to make Mitsuki feel unhurt. Mitsuki’s ability to ask does not provide control over the answer or the other person’s continued work. Kanoko’s concern does not authorize force, and Sumika’s sincere support does not eliminate her own assumptions. This is an interpretation of the depicted options and costs, not a claim that every participant has equal responsibility for every injury. [i052–054, i137–150; S36.1/i154–157.]

### 11.1 Hime’s language is not exhausted by the public/private binary

Hime’s public refusal of Tanabe is capable of conveying a genuine no. Her private responses to Mai and Mitsuki initially avoid the relevant answer. ### 11.2 Repetition changes the proposition being tested

Across the earlier volumes, the question “does she like me?” often concerned the reality of affection beneath performance. V07 makes that question insufficient. The answer can be yes to personal liking and no to romantic reciprocity. ### 11.3 Speech models remain state-bounded
