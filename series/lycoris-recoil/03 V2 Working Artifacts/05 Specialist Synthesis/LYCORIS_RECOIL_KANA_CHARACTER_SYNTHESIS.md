# Kana

At Café LycoReco, Kana once reminds the adults admiring her enjoyment of a dessert that they are welcome to treat her any time. They laugh and Tokuda sees her smile. What she privately makes of the adults’ laughter remains outside his view. A little later the manga artist Itō recognizes Kana’s school uniform. She wonders aloud what brings a middle-school girl all the way from Saitama. Kana’s hand stops. By the time the others are talking again, the ice cream in her cup has gone on melting. The girl who can charm a table of adults has good reason to be afraid of an ordinary question from someone whose company she likes. When she comes back to the same café late one night, in much greater distress, the first kindness Chisato does her is to let her sit down without asking for an explanation. [17.36–91, 18.82–99, 18.826–849]

## The name she gives

Kana introduces herself as カナ at the café. Her family name is 堅 and her given name is 香子. She dislikes both, the first gift she never wanted from her parents. Her teacher calls her by the family name. Her classmate Ruri Mizokakushi uses the given name with an intimacy that the girl has every reason to dread. Chisato greets her as カナちゃん, the name she actually offered. When the narration first follows her from the café into the night, the girl’s lowered head and falling fringe seem to disclose her usual self. A little further on, trying to put Mizokakushi off, Kana thinks of how easy it is to play somebody else at the café. Then she corrects herself. The person she is at the café is real. The person who cannot get the words out here is the one she feels compelled to play. Asaura lets both accounts stand in sequence. Her fear is quite real. So are the social ease, pleasure and wit that cost her so much to reach. [18.81–112, 18.147–169, 18.215–223, 18.829–839]

Three years earlier her mother left. The woman who had been involved with her father before the separation came to live in their Saitama apartment that same year and now uses the family name. Kana does not know what the family register says. She misses her mother, imagines that if her mother were there they might have gone to the police together, and fears that leaving her behind meant her mother did not miss her back. These are painful alternatives in the girl’s mind. Her mother never appears to tell us what she knew, felt, or can do now. Kana chose a middle school away from her immediate neighborhood, where she had felt the scrutiny that followed her mother’s departure. The journey she makes to avoid being known was once a choice. It did not free her from being known on other people’s terms. [18.115–146]

Her account of the woman at home is unusually discriminating. Kana can say that the woman may not be a bad person. She largely leaves Kana alone, and on weekdays sets out a thousand yen for lunch and pocket money. Kana understands the adult’s ability to keep the peace and hates sharing her home with her all the same. When a neighbor asks why the child comes back so late, the woman says she has lessons in Tokyo. What Kana hears outside her bedroom is a request to keep that account straight. The family pays little attention to the substance of her days. Her father appears in her imagination of another unbearable evening watching late television with a beer. That image matters as the future *she* expects. It gives little purchase on the whole of his life. The book supplies her experience of abandonment, withdrawal and intrusion. It leaves the parents’ histories largely inaccessible. [18.115–137, 18.439–447, 18.770–779]

## The cost of an ordinary afternoon

Tokyo offers a particular freedom. Kana likes being where strangers have no reason to take an interest in her. From that anonymity she finds a place where it matters very much that somebody recognize her. She saves out of the daily allowance, skips lunches, uses stationery other people give her or finds inexpensive replacements and sometimes walks considerable distances to keep the fare. With the adults at LycoReco she can listen to a conversation, acknowledge something she doesn’t know, ask a question or join in a joke. With the manga artist and other customers she takes part in a scene staged as visual reference. Later she asks Chisato and Takina whether they really work at night. Her question earns a playful answer and no disclosure of the work’s actual nature. She doesn’t need to understand what the staff do for a living to contribute something worth having at their table. [10.79, 15.21–37, 15.83–95, 15.106–110, 18.34–55, 18.225–252, 18.383–387]

At the counter it is possible to distinguish a treat from a debt. When the other customers learn she has never tried espresso, they offer to buy it. Kurumi brings the cups. Mika supplies vanilla ice cream because Kana doesn’t much like bitter tastes. Kana asks how to eat it. Pouring something hot on something so delicately cold feels wrong, and she waits for Mika’s reassurance before trying. Her face goes from a grimace to pleasure. When writer Yoneoka says how good it is to watch a young person enjoy a treat, she comes back with her invitation to buy her more, then tells Itō she looks forward to it. Enjoyment, curiosity and a small piece of theatrical timing all belong to Kana. Itō later notices that the round trip and a drink with a sweet could cost a schoolgirl nearly two thousand yen. She has found a real discrepancy. Her guesses about what girls with money should spend on their hair or clothes do not uncover the actual sacrifices. [17.36–72, 18.34–63, 18.225–252, 18.383–387]

The people at the café each allow a different relationship. Kana imagines Mika as the father she would like. His attention feels continuous without putting her on trial. Mizuki has the casual roughness of a sister or older cousin with whom conversation need never become solemn. Kana even thinks Mizuki quietly does much of the work that holds the place together. Kurumi first makes room for her by inviting her to join a game. Kana likes the distinction between Kurumi’s command of calculation and her fallibility in games that require reading other people. She admires Takina’s straight black hair, competence and steadiness, compares her to a finely honed Japanese sword, and finds no malice behind her eyes. Takina’s occasional smile makes Kana’s heart stir. The prose registers her awareness that they are both girls. That is a particular experience of attraction or admiration which the reader can take seriously without assigning Kana an orientation or Takina a reciprocal feeling. None of Kana’s comparisons confers kinship, privileged access to the staff’s private thoughts or the right to expect them to play a part on demand. [18.228–239]

Chisato offers her something else. Other people’s self-sacrifice can make receiving a favor feel like incurring a debt. Kana believes that Chisato takes such pleasure in sharing her own good time that it is possible simply to thank her. Chisato asks her to come again and, the following week, actually remembers her and sits down beside her for a rambling talk. Kana admits her side of the conversation contains quite a few lies. Her happiness in it needs no correction. She imagines how different school would feel if this girl were in her class. Friendship is named, affection is intense, and the wish is for ordinary shared days. Her love of Chisato’s voice and her later desire to be held have texture of their own. Collapsing these feelings into the flutter she describes at Takina’s smile would obscure how carefully Kana differentiates two people she cares about. [18.235–251, 18.395–408, 18.848–875]

## An invitation she cannot refuse

At school, “friend” is a word Mizokakushi uses while exercising power. Kana has endured ridicule and jostling. When she tried to stop her classmate from photographing her changing, two others restrained her, and humiliating images were made while other pupils failed to intervene. On the train Mizokakushi actually shows her one and threatens to circulate it. Kana starts with やめて, “stop,” and has to add ください when Mizokakushi pretends not to hear. She is adept at thinking through another person’s interests. Mizokakushi has more leverage while she controls the pictures. But an advantage a tormentor *might* wish to retain offers Kana no security. She nods to an invitation to go out after school with someone she has tried to refuse. Later, at the gates, teacher Ishihara holds up these same pupils’ volunteering as a model for Kana and attributes her near lateness to playing games. She had ridden past her stop in distress after the encounter. His confidence in the social world he thinks he sees helps explain why asking him for assistance looks so unlikely to work. [18.138–146, 18.147–191, 18.199–223]

At the supposed tea gathering, Mizokakushi introduces her boyfriend Kadowaki. Kana has held some of her savings away from her wallet because she is afraid she will be made to pay for someone else. She asks where they have brought her and what they are to do. She keeps her bag close. Before she knows what is wrong with the tea, before she notices that the others’ cups seem oddly full or empty, she merely pretends to drink. She cannot bear to put something supplied by these people into her body. She would prefer coffee. Her distrust is a sound exercise of judgment under conditions she has not chosen. The subsequent discoveries vindicate it without turning her into someone who knew the trap from the outset. Nor is the confused sensation she reports when Kadowaki touches her hair and praises her appearance a wish for his attention. She has already been frightened by his manner and has been given no meaningful choice about the touch. [18.255–271, 18.279–305, 18.309–331]

Kadowaki demands ten thousand yen when she tries to leave. Kana’s protest is precise. She hasn’t drunk the tea. More fundamentally, she never ordered it. When he threatens to contact the school and Mizokakushi takes out her phone, Kana pays with most of the money she has laboriously saved. She even asks whether Kadowaki intends to admit that the price he named was a lie if he will not let her go now. She sees girls being exploited as she leaves. A strange man recognizes her by name at the lift and reaches for her. She recoils and escapes. She concludes that Kadowaki and the stranger have seen her images. Their conduct gives her serious grounds for fear. Her reconstruction of who has already seen which image is still hers. In an alley afterward she distinguishes why she vomits from why she cries. What she saw and smelled made her sick. What she lost was the money that let her be Kana. When Chisato comes upon her there, the girl who so badly wants to hear that voice hides her face and runs. She feels that to be seen like this would destroy the self whose company Chisato enjoys. [18.329–365, 18.366–408]

## A girl capable of anger

Kana’s fury has objects and a history. It predates her teacher’s assault. The classmate who holds the photographs, the two who helped her and the two adults at home appear in her private reckoning. She has contemplated doing them grave harm. Sometimes the conviction that she could act is the one thing that lets her continue through a train journey. She can also mistake a look between strangers for derision. On one journey she is quite certain, in the absence of proof, that girls are laughing at her. On another, after the assault, the mirror supplies an obvious reason passengers might have stared: there is blood on her face. Her misreadings deserve a place beside the accurate calculations she makes about her tormentors. It takes no illusion to recognize the danger she is actually in. [18.99–112, 18.129–134, 18.186–201, 18.668–687]

We also learn how two people can inhabit the same carriage and be almost entirely wrong about one another. In the second story a fugitive sees a timid-looking schoolgirl get off. He is relieved to see her go and misjudges the two new passengers who are there to detain him. In Kana’s recollection the encounter was about two weeks earlier. She has heard Chisato’s voice, wants only a greeting or a look, waves through the window, even takes another train to try to catch up. She wonders whether the man sitting between the girls is one of their boyfriends and tells herself she would only have intruded. The reader knows what they were doing. Kana knows what it cost her not to be seen. Returning to the empty seat she finds the gun the man left behind and privately treats it as a means of regaining power over her own future. Nothing in either account makes the encounter an intentional gift from the café. [12.289–319, 18.610–664]

Ishihara’s earlier conduct is something Kana reconstructs from a rumor about trouble at another school. What he does to *her* is directly experienced. When he first offers a ride home, Kana is genuinely pleased at the thought of saving the fare. When he asks whether she has been selling sex, she denies it and protests that her family has nothing to do with it. He says a student showed him an online image. He offers to help. Kana almost begins to tell him what actually happened. Instead he restrains and sexually assaults her, speaks of protecting her, and hits her when she resists. His insistence that she leave everything to him accompanies her active physical resistance as she tries to make him stop. Kana wonders whether the old incident was of this kind and whether Mizokakushi anticipated the assault. The earlier school’s reasons for moving him, the identities of other possible victims and the full scope of her classmate’s foresight are unavailable. Mizokakushi later acknowledges having altered the image and threatens to add Kana’s name and contact details. That is a fresh, direct act of coercion. [18.141–146, 18.447–486, 18.487–530, 18.583–600]

An alarm and a woman in a white coat interrupt the confrontation. Kana runs past the adults, cries out of sight, then finds Mizokakushi beside her again. Her first explanation for having failed to shoot Ishihara involves the people she had already meant to harm. Later she admits how confused she had been. Faced with her tormentors, she can no longer maintain the simpler explanation. She sees them buying ordinary things, enjoying exactly the sort of effortless companionship she would like to know. She tries to sustain her anger, even calling up hypothetical future victims to justify acting. Her hand shakes and they walk away alive. She calls herself a coward, imagines school and home continuing indefinitely, and attempts to end her life. The later inability to shoot these particular people is significant beside her active physical resistance to Ishihara and prior deliberate test of the weapon, whose effects she had taken seriously. Kana experiences the failure to act as one more ground for hatred of herself. Her explanations shift with the danger, the memory and what she sees of the other girls. The passage cannot establish a general refusal to do harm, settle why she could not shoot the girls or assign survival to her actions alone. [18.516–520, 18.550–608, 18.647–664, 18.695–705, 18.720–780, 18.787–803]

## An order she can make

Before the attempt, what Kana remembers most urgently is the smell of coffee. She wants to go back. She believes the place that had room for anybody can have no room for the person she has become. The shot is heard. After a break in the narration Kana feels grit and pain, discovers she is alive and finds red powder that smells of rubber. She remembers a heavy impact and can no longer find the gun. Her phone, used first to look at her own face, has a handwritten voucher stuck to the back. Nothing in her witnessed account identifies who attached it, exactly when, or the precise mechanics of her survival. She can, however, read its offer. A free coffee *set* is something she can afford right now, no matter what Kadowaki took. She closes her hand on it, gets up, turns away from the station and walks to the café. [18.787–837]

The sign still says OPEN at an hour at which Kana would normally expect it closed. Kana opens the door. Chisato draws out a chair and accepts the crumpled voucher without asking why this child is out so late or what has happened to her clothes. She makes the coffee, comes around the counter and serves it at Kana’s side. Kana usually avoids black coffee. Tonight she tries it and says it is good. Only then does Chisato remind her that the set entitles her to one more order, anything she likes. Kana checks: *anything?* The pause and the question are hers. The scene gains its force from the last place food was put in front of her. Kadowaki imposed a cost for a drink she had never ordered. Here she has already received something she wants, owes nothing, and is allowed to supply the missing terms herself. [18.294–298, 18.333–362, 18.824–858]

Kana’s answer is 助けて, “Help me.” Chisato accepts the order and embraces her. Kana’s first question is how Chisato can speak as though she knows how bad things have been. She believes Chisato could understand all of it and simultaneously thinks Chisato could know nothing of what happened. Able at last to hear someone say how hard she has tried, Kana brings her own arms around Chisato. She says yes. When Chisato tells her she can leave the rest to her, Kana answers yes again, more emphatically. Ishihara used much the same promise over a girl who had been trying to pull away. The difference lies in whose request the other person answers and what Kana can do with her own voice and hands. Even this unequivocal appeal does not tell us whether she would have agreed to all the steps taken before that night. There is no later scene in which she judges them. [18.485–512, 18.850–875]

## What Kana does not hear

A late-night conversation belongs to Mika, Mizuki and Kurumi. Mizuki reports the earlier surveillance. At school Takina had wanted to kill Ishihara and then offered to claim responsibility if Kana shot him. Both are proposals. Mizuki says she set off the alarm, intervened in a white coat and physically punished the teacher. Kana witnessed the alarm, an interruption and the opportunity she took to escape. She did not stand in the room for this accounting of how it was done. Mizuki twice insists they should have helped sooner. They could at least have shortened the time Kana suffered, she argues. Mika argues that the formal request and a link to the earlier case brought the costs and responsibility within reach of Direct Attack, the organization involved in that case. He also speaks of Chisato’s need to set limits on whom she can help. Kurumi doubts that simply reporting the situation would have left Kana any better off. These are the adults’ and Kurumi’s claims about what it would take to help. They expose the choices and disagreements in the protection that was already underway before Kana could name her order. [18.567–605, 18.695–706, 18.877–905]

Kurumi reports that a DA operation disrupted one route supplying drugs to children and that she obtained the original image data from Mizokakushi’s phone. She says deletion work has begun and expects the images to disappear from the internet by the following day. The narrative supplies no completed check, no proof that every copy is gone and no finding about what happens to each of the adults or girls concerned. No one offers Kana a job as a Lycoris. Mizuki raises the prospect as a joke and is told Kana is too old. The speaker of that answer is unmarked. Nor does the last anonymous arrival in the novel identify a future Kana. The evening on which she returns and asks is sufficient to establish a change. The girl who heard Chisato ask about her after she had been sick and then fled has let Chisato hold her in clothes she thinks unfit for this beautiful place. We know how glad she is. We do not know what she is going to find at home, at school, with the police, or on her next trip to Tokyo. [18.395–409, 18.839–875, 18.883–910, 19.69–78]

Kana is easiest to misread when she is doing something at which she is good. An apparently sociable pupil can be counting the cost of every journey. A practiced listener can fall silent because she hears the risk in a perfectly innocent question. Her Japanese shifts with those conditions. She jokes in courteous language with the adults paying for espresso, falters when the topic turns to lessons, tries to refuse Mizokakushi without giving offense and challenges Kadowaki in a full, indignant sentence about her right to order. Her inner speech is often angrier than anything she says aloud. In the café at the end she can dispense with the polite circumlocution and say what she needs. The printed exchanges give vocabulary, pauses and explicit descriptions of shaking, without a performed voice. Given an invitation that can be taken at her own pace, established company and freedom from an unwanted debt, there is good reason to expect curiosity, humor and a return visit. Under threat, neither a nod nor silence signifies willingness. And when someone makes room for her to ask, answering yes is one thing she can do for herself. Her responses across these days leave her future recovery open. [17.56–91, 18.163–190, 18.329–362, 18.512–520, 18.837–875]
