# Kōsuke Mizukawa. What the dog was taught

People in Mizukawa’s organization call him a dog. He begins by wondering why. It might be how he got there, having been picked up by a man who found him during a violent encounter and decided to look after him. It might be the way he accepts what he cannot explain. At twenty-two he wears cheap glasses of the kind a student might buy. He is tall, slightly stooped and lacks a face that could frighten anybody on sight. Asaura gives this observation to the man his colleagues describe by what he will do. The narrator describes him as a man who leaves what he cannot understand and does the job in front of him. He spends the rest of the chapter trying to discover who can say what that job is. [A2 *Recovery days*, 『Dog』, 11.2–15.]

There is a real cost to the organization’s habit of giving him immediate tasks. At its gambling room his appearance initially makes him an implausible guard and he does mostly odd jobs. He inflicts a terrible punishment on a cheating customer and changes how people see him. No order to punish that particular person is given in the story. According to the narrator the staff as well as the customers become afraid to cheat, and the place gains a reputation for fairness. Mizukawa hardly follows what that means for profits or reputation. He regards himself as somebody who does the work. The story keeps the conspicuous outcome and the cost to another person in view. An orderly room can owe its order to conduct the story does not require the reader to admire. [11.46–52.]

The person whose work he wants is Okada. Six years before the current events Okada helped him in the aftermath of a fatal encounter, arranged to conceal him for a time, and took him into the organization. Mizukawa remembers trying to repay him and then staying because Okada was good to him. The boss has his own memory of Okada saying he had found a strange stray that would make a good watchdog. When the boss tells Mizukawa that he became Okada’s dog rather than the organization's, Mizukawa denies that he could have become anything as worthy as a watchdog. He will admit that Okada doted on him. The relationship is a refuge, a job and a place in which someone recognizes him. There is nothing equivalent in his account of the organization alone. [11.31–32, 11.53–66.]

## The first request

The night of the revenge attempt the organization tries to stop him. Mizukawa overpowers a succession of its members, recognizes that they mean to protect both him and the organization, and goes on. He addresses the old boss politely and apologizes for the trouble he will make. He asks to be formally severed from the group if he is going to cost it that much. The boss says the move will make little difference. When Mizukawa voices contempt for returning to work as though Okada’s death did not matter, he is speaking to somebody who remembers what a useful and disruptive man Okada was. The boss considers the death partly accidental and says a settlement is under way. Mizukawa knows the underboss is working on it. Mizukawa can follow the account of a sudden collision in a quarrel. He still reasons that Ijūin’s willingness to introduce a weapon could amount to lethal intent, and considers the loss itself enough. He takes a possible motive and a genuine outcome as reasons to act even though neither settles what the other man meant. [11.12–45 and 11.67–73.]

Mizukawa does hear an instruction from Okada in his memory. It is to go and eat ramen together after Okada gets back. The boss immediately corrects him when he supplies this as the last words. Those are the last he himself heard. The boss says Okada called his name while dying. Mizukawa imagines threats or a direction to avenge him, and privately resolves to do it with or without a command. The unspectacular meal has the clearest claim on his personal memory. The killing acquires its supposed sanction from a report he thinks he already knows how to finish. The scene gives the reader enough access to see an emotion doing interpretive work without treating his gratitude as false. [11.74–83.]

When the boss moves against him, Mizukawa wonders why he was spared more serious harm. He thanks the old man and immediately considers several explanations: kindness, softness, fear, or a wish to avoid trouble with the police. He leans toward fear or self-preservation. His suspicion is available to the reader because it is his own. Mizukawa also considers killing the ailing boss if he has to. In the event the older man lets him take what he came for. Mizukawa bows as he leaves, remembering that Okada taught him to acknowledge senior people. He will ignore a senior’s order and still observe the form of respect he learned. The two facts sit badly together only if obedience has been confused with affection or gratitude. [11.84–143.]

## Whom he can thank on the way

A useful distinction is visible in his reaction to the man who gives him information. The night looks too quiet. Mizukawa calls back and asks whether he has been sold out. His contact pauses, states that Ijūin is there, insists that part is true and tells him to be careful. Mizukawa can infer that something is wrong without treating the man as his enemy. He thinks the silence amounts to a warning under pressure and is grateful. Later Takina brings the bound man into view and takes him away. The restraint corroborates part of the inference. It does not show that Mizukawa understood the plan. This is a good case of his finding enough in someone’s words to recognize help without getting the situation right. [11.183–197 and 11.247–254.]

He notices a danger to people whose identity does not matter to him and is relieved that the crowded place he imagined does not materialize. The narrator says he does not want a massacre of the uninvolved. The immediately preceding sentence makes a less flattering commitment available. He had contemplated killing people who looked sufficiently like his target if searching for one man put him at a disadvantage. When he sees Ijūin, a woman is beside him. Mizukawa attempts to kill him anyway. He can prefer less harm and readily accept the possibility of harming someone uninvolved. Loyalty gives him a single aim without making him careful enough about what can be sacrificed to it. The space that appears to give him a clear task has been made by people with a different assignment. [11.204–223.]

Some of Okada’s quieter lessons remain accessible in that room. When the two young opponents name one another, Mizukawa repeats their names to himself, as Okada had taught him. This is a little act of respect carried into a situation in which he apprehends them as obstacles. He thinks they are probably guarding Ijūin and asks whether they are killers hired by the coffee-loving Kinshichō boss, a separate man from his own old boss. Chisato says that this café regular referred the work to her. She calls the work protection and leaves whom she guards unstated. Mizukawa guesses that Ijūin paid the intermediary. He takes the missing answer for granted. She calls him a faithful dog. He recognizes only afterward that members of his own group usually call him that. Once again he is good at identifying a piece of participation and misses the person for whose sake the others are in the room. [11.165–177, 11.241–281 and 11.383–391.]

## The second answer

Chisato incapacitates Mizukawa and leaves him badly hurt in the course of protecting him. Ijūin then tries to kill him. He calls Okada’s death an accident and treats Mizukawa with contempt. Mizukawa privately considers being killed in the same way as Okada an appealing prospect while still refusing the thought of Ijūin getting away. Chisato stops Ijūin and finally says whose job the two girls took. Mizukawa’s own boss hired them to protect him. He has intervened twice in this man's night, once to stop him himself and once by creating a reason for others to be there. Mizukawa objects that the explanation is all self-preservation. Chisato gives him a fuller fragment, `水川を…`. He repeats the object marker with surprise. His name might designate the person on whom somebody else was supposed to act. Only then does she relay the boss’s reading, “Stop Mizukawa.” She freely admits that nobody can know what Okada intended. The assurance she insists on is different. She believes Okada wanted the person in his care to be happy and would never have asked him to die on his account. Takina considers his safety sufficient for their work, then waits as Chisato says the extra words. [11.348–406.]

What he does after that assurance matters more than a scene in which he immediately agreed with it would have done. The two girls leave. Mizukawa picks up the blade. He has time to ask if he really is a dog, and if so what he has learned from the one who took him in. Okada taught him how to live as a person. He had never taught him how to kill. It is Mizukawa's own inventory of remembered instruction, not the completion Chisato supplied. The question “What should I do?” is still his when he hears the boss. The older man verifies that he is alive, hurts the fallen Ijūin, suggests putting the blame on the girl who was there, and says it is time to come home. He gives no name for that girl. Mizukawa waits a long time before saying yes. The answer gives him a different immediate action. It supplies neither a renunciation of violence nor a future outside the organization which brought him there. Nobody has recovered Okada’s intended last sentence. [11.407–425.]

## What might make him wait

When Okada was alive, Mizukawa tried to do what would please him. The evidence for care through concrete acts is strongest with that one person. Remembering people's names and the invitation to a meal that never happened stay particularly familiar to him. If Mizukawa heard a new, incomplete report about someone whose life mattered to him, he could again turn it into an immediate job of his own making. A question that put the exact words alongside an ordinary remembered lesson might make him wait. In this chapter he does so only after others have made an intervention and left him a little time to himself. There is no comparable test in which he freely questions a living Okada, unequivocally rejects the boss or takes responsibility for what the boss did to Ijūin. The next meaningful evidence would be something Mizukawa does after saying yes, when someone consults him again at the home he has agreed to return to.
