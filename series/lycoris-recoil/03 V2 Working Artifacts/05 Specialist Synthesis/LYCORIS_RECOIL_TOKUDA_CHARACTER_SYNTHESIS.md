# Kazuhiko Tokuda and the story he gets to keep

The photograph a professional calls his best of the day is taken after Kazuhiko Tokuda tells the models the shoot is over. He hears the shutter behind him. The photographer has found a moment Tokuda was still trying to understand. Chisato has offered Takina a bite of pancake; Takina, who had begun to decline, has leaned toward the fork. Tokuda asks if the photographer got it, looks at the result and expects the editors to use it. One thing troubles him. Who is going to read his article with a picture like that on the page? When he brings the printed issue to Café LycoReco, the regulars exclaim at every turn of the page. In that room, at least, his prediction comes true. He takes real pleasure in giving them something to look at and feels the slight to his own work just as keenly. Asaura allows him both reactions. [*Ordinary days* 22.2–6, 22.33–56; 19.28–49, 19.69.]

Tokuda is twenty-eight, divorced, has no children and earns a living as a freelance magazine writer. Chisato calls him Toku-san. He takes whatever commissions come his way, then suffers the fees and deadlines he agreed to. He has no signature work that makes the answer to “what do you do?” easy. The novelty of his Kinshichō pitch is that he takes a proposal to an editorial department himself. He found a remarkable café by chance. He wants other people to know about it, and wants to be the person entitled to say he found it. There is no particular financial prize beyond the fee. The narrator gives that little hunger for recognition its due before the reader gets a chance to admire his eye. [9.2–23, 9.51–56, 9.84, 9.113–125.]

## A regular with a proposal

Tokuda has a feature writer’s knack for putting a reader somewhere. His Kinshichō has a nightlife district on one side and, on the other, a redeveloped neighborhood, the damaged old tower and children in a park whose happy cries sound like a zoo. He sees the stained-glass windows and tended flowers of a wooden building and imagines a passerby stopping, noticing the café sign and reaching for the door. Then the prose catches up with his own hand. The supposedly typical customer is a man who has come with an agenda and is a little nervous about it. His favorite seat is in the center of the counter. Only since about the previous week has Mika been able to greet him by asking if he would like his usual light-roast American coffee. Tokuda’s pleasure in hearing いつもの predates his success at selling any story. [9.12–37, 9.148–149.]

When Kurumi offers him the spare place at a game, he turns it down to make his pitch. The staff’s reaction to the word “work” strikes him as peculiar. Even Kurumi stops setting up the game. The tension dissolves as soon as he explains that he wants to feature the café in a magazine. For an instant he wonders whether they customarily take an entirely different sort of job. When Mizuki later hints that the shop has another reason to refuse publicity, he privately dismisses the notion of a secret as something out of a manga. He really has noticed a break in the room’s behavior. He neither knows what caused it nor believes his own nearest guess for long. Dramatic irony here depends on a reasonably observant visitor. The reader who knows about the café can appreciate how near he came before he talked himself out of it. [9.62–79, 9.95–112, 9.137–149.]

Chisato’s enthusiasm nearly gives Tokuda the wrong answer to his question. He had imagined a sophisticated piece built around Mika. Chisato’s enthusiasm for the coffee, the sweets and the attractive staff persuades him there is another good version. She is practically booking a hair appointment when Mika declines. Tokuda asks about the shop's social-media presence; Mika explains its value to the regulars and Chisato’s wish to use it. Tokuda can distinguish that choice from permission to feature the café. Mika makes him two freshly toasted monaka and asks if he will still come back. Tokuda says he wanted to share the place precisely because he liked it. Of course he will come back. Mika's smile makes the adult petitioner feel like a boy who has been told he is all right. Even while he eats, he regrets losing the chance to write the place up. The pleasure is in the shell’s heat, the filling’s coolness, and the faintly ridiculous cigar shape. He speculates, without identifying the inventor, that a woman must have thought of a design so considerate of the person eating it. Tokuda can describe how a thing works more reliably than he can say whose idea it was. [9.120–158, 9.161–189.]

## How much a good question buys

His interest cools after Mika refuses just as the editors’ interest takes off. They give him the lead writing assignment for a ten-page café feature in a broader issue on places women can enjoy in Kinshichō and Kameido. Now he has visited enough other establishments to question Mika's proposed explanation for the local abundance of Japanese-style cafés. When he asks about LycoReco itself, Mika reminds him of the refusal. Tokuda says the question is personal, although he would be happy to turn it into an interview if Mika agreed. Mika talks about his taste for Japanese sweets and his preference for coffee, and mentions an old workplace. Tokuda asks which one. Mika looks away and smiles. This time the reporter knows that the conversation has reached its limit. When Mika changes the subject to a nearby place with excellent pancakes, Tokuda immediately notes the recommendation on his phone. He recognizes the change of subject and knows the source is worth listening to. [13.2–23, 13.45–49.]

He likes to think adults know where to stop. He has already been wrong about what it means to be modern. Divorced himself, Tokuda thinks of Mizuki's bridal magazine as a cursed book. He finds her attractive and imagines he might have fallen for her before his own unhappy experience. When he previously asked about her wish to marry, she replied that these ought to be times when an old-fashioned woman could choose that too. Tokuda felt ashamed of how readily he had treated his own idea of emancipation as hers. Later Mizuki's interest in his job lasts exactly long enough to learn he is a freelancer rather than a salaried employee at a major publisher. He can be candid about the sting of being sized up himself. There is no courtship between them and no account of why his marriage ended. He also finds something sensuous in Mika’s skillful hands. Mizuki’s joke about a confession to Mika is hers; the narrator records Tokuda’s embarrassment and his objections to the proposed setting. [9.34–56, 9.73–93, 9.113–119.]

What he supplies as an observer varies with the company. While the artist Itō stages a group scene for her manga, Tokuda imagines he is better equipped for his part than a novice and reads experience into Doi’s composure. Then Itō calls the shoot over. The suspense narrative has been a group performance. Chisato suggests correcting Tokuda’s pose and Takina explains the telltale error, an old habit from playing with friends as a teenager. He remembers confidently turning down coaching and now wishes he had taken it. Asking Chisato where she learned so much helps him cover the embarrassment. He builds an explanation out of her genuine enthusiasm for films and her habit of lending discs to Takina. In the same pictures he sees something incongruously practiced in Mika’s posture. Starting from Mika's appearance, he speculates about a childhood overseas. He remains capable of doubt about that account. Neither the imagined childhood nor the explanation for the girls’ skill becomes a fact about them. Itō, not Tokuda, made those reference photographs. [15.2–15, 15.30–56, 15.63–76, 15.96–105.]

The movie conversation gives him a more exact account of himself. As a student he used to search dwindling video-rental stores for obscure films, eager to say that he had discovered them. He thinks Chisato wants the people she lends things to to enjoy the same moment with her. That last judgment is Tokuda interpreting a friend. What he can say with greater authority is how little he has outgrown his own wish to be first. He recognizes the college film hunter in the working writer who proposed the café feature. At a less reflective moment he can also join the fun without first making himself its discoverer. When Chisato serves him hot takoyaki, he assumes the sauce and the water Takina has brought will see him through. He burns his tongue. Hearing the others laugh bothers him until he finds himself laughing too. He postpones the pancake research. For once the cost of liking the place makes itself felt quite literally on the tongue he relies on to write about food. [15.63–79; 13.37–72.]

## Who sees Kana’s spoon

Tokuda tells himself he needs a café where other people will tolerate his laptop and where he can safely leave it while he goes to the bathroom. He cannot very well write the feature in front of the proprietors it covers. Local regulars might tell him something he missed. He has an excellent answer to every question except Kurumi’s. If he needs only an hour of serious work, why has he already spent three here? His deadline was last night. Beneath the practical explanations he knows he wanted to work somewhere he liked. The sugar he tips into the carefully prepared espresso makes quick fuel of a drink whose subtleties he could otherwise describe. He feels a little guilty toward Mika and drinks it that way anyway. [17.2–35.]

Itō keeps drawing as she identifies the uniform of Kana, a middle-school regular, and asks why she makes such a long trip to come here. Tokuda sees the recoil before the artist looks up from her tablet. Moments before, all three writers had enjoyed watching Kana try an affogato, and Tokuda had agreed that seeing a young person visibly enjoy her food gave him pleasure. He knows an affogato is best at the instant when ice cream begins yielding to hot espresso. Chisato and Takina return and release the room from the awkwardness. Tokuda feels relieved too. Then he looks back. Kana's hand holding the spoon has stopped altogether, and the ice cream goes on melting. The earlier account of how to taste the dessert has given the reader a clock by which to measure how long a child has stopped enjoying it. This time Tokuda can see something after the collective mood has moved on. [17.36–68, 17.73–91.]

Seeing does not make him Kana’s confidant. Later, when Itō worries about the cost of her journeys and orders, Tokuda says Kana seems an ordinary girl and suggests she may simply have a generous allowance. Itō is trying to explain a real economic discrepancy; earlier she was the one who missed the first change in Kana’s face. Both observations can be good without either regular knowing the girl's circumstances. Mika then steers them away from rummaging through somebody else's privacy. The prose follows Kana away from the café and gives the reader access Tokuda lacks. No conversation makes him party to her eventual request for help, and there is no story he writes about her. His willingness to offer a benign explanation is consequentially different from understanding what she has endured. [18.32–64, 18.81–90, 18.829–875.]

## The photograph and its readers

Chisato asks to appear in the feature even though her café has refused coverage. Tokuda agrees to the shoot elsewhere, with no guarantee that the editors will use nonprofessional models. At one location he thanks the proprietor and tells him they have finished. The photographer, a professional Tokuda already knows, observes that the two girls look unusually composed through the lens. He speculates about physical training. Tokuda has long thought that the pair make a striking picture even when slouching. Watching the offered forkful, he thinks he now understands why he finds them attractive to look at. Simply being near these two can make another person happy. The shift from analyzing bodies to describing the spectator’s experience is a generous one. The spectator is still Tokuda. The girls’ willingness to eat together does not tell him everything they feel about each other. [22.2–32, 22.36–52.]

The people who make the picture possible have different decisions to make. Tokuda declares the assignment over and later permits Chisato to eat the prop. Takina accepts the offered bite. The photographer uses Tokuda’s position as partial cover and takes the additional picture on his own initiative. The writer asks about it after hearing the shutter and predicts the editors will want it. The finished issue confirms that the feeding image was printed. It does not show the participants being asked about that separate exposure. Chisato’s earlier enthusiasm for appearing in print, the editorial department's approval, and the delight with which the spread is received cannot answer the unasked question on Takina’s behalf. Equally, there is no scene of either girl lodging a complaint. A voice in the room recognizes that the picture was taken after the announced end; the rapid succession of unlabelled voices need not be distributed among named speakers. [22.2, 22.21–56; 18.17–22; 19.28–48, 19.69.]

Tokuda brings the newly printed magazine to a café in which something has plainly gone wrong. Doi is laughing strangely, Chisato is prostrate, and Takina looks irritated. He asks what happened, gets no useful explanation and hopes his news will cheer Chisato up. He says the issue is really for people involved, then lets the other regulars look. They make a marvelous, quite devastating first audience. Chisato will treasure it. Everyone around him wants to talk about how the girls look, who knows which seats and where they ought to go next. Nobody reads his copy there. Some of them now want to visit the cafés that the feature recommends. That tension between professional success and personal disappointment belongs to the man watching his friends. The magazine’s other readers may value the copy differently. [19.7–49, 19.69–70.]

Mika still objects to conspicuousness. Tokuda points out that the girls might have had a much less prominent part if the proprietor had let him feature the shop. But the wish to be the one who introduces LycoReco has dwindled. It has not disappeared, and the narrator catches him correcting himself on that very point. He once wanted to be special to the café. Now it matters more that the café is special to him. A queue out the door might destroy precisely the “world” he likes. At 19.62 the original prints 世界, *world*, with the ruby カフエ, *café*. He tells Mika what this place means to him while the others are laughing together. There is also pleasure in keeping a discovery for himself. Yet the closing bell neither expels the newcomer nor identifies who has come in. This might be a regular, or somebody about to become one. Tokuda has not taken ownership of the door. [19.49–78.]

## A question he could face again

Suppose a customer recognizes Tokuda as the writer and offers a revealing anecdote about another regular who has left the room. His most strongly supported first impulse would be curiosity. He might ask one careful question, welcome a usable lead to a place that does want publicity, or remember the incident without attributing it in print. Mika’s smile has stopped him before. An editor’s interest by itself has never unlocked Mika’s past. The harder variation would be an excellent photograph for a feature that already has approval, but whose subject understood that the camera had stopped. Tokuda notices the shutter after the shoot and goes directly to judging the picture's chances. The scene supplies no question from him about permission for that exposure. What he would do after a direct objection from the person photographed, under pressure from an editor, has never been tested. A new story would need to let him hear the objection before giving him credit for an answer. [9.126–147; 13.13–22, 13.45–49; 22.46–56; 19.44–65.]

### Source bounds

This is a portrait of Tokuda in Asaura’s Japanese *Ordinary days*. References are one-based OPF spine and nonempty paragraph/heading numbers. The novel prints the bonus scene after the signed afterword. The shoot happens before Tokuda brings in the magazine.
