# Madame. When a contract stops

The woman asks Takina whether she really is as young as she looks. Takina says yes and tries to ask the question back. The answer she gets is a name people call the woman, Madame. She says she will remember it and gives her own surname. The two are armed, watching one another, and already dividing the small amount each is prepared to say from what the other can infer. Takina thinks Madame heard the question differently than she intended. A mistake can be productive. She has the name by which the woman works, while Madame's exact age and ordinary identity remain unstated. The novel gives the scene largely as Takina encounters it. We hear Madame speaking with unusual frankness about this work without having her private narration. [A2 *Gluttony days*, 10.237–251.]

Madame says she wants to deal with the two guards separately. She guesses that Takina will accept because her eyes are those of someone doing work more like Madame’s than an ordinary escort’s. She describes the other girl with less certainty. Chisato seemed like a child, but her movements were too composed and she may be the more capable of the two. Takina says she is probably right. There is a real act of recognition in the exchange. A woman paid to deal with two opponents gives one a serious answer about both, and the girl can use it without agreeing to the older woman's evaluative category. Madame volunteered enough to make Takina reexamine how she thinks about presenting herself to a watcher. Competence supplies something each can recognize in the other. It does not change the terms under which they met. [10.250–269.]

## A limit put into effect

Madame tells Takina to put the weapon away. Immediately afterward a child’s voice can be heard, and Takina sees the boy and his mother approaching. Madame makes a gesture that Takina reads as an intent to pause. She proposes moving because neither should want to involve bystanders. Takina contests the choice of location. She thinks Madame’s suggestion could favor her. Madame accepts Takina's right to choose. What follows is a stretch of ordinary-looking walking the two have to sustain together without trusting one another. Positions matter because both are ready to interpret a change. The novel’s temporary armistice can show an actual act of restraint by Madame. The antagonists agree on something worth preventing, and the more experienced one has given up control of a detail to make the restriction workable. It supplies no assurance that either will show the same restraint once they are alone. [10.270–282.]

At the same time she tells Takina that she regrets taking work that may require killing somebody so young. She admits some reluctance and says it would be stronger for somebody who was older and had a child. Takina takes the conditional remark as a statement about her present life. Madame answers that even a good investigator would find nothing. Later Takina thinks back to the wording and notices that the older woman never actually said a child was alive. The phrasing allows a true past to be heard as a private present. Mika later says she lost her own child in retaliation for past work. He describes the history as known in the trade and proposes it as the reason she cannot kill children. That is an account from someone who knows her reputation. It is neither hers to refute in the scene nor ours to turn into directly observed bereavement. Chisato objects that they were shot at. Mika answers with his own distinction between the two guards’ ambiguous ages and Clarisse’s unambiguous youth. The difference between the child in the park, the girl Madame regrets having to fight and the child she has actually undertaken not to harm is more precise than a rule based on age alone. [10.283–290, 10.963–975.]

When Takina asks about the watcher, Madame names Monsieur and calls him a partner. She expressly says he is no husband. She does the fighting and credits him with almost everything else, while calling his following second-rate. Takina mentions what let her know he was there. Madame asks for the details, compliments her and remarks that she is more talkative than she expected. Information goes out as well as coming in during a conversation, she says. It is a good description of the exchange they have just had. Even when a question gets an answer it can expose the terms on which it was asked. Madame gives Takina knowledge about a partnership and enough personal reserve to spend the rest of the day reclassifying what she thought she learned. A functional division and a criticism do not reveal whether the partners have a friendship outside the business. [10.290–303.]

## A paying client can withdraw

Once the fighting starts, the younger woman has good reason to take Madame’s ability seriously. Madame persists after sustaining a substantial injury. Takina regards injuring her badly enough to interrupt pursuit as a way to keep Clarisse safe if she herself cannot go on. Both women maintain the confrontation after their first advantage has run out. They voice mutual respect and smile. Madame herself tells Inoue that she resembles the woman she was when young, which Takina takes as a compliment. Takina believes that retreat may expose her and infers that Madame thinks similarly. This is an important limit on the earlier agreement. The same person who made room to agree on the safety of a passerby can continue threatening a young opponent while hurt. We have Takina’s assessment of her opponent's resolve, with substantial evidence in Madame’s actions. The private thing Madame intends to do with her fear, her pride or her prospects of recovery is still beyond Takina. [10.483–560.]

Chisato first asks the two women to lower their weapons and both hesitate. Madame lowers hers before they hear the cancellation. Takina follows while staying tense. When Chisato brings Clarisse forward after the staged danger, Clarisse cancels the assignment and offers the unpaid success fee as compensation. Madame relaxes. Takina's tension breaks only after the target and reason become intelligible. When Takina asks about the target, Madame identifies the two of them, specifically Clarisse’s guards. Chisato then states the protection of Clarisse and frames the exact lethal commission against the guards as a question. The girl's confession makes sense of the mismatch between a commission undertaken by somebody with a boundary about children and a small girl apparently being hunted. It does not vindicate their judgment that they could undertake this work without grave risk. Mika later thinks Clarisse researched Madame’s reputation to secure her own protection. That may be right. Clarisse herself explains the family attention she sought and the answer she expected from guards. The adults’ ideas about how much she anticipated remain distinct from that account. [10.642–715, 10.963–975.]

The two women confirm the end of hostilities in a second exchange. Madame makes a clear gesture of disarming and looks for Takina's response. Takina acknowledges it and openly puts away her own weapon. The client’s instructions give them a new reason to stop, and each still interprets whether the other will. Madame walks over and flicks Clarisse’s forehead. She calls her a brat, says that adults are not to be caused this much trouble, and asks why she confessed. Clarisse says the guards were kind and that Chisato had warned her Takina might be in danger. Madame suggests that expecting muscular older guards and finding girls instead might have altered her feelings. Clarisse nods. Takina has a different explanation, that the hard shared ramen meal at Jirō made them companions. Neither adult nor almost adult has gotten permission to replace Clarisse’s own answer. Madame has nevertheless asked a real question of a girl who has considerable reason to be handled solely as somebody’s daughter or a source of payment. [10.716–743.]

After Clarisse says the money has been transferred, Madame says she trusts her and does not require a check on the spot. We have a report of payment and an observable choice about whom to believe. Madame looks back and tells Takina that if she keeps at the work for another twenty years she will grow stronger, and that she is still young. She leaves on foot despite the wound. The exit can preserve the respect she showed earlier without supplying Takina with a mentor, an alliance or an account of what the injury eventually did. Taking the contract seriously and releasing the people it exposed both have consequences. For Madame’s next commission, we do not know what either participant will carry forward. [10.790–798.]

## Where the answer would have to come from

If the job called for taking dangerous action in a familiar hall and an unanticipated young child wandered in, the actual park encounter supports her making a stop visible and requiring the other person to observe it. If an authorized client explicitly withdrew the commission, the agreed cancellation and subsequent disarming support treating that as a real change in what the client can expect. No equally good case shows what she would do if withdrawal left a direct threat to Monsieur, if the client lied about their authority, or if the guard were as unambiguously young as Clarisse in Mika’s account. The novel does test an older guard who acknowledges being a child when asked. Her respect for Takina’s skill and her concern about age are both legible without settling any of those decisions. A later account of the loss she does not describe directly, or a choice she makes alone in ordinary life, would give a kind of evidence the novel deliberately limits here.
