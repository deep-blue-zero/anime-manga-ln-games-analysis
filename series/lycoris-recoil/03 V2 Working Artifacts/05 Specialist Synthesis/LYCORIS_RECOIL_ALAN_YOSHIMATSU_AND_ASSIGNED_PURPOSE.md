# The Alan Institute and Shinji Yoshimatsu

Himegama has cooked a meal for Shinji Yoshimatsu. He thanks her, tells her that it was good and says she has talent as a cook. She asks whether the Alan Institute would have supported her if she had chosen cooking. He stops at the verb. *Choose?* Alan supports talents bestowed by God, he says. A person’s role is indicated at birth. Himegama observes that in that case one need never look for the meaning of one’s life. He calls this happiness. A little later he tells her to treat Nishikigi Chisato gently. She replies that circumstances might prevent her from promising that. He answers that *she* can do it. When she subsequently exploits a medical appointment to harm Chisato, she recalls his request for gentleness. In the conversation after a meal, he has enjoyed someone’s work, denied her authority to choose its place in her life, and used confidence in her abilities to close off an objection. [E08 00:08–01:04, 21:16–21:43; YOS-MB-E08-001]

Shinji Yoshimatsu is the man who arranged the artificial heart that saved Chisato as a child, a man intimately acquainted with the café proprietor and former DA trainer Mika, a patron to people with exceptional abilities, and a familiar customer whom the girls call ヨシさん. His distinctive power lies in passing between those relationships. He can obtain a technology nobody treating Chisato ordinarily has access to. He can get someone who cares for her to make a personal promise even after the man has already offered an assurance on behalf of his institution. He can also be fond of a friend’s cooking and hurt by the return of a present. The series supplies no secure account of Yoshimatsu’s childhood, route into Alan, precise organizational rank, or the origin and succession of the Institute’s authority. His choices give a much fuller picture of the man than of the administrative hierarchy in whose name he speaks. [E02 03:23–04:07, 20:55–21:20; E04 15:06–16:04; E09 10:20–13:26; E13 07:04–08:09]

## What Alan gives, and who says so

In E01 a television narrator praises the anonymous support that discovered an athlete during poverty. The public name is Alan Adams. The report identifies the charm as the common mark and extends the story to researchers and artists. There are also voices expressing dissatisfaction with the opacity. Robota later repeats the familiar public account of free help for exceptional people constrained by poverty or disability across sport, literature, entertainment, medicine and science. He admits he cannot say whether Alan is one person or an organization. The show gives the viewer concrete evidence that support happens and concrete reasons to distrust its advertised innocence. It supplies no corresponding demonstration that the public figure called Alan Adams is Yoshimatsu, that every recipient is subject to an identical condition, or that one known agent has final authority over the entire organization. [E01 04:04–04:39; E02 00:02–00:27, CLM-001; E04 15:49–16:04]

The difference between a gift and a gift with a concealed condition matters more than the claim that philanthropy is a fraud. Chisato really does get years she would otherwise have lost. A researcher really does develop a better heart. A public television broadcast at the end of Mika’s confrontation with Yoshimatsu in E04 speaks of a Chopin Competition participant disclosing being an Alan Child. The reference to a musician runs beside Yoshimatsu’s insistence to Mika that the world must receive a rare talent for killing. By placing the announcement and the conversation together, the episode asks whether the same vocabulary can praise the emergence of art and oblige a girl to kill. One can raise that question without inventing the musician’s private bargain or assuming that patients who benefit from a medical advance owe politically acceptable lives in return. [E04 15:49–16:04, CLM-014; E09-CLM-020/021; E11-CLM-005–007]

Alan’s prohibition on involvement with beneficiaries comes principally through its participants. Yoshimatsu invokes it when Mika asks why he has never told Chisato who saved her. In the historical material Kurumi retrieves, the heart researcher asks whether meeting him directly is forbidden. In the room with Robota and Yoshimatsu, Majima holds up the Alan charm as intervention in a disobedient recipient’s life is challenged as a breach of Alan’s own rule. There is convergence on a norm. Its precise reach, enforcement and authorized exceptions remain unavailable. Mika gives the best contemporaneous critique. If the rule explains Yoshimatsu’s silence, why does he keep coming to the café? Yoshimatsu does not offer an administrative distinction between buying ohagi and quietly arranging a girl’s future. He asks if Mika wants him to disappear, draws a personal response, and returns to the promise. The public denial of reciprocity may seem liberating to a beneficiary. It can also leave the terms of the gift known to everyone with power except the person whose life they describe. [E04 15:22–15:49, YOS-MB-E04-001; E10 04:41–04:54; E11 06:00–06:07]

Walnut’s encounter with him reveals a less exalted reason for secrecy. He acknowledges commissioning the breach involving DA, then tells the hacker that people are happier in ignorance. Robota, pleased by what he thinks is the elimination of the rival whose place he wants, receives praise for being usefully tool-like. At the café Yoshimatsu can describe his recent work as troublesome 掃除, cleaning, and laugh about the quick quarry while enjoying domestic hospitality. The intended victim turns out to be alive. The distinction is important to how to read his apparent composure. Yoshimatsu is prepared to expend someone who knows too much, but he does not know all the outcomes his own story presupposes. DA’s parallel devotion to an uninformed public makes these acts comparable on one dimension. It supplies no single command structure or common ultimate aim for DA and Alan. [E01 20:26–21:21, YOS-MB-E01-001; E02 16:51–17:25, 20:55–21:20, CLM-008/010]

## The man Mika remembers

Chisato recognizes a customer before she recognizes a benefactor. When Yoshi-san comes in after about a month, he sounds pleased that she remembers him. Chisato jokes that customers are scarce, then explains that he was Takina’s first customer. She guesses which country he has visited. He says Russia, produces a souvenir, and laughs off her question about whether he met her teacher there too. A visit to Russia is supported. The place where the men first met remains unestablished by this question. In another visit he says Mika’s ohagi are delicious and recalls a time when Mika could scarcely make coffee. Ten years changes things, Mika says. Their private history enters the scene through something the younger customers can taste for themselves. Yoshimatsu remembers what the man who raised Chisato used to be bad at. It takes more than a professional briefing to produce the ease of the exchange. [E02 03:23–04:14 and 20:55–21:15]

At their E04 meeting Yoshimatsu says he came back because he wanted to see Mika. Mika hears teasing and offers a different answer: Chisato. The two motives can coexist. The scene declines to resolve them by giving us a private monologue. Yoshimatsu observes that the girl did not remember him; Mika reminds him she only saw him once as a child. E07 gives the visit a history. Faced by a man who demands Chisato’s freedom, Yoshimatsu says his heart leapt on that first visit to the shop as it did ten years before. The scene brings back his formal introduction as 吉松シンジ, a remark about their many secrets and the old promise. Mika claims the resolve to shoot him. After Yoshimatsu leaves, he admits that he never had it. Each man has tried to move the other personally. The original undertaking continues to bind them affectively long after they have ceased to agree about its object. [E04 15:06–15:42; E07 18:00–18:47, MIK-MB-E07-001, REL-MIK-YOS-E07-001/002]

The terms of that undertaking become available retrospectively in E09. Mika tells Kurumi about events ten years earlier. In the flashback Yoshimatsu calls the device another achievement of Alan-supported talent. It has limited durability, expected at best to carry the girl to adulthood. The younger Mika remarks on the working life of a Lycoris and begins to say that long enough would be sufficient. Yoshimatsu cuts in with a question about whether she will *kill* enough. To the man who might want an exceptional operative, he makes the condition of aid unmistakable. Mika accepts the expectation. When Kurumi hears this, she asks when he ceased to see Chisato as an instrument. The portrait requires the complicity of *both* men at the beginning. If the kinder adult had always been independent of the bargain, little of Mika’s later shame or Yoshimatsu’s dismay would make sense. [E09 10:20, 11:31–12:27, MIK-MB-E09-001, YOS-MB-E09-001]

Then the older language of expectation changes registers. Does Mika get along with children? He says taking care of children has been his job. Yoshimatsu asks him personally to help the girl after the operation. Mika offers reassurance that DA will raise her responsibly. Yoshimatsu insists he is asking *him*. He calls her 私たちの娘, their daughter, and the undertaking a promise between the two men rather than an assignment. The question ダメか, “can’t you?”, permits the sound of a request in circumstances already established by a conditional offer of life. He wants something from Mika that mere compliance by DA cannot provide. Mika’s later description 親子ごっこ carries guilt about playing at parenthood. His care through a decade of naming, teaching and making a home gives the relationship a reality its compromised origin cannot exhaust. Yoshimatsu’s affection is likewise substantial enough to matter. The evidence supports a prior intimate bond and shared paternal language. It leaves their legal relationship, private sexual history and exact domestic arrangements open. [E09 12:32–13:26, CLM-022–024; E10-CLM-019–030; REL-MIK-YOS-E09-001]

Chisato received a different proposal. The child asks whether Yoshimatsu is the person who will save her. He tells her she has a great mission and uses the word 救世主 for the saving role he has taken. She thanks him and wants to become a savior too. When a gift from the benefactor reaches her after surgery, she understands it as something with which to help people. At that point no adult has given her the terms exchanged with Mika. She owes the giver much in the ordinary sense of having received a benefit. There has been no commitment by her to the adults’ design. At E07 her wish to thank him, and his disappointment that she is not using her talent as he expects, reach the same table. When he begins to name the talent, the formulation is interrupted. Chisato can know him as her benefactor before she has heard him tell her in full what he thinks she ought to do. [E07 15:18–17:56, CLM-015–021; E09-CLM-025–027; E10-CLM-022–029]

## How Yoshimatsu speaks to different people

To a stranger subordinate to him, Yoshimatsu can sound politely appreciative while assigning that stranger the social standing of a tool. With Mika, the familiar name, the shared joke and the appeal to a promise have a very different weight. With Himegama he can say 君なら, *you* can, in declining her attempt to reserve judgment about how gently she can carry out his wishes. She has given him an opportunity to distinguish what he would like from what she can promise. He replies with a compliment that makes the distinction harder to maintain. His professional capability is social as well as material. He learns what makes an offer sound like recognition to its addressee. His regular error is to treat the recognition he supplies as a debt that fixes what the person should do next. [E02 16:51–17:25; E07 18:00–18:47; E08 00:45–00:55]

Takina’s encounter with him exposes the maneuver particularly clearly. She tries to repair the awkward reunion at the club by telling him Chisato was happy and inviting him back to the shop. He addresses her as たきなちゃん. Where she spoke of what her friend *felt*, he tells her she should know Chisato’s proper place is elsewhere, and says he expects something of her. He presses Takina’s care for Chisato into prospective service without securing Takina’s agreement. By E12 she has turned that same care directly against him. Having gotten close enough to the source of her partner’s distress, she wants the replacement heart more than she wants to punish him. His expectation could scarcely have found a more active friend or a less obedient agent. [E07 18:52–19:20, YOS-MB-E07-002; REL-TAK-YOS-E07-001; E12 05:41–06:23, CLM-008–014]

At the club Yoshimatsu tells Chisato that Alan children have roles and directs her to talk to Mika. After leaving her, he tells Mika that talent belongs to God and is neither an individual’s property nor the property of the patrons who support it. The daughter hears the instruction. The father hears the explicit claim that justifies it. The distinction answers a genuine objection to describing him as possessive. He expressly disclaims personal title to the talent. In the E08 exchange about cooking he even presents release from the burden of choosing as a kind of happiness. Yet he reserves for those who recognize a calling the right to say which capacity is the decisive one. Himegama’s talent for cooking is welcome as a social fact and unavailable as a counterfactual chosen career. Chisato’s gifts for easing a stranger into a room, making an ordinary day worthwhile, protecting opponents and inducing trust are observable. Yoshimatsu can enjoy some of their fruits. Only killing is allowed to tell her what she is for. Divine ownership still requires a human interpreter, and nobody in his system has explained how the recipient could successfully correct that interpreter. [E04 15:49–16:04, E07 17:19–17:34/17:39–17:59, E08 00:08–00:35; YOS-MB-E04/07/08-001]

Consider the researcher. In the historical sequence Kurumi recovers, an earlier meeting introduces a suitable experimental subject. A second record, represented as last year, has the investigator credit Alan for what he accomplished. Yoshimatsu is modest about his part, “we only helped,” and then works to contain precisely where the researcher's gratitude might lead. The researcher wants the new device given to the girl to save her. Yoshimatsu redirects him to the many patients who will need him. He makes the price of one girl’s life sound small against the realization of the man’s talent and treats responsibility for the subject as his own prerogative. Both are Alan’s children, he says. The researcher’s request to save a particular person and Yoshimatsu’s appeal to future patients are two ethically distinct responses to the same success. The network made the achievement possible. Yoshimatsu’s intervention seeks to prevent its maker from choosing its first beneficiary. This is also a limit on what he knows: the better heart becomes a lead the café can pursue because Kurumi recovers evidence beyond Yoshimatsu’s immediate control. [E11 05:35–06:44, CLM-005–008; YOS-MB-E11-001]

Majima offers a comparison from within the circle of marked recipients. His own account of being supported for lethal talent and the pendant he shows in E08 initially disconcert Chisato. She considers an explanation that would preserve her trust in Yoshi-san. In E10 Yoshimatsu calls Majima an excellent Alan Child, acknowledges supplying weapons and counts Chisato’s divergence as a mistake of his own. When Majima suggests he could kill Yoshimatsu, the latter declares that he would give his life for Alan’s ideal. Majima objects to institutions which secretly try to steer the world by their own account of justice. His hostility has its own limit. He in turn forces civilians into an experiment intended to make hidden power visible. When he shares a drink with Chisato or discovers they like the same film, common experience makes possible an exchange neither side needs to control. His claim that this makes them the same kind of person receives a direct refusal. These are rival moral agents, not three ranks in a single chain of command. [E08-CLM-013–018; E10 04:18–05:24, CLM-013–015; E11-CLM-028–033; E13-CLM-008–016]

## The cost of making the test come out right

Yoshimatsu does not rely on an argument alone. E05 lets us see how much work it takes to turn a day's kindness into an apparent obligation to kill. A man presented to the café as Matsushita asks for a tour of Tokyo. He later claims that Jin murdered his family and that Chisato owes him vengeance. The claim reaches her after she has already spent hours helping him. It borrows the trust of that day and the authority of something the purported client should scarcely know about her benefactor. Chisato refuses while she still has reason to take his suffering seriously. The team subsequently learns that the supposed client was an impersonation. The tour appears on a remote-control display, Yoshimatsu is heard giving an instruction, Himegama answers, and the Alan emblem follows. This is strong evidence that his side staged the test. It does not identify the operator who supplied every utterance. When Takina later lists “Matsushita” among Yoshimatsu’s offenses, we learn that she holds him responsible. Her accusation can reinforce the connection without filling that missing credit. [E05 16:22–21:07, CLM-013–018; E12 05:41–06:17, CLM-008]

The test succeeds in one respect even though Chisato refuses. When she learns the truth, she thinks even the praise she received as a guide must have been false. Takina distinguishes the fraud from the quality of what Chisato did. Chisato thanks her. The anonymous planner cannot retrospectively own the whole encounter because there was another observer, someone who saw how Chisato treated a person she believed to be dying. This is personal knowledge of the kind Yoshimatsu frequently discounts. Takina has grounds for her judgment even if she never learns who was at the controls. [E05 21:58–22:11, C0418–0421, CLM-020]

In E06 Robota tells Himegama that her boss’s added request looks impracticable. Majima simply has no interest in this particular Lycoris. Himegama comes back with an injunction to keep trying. A third person's interest has to be cultivated before he will play his assigned part. The episode supplies evidence of a relay, and a counterexample to the idea that Majima belongs to Alan in any straightforward sense. In E08 Himegama has to decide what “gently” can mean as she carries out her part. The man giving this order calls the café ままごと, playing house. Himegama’s employment gives him access to the sites where Chisato’s grown-up trust is most necessary. The resulting harm narrows her remaining time. No depicted instruction establishes that Yoshimatsu dictated every medical detail of Himegama’s intervention. [E06 06:51–07:07, 12:17–12:25, CLM-012; E08 00:45–01:04, 21:16–21:43, CLM-003/004; E09-CLM-003–007]

Three clocks matter here. As a child with congenital disease Chisato had perhaps half a year to live, in Mika’s account. The device then available might take her to adulthood, in Yoshimatsu’s estimate. After the attack Dr. Yamagishi initially says the charge left in the device can last at most two months. She immediately adds that it might last a little longer if Chisato moves around less. The clinicians can no longer recharge it. Ten years previously Yoshimatsu gave a girl time. His side has now forcibly shortened it. For Chisato the harm is legible without a trial of his intent. She tells Takina that killing Himegama will change nothing. She also rebuffs Kusunoki’s suggestion that she should use what time she has left in service to DA and tries to make a return to DA possible for Takina. She wants bounds on what another person can claim from her life and is willing to incur a cost to widen the options available to someone else. [E09 03:25–04:44, 06:06–07:36, 10:20–12:27, CLM-003–013]

## What each adult has promised

When Mika realizes that this girl may never reach an ordinary coming-of-age celebration, he brings out a haregi, the formal clothes for the occasion, so she can wear it now. The pleasure he takes in her appearance neither needs a public audience nor produces a future obligation. In the same encounter he tells her that when he asked for help he had a commander's interest in obtaining her talent. He had accepted a specific expectation from Yoshimatsu and kept it from her. Chisato imagines another version of her childhood in which knowing what the adults wanted might have induced a miserable compliance. This is *her* counterfactual. It cannot establish that the original secrecy was indispensable or authorize continuing to hide what concerns her. What matters is that she has information now and uses it to distinguish the two adults’ motives from the life she actually had. [E09-CLM-020–027; E10-CLM-019–030]

A further question in the same room is one only Mika can answer. Does he like this Chisato, the one his bargain did not call for? Mika names her his proud daughter. We have already heard Yoshimatsu use “our daughter” in asking for a promise about the future. The difference in the two gestures lies in their timing and the people being addressed. Yoshimatsu asked Mika to undertake something for a child whom they expected to become a particular adult. Mika answers the grown girl who wants to know whether her actual life has made her dear to him. Gratitude survives in all three directions. It establishes neither Chisato’s consent to the old promise nor her confidence that Yoshimatsu will tell her the truth today. When she chooses to go after him as a hostage she tells Mika she does not trust him. She wants to meet him and ask him questions. Mika helps her go. She is willing to rely on Takina and Fuki elsewhere. Chisato can choose which uncertainty to meet in person because other people can act without her. [E10-CLM-022–030/039–044; E11-CLM-020–027]

Yoshimatsu sees someone make that distinction in front of him when Chisato reaches his side in E11. She shields him. The direct scene and its retrospective check distinguish impacts on her bag around 19:06 from impacts on her body. Her rescue is visible even though no bodily impact or wound is established. Later, when he asks whether she killed Majima for what he did to him, he will treat his own rescue as a missed opportunity for another act entirely. Each has seen evidence of what the other values. Chisato wants to hear him speak for himself. Yoshimatsu wants another demonstration. [E11 18:58–19:18, CLM-024–027, CHI-MB-E11-002, VIS-010; E12 04:10–05:06, CLM-005/006]

## The person who offers to die

In E12 Yoshimatsu tells Chisato that few people are fortunate enough to have a role so clear. Killing would contribute to humanity and the world. She asks whether killing is supposed to be *her* happiness. She tells him she has been quite happy and wanted to help someone as he helped her. That answer gives him a basis for revising his theory. Someone with the very talent he identified can describe an accomplished life in which something else made her happy. His response compares her to a dying doll whose mechanism he wound for a different reason. The conversation has gone beyond a dispute about the scope of a promise. It now concerns whose testimony can count as evidence about a life from the inside. [E12 04:16–05:32, CLM-005–007]

Takina introduces a rival interpretation of the object everyone has pursued. The case, she thinks, contains Chisato's life. Yoshimatsu points to a scar and claims the replacement is in his own chest. He asks Chisato to kill him to obtain it and restore what he calls her value. E11 had established that a better device exists. E12 shows the scar and supplies his testimony. Neither the device's precise location then nor an intervening operation is independently pictured. Chisato need not settle the anatomy to refuse the demand. She has a reason that would hold even if he were telling the whole truth. She cannot call a life secured on these terms her own. [E11-CLM-005–008; E12 06:09–07:36, C0104–0110, CLM-010–014; E13-CLM-033, retrospective REV-E13-006]

What would he gain by dying? Yoshimatsu has already told Majima he would give his life for Alan. Here he is prepared to make his death serve as the girl's first surrender to his reading of her talent. His willingness is good evidence of a genuine commitment to the ideal and a sharp objection to explaining him through personal survival, greed or a wish simply to keep the girl with him. Its sincerity also raises the cost of refusing him. Chisato must reject something that looks to him like the fullest sacrifice one person can make for another. When she will not accept his gift on those terms, he turns to the person she might be less able to bear losing. Takina herself wants Yoshimatsu’s heart for a radically different reason. Even people who love the same girl may work to produce an outcome she refuses. The distinction between their reasons matters most precisely where the proposed outcomes draw close. [E10-CLM-013–015; E12-CLM-010–016]

The series makes the threat to Takina consequential while preserving uncertainty about the moment of injury. The E12 mix peaks on the tower exterior at 08:10.035750. At 08:11.199 the image returns to Chisato aiming, then shows Yoshimatsu wounded. The order and the immediate threat strongly support that Chisato intervened to save Takina. The point of contact is withheld. We cannot recover a deliberately selected medically safe injury or the load from that sequence. What Chisato does once she can reach the wounded man is much clearer. She examines him, gives aid, condemns his treatment of his own life and returns his token. She tells him, directly, that she dislikes him. He answers that he is doing this for her. For the world, surely, she replies. His 同じことだ, “the same thing,” gives the charitable reading of his conduct both its strongest evidence and its limit. He believes an obligation to the world can tell him what is good for Chisato even while Chisato is telling him she has many things more important than the world. Moments later she says she is grateful for her time and asks him to stay well in her place. She wants him to live, although his continued life might foreclose her own. That request gives him room which he has systematically denied her. Her anger and her tenderness toward him are both earned by this encounter. [E12 08:07–09:39, CLM-015–020; Q17, VIS-003]

Takina's protest after he escapes is a second ethical claim that has to be answered in person. She sees the heart getting away, wants Chisato alive and does not want to let him go. Chisato physically stops her. She insists she cannot live in his place and tells Takina that everyone's farewell will come, “but it isn't today.” It would be inadequate to say, simply, that Chisato respects other people's choices. She prevents her partner from carrying out one, over her protest, because Takina is trying to buy *Chisato’s* survival at the expense of someone else. The episode gives Takina the words to object to the cost without making them a mandate for Chisato to act against herself. [E12 10:03–10:53, CLM-021–024]

## The information Mika takes home

At the tower Mika lets Yoshimatsu discover another way a man can be untruthful to someone he loves. His performance of limited mobility has concealed a capacity Yoshimatsu did not expect. Yoshimatsu calls him a man of lies. When Mika says it was all for Chisato, Yoshimatsu answers that she did not understand him. He shows the returned token and says he seems no longer to be needed. Two listeners to the Japanese hear あたしは in both these lines, around 07:20 and 07:29. The subtitles write 私. The attributed hearing gives local texture to the shift from the public “we” who only helped to an individual who has been refused. It cannot found an invariant speech habit or identify a personal identity the series has not declared. Rejection hurts this man even though, by his own doctrine, the talent never belonged to him. [E13 07:04–07:33, REL-MIK-YOS-E13-001; Q13]

Mika replies that children can lead adults into a world the adults do not know and asks that their choices be left unobstructed. Then he asks whether the thing is there. Yoshimatsu says Chisato did not believe him. Mika answers that Shinji does not lie. The speakers have already given the viewer a literal counterexample. As a child Chisato correctly recognized the man who came to save her. He claimed she had mistaken him for someone else and that he worked there. She called the claim a lie and pointed to his conspicuous suit. The echo makes Mika’s declaration of trust specific and painful. In the very scene in which the man who once promised to raise their daughter affirms a child's freedom, he is willing to act on a man’s word about something he cannot demonstrate to us. His profession of trust is evidence about Mika. It cannot settle what is in the chest. Yoshimatsu says this affirmation no longer makes him happy and allows that the girl has driven Mika off course too. Mika agrees. It is the pair’s last direct exchange. Yoshimatsu’s hurt, affection, disapproval and capacity to recognize a change in a man he knows all survive the scene. Nothing establishes a final recantation of his doctrine. [E09 13:44–13:58; E13 07:29–08:06, CLM-031–033]

The cut supplies a firm limit on what can be said of Mika’s act. Two metallic-sounding events precede the exterior image at 08:09.197. Eight source presentations of a window light run from 08:09.572 through .864, counting the faint edges. The mix under them is low and nonzero. Neither independent listening result identifies an unambiguous exterior report. Later evidence associates Mika’s intervention with Yoshimatsu’s death, the case and the replacement that reaches Chisato. It does not show the particular lethal act or the mechanics of retrieval. E10 provides a useful formal comparison with different people and a different outcome. A mixed report peaks about 49 milliseconds *after* the E10 cut away from the park to architecture at 20:04.328. The subsequent appearances of blood and a phone do not identify who fired, whose blood it is or the participants’ final fates at that episode's horizon. One exterior hides information even as we hear a report. At the other exterior we do not securely hear that report at all. The series asks an audience to register the effect of violence without always granting it the scene-specific facts it might like to possess. [E13 08:05–08:12, Q13, REV-E13-006; E10 19:50–20:16, Q09]

A final letter makes that distinction between audience and character unusually exact. At the beach Takina tells Chisato that Mika brought Yoshimatsu’s case and that, according to what she heard, the object came with the heart. Chisato handles an outer greeting marked *HAPPY NEW BIRTHDAY* and the corded charm. Later in the edit the tower scene shows a separate decorated, written farewell in Mika’s gloved hands. The voice credited to Yoshimatsu gives us its words, including the claim that his death completed her new beginning and the wish that she be happy. We see Mika read it; we do not see Chisato receive that text, hear him speak it or play a recording. Kurumi’s remark about the cane and her report that she has contracted a surgeon are distinct from knowledge of the wording on that paper. The montage strongly supports paternal withholding. It neither shows the exact moment at which the insert was separated from the greeting nor gives us a transcript of everything Kurumi knows. [E13 18:52–19:21/19:33–19:58, Japanese ASS L418–420/424–427/436–439; retained pictures 001049/001051/001087/001089/001093; CLM-034–036 read with this qualification]

This distinction changes what we can attribute to the three of them. Yoshimatsu writes a last effort to give his death a place in Chisato’s future. Mika can read what his old intimate wanted that future to mean. Chisato learns that she has a future and who brought the case. She may understand what kind of loss made it possible. She has never been shown assenting to the words the audience hears. Her eventual disposal of the pendant sets a limit on what she wants to carry into her next ordinary day. Nor does her survival cancel the ethical cost of the means by which adults secured it. Mika did not require her to kill. He did make a decision about what she should know, after explicitly saying children should be allowed to lead. [E13 18:52–20:36, CLM-034–037/048]

## Other authors’ versions of a benefactor

In 竹本蒼太郎’s *Reload* contribution the girl’s drawing names her benefactor generically; a configuration that puts Shinji beside the child belongs to Mika's imagining. A spent cartridge casing and a solitary figure against the night skyline leave the decisive action outside the story's pictures. The artist gives Mika a way of seeing a family that might have been. Those images cannot identify the man the child actually drew or restore a missing TV shot. In 大倉ナタ’s *Reload 02* story, even after Takina points out what Yoshimatsu did to her, Chisato looks at a family picture and still calls him a benefactor and family. Later Chisato calls Fuki on a work phone because she worries that Takina has been lonely. Fuki remembers *Takina* requesting the grave locations of the fallen Lycoris. Fuki refuses to release that information, takes a separate request about Enkūboku to Kusunoki and helps make a memorial visit possible. Takina wants to thank her fallen companions. The two objects of remembrance are distinct. No one here locates Yoshimatsu’s grave. The first contributor works through Mika’s imagined family picture. The second lets Chisato name a difficult tie as her own, then lets us see what someone else did for her partner in her absence. [*Reload*, 竹本蒼太郎 S135–142; *Reload 02*, 大倉ナタ S129–136]

A smaller comic makes another detail of his life available. In ぎんもく’s opening *Repeat* contribution Yoshimatsu comes to the café for the ohagi Mika makes. They let the contributor use an ordinary appetite which TV E02 has already given him. The recurrence can illustrate how readily the public benefactor becomes a recognizable regular. It cannot make the taste twice as well attested in the anime. The extra story at the end of *Recollect* V02 lets a chest gesture suggest what the gift of a longer future means to Chisato, then grants her a joke about another snack. It identifies neither a specific donor nor the mechanism that extended her time. Both artists can make an appetite meaningful to a person with a history without turning every meal into a statement about that history. [*Repeat*, ぎんもく S9–20; *Recollect* V02 S115–118; E02 20:55–21:15]

One further comparison helps judge Mika without inventing information about Yoshimatsu. In *Gluttony days*, Akutsu hires the park after the emergency and tells Clarisse a longer-planned story about the visit. Mika privately tells Takina about the timing. A photograph shows Clarisse smiling as she embraces her father, while the prose leaves open whether she recognizes and accepts the revision. It is possible to give someone a genuinely happy occasion and make oneself the author of a piece of their past without their knowledge. There the hidden fact concerns the timing of a family outing. In the tower story it concerns a death, an old promise and Chisato's opportunity to decide what her own survival means. The analogy clarifies the difference in moral stakes. It adds no second witness to either the letter or the unseen act. [*Gluttony days* Japanese spine 10 ¶878–959; E13-CLM-034–037]

Yoshimatsu’s last wish for Chisato's happiness deserves to be heard alongside his confidence that killing is her happiness. The evidence allows for affection operating inside an ethic that makes it difficult for him to recognize the person who has his affection. The researcher wants to treat her. Himegama imagines cooking. Mika comes to be proud of a daughter who disappointed both men's expectations. The girl herself remembers an ordinary day guiding a stranger. Someone who saw her do it gives that memory back to her. These are cases in which another person learns enough about what the recipient wants and does to change an answer. Yoshimatsu can change his tactics, risk his own life and lament the loss of his place in hers. The distinctive limit of his character is how little space those extraordinary commitments leave for the simplest reply Chisato makes to him: she was already happy.
