# Clarisse Akutsu

At a café in Tokyo, Clarisse lets Nishikigi Chisato’s outstretched hand hang in the air. Her father prompts her even to give her name. At the end of the day Chisato shows her partner a photograph in which Clarisse has put both arms around her father and is smiling. In between, the girl asks two people to stop risking their lives for her and takes an extraordinarily long time to tell them why their lives are in danger. She commissioned the danger herself. What she wants is easy to understand. She would like her father to leave work and come for her. The unsettling part is how readily she can arrange for other people to pay the price of finding out whether he will. [9.148–172, 9.456–479, 10.110–124, 10.674–712, 10.940–950]

## A daughter who knows her father

Hikaru Akutsu is a Japanese businessman who founded a company in South America, now works chiefly in the Middle East and has come to Japan to conclude a deal. His daughter is visiting Japan for the first time. She is small, with long, wavy red hair, frills and ribbons. The first estimate of her age is around thirteen. Later, as Inoue Takina considers whether old Tokyo would interest her, the prose places Clarisse in her middle teens. Neither passage supplies a birthday or a school year. Clarisse says her mother died when she was little. Her father’s travels and the observers’ guesses about her appearance tell us little about where Clarisse grew up, her mother’s background, or who looks after her when he is working. Even the elementary school in the story is a landmark the party passes. We never meet a classmate or see her at school. [9.103–110, 9.130–150, 9.313–328, 9.787–801, 10.120–125]

She enters LycoReco pale from the journey. Hikaru has let her stand outside for air. He puts his arm around her, pats her head to encourage an introduction, then kisses her cheek when he leaves. Clarisse plays with the tips of her hair and calls his departure business as usual. Anticipating a ride with him, she has taken only liquids that morning. She likes Mizuki’s driving because she actually feels well getting out of the car. Yet when Mizuki jokes that a man like her father must drive wildly, Clarisse pushes past Chisato to ask whether Mizuki is insulting him. A daughter can arrange her meals around a parent’s failings and still object to a stranger taking liberties with him. Her willingness to criticize him depends on who is speaking, what they presume to know and whether he is there to answer. [9.132–157, 9.241–249, 9.313–341]

Clarisse has grounds for saying she knows Hikaru. They eat ramen together. She knows his film tastes, his Mustang, even the sorts of things he has with an evening drink. When Chisato attempts a consoling explanation of how Hikaru named her, she has particulars of her own to contribute. Her much harsher claim is that she comes after work, after his own interests, even after his cars. When Takina asks whether he would really abandon his business to pick her up, Clarisse starts insisting that if she were *in danger*, surely he would. The sentence trails off. Takina believes she can read the answer in the girl’s face. What Clarisse herself expresses is the wish to find an exception to a pattern she has experienced. We do not have a history of earlier emergencies in which Hikaru failed to come. [9.276–280, 9.340–346, 9.470–480, 10.879–895, 10.925–931]

## Learning what a guard can do at lunch

When Chisato recognizes Clarisse’s name and trades knowing looks with the adults, Clarisse asks her father why he has entrusted her to a “geek.” Takina does not understand the reference until much later. Once Chisato and Takina demonstrate their competence, Clarisse takes the hand Chisato offers a second time and stammers through a much more courteous introduction. Her judgment can change in the space of a greeting. It has changed only so far. On the way to lunch she hears her supposed protectors talking about ramen and asks whether either is taking her predicament seriously. Her objection, that a demonstration at the café was one thing and keeping them alive would be another, gives even Takina pause. Clarisse asks them to withdraw and says she would sleep badly if the two of them died. The words could advance her wish to summon Hikaru. They also make it harder to accept Takina’s later guess that Clarisse simply did not care what happened to a guard. [9.152–198, 9.439–479, 10.725–742, 10.879–888]

Food gives her a way to challenge people at the café who think they know what a first-time visitor will enjoy. Chisato starts to offer something characteristically Japanese. Clarisse interrupts before Chisato has finished the word. She has encountered Japanese culture before and has no wish to perform delight at familiar tempura or sushi. Mika’s suggestion of a fine traditional restaurant strikes her as another way of serving much the same things. Yet she turns her whole head toward the scent coming out of a soba shop. Her quarrel with a ready-made tourist experience leaves room for plain curiosity and for hunger. On the walk she can acknowledge the ramen she eats with her father and still have no idea what this particular shop will ask of her. There is no ground for making ramen her favorite dish, for making fine food her abiding preference, or even for assuming she would choose this shop again. [9.250–279, 9.340–347, 9.376–382, 9.395–403]

At 弐郎, Clarisse first objects to the waste of standing in a queue without so much as a chair. Once she gets to the counter she cannot give an order. Two different people help at two different moments. Kurumi has recommended a small portion over the headset. Clarisse has already taken hers off, so Chisato asks for one-third of the noodles for her. Clarisse looks relieved to learn that a manageable portion exists. Then the cook asks about garlic. Clarisse can barely get it out that she dislikes it raw. Takina finishes the order, leaving the garlic out as the girl wanted. Clarisse smiles a little and tells her that helped. Later, after Takina gives both companions hair ties, Clarisse gathers up her carefully arranged hair and applies herself to the bowl. Takina wonders whether the child has mistaken a convenience for another local rule. What she sees is a girl watching her neighbors, borrowing what works and getting on with her meal. Written from Clarisse’s side, the scene might have a different explanation. [9.395–403, 9.456–461, 9.491–517, 9.537–558, 9.601–610]

Takina eventually imagines them as comrades who got through 弐郎 together. Lunch gives that thought real force. Nobody has had to tell Takina to notice what Clarisse wants omitted. Nobody has to tell Clarisse, when Chisato later looks ill, to offer her own bottle of oolong tea. Chisato is comically overfull and declines it. The girl’s offer still matters. She has seen a way to help that costs another person nothing. The prose gives us that small exchange without asking us to believe that one bowl of noodles effected a moral conversion. Even the After Meal belongs to Takina, who wonders whether having more suitable food to offer at the café might have prevented the entire business. Clarisse never supplies an answer. [9.550–558, 10.69–86, 10.728–743, 11.19]

## What she did and when she tells it

Clarisse’s father and the café staff believe they know the secret from the beginning. His company has traced a threatening message to her home computer. The adults understand that she wants to interrupt his business so that he will spend time with her. He arranges to give her a day out with convincing-looking protection while he continues to work. Kurumi later questions whether the origin of a message alone identifies who sent it. More importantly, knowing the story behind the message has led the adults to miss something much worse. Clarisse has separately engaged Madame and Monsieur to attack the guards. The protective arrangement belongs to Hikaru. The arrangement that endangers the people accompanying his daughter belongs to Clarisse. [9.641–654, 9.939–947, 10.659–685]

When Clarisse explains herself she first says that she wanted to punish her father for neglecting her, then that she wanted to frighten him. She had counted on experienced guards surviving long enough for him to be summoned and the undertaking to be called off. Hearing her talk about what professionals ought to withstand, it is possible to understand how she could think herself careful. She had expressly stipulated her own safety. Kurumi traces the advance payment to Hikaru’s personal account (10.384). Clarisse makes decisions for people who never consented to be endangered. The source of the later cancellation transfer and whether Hikaru authorized access to his account remain open. She later starts to say she never thought anything like this would happen and corrects herself. That would be a bit of a lie. The admission makes sense beside her earlier warning that the guards could die. Madame identifies both guards as the targets. Chisato describes the engagement as one to kill the guards alone, in a question that marks it as her understanding (10.672–685). In her own account she wanted a frightening interval that would end before either guard died, precisely when she chose. She never had that control. [9.456–479, 10.384, 10.672–709, 10.720–735]

Her attempts to interrupt the day change before her account of it does. After lunch she asks about someone who might have contacted her father if the girls were overcome. She acknowledges both are trustworthy, then cannot finish explaining why she remains anxious. When danger comes she asks Takina to call her father and learns that the group cannot get through. She offers to let them abandon her. Later, as Takina prepares to leave her and Chisato, Clarisse again asks Takina to give up the work. Takina thinks Clarisse has read the determination on her face. The girl has enough knowledge to be afraid for her without access to Takina’s much more specific private intentions. She also withholds information that could have given Takina a chance to choose differently. The reader has reason in retrospect to hear several things in these appeals: send for my father, save yourselves, and do not make me explain. Only the first two are expressly voiced. [9.773–785, 10.105–124, 10.186–208, 10.222–231]

The sequence of disclosure matters. Chisato says Clarisse told her at Deme’s home, while Takina was away. Clarisse later explains that Chisato told her Takina might be in danger. We have Chisato’s account of that private conversation and Clarisse’s reason for speaking, without a transcript of the whole exchange. By the time the other two see Chisato apparently threatening a tearful Clarisse, Chisato already knows. The spectacle interrupts a genuine, dangerous confrontation. Clarisse looks directly at Takina to apologize. Chisato identifies her as responsible and prompts her forward. It is Clarisse who asks Madame to cancel and offers the outstanding fee for doing so. A confession to one guard, a public admission under pressure and an instruction given as the client are three different things this girl does. [10.559–574, 10.627–656, 10.674–714, 10.728–743]

## Three different answers to the same child

Madame asks why Clarisse spoke up. Clarisse answers that the two guards were different from what she had expected, that they were kind, and that Takina might be hurt. Madame recasts the change in her own terms. Perhaps Clarisse had expected muscular men and instead grown fond of two cute girls. Clarisse nods, crying. Takina privately discounts an explanation based on appearance, partly because all three are girls, and reaches for the afternoon at the ramen shop. It is quite possible that appearance, an unexpectedly welcome kindness, lunch and the prospect of an injury all mattered. Clarisse herself names kindness and the danger to Takina. Neither the older woman’s formulation nor Takina’s confidence gives access to a private hierarchy of motives. Nothing further about romantic interest or preference follows from that nod. [10.728–743]

With Madame the girl is a client obliged to answer for what she bought. She is also young enough for the sight of the injured woman’s blood to make her tremble harder. Madame flicks her forehead, calls her a troublesome kid and has advice that a polite tour guide would hardly offer. Take that determination to your father and make him look at you. In Madame’s own account she underestimated her child and has lived to regret it. Clarisse nods. Much later Mika tells the guards about that loss and speculates that Clarisse deliberately chose someone who could never harm a child. Clarisse had said she wanted people capable of carrying out her instructions without hurting her. She never claims knowledge of Madame’s family history. Nor do we see her tell Hikaru to look at her in the words Madame proposes. Monsieur shares the professional engagement with Madame. The questioning, painful parental advice and decision to trust the girl are Madame’s. [10.684–700, 10.720–743, 10.752–768, 10.791–795, 10.961–975]

There is a small act of trust that Madame can give her immediately. During cancellation Clarisse offers to pay the balance, initially cannot make the transfer, then announces that she has completed it and starts to ask Madame to check. Madame answers 信じてるよ, “I trust you,” and leaves. The reader witnesses the report and Madame’s acceptance. No independent receipt or account balance appears. Madame allows Clarisse to carry the responsibility of having given her word, a significant choice after a day when adults have managed what she was allowed to know. A generous answer from a creditor also leaves the injuries exactly where they were. [10.653–656, 10.763–768, 10.790–798]

With Takina, Clarisse tries something different. She stands in front of the person who suffered, bows until the crown of her head almost brushes Takina’s skirt, and stays bowed even after the words run out. Chisato leaves the answer to Takina. Takina crouches below Clarisse’s eye level, puts a hand on her shoulder, acknowledges her own inexperience, smiles and asks Clarisse never to do this again. The girl first picks out the smile through her hair. Relief appears, her face crumples and she embraces Takina. Earlier she had been willing to set an unnamed guard’s future against her claim on her father. She now waits for the judgment of a particular person whom she has hurt. Takina’s generosity makes that encounter possible. Her request against repetition preserves Clarisse’s responsibility. The separate pat on the head comes from Chisato to *Takina*, who continues to hold Clarisse. [10.795–819]

## The name and the photograph

In Mizuki’s car on the way back, the guards tell Clarisse what Hikaru really asked them to do. Her own verdict is that, just as Madame said, the adults underestimated her. A few paragraphs later she suggests that next time she should do a better job. Chisato and Mizuki shout in unison that she should do no such thing. Clarisse laughs. The story gives us a child able to share a joke with people she has just faced in tears. It leaves open what she will do the next time she feels disregarded. [10.843–848, 10.865–875]

The conversation about her name gives a more exact measure of what has changed. Clarisse dislikes the name because she thinks of it as another of Hikaru’s enthusiasms. She can identify the red-haired film heroine and the Mustang that associate the heroine with her father. She even suspects Hikaru would like her to drive the car one day. Chisato proposes another origin. The Latin meaning of “Clarisse” evokes light, as does “Hikaru.” Perhaps he worked hard to choose a name they could share across languages. Clarisse looks close to tears, then smiles in a way the narration struggles to describe. Takina asks the obvious, unwelcome question. How does a name chosen years ago prove that her father pays her sufficient attention *now*? Clarisse laughs and expressly sides with Takina. After all, she says, her father knows no Latin. She can welcome Chisato’s kindness and still test her claim. [10.876–911, 10.916–921]

Mika speaks over the car’s speakers as someone who has known Hikaru a long time. He says that Chisato has it broadly right and that Hikaru often works on things out of sight. He also knows how intensively his friend pursues one thing at a time. He thinks Hikaru never intended his daughter to feel neglected. Clarisse answers with a joke about the movie names she might have had if born a boy, then meets the assurance about his intentions with a rather cool thank-you. Mika himself can hear the doubt. The scene leaves a distinction a young person can make even about a parent she clearly loves. Someone who meant well when he named you can still have been absent this morning. [10.921–931]

At the theme park Hikaru finally spends the evening with his daughter. The photo on Chisato’s phone shows Clarisse smiling and throwing her arms around him amid the costumed characters. Hikaru’s account is that he had quietly planned the outing well in advance to make up for his absences. After the photograph appears, the narrative explains that Mika had already told Takina, once Clarisse had gone, that Hikaru booked the park immediately after the emergency. The narration first says Clarisse believed him, then retreats to what can be witnessed: she made those around her believe she believed him, and she went smiling. She may believe him, suspend a doubt because the invitation matters, or understand perfectly and let him preserve a little dignity. All three readings accommodate the photograph. The embrace and the time he gave her belong to this day, whatever she made of his explanation. How they talk about his next absence is outside the frame. [10.937–955]

## How to recognize her within this story

Clarisse’s Japanese can be exacting without being loud. At the café she couples アタシ with courteous phrasing about what she would be pleased to have chosen for her, while making it quite plain how little she thinks of the options. With her father present she says パパ. She uses 父 when correcting Mizuki’s presumption and, under pressure, visibly catches herself moving from the familiar to the formal term as she explains her conduct. With the cook, syntax breaks down around the raw garlic she wants left out. With Madame, even while crying, she reaches for the language of a client cancelling a commission. With the woman she has injured she says たきなさん、ごめんなさい. Later she can playfully cast a vote for Takina’s dissent. These are shifts in written address, hesitation and word choice. Their pitch, accent and performed timing are unavailable in this prose. [9.156–172, 9.251–265, 9.313–322, 9.543–558, 10.650–656, 10.684–693, 10.795–814, 10.905–912]

Expect a challenge when someone assumes her tastes or speaks lightly of her family. Expect her to observe others closely when she enters an unfamiliar social setting, and relief when help preserves a preference she has actually expressed. Those expectations have repeated support in one day. Her dangerous response to Hikaru’s absence has a narrower set of conditions: a valued visit, an imminent business commitment, access to resources for hiring the pair and her conviction that a frightening event can still be called off. The same child later tries to call off the danger to protect a person who has helped her. Neither response should be projected onto ordinary disagreements at school, friendships, dating, future wrongdoing or a later family routine. Her school life, a sustained hobby of her own, any relationship with a guardian besides Hikaru, and her lasting response to Madame’s advice are all undisclosed. [9.154–198, 9.251–279, 9.454–479, 9.511–558, 10.684–712, 10.728–762, 10.843–875]

Asaura leaves Clarisse capable of giving other people exactly what they want to see. Before lunch that talent conceals a dangerous decision. At the end it may be part of the tenderness between father and daughter. The prose lets the reader learn when Hikaru made the booking and withholds the answer to whether Clarisse needs his story to be true. That final reserve belongs to her. [9.641–652, 10.946–950]
