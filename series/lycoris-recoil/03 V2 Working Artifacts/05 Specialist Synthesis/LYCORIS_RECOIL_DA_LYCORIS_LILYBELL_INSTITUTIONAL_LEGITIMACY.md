# Who gets to call Tokyo peaceful? Direct Attack, Lycoris and LilyBell in *Lycoris Recoil*

A month after a battle on the subway, passengers still cannot use the damaged station. A company president stands before cameras explaining how fortunate everyone was that the derailed train was out of service. He promises to establish the cause. Takina feels sorry for him. Detective Abe later finds a bullet and shows it to his colleague Mitani, who wants to report it. Abe already knows that incidents have been turned into accidents, though he cannot say who settles them or whether some are merely hidden. All these people live with the same damaged railway. What differs is who must account for the damage, who gets to examine it, and who can act on what they find. Direct Attack’s most durable exercise of power is the ability to keep those three groups apart. [TV E04-CLM-016/017; E05-CLM-001/006/007, 00:01–00:28 and 07:16–08:47.]

Direct Attack (DA) raises and commands Lycoris, girls who work as clandestine security agents. Their school uniforms help them pass among the people they protect. When the girls themselves become too conspicuous, senior command deploys LilyBell, a separate force of young men. That decision gives a particularly severe test of the claim that secrecy keeps people safe. The study covers spoilers for all thirteen TV episodes and six *Friends are thieves of time* shorts. The anime supplies the events and the changing distribution of knowledge. Later sections identify what Asaura’s three novels, Abe Kanari’s *Recollect* and individual licensed anthology contributors make visible within their respective stories. None of those sources supplies a missing constitutional charter, an independent opinion poll or a complete institutional history.

## The city whose danger never existed

The morning broadcast welcomes spring and recalls the old radio tower as the site of a painful “accident.” The new Enkuboku tower is already measured and celebrated. Residents can see the damage to the old structure without knowing who suffered there or how. The building remains a plausible emblem of peace. Chisato’s morning voice moves from clean streets and law-abiding residents toward the work that makes those residents’ self-image possible. The words take the narrative from removing danger to making it as though the danger had never been there. At 01:29 she identifies this work as the Lycoris’ role. Her last なんだってさ attributes the maxim she has just recited. Kusunoki’s aide supplies a firmer institutional voice when she calls maintaining the public’s belief in that myth a responsibility of DA. These are two speakers with different commitments. Chisato’s pleasure in an uneventful morning and her distance from the official account of how it came about can coexist. [E01-CLM-001, E01 00:20–01:47; REV-E01-002.]

There are three distinct achievements available to a security institution in this story. It can prevent harm. It can stop further harm or repair the damage after an incident. It can maintain a public account under which people continue to believe that neither intervention was needed. Success on the last does not establish success on either of the others. Equally, an undisclosed protective act can save somebody who will never be able to identify the person who helped. The girls’ actual labor is part of what makes the political question difficult. Citizens experience ordinary safety and the girls sometimes earn it for them. They lack the means to distinguish occasions when DA protected them, endangered them, concealed a failure or had little to do with the outcome. That uncertainty is a condition of the system’s operation. [E01-CLM-001/003/007; E04-CLM-016; E07-CLM-008–010; E13-CLM-021–024.]

At the warehouse, the audience can follow several histories where the subsequent report needs only one. Fuki has a standing order to wait and requests permission to act. Erika is held and asks for intervention. A new decision on Fuki’s request has not reached her when Takina acts. Erika lives, the traders die and the consignment is missing. DA’s superior officers also direct that the interference with its information system stay secret. Chisato hears the report of a Lycoris acting on her own and asks Mika whether this is why Takina was sent away. The disciplinary case contains a real disobedience and a real dispute about what risks her skill entitled her to take. It also shifts attention from a loss of institutional control. When Takina later uses Saori as bait without giving her the same opportunity Erika had to ask for help, the legitimate question about Takina’s judgment becomes particularly clear. Serving as institutional cover does not give her a blameless tactical record. Conversely, her responsibility does nothing to excuse the institution’s selective report. [E01 01:49–03:21, 08:19–09:10, 16:31–18:47; E01-CLM-002/003; attributable correction REV-E01-003.]

The café receives a stalking complaint the police have treated as a private affair. Detective Abe brings Saori to people who will take the complaint seriously. At the café, the time of her photograph places the transaction three hours before the operation. Mizuki later says she had DA intelligence analyze the blurred picture and hoped the figures’ builds might narrow identification. Kurumi produces a differently processed view. These are separate uses of Saori’s evidence. Later, Saori’s ordinary noticing again supplies a lead on Chisato while the island affords little of the network coverage Kurumi could use. A civilian’s control over a modest piece of evidence can correct a picture prepared by an intelligence service without making the citizen privy to DA. The photograph’s value was never a measure of whether the stalking itself deserved attention. It was a consequence of somebody being prepared to listen to Saori. [E01-CLM-005/007, 21:38–21:50; E02 06:47–07:06, E02-CLM-003; E03 02:16–02:38 and TV verifier Q04, 09:02–09:10; E13-CLM-027.]

## The parent, the license and the way out

Fuki uses the first person plural when she tells Chisato that DA rescued them as orphans and raised them there. Her next question asks whether Chisato feels gratitude toward a parent. Chisato says she does, then describes something like leaving a parent’s influence, 親離れ. Fuki’s rejoinder has a point: Chisato took her teacher with her. For Fuki, Mika’s continuing presence counts against a simple claim to independence. For Takina, trying to get back into the dormitory and her former role, the parent metaphor describes a whole place from which she can be excluded. Fuki points out that upper management decides whether she returns. Kusunoki confronts Chisato about renewing a license whose privileges she wants while shirking what DA regards as its duties. Chisato answers by naming actual work she has done and challenging command’s own responsibility at the warehouse. Takina’s ability, Chisato’s license, senior discretion and filial gratitude are four different kinds of claim here. Only one of these agents has much room to press her interpretation on the commander. [E03 07:47–09:23 and 09:35–10:07; E03-CLM-004–010.]

The institution both clothes the girls in civic familiarity and restricts their ordinary civic legibility. In E02 Chisato explains that without a family register they cannot obtain passports. This is specific testimony about the Lycoris’ predicament. It does not provide a comprehensive statement of civil status or the source of any coercive power. E10 adds Kurumi and Mizuki’s inference that the absence of a register made young people especially convenient for an illicit medical experiment. That is their reading of an opportunity for exploitation. In E13, Chisato answers a call from Hawaii, declines the work, and deflects the passport question. The journey supplies a counterexample to an absolute inability to travel. It never depicts a change to their status, the authority issuing a document, or a countrywide remedy. Fellow members born into the original arrangement still have no demonstrated route to contest it. [E02-CLM-004, 09:32.68; E10-CLM-001, 00:05–00:20; E13-CLM-042, 22:31–22:56.]

The uniform has several audiences. DA entrusts its ranked girls with responsibilities and powers of force which most adults in its streets do not possess. In Short 05 Fuki and Takina forbid Sakura’s proposed trial of the red First uniform. Its wearer Chisato gives permission for that garment. Sakura wants the view from inside the rank. Takina tries it herself in private. A girl can desire the recognition, the view or the prohibited experience without having earned an appointment. The three film fans who discuss the uniform with its wearers in Short 06 lack this access. The fans start from its tailoring and from a film. They can recognize the outfit with enthusiasm and still misclassify the wearer. Among the girls it reveals rank, on the street it passes for ordinary clothing, and before film fans it is a recognizably *fictional* costume. A conspicuous badge and effective concealment can be the same piece of cloth. [E03-CLM-004/008; SHORT05-CLM-003–005, C032–43; SHORT06-CLM-001/002, C003–20.]

What the girls have internalized is visible in the very different decisions they make about a post. Takina first believes restored recognition would give her a life again. By E09 she can use reinstatement to seek information which might help Chisato. In E11 Fuki tells her that abandoning the mission will close the way back. Erika offers to fill her position. Fuki ultimately says 行けよ, then invites ideas about what to tell headquarters. Sakura sees teams competing for credit, Hibana says it is no competition, and Erika accepts the costly part of covering the vacancy. Fuki’s permission does not reverse headquarters’ decision. The important thing she can release is a partner under her immediate command. In E12 Fuki in turn acts on behalf of the wounded despite a standby order. In E13 she and Takina decide between them who can reach Chisato and who can help Sakura. This is agency inside an institution whose rules remain in force. The agents’ professional solidarity gives them partial power to choose who will have to bear a rule. [E09-CLM-012–015/028–030; E11 10:15–11:57, E11-CLM-009–013; E12 12:37–13:05, E12-CLM-032–034; E13-CLM-001–005.]

## Who has to tell the lie?

The subway gives the story its most precise comparison among distributors of an official account. Majima and his men have been in a battle. The news announces collision and derailment. People in the café receive a report of an accident. At the scene, detectives Abe and Mitani face restricted access and the conspicuous absence of witnesses. A month later, passengers have a complaint independently of the cause: their station still has not reopened. The railway company president tells them no one was harmed because an automated train happened to be out of service. The café conversation offers a supposition that he himself knows nothing. The press conference proves he publicly answers for the accident story. His private knowledge remains an inference. A government or private operator can make itself accountable for remedying apparent equipment failure while no one answers publicly for the violence. [E04 19:09–19:56 and 23:21–23:50, E04-CLM-016/017; E05 00:01–00:28, E05-CLM-001.]

Abe finds a bullet and tells Mitani he wanted to show it to him. Mitani wants to tell the police chief. Abe explains that the incident is supposed to be an accident and that numerous cases since the old tower have gone the same way. He cannot tell whether someone is actually resolving them. He does not know everything the chief has been told. Seeing Chisato, Abe says that if a girl like that can live safely, he can accept whatever someone is hiding. The viewer knows whom he has chosen as his example. A girl for whose safety he is willing to tolerate the practice is a girl doing its dangerous work. His concern is genuine, his assent limited to what he thinks the arrangement buys, and his judgment deprived of evidence that might change it. Mitani’s objection concerns the truth he can already establish and his obligation to report it. Neither detective can license the arrangement on behalf of everyone else. After the tower crisis Mitani now speaks of having been decorated for securing Robota. He calls himself the chief’s dog and allows that there must be various reasons. The change in his talk suggests the force of recognition from inside the body he had wanted to challenge. The remark alone cannot tell us whether he feels gratitude, resignation, amusement or all three. [E05 07:16–08:47, E05-CLM-006/007; E13 14:53–15:03.]

A second classification in E07 is instructively *less* ambitious. A police station has visibly been attacked. The public report attributes the attack to an organized-crime group. DA does not have to make anyone believe the police station was never endangered. It needs the public to misidentify the agent and the reason. While Sakura takes a covered news report for granted, Mizuki reminds her that the organization was supposed to prevent the attack before it happened. Earlier Robota treated DA’s systems as technically formidable. The result reveals the human limitation on action when simultaneous threats make conflicting claims on attention. Later, Fuki and Sakura take police-station images to the café, where Chisato identifies Majima and Takina concurs. Fuki reports the identification to Kusunoki. Outsiders to the central command worked out part of what happened, and the organization was sufficiently flexible to use their knowledge. None of that flexibility supplies the missing prevention. Blaming a different group and keeping an incident invisible are different demands on journalists, police and witnesses. [E07-CLM-008–010/025; E07 10:40–11:44.]

Secrecy can also harm the people meant to make it effective. In E06 Chisato first seeks information on the attacks on Lycoris through the official route and learns it is classified. Kurumi’s inquiry leads to an earlier surveillance record that exposed individual girls to danger. Kurumi reports that DA had used its own girls as bait to draw someone out, and Mika condemns the decision. This is reported institutional conduct within the scene, not footage establishing what every girl in the operation agreed to. Majima dismisses operatives as disposable pieces while wanting to reach the power behind them. His contempt for the girls meets a terrible echo in the risk the institution is willing to impose. Neither owns the girls’ account of the risks they take for one another. [E06 05:02–05:42, 17:43–17:52; E06-CLM-008–011/013–015.]

## Challenging the right to rule

The argument between Majima and Kusunoki in E10 is unusually exact about what is at issue. He names law as the modern measure of good and bad and asks whether DA itself exists under it. Kusunoki answers that her organization predates the present law, crosses political regimes, and has helped cultivate the country’s public safety and morals. She declines further explanation and appeals to the resulting national interest. For her, true peace means a world in which one does not even feel the existence of malice. This is an account of historical service, continuity and consequence offered in reply to a question about legal authority. It does not establish which public body granted present authorization, the scope of that grant, or who can hear a citizen’s appeal. Robota’s earlier account of the pre-Meiji Higanbana is a second in-story history, supplied by an interested opponent. The two accounts make antiquity intelligible as a belief about DA’s origins. The program supplies no independent institutional archive or charter which adjudicates the claim. [E06 05:18–05:31; E10 10:00–10:49, E10-CLM-031–033.]

Majima’s question deserves an answer even though his next act deprives people of the conditions for giving one. He has distributed weapons in Tokyo and tells people to decide for themselves. An unsuspecting man wonders whether he has found a toy. A police officer tells him to drop it. The E10 cut leaves the confrontation for architecture before the mixed report. The subsequent image of blood and a phone cannot establish who fired, whose blood it is, or the individual outcome. The people have been put at risk without choosing to participate. Kusunoki orders the girls to avoid involvement with armed civilians and forbids firing, recognizing that visible force is exactly what Majima wants to expose. This limits one risk while leaving the civilian and officer facing another. He makes a genuine criticism of concealed violence by deploying a coercive spectacle of his own. His “up to you” confines the public to options he has already set dangerously in motion. [E10 19:32–20:20, E10-CLM-033–036; TV verifier Q09, source picture cut 20:04.328 and report peak 20:04.377.]

E11 supplies a new street encounter, and for this one there really are witnesses. Robota interrupts the screens. Majima names the girls, identifies their uniforms and tells his audience a recorded image can be faked. The spectators who see an armed man and a uniformed girl fall can compare a live encounter with his account. The girl is visibly wounded and fires. The man’s subsequent injury is also visible. Neither person’s eventual fate is given in that street sequence. Someone in the street actually cries out “Lycoris.” It matters how little that cry proves. The witness has a name newly made available by the broadcast and a frightening event to attach it to. That does not mean the witness knows the girl’s orders, recruitment history, command structure or responsibility for past disappearances. Majima invokes *three thousand* missing persons a year, then asks how many Lycoris made disappear. He supplies no attributed total. His words make the question actionable as a threat to listeners while failing to substantiate an answer. On the tower assault the girls had explicitly been ordered to annihilate the opposition without mercy. On the public streets firing is forbidden. Same institution, different audiences, different rules. [E11 08:54–09:12, 12:31–15:02; E11-CLM-014–020. E10’s park and E11’s street must remain separate.]

The senior official Itadori (虎杖) reproaches Kusunoki for the mere fact that DA’s existence was suggested to the nation and tells her to use Chisato. He describes her in the vocabulary of keeping an animal, 飼っていた. His rebuke makes an exceptional girl sound like a capability held in reserve for precisely this emergency. Alan has its own doctrine about Chisato’s gifts, but the command whose authority Itadori exercises here is DA’s. Neither the similarity in how adults justify control nor Majima’s encounters with Yoshimatsu turns the organizations into one. Chisato has defended her freedom to determine what she does with her time and skill. The fact that an official can request her work, demand it through a subordinate, or ask Mika to intervene has never meant that all three routes will produce the same answer. [E09-CLM-010–013; E10 20:20–21:08, E10-CLM-015/037–040.]

## The order to erase the girls

E12 stages two chains of command in the same operation. Itadori announces an upper-level decision: リコリスは処分する. Fuki hears a standby order. Sakura wonders why they have not been told to withdraw. In headquarters an armed officer stops Kusunoki’s aide. Chisato names the incoming force and describes LilyBell as something like a boys’ version of Lycoris. Mizuki reports previous visits to kill Chisato and identifies Mika and Kusunoki as the adults who negotiated with their superiors. Chisato adds the price: she had to help DA. Her safehouses and E06 reference to earlier visits establish that this was a real constraint in her own history. Neither witness supplies a general organizational chart for LilyBell, an independent mandate, a recruitment history identical to the girls’, or a private relationship between Chisato and the field captain. What the viewer can know in E12 is what this force is ordered to do. Itadori explicitly directs it to dispose of the surviving Lycoris at Enkuboku and permits the bodies to be left. The scene concerns identifiable agents exposed in this operation. It supplies no execution order covering every Lycoris throughout Japan. [E06 10:44–10:49; E12 03:05–03:44, 11:41–12:30; E12-CLM-003/004/030/031.]

The covert counteraction also has an author. Mika confirms that he kept Kusunoki’s commission from the departing rescuers. Kusunoki thanks him and reports how long they have. Her aide guards her brief opportunity to communicate. Chisato speaks of opening the café for business again, Kurumi contributes the information and media expertise, Mizuki brings practical help, and the girls move to save people they know by name. Fuki refuses to leave the wounded simply waiting to learn whether command will protect them. Erika takes responsibility for the place opened by Takina’s departure. Some participants therefore act without knowing that a commander also wanted this result. Some act from within DA. Attributing the rescue to an undifferentiated heroic café erases Kusunoki’s deliberate act of insubordination. Treating the effort as the straightforward execution of her plan erases the rescuers’ independent reasons to take part, and the fact that the official order still threatens them. [E12 12:43–13:05, 13:33–14:06, 14:26–14:35; E12-CLM-032–038, REL-MIK-KUS-E12-001.]

Kurumi makes possible a different kind of retreat. During the confrontation the audience still has screens. The operators report Radiata’s function restored and something else controlling it. The system identifies Walnut. Senior staff can recognize a handle while describing its bearer as an old man, unable to decide whether that person is an enemy or an ally. At 18:57 the episode captions the onset of the *Ukiyo Dōchū* intro. Erika reacts. At 19:14 a presenter greets the public and describes the preceding scene as so realistic that she too thought it might be genuine. The attraction has a name and a projected opening. Two men react differently to the same proposal, one admitting he was fooled and the other marveling at the cost. The operator reports the start of online and wider media cover *after* those reactions. A tune alone neither stops a unit nor proves the civilians have forgotten what they witnessed. Broadcast music, a presenter who offers an identity for the show, real crowd reactions and subsequent administrative continuation do different pieces of institutional work. [E12 18:24–19:40, E12-CLM-039/040; TV reconciliation E12-C09, 18:56.8–20:09.02.]

It matters especially who gives the last order. Chisato is visible before the male field captain raises a hand to his headset. The first Japanese cue addresses command, 司令. The next says 錦木千束です　続けますか？ over a wide view of headquarters. The name with です can report the person encountered. It need not be Chisato identifying herself. The strongest picture-supported reading is a field report asking whether to continue in Chisato’s presence. The unlabelled words alone establish neither that speaker’s identity nor the sole named addressee. The scene then brings **Itadori** into focus in the foreground, with Kusunoki behind him. His mouth changes position during 状況が変わった　撤収しろ, and he vacates the chair. The field captain relays the ceasefire. **Kusunoki** asks to be connected to Fuki and explicitly takes command of what follows. Distinguishing these acts materially improves the account of institutional authority. Kusunoki procured a rescue *behind* her superiors’ order. Itadori, who could authorize death, withdraws his force when the situation changes. Kusunoki subsequently regains the power to issue the girls’ orders openly. Their interests align at that moment without evidence of a shared conversion. [E12 C0419–0426, 19:53.34–20:11.06; direct static source checks below; current correction to E12 §21, E12-CLM-041/042 and E12-AUD-017.]

This correction also changes what counts as Chisato’s contribution. Her presence at Enkuboku, her role in the rescue and the change in what the broadcast can accomplish are real. The scene provides much weaker grounds for the attractive story that *she personally called Kusunoki*, challenged the commander in a deliberately controlled tone, and made her withdraw LilyBell. The Japanese report, editorial placement and mouth positions strongly favor the field captain reporting her presence and Itadori’s decision to stand down. There is no need to credit a heroine with an exchange the formal command structure can explain more convincingly. An energetic intervention by people outside the hierarchy produces conditions in which a superior can reverse an order. That is a different kind of power from possessing a legal or operational veto inside it. [E12 C0419–0426; E12-C09; REL-CHI-KUS-E12-001 under the bounded revision below.]

## A government answers questions about the show

The finale’s first civic question concerns cost. A reporter attributes an estimate of ¥500 million or more to experts. The governor promises a later report and points to attendance. Another reporter asks about videos being deleted and insists that taxpayers have funded the event. The governor asserts metropolitan ownership of the copyrighted material and contemplates official distribution. A reporter can object to censorship, tax expenditure or extravagant production under the very description that conceals the original use of force. The objection can be sincere and politically consequential on those terms. The governor’s statement is evidence of an official position on footage. It is no evidence by itself that he knows the system behind the incident, that the estimate is an audited cost or that a court has endorsed his copyright claim. The series has found a stronger device than public credulity: it gives skeptical questions a different object. [E13 11:57–12:23, E13-CLM-020/021 and E13-VIS-005.]

Fuki and Sakura encounter their public likeness on the other side of that exchange. Sakura wants a photograph, thinks the figures resemble them, and wonders whether this too is Radiata’s cover. Fuki says they look different and comments on the implausible weapon. In a café later, Yamadera notices that the uniform resembles Chisato’s. His next concern and the others’ conversation are about a person they miss. Another newscast calls the “villain” an actor whose name has yet to be made public. Talent agencies deny involvement. The report cuts off as it turns to the police. This broadcast reframes his identity. Whether any investigation is still pursuing him by a different description is left open. Yet the audience has just seen Majima alive. Enough people and institutions take up the revised category for ordinary business to resume. Nothing measures the private beliefs of every direct witness or proves all removed footage ceased to exist. [E13 12:30–12:51, 13:34–14:01, 14:39–14:53; E13-CLM-021–027/049.]

The internal count tells another story. On Robota’s testimony, Kusunoki gives 1,151 as the weapons total and 211 as recovered by DA. The aide reports that 323 are understood to have gone to sellers, and Itadori says LilyBell disposed of those. Subtraction leaves 617 outside those two categories if the cited figures and treatment of the categories hold; “about half still in Tokyo” is Itadori’s inference. These are an internal count resting partly on Robota’s testimony. The audience sees no independent census. Then the aide describes a *different* list: Radiata has identified 213 people for disposal, says Lycoris dealt with numbers 2 through 135 and LilyBell with those after them. Itadori names outstanding number one as Majima. This is the sharpest evidence that the two services can divide assignments after an operation in which one was ordered to destroy the other. Reported disposal of target numbers is not a list of identified bodies or a warrant the viewer can inspect. A viewer cannot infer exactly who died in each numbered case. The separate sight of an anxious civilian dropping a weapon and Majima calmly returning it is enough to make the material risk real. No later account of that man’s decision is supplied. [E13 12:54–13:55, E13-CLM-022–024.]

Short 06 makes the change in public categories durable without dating it to a precise day. Three fans come to the café after spotting a convincing likeness at the station. Takina starts explaining how the uniform is issued, including evaluation and aptitude. Chisato offers a custom-made-costume story. To the fans both answers demonstrate first-rate role-play. They recount their movie’s version of hidden guardians operating since antiquity, wish the group existed in reality and invoke local graffiti as the sort of nuisance they would like dealt with. Chisato stops Takina as she offers instruction. Later Fuki opposes the uniform changes suggested in the café, while Erika predicts the rumor will pass. Neither their informed skepticism nor a trio of delighted fans establishes a national attitude. There is no evidence that DA commissioned this particular film or that its makers possessed the secret. Some civilians can recognize more of the imagery than ever before and still have fewer incentives to ask the question that would uncover its origin. [SHORT06-CLM-001–005, C003–20/025–47/052–61; REV-SHORT06-002.]

## The supplementary sources ask who can use the institution

Asaura’s *Ordinary days* presents a case where recourse to the hidden organization may help a particular child. Kana’s schoolmates have used images to coerce her and a teacher has assaulted her. The café gives her the opportunity to ask for help and arranges a formal commission. Mika explains that the provenance of the gun brings the case within a prior agreement for DA to bear expense and responsibility. Kurumi reports that Lycoris intervened in the operation, and predicts the removal of the coercive images after obtaining the original from 溝隠瑠璃’s phone. Mizuki objects that helping earlier would have reduced the suffering Kana bore while the adults organized a more comprehensive intervention. The price of an eligibility rule falls on a child before it becomes the adults’ dispute. The exchange even considers recruitment: Mizuki raises it as a worried joke, and receives the answer that Kana is already too old even if she wanted to join. The short reply is not separately speaker-tagged. That exchange belongs to this novel. It gives no exact anime age threshold, and does not establish Kana’s own wish. Elsewhere in the book Takina raises handing a case to the police and Chisato objects because an armed fugitive could produce a major incident. People who help outside DA still consider what must stay publicly invisible. [*Ordinary days* 12.229–239, 18.279–407, 18.877–906; compare A1 E01-CLM-005/007/011.]

In *Recovery days*, a narrator describes visible rank, scarcity and workload as different things. Firsts wear red and are rare. Numerous Thirds do much of the work. Seconds have a particularly significant role in cities. The necessary appearance of a harmless girl sets an upper limit on the time available for training and for rising through the ranks. Attrition has a different institutional cost from the loss of any particular child. Those perspectives establish why the highly valued First can have an ordinary supervisory day, and why the marketable silhouette of Japanese girlhood has costs for the girls required to maintain it. Fuki’s own experience eventually interrupts the classification. Her haste to reach Sakura can disrupt her judgment. Sakura provides help of her own and receives Fuki’s recognition. Fuki refuses a seat in the helicopter and sees in the departing party two former partners and her teacher. Her feeling that there is “no seat” belongs to someone who has actually been invited. A limited institutional role and the voluntary distance a person maintains are analytically different losses. No numeric staffing estimate for anime DA follows from the novel’s account of relative abundance. [*Recovery days* 13.4–32, 13.617–675/709–748/798–817.]

*Gluttony days* brings the effect of institutional training down to a meal. Fuki wants to thank Sakura and expects that a local dish would be unfamiliar to a girl from Hokkaidō in a highly managed dormitory. She has checked that the dining hall will lend a hot plate for a team recreation. She has not eaten the dish herself and seeks instruction from Chisato. Sakura has already tried it with visiting former companions from Hokkaido (12.618–627). Hibana and Erika know about the outing. Fuki’s institutional knowledge is good enough to arrange a scarce opportunity and poor evidence for what one specific partner has actually experienced. That combination gives an individual girl authority to contradict a superior’s sincere belief about her. Compare the café’s finances in anime E08 and Fuki’s reminder in Short 05 that the building is a DA facility. The café can use institutional resources to do something else with a day. Neither it nor dormitory life completely owns the meanings the girls make of a place. [*Gluttony days* 12.263–277/477–490/637–749, 13.6–10; E08-CLM-005/006; SHORT05-CLM-001.]

Abe Kanari’s *Recollect* specifically tests knowledge at the lowest level of the hierarchy. Erika wishes to apologize to Takina directly, considers a letter and lacks the address. The reader can see her hesitation and the cut to ordinary play at the café. Erika cannot see the other side of that cut. In the second volume Fuki corrects Sakura’s way of talking about victims and herself quietly calls Mika. Mika thanks the caller and says that Kusunoki had previously supplied some information. The reader subsequently learns the caller was Fuki. Her report is not printed, so we cannot identify its overlap with Kusunoki’s information or say she passed on Erika’s private words. A separate story presents a deliberately generated café horror narrative which starts to disclose an institutional name. Mika interrupts. The customers’ recognition and later circulation remain unshown. The cases add multiple local loyalties and genuinely partial information without providing another attestation of national media control. [Abe Kanari, *Recollect* V01 S55–66; V02 S23–34/37–48.]

Several anthology contributors stage competing answers to what command knows. 野上武志’s *楠木司令の植木箱* has Kusunoki compare ordinary stock and exceptional reserves. Her aide reads Takina’s placement as an intentional maneuver. Kusunoki doubts the explanation, and her assumption that Walnut is an old man gives the reader a check on her expertise. A real covert request in E12 cannot retroactively prove the aide right. In 大倉ナタ’s *安心しとけバーカ*, Fuki refuses to disclose restricted grave locations yet seeks Kusunoki’s permission when Takina asks about a distinct memorial site. Restricting information about the fallen and arranging an actual place in which to thank them are separate duties. In 弐尉マルコ’s *Reliant on Lycoris*, one official reports three casualties and another asks for the usual files. A returning girl voices relief. The images invite a comparison between what an institution records and what a person brings home without supplying a diagnosis, confirmed remorse or an anime casualty count. These three works have different creators and purposes. They cannot be combined into a single commander’s career or a standardized DA procedure. [*React* S5–14 and S91–100, especially S100; *Reload 02* S129–136.]

## What authority survives?

DA repeatedly exercises practical jurisdiction. It can classify events, allocate work, influence ordinary police, remove a girl from her post and deploy the force which might kill her. Its resistance to criticism rests variously on genuine protective work, the girls’ gratitude, its claim to antiquity, senior control of information, and on other people’s willingness to go on with their lives. The series directly tests these grounds against each other. Takina and Fuki owe something to the institution which raised them, yet Fuki will disobey to protect colleagues. Abe can value the quiet life his silence appears to purchase while knowing that inquiry has been obstructed. Mitani wants to report the bullet and later mentions receiving a decoration for securing Robota. He calls himself the chief’s dog, without saying how the recognition felt. Majima can correctly dispute who authorized DA while denying civilians a safe way to refuse his alternative. Neither the successful audience management at Enkuboku nor an unaccounted-for weapon decides what collection of powers ought to be legitimate.

The hardest judgment is also the narrowest. Itadori’s order and its reversal establish the girls’ vulnerability to senior discretion. Kusunoki’s covert request and later command establish that individuals inside that system can take real risks for them. Both propositions are stronger once the decisions are given back to the people who made them. By the end there is still no routine process shown through which a Lycoris could challenge an order for her own disposal, or a citizen could obtain an evidentiary hearing about the station. What survives alongside DA is the capacity of particular people to offer information, decline an assignment, rescue a colleague, preserve a question or disbelieve a presentation. Those acts constrain what the institution can do in some circumstances. The story leaves open whether they could secure public accountability under less exceptional conditions.
