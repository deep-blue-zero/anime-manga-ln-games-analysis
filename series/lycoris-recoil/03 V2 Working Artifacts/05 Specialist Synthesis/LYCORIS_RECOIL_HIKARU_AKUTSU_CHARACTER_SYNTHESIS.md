# Hikaru Akutsu

When Hikaru Akutsu walks into Café LycoReco, he tells his old friend Mika that he hasn’t changed. Then he tastes the coffee. Mika has changed quite a bit, he says. The last cup he made was awful. Ten years have passed since the men saw each other, and the joke gives them back the ease of sitting at the same counter. It also gives Hikaru a chance to do something he does less readily with his daughter. He notices a change in someone he already knows and lets the other person hear that he has noticed. With Clarisse, he recognizes that she badly wants a day with him and goes to considerable trouble to give her an enjoyable day with somebody else. [9.103–129, 9.148–151, 9.641–652]

## What he has kept in running order

Hikaru is a Japanese businessman who established his company in South America and now does much of his business in the Middle East. Back in Japan for negotiations with an energy company, he asks Mika to take a job on very short notice. The men first met through work when Mika was leading what Takina rather discreetly calls a wild life. Mizuki places the acquaintance before Mika’s time at Direct Attack. The novel leaves the actual work unspecified. Hikaru is tall and lean, with a fine beard, small wrinkles, an expensive-looking suit and eyes that strike Takina as young. He looks about fifty. He is raising a daughter whose mother died when the girl was little. Clarisse says her father is currently single. Neither the circumstances of the death nor Hikaru’s experience of bereavement is recounted. [9.72–85, 9.103–119, 9.208–219, 9.313–323]

The café hears his Mustang before seeing him. Hikaru left it in storage in Japan and paid to keep it inspected and serviced. Its upkeep has cost more than the car and its modifications together. He laughs at the bill and praises the people who have kept it driving the way he remembers. Different models of Mustang await him in different parts of the world. The men can call that devotion to a single taste. His daughter, who has had almost nothing to eat that morning in anticipation of riding with him, would prefer a taxi. He does notice that she is carsick. He lets her recover in the fresh air, puts his arm around her and asks if she feels better. What he likes about the old car and what the girl has learned about her own stomach can both be true. Clarisse knows the financial and emotional care a car can command, which gives unusual bite to her later judgment that she ranks somewhere below it. [9.98–115, 9.130–139, 9.233–247, 9.313–318, 9.340–346, 9.470–479]

The car and the coffee open onto different parts of him. Hikaru likes continuity and can afford to produce the experience of returning to an unchanged world. He also takes genuine pleasure in being surprised. Chisato catches a connection among Clarisse’s name, her red hair and the car. Hikaru turns to Mika, compliments him on the girl he has raised and tells Chisato that her guess is right. In a demonstration that Mika proposes to answer Clarisse’s doubts about her guards, Chisato and Takina get the better of him. He laughs from the floor and praises how cleanly it was done. The interruption to his dignity gives him evidence he is happy to have. Takina wonders whether he has studied judo. Mika identifies the practice as systema. Hikaru confirms that he still does it as exercise. The book establishes enough physical assurance to participate, appreciate skill and affect how safe another person feels near him. It gives no professional history as a fighter. [9.152–198, 10.575–592]

## The father who employs a substitute

Clarisse is visiting Japan for the first time. In her presence Mika explains that a threat has arrived against her life. Hikaru asks the two girls at the café to show his daughter something of the country while protecting her. When Chisato asks whether he really intends to continue his deal under threat, Hikaru raises a hand to answer for himself. The stakes, he says, amount to a few hundred thousand dollars in profit over seven years. He could have called it off for his daughter’s sake. Being threatened, however, makes him all the more determined to sign. Kurumi points out that a man who always defies a threat is as predictable as one who always yields. He appears to understand. He simply says he wants to be himself, 俺は俺らしくありたいのさ. Quietly amused assent from Mika gives way to silence when Kurumi asks the two men to keep other people out of the price of that self-image. [9.148–151, 9.210–243]

There are two audiences for that fine display of stubbornness. The reader initially sees it from Takina’s side. She finds the second briefing odd, and the timing of so much preparation in front of the person to be protected odder still. Once Mizuki drives away, Mika privately tells the staff the assignment is a performance. The company has traced the threatening message to Clarisse’s home computer. The adults understand she hopes to interrupt Hikaru’s negotiations and get the day with him. Rather than tell her that they have seen through her or behave as though a threat to her life deserved no response, he makes sure that she sees serious people accept the work. They are to take her out with the appearance of being her guards. The girl gets a novel experience and the dignity of being taken seriously, at least outwardly. Hikaru gets to work. These are benefits he can arrange simultaneously, and the one thing the girl expressly wants has dropped out of the arrangement. [9.202–213, 9.283–304, 9.470–479, 9.641–653]

Hikaru’s declaration of principle is consequently poor evidence that he would choose a contract over a daughter whose life he *believed* to be under threat. He knows more about the original message than he lets his audience see. Yet staging the entire morning to avoid cancelling also makes his resolve more than empty talk. Clarisse knows how often work comes first. When he kisses her cheek and charges Mika and the young women to look after her, she plays with the ends of her hair and calls this departure the usual thing. They do eat ramen together on other occasions. She bristles when Mizuki criticizes his driving. A father can be affectionate, known in close detail and defended by his daughter, and can still give her grounds to believe that only an emergency will command his time. [9.216–246, 9.313–346, 9.456–479, 9.641–652]

Hikaru’s first explanation also has a flaw that is independent of how much he loves her. He and the staff think that identifying a message as a child’s contrivance has disposed of the danger. Kurumi is later willing to question even the inference from the machine that originated it to the person who wrote it. More consequentially, Clarisse has made a second, real arrangement with two professionals that none of the adults grasped. Hikaru hired the café staff to act as though she needed guards. Clarisse hired Madame and Monsieur to attack those guards, with her own safety expressly stipulated. She tells the girls she wanted to punish or frighten her father and had counted on being able to call the whole thing off. Her confession finally explains why this supposed day of play put people in danger. The resemblance between father and daughter that amuses Mika at the end is uncomfortable here. Each had counted on a reaction from the other that could be produced by arranging the right scene. [9.641–653, 9.939–947, 10.674–712, 10.961–975]

## How far he trusts Mika

Out with Clarisse, Chisato briefly thinks that a man following the girls might be a second precaution Hikaru has taken. Takina privately considers that after ten years away he might no longer trust his old friend. Both are reasonable guesses given what the girls have seen. Once reached, Hikaru says that he put no watcher on anyone. Mika had his trust, so he saw no need. That answer fits Clarisse’s later admission that she engaged the professional pair. He has delegated one difficult matter and says he saw no need to put anyone else there to watch over it. Separately, when the people doing the work need him, his phone is off. Kurumi creates an apparent office emergency that prompts him to switch it on. The staff then reach him to check whose watcher it was. The report does not establish that he heard the full danger to Clarisse at that point. [9.700–722, 9.910–945, 10.9–14, 10.323–340, 10.674–685, 10.843–846]

When word finally gets through, he agrees to come at once. The deal appears already to have been signed. It would be easy to give Clarisse either the ideal answer or the worst one to the question she has been asking. What happens is more particular. A father who believed he had left his daughter with competent friends comes straight back once his office reaches him. How much he had been told about the danger remains unclear. The moment does not require him to forfeit an unsigned deal. Arriving at the café in a hurry, he has one short question, クラリスは？, “Clarisse?” It is Kurumi who answers. She judges the girl alive while acknowledging that her condition has yet to be confirmed. In Hikaru’s brief absence, the child he addressed that morning as お嬢ちゃん has become the person who can tell him most. [9.222–229, 10.323–340, 10.371–383]

Kurumi says she has identified the advance paid to the hired pair. It came from Hikaru’s personal account. That is important evidence about the money, and Kurumi turns it into an accusation about intent. After answering his urgent question about Clarisse, Kurumi asks whether he is disappointed to hear his daughter is alive. Mika tenses. Chisato’s voice interrupts the confrontation. What she has learned and what Clarisse later admits disentangle the two matters. Hikaru is the account holder. Clarisse arranged the attack on the guards. She later offers the remaining fee for cancellation, reports having transferred it and asks Madame to check. Madame says that she trusts her. Neither the first transfer’s source nor the second transfer’s reported completion supplies evidence of Hikaru authorizing an attack or personally settling the cancellation. Nor do we get his own response to his daughter’s use of the account. [10.373–393, 10.616–622, 10.650–656, 10.674–700, 10.763–768, 10.790–795]

The question about Hikaru’s intentions toward Kurumi is harder, and the innocence of the account holder on the first charge does little to answer it. As Kurumi turns toward Chisato’s voice, a much larger man reaches toward her so quickly that she freezes. Mika intervenes and restrains his friend. When the narration returns to the café, Hikaru taps Mika’s shoulder to be let up. He gets to his feet, straightens his suit and adjusts his collar. Asked when he took to laying hands on women and children, he says he only wanted to see what was on the computer. Mika replies that he sensed menace. Hikaru says anyone accused of trying to kill his own daughter would have given some of that off. He has both offered a narrower account of his purpose and conceded that his anger was perceptible. Kurumi’s frightened reaction needs no guess at his private thoughts to make it intelligible. The prose expressly leaves open whether all he would have done was look. [10.375–393, 10.575–598, 10.616–623, 10.961–964]

Why does the friend who knows him best lay hands on him first? Mika remembers a man capable of rushing in, going too far and on occasion entirely losing his sense of limits. He names no earlier offense. Hikaru gives a short, dry laugh, concedes the point and asks what he should do now. That dry laugh sounds different on the page from the smile that accompanied his insistence on being himself earlier that morning. Long familiarity gives Mika grounds to intervene and Hikaru grounds to take the intervention seriously. Mika can also admonish Kurumi for her accusatory words and suspicions, then later say he himself let Kurumi have a fright. Each judgment concerns a different act. The scene gives Hikaru no explicit apology to Kurumi. Nor does it establish that he attacked her. His capacity to frighten someone with less power survives the explanation of the financial evidence. [9.222–239, 10.575–598, 10.616–625, 10.961–964]

Kurumi tells Hikaru to stay where the girls can find him and let them finish their work. His face shows dissatisfaction. Mika puts a hand on his shoulder and asks whether Hikaru trusts him. Hikaru says that he trusts Mika to come through however unreasonable the request. Then extend that much trust to those girls, Mika tells him. All three will come back. Hikaru finds the children remarkable. Mika includes *his* child in the compliment, drawing a wry smile from Hikaru and a laugh from Kurumi. The trust changes direction. In the morning Hikaru tested the girls at Mika’s urging for Clarisse’s benefit. Now it is Hikaru who needs reassurance and the girls whose judgment must stand in for his. He makes a show of still being able to do something impressive himself, takes out his phone and leaves while making a call. The addressee is withheld. He does give the girls room to handle the crisis and turns his own energy elsewhere. The receiving end of that call cannot be recovered by reading the later outing backwards. [9.173–198, 10.598–615, 10.940–951]

## An evening he can still give her

Hikaru has something like a defense counsel in Mika and an unusually well-informed critic in his daughter. Clarisse thinks he named her after the red-haired Clarice Starling, whose taste in cars she associates with Hikaru’s. She suspects that one day he would like his daughter to drive the Mustang. Chisato proposes that he also chose a name whose meaning, light or radiance, could carry his own name into hers across languages. Mika broadly vouches for him. He has always made efforts other people do not see, he says. The same man gets wrapped up in things and lately the thing has been work. Clarisse observes that her father knows no Latin and sides with Takina’s objection that even a lovingly chosen name says little about the present. Hikaru’s early exchange of looks with Mika, followed by his compliment to Chisato, supports his pleasure in her recognition of the film reference. He does not personally give an account of every reason for the name. A friend’s explanation of his intent cannot settle the daughter’s experience of its effects. [9.156–167, 10.879–911, 10.916–931]

What he actually does is rent out a theme park after closing. Its costumed characters gather around him and the smiling girl who has flung her arms around him in a photograph that Chisato shows Takina. He has found an evening when no customer, business partner or competing obligation need take his attention away from her. His wealth, which had made a substitute excursion so easy, now lets him give them time together. The father says he had been preparing this treat in secret for some time, to make up for past absences. Mika told Takina, after Clarisse had gone, that Hikaru secured the booking using his money immediately after the emergency. Those are distinct accounts with distinct audiences. The book identifies the claim of advance preparation as false. The picture documents an embrace, and it gives the reader no access to a calendar or to Clarisse’s thoughts. [10.933–951]

Hikaru seems able to act on a difficult realization more freely than he can say when it came to him. Saying “I arranged this this afternoon” could have given his daughter evidence that she had reached him. The invented lead time offers something else: an assurance that he was thinking of her even when he seemed absorbed in himself. He might be trying to spare her the idea that only an emergency can win time with him. He might also want to spare himself the admission that this one did. He never chooses between those motives for us. The narrator first says Clarisse believed him, then qualifies the claim. Clarisse at least let those around her think she believed him and went smiling. She may know him well enough to let him tell the story he needs. Or she may simply welcome an explanation and an evening she wants. We learn of no new rule about his travel, conversation with his daughter about the hired pair, pledge of ordinary time together or permanent change to his priorities. The incident does establish that when he decides to change an evening, he does it on a scale few people could manage. [9.241–249, 9.470–479, 10.929–931, 10.940–951]

## His terms of address and the limits of prediction

Hikaru’s written Japanese has a companionable self-assurance. With Mika he dispenses with honorifics, opens with a familiar remark and turns bad coffee into a compliment his friend can enjoy. He can end a casual order to a small group with 娘を頼むよ as he entrusts his daughter to their care. The easy お嬢さん方 for the young women and お嬢ちゃん for Kurumi can sound courteous or diminishing depending who has to answer. He credits a young person’s perception and skill when he sees it. Kurumi becomes much harder for him to hear when she questions what kind of man he is. His formulations about 俺, “I,” cluster around precisely those moments when someone else would tell him how a man ought to act. When Hikaru comes hurrying back to the café, the bare question about Clarisse does the work of a whole speech. When Mika challenges his reach toward Kurumi, Hikaru counters with a question about the friend’s judgment of *him*, then asks Mika and Kurumi what to do. These are properties of the written exchanges. The novel offers no performed accent or vocal timing. [9.108–129, 9.159–179, 9.217–243, 10.371–386, 10.584–613]

His preferences have better evidence in some parts of life than others. The recurring car, the familiar movie reference, his appraisal of Mika’s coffee and Clarisse’s account of eating ramen with him give him interests and habits outside his business. His capacity to keep an old friendship over a decade is unusually well demonstrated. His romantic life after his daughter’s mother died is opaque. Mizuki takes his glance toward her elaborate outfit as interest in women. Takina privately thinks the outfit is simply conspicuous in a traditional café. The scene gives him no response to her wink. Mizuki’s enthusiasm about finding a husband, even her sudden eagerness to become Clarisse’s new mother, belongs to Mizuki. We know little about Hikaru’s education, daily life in either region where he works, specific old business with Mika, temperament with employees, or conduct as a partner. [9.92–126, 9.236–247, 9.313–346, 10.879–895]

The most reliable expectation for Hikaru is that he will act energetically on a problem as he understands it and will often try to let the beneficiary see only a finished solution. His maintained car, morning arrangement and after-hours park all support that reading. The analogy stops where another person has a say. When an old friend asks him to yield the immediate decision, Hikaru can give that friend considerable latitude. When a stranger accuses him of helping to hurt his daughter, he can forget what his own approach looks like from the far side of a counter. Evidence is strongest in those particular relationships. It is much weaker for a claim that he will always choose business first, always act violently under accusation, or always use money to avoid giving his time. The final picture matters because, on this occasion, he is there. Whether he can tell Clarisse how late he understood what she needed, without first arranging a scene that speaks for him, remains open. [9.108–126, 9.217–249, 10.335–340, 10.575–623, 10.940–950]
