# 『変身』 / *Henshin*
## Japanese-Primary Analytical Method and Reading Protocol v2.0
### Second-pass close reading for literary, linguistic, visual, ethical, and eromanga-form analysis

# 0. Purpose

This document defines the governing analytical method for the **Japanese-primary second pass** of ShindoL's 『変身』 / *Henshin*, commonly known in English as *Metamorphosis* or *Emergence*.

It supersedes the earlier English-source reading protocol **for the purposes of this Japanese edition reread**, while preserving the strongest parts of that method.

The earlier protocol was built around a real source limitation: the English edition preserved the work's plot, page sequence, visual grammar, chapter structure, broad semantic content, and causal progression, but it could not support rigorous claims about Saki's original Japanese voice. The older method therefore had to classify Japanese linguistic evidence as unavailable.

That limitation no longer applies.

The Japanese edition permits the project to analyze Saki's transformation simultaneously as:

- body;
- social presentation;
- self-conception;
- relationship structure;
- sexuality;
- agency;
- dependency;
- material security;
- substance use;
- future horizon;
- visual subjecthood;
- **and linguistic voice**.

The governing principle remains:

> **Treat 『変身』 simultaneously as a pornographic commodity, a serialized character narrative, and a tragedy. None of those identities should be allowed to explain the other two away.**

The analysis must not "rescue" the work as serious literature by pretending its pornographic form is merely packaging around a separate tragic story.

It must not dismiss the work as analytically empty because it is commercial adult manga.

The central methodological problem is still:

> **What happens when the same formal machinery is performing pornographic, characterological, and tragic work simultaneously?**

The Japanese second pass adds another governing question:

> **What does the original language reveal about continuity and change in Saki that the translated version could not securely establish?**

The method must remain adversarial enough to allow the strongest earlier interpretation to fail.

The purpose is not to prove that 『変身』 is profound.

The purpose is to determine, as rigorously as the primary source permits, what its:

- Japanese dialogue;
- serialized causality;
- visual transformation;
- eromanga grammar;
- character psychology;
- agency structure;
- reader positioning;
- and tragic architecture

actually do together.

- visual motifs;
- causal progression;
- relationship progression;
- narrative consequence.

When Japanese and English differ, the Japanese text governs claims about the original work.

# 2. Tier 2 — English translated edition

The English edition is a **comparative source**, not the primary text.

Use it selectively to identify cases where translation changes:

- agency;
- tone;
- intimacy;
- age/social register;
- coercive force;
- ambiguity;
- relationship labeling;
- degradation;
- emotional distance;
- self-conception;
- comic timing;
- or characterization.

Do **not** conduct an exhaustive line-by-line translation audit unless a later project explicitly requires one.

The purpose is to identify **analytically material divergence**, not stylistic difference for its own sake.

# 3. Tier 3 — Prior English-language project analysis

The previous chapter readings, synthesis, and character analyses are not discarded.

They become a **hypothesis archive**.

They may contain:

- strong visual readings;
- causal insights;
- agency distinctions;
- formal hypotheses;
- valuable counterreadings.

But no first-pass claim is protected from revision.

Each load-bearing first-pass conclusion should be eligible for one of these statuses:

```text
STRENGTHENED
WEAKENED
COMPLICATED
OVERTURNED
NEWLY_VISIBLE_IN_JAPANESE
STILL_UNDERDETERMINED
```

The Japanese edition should not be used merely to find confirming quotations for conclusions already reached.

# 4. Tier 4 — External genre, historical, or production evidence

External sources may later be used for:

- publication history;
- eromanga history;
- ShindoL interviews;
- production context;
- author annotations;
- Hard Edition drafts or deleted material;
- terminology;
- broader genre comparison.

They should **not** enter the prospective sequential reading unless explicitly required to establish something the Japanese pages cannot.

The sequential reread should remain primarily text-internal.

Paratext and production history belong mainly in the retrospective phase.

# III. Commercial and Genre Classification

『変身』 should be treated as **commercial eromanga**, not technically as dōjinshi.

The analysis may compare it with broader:

- H-manga;
- dōjin;
- corruption/degradation;
- gyaru-transformation;
- coercion;
- transactional-sex;
- and adult-manga visual grammars,

because those visual and narrative repertoires cross-pollinate.

But production categories should not be collapsed.

Maintain distinctions among:

- Japanese social reality;
- mainstream manga convention;
- commercial eromanga convention;
- dōjin convention;
- pornography cataloguing tags;
- ShindoL's individual formal choices.

Pornographic tags are not self-interpreting literary categories.

They may assist retrieval or comparison.

They do not prove meaning.

# IV. Evidence Classification Standard

Every chapter artifact and synthesis should distinguish the following evidence classes.

```text
TF = textual fact
VF = visual fact
CB = character belief
SI = strong inference
TI = thematic interpretation
EI = ethical interpretation
GI = genre/form inference
LI = linguistic interpretation
TR = translation-relevant observation
RC = retrospective correction
FP = first-pass claim
UA = unresolved ambiguity
SP = speculation
```

Optional confidence grades:

```text
HIGH
MEDIUM
LOW
```

Examples:

```text
[TF-HIGH]
[VF-HIGH]
[LI-MEDIUM]
[SI-MEDIUM]
[UA]
```

## 1. Textual fact

Directly supported by the Japanese words on the page.

Examples:

- Saki says a particular phrase.
- A character uses an imperative.
- Saki refers to herself with a particular first-person form.
- A price is stated.

## 2. Visual fact

Directly observable in the artwork.

Examples:

- Saki's face is absent from several consecutive erotic panels.
- a mirror occupies a compositionally privileged position;
- money is handed over;
- a phone is visible;
- a bodily mark changes.

## 3. Character belief

What a character says or appears to believe.

Character belief is not narrator-certified fact.

A manipulative character's explanation of Saki's situation should not be adopted automatically by the analysis.

## 4. Strong inference

The best explanation of repeated textual/visual evidence, while still not literally stated.

## 5. Thematic interpretation

A literary reading connecting repeated evidence to themes such as:

- recognition;
- transformation;
- self-authorship;
- visibility;
- subjecthood.

## 6. Ethical interpretation

A normative judgment concerning:

- autonomy;
- exploitation;
- coercion;
- responsibility;
- care;
- culpability.

Ethical claims must remain distinguishable from descriptive facts.

## 7. Genre/form inference

A claim that a scene activates a recognizable eromanga convention.

Such claims should be supported by actual formal features rather than merely by pornographic tags.

## 8. Linguistic interpretation

A claim derived from Japanese register, wording, syntax, address, pronouns, sentence endings, ellipsis, repetition, or speech-act structure.

## 9. Translation-relevant observation

A case where English and Japanese differ in a way that matters analytically.

## 10. Retrospective correction

A later-evidence revision of an earlier prospective interpretation.

## 11. Unresolved ambiguity

Used when the available evidence does not warrant choosing one interpretation.

## 12. Speculation

Plausible but weakly evidenced possibilities.

Speculation must never be silently upgraded to fact.

# VI. Spoiler-Blind Sequentiality and the Prospective-Reading Rule

The strongest part of the older method remains mandatory.

During the first chapter-by-chapter pass, interpretation must be **prospective rather than retrospective**.

The governing rule is:

> **At the end of Chapter N, interpret Saki and the story only from evidence available through Chapter N. Future events must not be used to explain, condemn, validate, or render inevitable choices that have not yet produced their later consequences.**

This is a methodological embargo, not literal ignorance.

The analyst may know the ending.

That knowledge may not be used as chapter-local evidence.

# 1. Future-event embargo

During the first pass through a chapter, do not use:

- later chapter events;
- later bodily changes;
- later relationship outcomes;
- later substance consequences;
- the finale;
- later explanatory dialogue;
- later motifs whose significance is not yet established;
- author interviews;
- Hard Edition annotations;
- deleted drafts;
- fandom commentary;
- later first-pass conclusions.

A chapter-level claim must be defensible at that chronological point.

# 2. Prospective Saki model

At each chapter boundary reconstruct:

- what Saki currently knows;
- what she currently believes;
- what she currently wants;
- what risks she can reasonably foresee;
- what alternatives she can currently perceive;
- what alternatives are objectively visible to the reader;
- what relationships she considers trustworthy;
- what material resources she has;
- what dependencies exist;
- what future she imagines;
- what the reader could reasonably predict from evidence so far.

The question is not:

> "Knowing the ending, why did Saki make this mistake?"

It is:

> **"Given what Saki knows, wants, fears, and can realistically do now, how intelligible is this choice now?"**

# 3. Versioned hypothesis ledger

After each chapter, record provisional hypotheses without rewriting older entries.

Each hypothesis should include:

- claim;
- evidence available so far;
- confidence;
- plausible alternatives;
- what would strengthen it;
- what would falsify it.

Later evidence should add a new revision entry.

Do not silently edit history.

# 4. Foreshadowing test

Call an earlier detail **foreshadowing** retrospectively only if it passes:

### Availability
Was the relevant signal actually present?

### Specificity
Did it meaningfully narrow possible future developments?

### Alternative-future test
Could the same detail fit several very different futures equally well?

If significance is created mostly by knowing the ending, classify it as:

- retrospective resonance;
- recontextualization;
- motif completion;
- hindsight patterning.

# 5. Inevitability-bias check

At every chapter ending ask explicitly:

> **Could Saki's life still plausibly have gone differently from here?**

Identify:

- live alternatives;
- accessible interventions;
- potentially repairable relationships;
- institutions that could still matter;
- reversible consequences;
- current dangers;
- dangers visible only in hindsight.

If the answer becomes "no" or "barely," specify **which conditions closed the alternatives**.

# 6. No retroactive moral simplification

Later catastrophe must not transform earlier ambiguity into simple judgment.

In particular:

- an earlier autonomous choice does not become non-autonomous merely because it later contributes to harm;
- an earlier pleasurable experience does not become "secretly bad all along" merely because the relationship later becomes exploitative;
- later coercion does not prove every earlier encounter was coercive;
- later addiction does not prove early intoxication was already full dependence;
- later tragedy does not erase Saki's genuine earlier desires.

Conversely:

- later evidence may reveal that manipulation, fraud, or unequal power already existed earlier but had not yet been legible.

Temporal precision matters more than moral tidiness.

# VII. Three-Pass Chapter Reading

The Japanese second pass should expand the older two-pass model into **three distinct close-reading passes**.

This is the largest procedural change in V2.

# Pass A — Prospective narrative and causal reading

Temporarily bracket eromanga genre interpretation and later knowledge.

Read the chapter as a story about Saki.

Record:

- what Saki wants;
- what she thinks is happening;
- what actually happens;
- what she chooses;
- what she refuses;
- what she agrees to;
- what she does not understand;
- what information she possesses;
- what relationships change;
- what material conditions change;
- what she gains;
- what she loses;
- what she believes she gained or lost;
- how her future horizon changes.

The purpose is to establish the **chapter-local human situation** before genre theory enters.

# Pass B — Japanese linguistic and speech-act reading

Return to the same chapter and read specifically for language.

Track Saki's:

- first-person reference;
- forms of address;
- honorific use;
- politeness level;
- sentence-final particles;
- contractions;
- ellipsis;
- hesitation;
- repetition;
- slang;
- youth register;
- feminine-coded forms where relevant;
- emotional intensifiers;
- laughter;
- embarrassment;
- refusal language;
- consent language;
- request language;
- apology language;
- gratitude;
- money vocabulary;
- substance vocabulary;
- sex/relationship vocabulary;
- future-oriented language;
- self-descriptions;
- evaluative adjectives;
- expressions of obligation;
- expressions of dependency;
- expressions of fear;
- expressions of affection.

For other characters, especially Hayato, track:

- imperative versus request forms;
- euphemism;
- normalization;
- reassurance;
- pressure;
- endearment;
- minimization;
- threat;
- economic framing;
- sexual framing;
- drug framing;
- future promises;
- semantic reframing of Saki's situation.

The key question is:

> **Who is linguistically allowed to define what is happening?**

# Pass C — Genre-aware visual/formal rereading

Now ask:

> **What commercial eromanga conventions are being activated, and what does their presence make the reader expect or consume?**

For explicit sequences identify:

- scenario grammar;
- escalation logic;
- body framing;
- visual shorthand;
- expression conventions;
- reader positioning;
- transformation/corruption coding;
- POV;
- repetition;
- novelty;
- panel rhythm;
- aftermath;
- relation between erotic payoff and cumulative consequence.

The goal is not to make the pornographic material disappear into abstraction.

The goal is to describe its **formal function without mechanically reproducing it**.

# Pass D — Chapter synthesis and freeze

After the three readings are complete:

- reconcile contradictions;
- update ledgers;
- identify strongest interpretation;
- identify strongest counterreading;
- list unresolved questions;
- record open alternatives;
- freeze the chapter.

The freeze must not later be rewritten.

# VIII. Japanese Linguistic-State Ledger

This is the major new evidence stream.

At each chapter boundary create a compact linguistic profile for Saki.

## 1. First-person and self-reference

Track:

- pronoun or name use;
- omission patterns;
- any meaningful shifts.

Do not assign significance to ordinary Japanese pronoun omission by itself.

Look for patterns.

## 2. Address and relationship terms

Track how Saki addresses:

- parents;
- female peers;
- male peers;
- Hayato;
- older adults;
- clients/strangers as relevant.

Changes in address may indicate:

- intimacy;
- distance;
- dependency;
- deference;
- hostility;
- performance.

## 3. Politeness and register

Track when Saki:

- uses polite forms;
- drops politeness;
- performs maturity;
- becomes deferential;
- becomes blunt;
- becomes linguistically passive;
- becomes strategically accommodating.

Do not assume polite speech equals consent.

Politeness can coexist with:

- discomfort;
- fear;
- social hierarchy;
- transactional performance.

## 4. Refusal capacity

Track how Saki says:

- no;
- stop;
- wait;
- maybe;
- I don't know;
- I can't;
- I don't want to;
- that's enough;
- conditional agreement.

Also track moments where refusal language disappears despite worsening conditions.

Absence can be meaningful only when contextualized.

## 5. Desire and pleasure language

Separate:

- explicit desire;
- curiosity;
- acquiescence;
- performative enthusiasm;
- intoxicated response;
- dependency-driven language;
- retrospectively rationalized language.

Do not infer inner desire solely from visual erotic expression conventions.

## 6. Money and material vocabulary

Track the language surrounding:

- cost;
- debt;
- payment;
- gifts;
- earning;
- owing;
- housing;
- survival.

Economic language may become part of Saki's changing self-understanding.

## 7. Substance language

Track:

- names;
- euphemisms;
- slang;
- promises about effects;
- risk language;
- normalization;
- need/craving language.

Do not identify a pharmacological substance more specifically than the Japanese text allows.

## 8. Future-oriented language

Track temporal horizon in the words themselves.

Examples of useful categories:

```text
LONG_TERM_FUTURE
NEAR_FUTURE
SHORT_TERM_SURVIVAL
IMMEDIATE_REWARD
NO_ARTICULATED_FUTURE
RESTORED_FUTURE_IMAGINATION
```

The future-horizon metric should therefore be **textual as well as thematic**.

## 9. Linguistic continuity test

At each phase ask:

> **Does late Saki still sound recognizably like earlier Saki?**

Possible findings:

- strong idiolect continuity;
- register adaptation without identity rupture;
- progressive lexical narrowing;
- increased social-performance language;
- increased dependency language;
- meaningful discontinuity;
- insufficient dialogue for confident judgment.

Do not assume that "transformation" requires linguistic transformation.

## Translation-audit questions

Ask:

- Does English make a request sound like a command or vice versa?
- Does English make Saki sound more assertive?
- less assertive?
- more mature?
- more sexually knowing?
- more degraded?
- more emotionally detached?
- Does the translation remove Japanese ambiguity?
- Does it add certainty about motive?
- Does it obscure politeness under pressure?
- Does it collapse relationship-specific address?
- Does it change who appears to author the meaning of an encounter?

Translation differences matter only when they alter interpretation.

Do not fetishize untranslatability.

# XI. Agency, Consent, Constraint, and Decision-Environment Authorship

The older method's four-category agency system should be expanded.

Avoid both simplifications:

> "Saki makes terrible choices."

and:

> "Saki has no agency."

For every consequential decision distinguish:

- physical action;
- expressed agreement;
- expressed desire;
- understood consequences;
- perceived alternatives;
- objective alternatives;
- realistically accessible alternatives;
- intoxication;
- dependency;
- social isolation;
- threat;
- violence;
- fraud;
- information asymmetry;
- affection manipulation;
- financial pressure;
- prior commitments;
- exit costs.

## Recommended classifications

```text
A1_AUTONOMOUS
A2_UNEQUAL_BUT_VOLUNTARY
A3_PRESSURED
A4_DEPENDENCY_SHAPED
A5_IMPAIRED
A6_BARGAINING_WITHIN_COERCION
A7_MATERIALLY_CONSTRAINED
A8_MANIPULATIVELY_ENGINEERED
A9_EXPLICITLY_COERCED
A10_NON_CHOICE
UA_UNRESOLVED
```

These are **analytical categories, not legal determinations**.

## Decision-environment authorship

For every major choice ask:

> **Who created the conditions under which this choice became salient, necessary, attractive, or apparently unavoidable?**

Potential environment authors include:

- Saki;
- Hayato;
- peers;
- customers;
- family;
- institutions;
- economic circumstances;
- substance dependence;
- cumulative prior decisions.

The most important distinction is:

> **local negotiation**
>
> versus
>
> **global authorship of the situation**.

A person may bargain successfully over terms while still lacking meaningful control over why the transaction is occurring at all.

## Agency revision rule

Later evidence may revise an earlier classification only if it reveals:

- hidden manipulation already active;
- previously unknown threat;
- fraud;
- intoxication;
- dependency;
- or other conditions actually present at the time.

Do not reclassify earlier agency simply because the outcome later becomes disastrous.

# XII. Substance, Intoxication, and Clinical Caution

Substance use is central but must be handled carefully.

## 1. Distinguish textual fact from pharmacological inference

Record:

- what the substance is called;
- what form it takes;
- what effects are depicted;
- what characters claim it does;
- how frequently it appears;
- what dependence language emerges.

If the manga does not name the drug, do not state a specific identification as fact.

Use formulations such as:

> "the depicted effects are consistent with..."

only when useful.

## 2. Intoxication and agency

Do not assume:

> intoxicated = no agency

or:

> responsive = valid consent.

Assess:

- degree of impairment;
- awareness;
- memory;
- initiation;
- refusal capacity;
- environmental pressure;
- dependence.

## 3. Addiction/dependence claims

Avoid diagnosing a formal substance-use disorder from manga panels alone.

The project may describe:

- escalating use;
- craving language;
- withdrawal-like behavior;
- prioritization;
- dependence;
- narrowing temporal horizon

when supported.

Clinical labels require greater caution.

# XIV. "Tag Accumulation" as Narrative Architecture

A central hypothesis remains:

> **『変身』 may transform the horizontal taxonomy of pornography into the vertical chronology of one person's life.**

Typical reset logic:

> scenario occurs → erotic payoff → heroine resets

Possible 『変身』 logic:

> scenario occurs → Saki changes → changed Saki enters next scenario → consequences accumulate.

The stronger formulation remains:

> **『変身』 converts the taxonomy of pornography into the chronology of tragedy.**

This is a hypothesis.

To test it, each chapter should record:

- what prior state makes the new scenario possible;
- what new state the scenario creates;
- what residue persists;
- whether later chapters genuinely depend on that residue.

If the work merely piles unrelated extremes together, the hypothesis weakens.

# XV. Serialization Pressure as Form

Each installment had to function as:

1. continuation of Saki's life;
2. commercially viable adult-manga installment.

Track for every chapter:

- opening state;
- narrative bridge into erotic premise;
- duration of erotic sequence;
- degree of novelty/escalation;
- aftermath;
- new status quo;
- hook into later material.

Then test:

> **Does the commodity requirement for renewed erotic novelty become one engine of Saki's narrative deterioration?**

A useful counterreading is that the tragedy may be partly an artifact of serial escalation rather than a fully organic causal design.

That possibility must remain open.

## Subject/object questions

Ask:

- How often is Saki's face visible?
- How often are her eyes visible?
- How often do we see her looking?

- How often are male reactions privileged?
- How often does the page privilege Saki's reaction?
- Does the panel sequence return to her subjectivity after erotic spectacle?

Do not assert reader complicity in advance.

Demonstrate the formal alignment.

# XVII. Erotic Legibility Versus Narrative Legibility

Every major explicit sequence should be read twice at the synthesis stage.

# As pornography

Ask:

- What is made visually salient?
- What erotic expectation is fulfilled?
- What novelty is offered?
- How are bodies framed?
- Which expression conventions appear?
- What rhythm of escalation/repetition is used?

# As narrative

Ask:

- What changes because this occurred?
- What does Saki understand afterward?
- What does she misunderstand?
- What new dependency exists?
- What relationship changes?
- What new material constraint appears?
- What belief changes?
- What future becomes more or less accessible?

The strongest scenes may be those where these functions conflict.

A scene can be visually organized as erotic spectacle while narratively marking a severe reduction in control.

Do not resolve that contradiction too quickly.

# XVIII. Institutional Presence and Absence

Track:

- parents;
- teachers;
- classmates;
- friends;
- medical systems;
- law enforcement;
- social services;
- reproductive healthcare;
- employers;
- shelters;
- bystanders.

For each institution distinguish:

1. present and helpful;
2. present and ineffective;
3. present but inaccessible;
4. avoided by Saki;
5. absent from Saki's social reality;
6. merely absent from the depicted narrative.

Do not infer:

> "no help existed"

solely because the story does not depict help.

This distinction is essential to the **misery-machine objection**.

Ask:

> Does the story causally establish why support becomes inaccessible?

or:

> Does it simply remove support because catastrophe requires it?

Track how far Saki can imagine herself existing.

# XX. Metamorphosis Versus Continuity

Never assume that visual or social transformation equals replacement of personhood.

At every chapter identify continuities.

Potential recurring traits may include:

- desire for acceptance;
- loneliness;
- romantic imagination;
- eagerness to please;
- sensitivity to praise;
- curiosity;
- embarrassment;
- desire to feel mature;
- hope;
- adaptability.

The central continuity question is:

> **What remains Saki when almost every external condition around Saki changes?**

The Japanese voice now provides a new way to test this.

Possible outcome:

> outward discontinuity + linguistic/psychological continuity.

Possible alternative:

> meaningful linguistic discontinuity indicating changing self-performance.

Neither should be assumed.

---

# XXI. Erotic Transformation Versus Self-Authorship

The early makeover must not be coded automatically as the first step of moral degradation.

At each change ask:

> **Did Saki choose this because it expresses the person she wants to become, or because recognition has become conditional on performing another person's desire?**

Classify authorship where useful:

```text
SELF_AUTHORED
CO_AUTHORED
SOCIAL_PERFORMANCE
RELATIONSHIP_CONDITIONED
ECONOMICALLY_INCENTIVIZED
DEPENDENCY_SHAPED
COERCED
SURVIVAL_DRIVEN
UA
```

The body is a contested authorship surface.

It is not a moral scoreboard.

# XXII. Relationship and Interpretive-Authority Audit

For every important relationship ask two different questions.

## Material authority

Who controls:

- money;
- housing;
- substances;
- transport;
- employment;
- access to clients;
- physical safety;
- social connections?

## Interpretive authority

Who gets to tell Saki:

- what happened;
- what it means;
- whether something is normal;
- whether she wanted it;
- whether she owes someone;
- what kind of person she has become;
- what her future should be?

This distinction is especially important because exploitation can proceed through **semantic control** as well as direct force.

Track moments where Saki begins to adopt another person's framing of her own behavior.

Do not assume adoption equals sincere belief.

# XXIII. Three Adversarial Readings

Every major thesis should survive three hostile tests inherited from the older method.

# Test A — "It is simply pornography"

Assume the primary purpose of explicit sequences is arousal.

Ask:

> **Does the literary reading explain anything that the pornographic explanation does not?**

If not, the analysis may be overreading.

# Test B — "It is simply cautionary melodrama"

Assume Saki is moved through an implausibly comprehensive catalogue of disasters.

Ask:

> **Does the causal architecture actually make the escalation persuasive?**

If not, extremity may be substituting for depth.

# Test C — "The work critiques what it eroticizes"

Ask:

- Does the work create formal distance from exploitation?
- Does aftermath alter erotic meaning?
- Does it condemn objectification while reproducing it?
- Is tragedy a critique?
- an intensifier?
- an alibi?
- an unstable mixture?

Do not demand a clean answer.

## Added V2 adversarial test — "The Japanese does not materially change the earlier reading"

Because the second pass is Japanese-primary, it is easy to overstate linguistic novelty.

Ask:

> **Does the Japanese actually revise interpretation, or merely make an already sound reading more precise?**

Not every untranslated particle is analytically important.

The second pass should distinguish:

- materially new insight;
- finer characterization;
- confirmation;
- irrelevant linguistic detail.

# XXV. Handling Explicit Sexual Material

The analysis must not skip explicit scenes merely because they are explicit.

Doing so would remove much of the work's formal structure.

But literary analysis should not mechanically reproduce anatomy or pornographic detail.

Focus on:

- initiation;
- control;
- consent;
- refusal;
- gaze;
- power;
- intoxication;
- escalation;
- affect;
- framing;
- repetition;
- consequence;
- changed relationship;
- changed material conditions.

Where a specific visual convention matters, describe the formal device.

Examples:

- body fragmentation;
- POV positioning;
- expression coding;
- impossible-perspective framing;
- sectional/cutaway imagery;
- repetition patterns;
- transformation markers;
- emphasis on bodily evidence over interior response.

The analytical question is:

> **What does the formal choice do?**

not:

> "How graphically can the scene be restated?"

# XXVI. Clinical, Legal, and Ethical Boundary Rules

## 1. Clinical claims

Do not diagnose:

- depression;
- PTSD;
- dissociation;
- addiction disorder;
- personality disorder

unless the work explicitly provides a diagnosis or external clinical analysis is later introduced.

Describe observable patterns first.

## 2. Legal claims

Do not convert literary agency categories automatically into legal conclusions.

If legal classification becomes relevant, it requires jurisdiction-specific research outside this textual protocol.

## 3. Ethical claims

Clearly distinguish:

- causal responsibility;
- moral culpability;
- foreseeability;
- exploitation;
- failure to help;
- deliberate harm.

Multiple actors may contribute causally without bearing equal responsibility.

## 4. Sexual agency

Avoid both:

- puritanical assumption that sexual experimentation is itself degradation;
- libertarian simplification that any verbal assent exhausts the autonomy question.

The method's concern is **authorship under changing conditions**.

# XXVII. First-Pass Revision Protocol

The second pass should explicitly test the prior English analysis.

For each major first-pass claim:

1. restate it neutrally;
2. identify its original evidentiary basis;
3. inspect relevant Japanese passages;
4. inspect relevant visual evidence again;
5. identify supporting evidence;
6. identify counterevidence;
7. assign revision status;
8. record final formulation.

Recommended table:

| Claim ID | First-pass formulation | Japanese evidence | Visual evidence | Counterevidence | Status | Revised formulation |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|

Do not make revision performative.

If the English reading was correct, say so.

The goal is accuracy, not novelty.

# XXXI. Finale Protocol

Analyze the finale prospectively first.

# XXXIII. Core Hypotheses to Test

The following are **hypotheses**, not conclusions.

## Hypothesis 1 — Taxonomy becomes chronology

> **『変身』 converts the taxonomy of pornography into the chronology of tragedy.**

Test through chapter-to-chapter residue.

## Hypothesis 2 — Transformation changes authors

> **The deepest change is not that Saki stops transforming, but that authorship over transformation progressively moves away from her.**

Test through body, language, money, dependency, and decision environments.

## Hypothesis 3 — Outward discontinuity, inward continuity

> **Saki's external identities change radically while key desires remain continuous.**

The Japanese voice now provides a stronger test.

## Hypothesis 4 — Recognition becomes vulnerability

> **The ability to become socially visible initially liberates Saki but also makes her increasingly legible to people who can exploit the need underlying that visibility.**

## Hypothesis 5 — Choice survives longer than authorship

> **Saki continues making local choices after meaningful control over the larger conditions of choice has begun collapsing.**

## Hypothesis 6 — Pornographic gaze and tragic subjecthood remain unstable

> **The manga preserves Saki as a narratively significant subject while repeatedly rendering her body available through an objectifying erotic grammar.**

The work may critique and reproduce the same gaze.

## Hypothesis 7 — Japanese voice will refine more than overturn

Because the manga is visually driven, the Japanese may:

- sharpen;
- qualify;
- and longitudinally map Saki

without necessarily overturning the first pass's major structural conclusions.

This should be tested rather than assumed.

# XXXIV. Strong Counterreadings Required in Final Analysis

At minimum preserve:

## Counterreading A — Pornographic primacy

The tragedy is secondary to the work's commercial erotic function.

## Counterreading B — Misery machine

The causal chain is less organic than it appears because the story continually removes plausible exits and adds new catastrophe.

## Counterreading C — Moralistic corruption tale

The work may reproduce a conservative causal fantasy in which female sexual experimentation is narratively punished.

## Counterreading D — Reader indictment is overstated

Objectifying visual grammar may be ordinary pornography rather than deliberate self-critique.

## Counterreading E — Saki continuity is overread

Apparent continuity may partly reflect the analyst's desire to rescue a stable "real Saki" behind changing behavior.

## Counterreading F — Institutional absence is narrative convenience

The world may be less an intentional analysis of failed care than a deliberately evacuated stage built to permit escalation.

## Counterreading G — Linguistic change is ordinary contextual register shift

Not every Japanese voice change reflects identity transformation.

The final synthesis should answer these counterreadings rather than suppress them.

# XL. Final Governing Principle

The Japanese second pass should preserve a difficult balance.

Do not romanticize Saki into a purely passive victim.

Do not moralize her sexuality into a fall from purity.

Do not reduce her choices to proof of free authorship.

Do not erase the pornographic function of the work in order to make the tragedy respectable.

Do not erase the tragedy in order to make the pornography analytically simple.

Do not assume Japanese language must overturn the English reading merely because it is primary.

Instead reconstruct, as precisely as possible:

- what Saki wants;
- how she speaks;
- how she changes;
- what she continues to value;
- what she can choose;
- what conditions other people create around those choices;
- what the manga asks the reader to see;
- and what remains of Saki as a subject while her life becomes increasingly authored from outside.

The core second-pass question is:

> **How does 『変身』 depict the movement from self-authored transformation to externally authored life, and what does the original Japanese reveal about the continuity of the person living through those changes?**

Everything else in the method exists to keep the answer evidentiary rather than predetermined.
