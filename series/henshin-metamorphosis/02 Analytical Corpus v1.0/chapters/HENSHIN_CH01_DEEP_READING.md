# 『変身』 / *Henshin*
## Chapter 1 Deep Reading
### Learning to be seen: self-authored transformation, unstable recognition, and the first contest over interpretive authority

## 1. Scope and evidentiary boundary

This artifact analyzes **Chapter 1 only**, corresponding to printed pages **003–030** and CBZ images **JP_CBZ_IMG_0006–JP_CBZ_IMG_0033**.

Nothing from Chapter 2 or later is used to establish Chapter 1's local meaning.

The reading follows the governing V2 protocol:

1. prospective narrative and causal reading;
2. Japanese linguistic and speech-act reading;
3. genre-aware visual/formal rereading;
4. synthesis and immutable chapter freeze.

The earlier English-language analysis is consulted only **after** the Japanese chapter has been read independently. It is treated as a hypothesis archive rather than authority.

Because Saki has just completed middle school and enters high school during this chapter, the sexual material involves a minor. This analysis therefore discusses sexual events only at the level necessary for literary, agency, and formal analysis. It does not reproduce graphic sexual content.

---

# 2. Chapter-local thesis

The Japanese Chapter 1 strengthens the core insight of the first pass:

> **Saki's first metamorphosis is genuinely self-authored. The danger introduced by Chapter 1 is not transformation itself, nor sexuality itself, but the speed with which Saki begins using other people's reactions as evidence for whether the transformed self deserves love.**

The Japanese makes that structure unusually clear.

Saki begins by saying, in effect:

> **「変わりたい」 — I want to change.**

She changes because she dislikes the defensive, isolated person she has become.

The transformation initially works.

It produces:

- renewed intimacy with her mother;
- unexpected warmth from her father;
- successful contact with female classmates;
- plans for ordinary adolescent friendship;
- increased confidence;
- and the discovery that presentation can alter how the social world responds to her.

The chapter therefore cannot responsibly be read as:

> makeover → corruption.

The makeover is not the fall.

The chapter's first structural problem appears when Saki begins to interpret **recognition itself as proof of personal worth**.

The Japanese gives us one of the clearest formulations:

> **「カワイイって言われるの気持ちいいし……なんか、自分が好きになれそう！」**

A restrained working translation is:

> Being told I'm cute feels good... somehow I feel like I might be able to like myself.

The grammar matters.

`好きになれそう` is not stable self-love.

It is a possibility produced by another person's response.

Recognition has become epistemic:

> **someone sees me positively → perhaps I am someone I can value.**

Hayato enters exactly at the point when Saki has discovered this new way of knowing herself.

His most consequential power in Chapter 1 is therefore not simply sexual access.

It is **interpretive authority**.

He repeatedly supplies Saki with explanations of:

- what kind of girl she is;
- what her behavior means;
- what she wanted;
- whether an unfamiliar behavior is normal;
- whether she is desirable;
- whether she is loved.

The Japanese second pass makes this contest over interpretation even clearer than the English pass did.

# 3. Narrative reconstruction

## March 8: graduation

Chapter 1 begins on **March 8**, at Saki's middle-school graduation.

Saki states that she has gone through all three years without making even one friend.

This is not presented as a simple case of peers refusing her.

The chapter immediately shows that she had previously been invited to join others.

Her response was closer to:

> **「あっ 私はいい……よ」**

She turned invitations down.

Her internal explanation is that she was frightened that she would be mocked if she joined.

That distinction is foundational.

Saki's isolation is partly imposed by social fear and partly maintained as self-protection.

After enough time alone, the defense hardens into contempt.

She begins mentally looking down on the groups around her.

Then she notices what has happened:

> **「気付けば そんな自分が嫌いになっていた」**

She has come to dislike the person she has become.

The tears that follow are therefore not merely grief over being lonely.

They mark the collapse of a defensive identity.

She has spent years telling herself she does not need the social world and suddenly recognizes that this stance is no longer protecting a self she wants to inhabit.

Her conclusion is concise:

> **「変わりたい……」**

She wants to change.

## Home: transformation begins as relational repair

Saki goes home and asks her mother to teach her makeup.

The request is self-initiated.

Her mother responds warmly and practically.

This is important because the first transformation is not produced by:

- a boyfriend;
- a bully;
- a fashion-conscious peer;
- a pornographic customer;
- or an exploitative adult.

Saki herself identifies a problem in how she is living and seeks help from someone she trusts.

Her mother's role is collaborative rather than dominating.

The makeover also reopens family intimacy.

Saki's father, whose emotional distance is immediately legible from her response to him, reacts positively to her changed appearance.

Saki is visibly embarrassed, but internally pleased.

The first reward for transformation is therefore not sexual attention.

It is **family recognition**.

## April 7: high-school entrance

The story then jumps to **April 7**, Saki's first day of high school.

Visually, she is transformed:

- no braids;
- no heavy framing glasses;
- styled hair;
- makeup;
- a much more conventionally glamorous school presentation.

Linguistically, however, the old Saki remains strikingly present.

During her self-introduction she begins:

> **「趣味は……読書とゲー……」**

She is about to say that her hobbies are reading and games.

Then she self-corrects:

> **「えっと……買い物です」**

She substitutes shopping.

This is one of Chapter 1's most important language moments.

Saki has not simply improved her appearance.

She has created an imagined category of **ordinary girlhood** and is trying to perform it.

Her internal reaction when classmates whisper makes this explicit:

> **「バレた……？ 見ただけ 普通の人を装っているって……」**

The key phrase is:

> **「普通の人を装っている」**

approximately:

> pretending / disguising myself as an ordinary person.

This is stronger than merely saying Saki feels awkward.

She does not yet experience the new social persona as transparent expression of a newly confident self.

She experiences it partly as **disguise**.

Her immediate fear is that others will see through it.

She wonders whether the whole transformation has failed and whether someone like her can really do this.

The makeover has therefore produced:

- expanded possibility;
- increased visual legibility;
- and impostor anxiety.

These states coexist.

## Female classmates: the transformation genuinely succeeds

Two girls invite Saki to go out with them.

She initially hesitates:

> **「えっ……あっ……うん……行く……」**

The hesitation is still recognizably the old Saki.

But she goes.

They have an ordinary outing.

They make plans to meet again on a day off.

Saki is surprised by the social form itself.

She recognizes that this is the thing she has watched other students do:

> gathering with friends.

The scene matters because it establishes a nonsexual, nonexploitative consequence of transformation before Hayato appears.

Saki has not merely become visible to men.

She has become easier for same-age female peers to approach.

At the Chapter 1 horizon, the makeover has therefore opened at least two different futures:

> **ordinary peer friendship**

and

> **predatory adult attention**.

Neither has yet displaced the other.

## The fashion magazine: social competence is something Saki studies

Wanting to keep up with her new peers, Saki goes to buy a fashion magazine.

Her internal monologue shows that she still does not naturally inhabit the world represented by it.

The models seem extremely cute.

The clothing seems elaborate.

She is uncertain about the cultural grammar.

This is important.

The new Saki is not a revealed fashion enthusiast.

She is a student of social fluency.

Fashion functions almost as a textbook:

> if socially successful girls know this language, perhaps I should learn it too.

## Hayato: adult recognition arrives after peer recognition

Hayato approaches Saki in the bookstore.

Saki immediately frames him internally as an **adult man**.

That phrase is important because the Japanese text itself establishes the age/status asymmetry without requiring outside inference.

He compliments her intensely.

Saki initially assumes she is being mistaken for someone more sophisticated than she is.

Her internal response moves quickly from surprise to the realization that this may be a pickup attempt.

Then comes a key sequence of thought:

> **「うぅ……褒められるって恥ずかしいんだなぁ……」**

followed by:

> **「この人……私のことカワイイって思ってるのかなぁ……」**

and finally:

> **「カワイイって言われるの気持ちいいし……なんか、自分が好きになれそう！」**

Recognition is already altering self-evaluation.

The sentence does not mean Saki is simply vain.

It means she has almost no stable evidence base for believing she is socially valuable.

The changed reactions of:

- mother;
- father;
- female classmates;
- Hayato

are all teaching her the same broad lesson:

> **the world responds differently to the changed Saki.**

That lesson is true.

The danger is that it does not yet contain a reliable method for distinguishing supportive recognition from exploitative recognition.

## Karaoke: Saki experiences social success before chemical impairment

Saki goes with Hayato to karaoke.

Before alcohol or the unidentified pill enter, she is already experiencing the encounter as a major social breakthrough.

A particularly important line is:

> **「本当はつまらない人だと思われないようにしないと」**

roughly:

> I have to make sure he doesn't think I'm actually boring.

This is the same impostor structure from the classroom.

The new appearance has generated social opportunities, but Saki still believes those opportunities may depend upon successfully performing a more interesting person than she thinks she is.

Yet she also begins genuinely enjoying herself.

She reflects that:

- he sings well;
- he makes her laugh;
- she had not known interacting with another person could be this enjoyable;
- she had not known she herself could talk with someone this much.

Then comes another central sentence:

> **「私……変われたの……かな……」**

> Have I... changed?

Again, the Japanese does not give us triumphant certainty.

It gives us a question.

Saki is experiencing evidence of change but still needs the world to confirm what that change means.

## Alcohol: asymmetry becomes practical

When alcohol appears, Saki asks:

> **「お酒……ですか？」**

The polite form matters.

Her language toward Hayato remains deferential and uncertain.

Hayato's speech toward her is much more casual.

He minimizes the alcohol as essentially like juice and encourages her to try it.

This is the first clear example of him using his greater confidence and social experience to define risk for her.

Saki drinks.

The chapter represents her as increasingly impaired.

## The unidentified pill: attempted refusal becomes explicit textual evidence

The pill sequence is the most important agency clarification produced by the Japanese edition.

Hayato describes the substance as something that will make her feel good and normalizes it as something everyone does.

Saki is unfamiliar with it.

Most importantly, her internal narration states:

> **「あれ……今断りたかったのに……出来なかった……」**

A restrained translation:

> Huh...? I wanted to refuse just now... but I couldn't.

This is stronger evidence than any broad summary.

It tells us that:

- Saki identifies an internal wish to refuse;
- the refusal is not successfully enacted;
- and she notices that failure herself.

The chapter does not require us to infer reluctance solely from facial expression or later regret.

The Japanese gives us the counterfactual desire directly.

## Sexual escalation: pleasure and impaired autonomy are both present

After intoxication, Hayato escalates the encounter.

Saki expresses verbal hesitation.

She notes that they have only just met.

Hayato responds by pressing forward rather than treating the hesitation as a stopping point.

The analytical structure here must remain precise.

The manga also depicts Saki as:

- curious;
- increasingly physically responsive;
- capable of pleasure;
- and emotionally excited by being desired.

Those facts are real within the chapter.

They do not cancel:

- intoxication;
- attempted refusal of the pill;
- unequal experience;
- Saki's repeated uncertainty;
- direct pressure;
- and Hayato's control over how the encounter is being interpreted.

The chapter therefore gives us neither:

> Saki freely chooses everything

nor:

> Saki has no agency at all.

It gives us **progressively degraded conditions for agency**.

## "Even if it's a lie": recognition becomes more important than truth

At the point where Saki continues to hesitate, Hayato frames his desire through affection.

Saki's internal response is one of the most revealing in the chapter.

She recognizes that the claim may be false.

Then she thinks, in effect:

> **「ウソでも嬉しい……!!」**

> Even if it's a lie, I'm happy.

This line strengthens the first-pass recognition thesis substantially.

Saki is not simply fooled because she cannot imagine deception.

For a moment, she explicitly imagines deception.

The emotional value of hearing that she is wanted is greater than the immediate need to determine whether the declaration is true.

This matters because Chapter 1 has already established the relevant wound.

Saki wanted:

- friends;
- ordinary participation;
- approval;
- evidence that she could become someone worth being around.

Within a short span, Hayato supplies compressed versions of all of those needs:

- you are attractive;
- you are interesting enough to spend time with;
- I want you;
- I care about you.

The risk is therefore not stupidity.

It is that Saki has just discovered recognition as a powerful regulator of self-worth and has almost no experience evaluating the motives of the people providing it.

## Aftermath: the objective relationship and Saki's imagined relationship diverge

The end of the chapter creates a severe interpretive gap.

Hayato's behavior is not presented as ordinary boyfriend formation.

He:

- registers his contact information;
- treats a future encounter casually;
- records an image of Saki while she is still impaired;
- leaves her behind;
- and does not establish mutual relational expectations.

Saki only fully regains her situational awareness after an employee enters.

The Japanese uses:

> **「我に返った」**

she comes back to herself / regains her senses.

Even while walking home, she says:

> **「まだ頭がふわふわする……」**

her head is still woozy.

Then she receives a message from the man.

Only at this point does she register his name:

> **「ハヤトって言うんだ……」**

That is a remarkably stark relationship fact.

Saki has gone through the entire encounter before properly learning his name.

Yet she immediately interprets the message through the relational framework she wants:

> perhaps this means she has a boyfriend now.

This is not simply Hayato imposing a fantasy.

Saki participates in constructing it.

That distinction will be important later.

Hayato supplies:

- desire;
- attention;
- contact;
- suggestive future availability.

Saki supplies:

> **romantic coherence.**

# 4. Saki at chapter opening

At graduation, Saki is:

- socially isolated;
- accustomed to declining invitations;
- frightened of ridicule;
- defensive about other people's sociability;
- unhappy with the person that defensive contempt has produced;
- still living with both parents;
- still capable of asking her mother for help;
- not materially precarious;
- not chemically dependent;
- not romantically attached;
- not cut off from institutions.

The most important opening fact is that Saki's problem is **not an absence of desire**.

She wants connection.

She has been protecting herself from the risk required to obtain it.

Her first transformation therefore emerges from frustrated ordinary desire, not from deviance.

# 5. What Saki wants

Chapter 1 gives us a surprisingly coherent hierarchy.

Saki wants:

1. to stop hating the defensive person she has become;
2. to become capable of ordinary social participation;
3. to make friends;
4. to feel like a normal high-school girl;
5. to be liked;
6. to become someone she herself can like.

Sexual experience enters later.

It is not the chapter's initiating motive.

This point should remain frozen because later readings may be tempted to reinterpret Chapter 1 through a "sexual corruption" framework.

The Japanese does not support that as the starting psychology.

# 6. What Saki knows and believes

By the time she meets Hayato, Saki has learned several things.

## What she knows

- changing presentation changes other people's responses;
- her mother can help her;
- her father can respond warmly;
- female classmates can like her;
- she can participate in ordinary peer outings;
- she still lacks fashion fluency;
- she can talk to a stranger more successfully than she expected;
- Hayato is an adult man;
- alcohol is alcohol;
- the pill is unfamiliar;
- she wants to refuse the pill;
- she is becoming impaired.

## What she does not know

- Hayato's intentions;
- whether his affection is sincere;
- how much risk the pill carries;
- what kind of future he imagines with her;
- how to distinguish pickup behavior from relationship formation;
- whether being desired is the same as being cared for.

## What she increasingly believes

- the changed Saki works;
- being called cute can help her like herself;
- successful social interaction is evidence she really can change;
- Hayato's attention may signify romance;
- physical intimacy may signify emotional selection.

The key epistemic vulnerability is:

> **Saki is using social response to answer questions about self-worth and relationship status that the social response alone cannot reliably answer.**

# 7. Causal developments

Chapter 1 creates several durable conditions.

1. Saki discovers self-transformation as a practical tool.
2. Family relationships temporarily improve.
3. Female peer relationships become possible.
4. Saki begins performing a "normal" persona.
5. External recognition becomes tied to self-evaluation.
6. Hayato gains contact access to her.
7. Saki experiences alcohol.
8. Saki experiences an unidentified recreational substance.
9. Hayato establishes himself as someone who can define unfamiliar experiences for her.
10. Saki gains sexual experience under impaired and pressured conditions.
11. Hayato records an image of her while impaired.
12. Saki leaves believing the encounter may represent boyfriend formation.

The chapter does **not** yet establish:

- financial dependence;
- housing dependence;
- established chemical dependence;
- estrangement from family;
- school withdrawal;
- loss of female peers;
- a coercive contract;
- social entrapment.

Risk has increased.

Destiny has not been established.

# 8. Japanese voice and speech-act analysis

The Japanese second pass's largest Chapter 1 gain is that Saki's visual transformation is **not matched by a sudden linguistic transformation**.

She continues to sound like the same uncertain girl.

## First-person reference

Saki uses ordinary **私 / watashi**.

Nothing in Chapter 1 suggests that the makeover comes with a new performative first-person persona.

This is modest evidence, but useful.

The external visual change is dramatic.

The first-person self remains stable.

## Ellipsis and self-interruption

Saki's speech and thought repeatedly use:

- ellipsis;
- interrupted phrases;
- self-correction;
- questions to herself.

Examples include:

> 「趣味は……読書とゲー……えっと……買い物です」

and:

> 「私……変われたの……かな……」

This linguistic uncertainty survives the makeover.

## "Pretending to be ordinary"

`普通の人を装っている` is one of the strongest Japanese-specific refinements.

`装う` carries the sense of:

- putting on;
- presenting;
- disguising;
- assuming an appearance.

Saki therefore does not merely worry that she is awkward.

She conceptualizes ordinary social identity as something she is **wearing**.

This makes the title's transformation logic visible very early:

> the new appearance may be self-authored, but the imagined standard of "ordinary" comes from outside her.

## Politeness asymmetry with Hayato

Saki's speech toward Hayato remains more polite and hesitant than his speech toward her.

She uses forms such as:

- 「すいません」
- 「お酒……ですか？」

while Hayato immediately adopts casual familiarity.

This asymmetry matters because it reinforces:

- stranger versus familiar;
- teenager versus adult;
- socially inexperienced versus socially confident.

It should not be overread as proof of coercion by itself.

But it is part of the decision environment.

## The language of change remains interrogative

Saki never simply declares:

> I transformed successfully.

The important line is:

> 「私……変われたの……かな……」

The `かな` matters.

She is asking.

Her own transformation remains something she is verifying through other people's responses.

## Refusal language

The pill sequence is decisive:

> 「今断りたかったのに……出来なかった……」

This contains:

- a desire to refuse;
- awareness of that desire;
- awareness that the refusal did not become action.

That is unusually strong evidence for the agency ledger.

## Love and truth

`ウソでも嬉しい` is equally important.

Saki knows truth may be uncertain.

She chooses emotional meaning over certainty.

That is not simple gullibility.

It is a recognition hunger strong enough to tolerate ambiguity.

## End-of-chapter register

Saki's final thoughts remain tentative and romantic.

She does not emerge from the encounter speaking as a hardened or sexually transformed persona.

She is still trying to answer:

> what does this relationship mean?

That strongly supports **linguistic continuity** across Chapter 1.

# 9. Agency, consent, and decision-environment audit

The V2 taxonomy is most useful when applied event by event rather than assigning one label to the whole encounter.

| Event | Primary classification | Secondary factors | Analytical note |
|---|---|---|---|
| Deciding she wants to change | `A1_AUTONOMOUS` | emotional distress | Self-initiated response to a life she dislikes. |
| Asking mother to teach makeup | `A1_AUTONOMOUS` | supportive collaboration | Clear self-authorship. |
| Editing hobbies during self-introduction | `A2_UNEQUAL_BUT_VOLUNTARY` | perceived peer norm | Social-performance pressure, not interpersonal coercion. |
| Accepting female classmates' invitation | `A1_AUTONOMOUS` | nervousness | Ordinary voluntary social expansion. |
| Buying fashion magazine | `A1_AUTONOMOUS` | desire to fit in | Self-chosen competence-building. |
| Talking with Hayato / going to karaoke | `A2_UNEQUAL_BUT_VOLUNTARY` | adult/teen asymmetry, flattery | Saki chooses to continue interaction. |
| Drinking alcohol | `A3_PRESSURED` / `A8_MANIPULATIVELY_ENGINEERED` | risk minimization | Hayato defines it as essentially harmless and encourages compliance. |

| Initial sexual escalation | `A3_PRESSURED` + `A5_IMPAIRED` | direct verbal hesitation, intoxication | Saki's uncertainty is not treated as a reason to stop. |
| Continued participation | `UA` across mixed states | pleasure, impairment, emotional need, pressure | Positive affect is real but does not erase degraded autonomy. |
| Interpreting later message as boyfriend formation | `A1` at level of interpretation | severe information deficit | Saki authors romantic meaning from weak evidence. |

## Who authored the decision environment?

### Saki authors

- the original makeover;
- the choice to enter peer social life;
- the choice to study fashion;
- the choice to continue speaking with Hayato;
- the hopeful romantic interpretation at the end.

### Peers and social norms author

- the imagined category of ordinary girlhood;
- the fashion-performance standard Saki thinks she must learn.

### Hayato increasingly authors

- the private location;
- the risk framing around alcohol;
- the normalization of the unidentified pill;
- the pace of sexual escalation;
- the interpretation of Saki's physical reactions;
- the claim that her behavior reveals a sexual identity;
- the affectionate framing used to overcome hesitation.

Chapter 1 therefore ends with a new distinction:

> **Saki still has substantial behavioral and strategic agency, but Hayato has demonstrated that he can shape the informational and emotional environment in which some of those choices occur.**

That is risk.

It is not yet total capture.

# 10. Relationships, power, and interpretive authority

## Mother

Saki's mother is the healthiest relationship depicted in Chapter 1.

She:

- responds when asked;
- teaches rather than dictates;
- helps Saki pursue Saki's own goal;
- takes pleasure in renewed closeness.

She is evidence that Saki begins with a real care pathway.

## Father

The father's role is smaller, but his praise matters disproportionately.

Saki's internal pleasure suggests that paternal recognition has emotional scarcity value.

The chapter does not yet establish abuse or hostility.

At this local horizon, his warm reaction is simply a positive consequence of Saki reaching outward.

## Female peers

The female classmates are crucial anti-fatalist evidence.

They like Saki.

They invite her.

They make future plans.

Nothing in Chapter 1 suggests that the friendship is fake.

They prove that the makeover can create **healthy social possibility**.

## Hayato

Hayato's power arises from several asymmetries:

- age/status;
- social experience;
- sexual experience;
- familiarity with substances;
- control of the private setting;
- greater linguistic confidence.

But the deepest asymmetry is epistemic.

Saki asks:

> What does this mean?

Hayato repeatedly answers.

He tells her:

- what is normal;
- what the pill is for;
- what her reactions mean;
- what kind of girl she is;
- what his own desire means.

Saki is especially vulnerable because she has just spent the chapter looking for external confirmation that she has successfully become someone worth liking.

# 11. Material conditions

At Chapter 1's end, Saki remains materially secure.

She has:

- a home;
- parents;
- school enrollment;
- access to ordinary consumer life;
- transportation;
- a phone;
- female peers;
- no established debt;
- no established need for Hayato's money;
- no established dependence on his housing.

This materially distinguishes Chapter 1 from later possibilities.

Whatever risk Hayato represents, Saki is not yet trapped by survival needs.

---

# 12. Substance-use state

Chapter 1 establishes:

- alcohol exposure;
- one encounter with an unidentified recreational pill;
- clear subsequent impairment.

It does **not** establish:

- tolerance;
- craving;
- withdrawal;
- repeated use;
- clinical dependence.

The correct freeze is:

> **SUBSTANCE_STATE: introduced, unfamiliar, experimentally administered under manipulation, acute impairment shown.**

Any stronger conclusion would import later evidence.

# 13. Visual transformation and body authorship

The chapter's visual architecture mirrors its social argument.

## Opening composition

At graduation, Saki is surrounded by groups but compositionally isolated.

Her:

- glasses;
- braids;
- inward posture;
- central solitary framing

make her visually separate from the social clusters around her.

She is present but not integrated.

## Post-makeover introduction

At high-school entrance, Saki occupies much more of the page.

Her visual prominence changes radically.

The reader is made to experience the same basic fact as the school:

> Saki is now hard not to look at.

That is a formal version of increased social visibility.

## Authorship

The first transformation is visually authored through:

- mother/daughter collaboration;
- deliberate styling;
- Saki's own decision.

Nothing in the artwork requires the change to be read as moral degradation.

## The body becomes a social text

After the makeover, other people increasingly read Saki's body as information:

- classmates read it as social attractiveness;
- Hayato reads it as sexual availability;
- Saki reads their reactions as evidence about her worth.

That is the first step toward the body becoming a contested interpretive surface.

# 14. Eromanga grammar

Chapter 1 clearly deploys several commercial adult-manga conventions.

These include, at a high level:

- dramatic makeover;
- newly visible schoolgirl;
- experienced older pickup figure;
- intoxication;
- rapid sexual escalation;
- transformation of hesitation into physical responsiveness;
- degradation language;
- eroticized visual fragmentation.

But the chapter does something structurally important before these conventions take over:

> it spends substantial space establishing that Saki's transformation has already succeeded in ordinary social life.

The sexual scenario is therefore not the only "reward" of the makeover.

This weakens a crude reading in which the first eight pages merely exist to set up erotic content.

They also establish:

- a real social wound;
- a self-authored intervention;
- positive family response;
- successful peer connection.

The erotic scenario grows out of the same new visibility.

That is more causally integrated than a standard resettable transformation fantasy requires.

# 15. Pornographic function versus narrative function

## Pornographic function

Once the private encounter begins, a majority of the remaining chapter pages are organized primarily through adult-manga spectacle.

The visual grammar increasingly privileges:

- bodily display;
- fragmented framing;
- escalating novelty;
- stylized physical reaction.

This is commercially legible eromanga.

The analysis should not pretend otherwise.

## Narrative function

At the same time, the encounter establishes durable facts:

- Saki can be manipulated through recognition;
- she has limited experience reading adult male intent;
- Hayato can normalize risk;
- her refusal capacity can fail under pressure;
- she is willing to treat physical intimacy as evidence of romance;
- she now has his contact;
- he possesses an image of her;
- she leaves believing a boyfriend relationship may have begun.

These are not neutral erotic resets.

They alter the starting conditions of the next chapter.

That is the first prospective support for the cumulative-causality hypothesis.

# 16. Reader-position analysis

The reader's position changes sharply across the chapter.

## Early pages: WITH_SAKI

The opening strongly privileges Saki as a subject.

We receive:

- her memory;
- her fear;
- her embarrassment;
- her self-disgust;
- her hope;
- her mother's response;
- her anxiety at school;
- her excitement at peer inclusion.

Saki is the perceiver.

## Post-makeover: OBSERVING_SAKI + WITH_SAKI

The high-school introduction gives her large visual prominence while preserving internal monologue.

The reader simultaneously:

- sees her transformed body;
- hears the insecure old Saki thinking inside it.

This produces a useful split:

> externally legible transformation / internally unstable transformation.

## Explicit sequence: DESIRING_SAKI + WITH_SAKI

The erotic pages increasingly fragment and objectify the body.

Yet Saki's internal monologue is not entirely removed.

The reader is placed in an unstable dual position:

- invited to consume the erotic image;
- still given access to the uncertainty of the person being consumed.

This contradiction is **demonstrable**.

Its intentional ethical meaning is not yet demonstrable.

## Photograph motif

Near the end, Hayato uses a phone camera to turn Saki into an image he can retain.

This creates a potentially important formal rhyme:

> the manga has spent many pages presenting Saki as erotic image;
> a character inside the manga now explicitly captures Saki as image.

At Chapter 1's boundary, this should be logged as:

> **GI/SI — strong formal rhyme, intentional self-critique still unresolved.**

Do not upgrade it yet to reader indictment.

## Final page: return to WITH_SAKI

The chapter ends by returning strongly to:

- Saki's face;
- phone;
- home;
- internal interpretation.

The last meaningful note is not bodily spectacle.

It is:

> **What does this mean for my relationship?**

The chapter re-subjectivizes Saki before closing.

# 20. What remains continuous in Saki

The Japanese makes continuity especially visible.

After transformation Saki:

- self-edits her hobbies;
- studies fashion from the outside;
- wants connection;
- doubts her ability to perform the new self;

The same underlying social psychology remains.

Her external appearance has changed faster than her internal social confidence.

The first metamorphosis is therefore:

> **new presentation around an old relational need.**

Several things nevertheless change meaningfully.

This is a genuine increase in perceived agency.

## Social opportunity

## Self-evaluation

She begins to use external compliments as evidence that she may be worth liking.

## Romantic/sexual experience

She gains new experience, but under conditions that make its meaning ethically mixed.

## Exposure to manipulation

## Interpretive dependence

- what her responses mean;

# 23. Strongest interpretation

The strongest Chapter-1-only interpretation is:

> **『変身』 begins by showing Saki discover that self-presentation is a real form of agency. She changes herself because she wants a different social life, and the change genuinely produces family warmth, friendship, possibility, and pleasure. The chapter becomes dangerous only when Saki begins treating recognition as a verdict on the value of the self. Hayato does not create her need to be seen; he enters after she has discovered that being seen can make her feel real, lovable, and socially possible.**

The Japanese adds a crucial refinement:

> **Saki's body changes dramatically while her voice remains tentative, self-correcting, and unsure of its own social legitimacy.**

The visible new Saki is not yet internally secure enough to author what other people's attention means.

That gap is where Hayato acquires leverage.

---

# 24. Strongest counterreading

The strongest skeptical reading remains that the chapter's psychological setup primarily exists to intensify a conventional adult-manga transformation scenario.

Under this reading:

- shy/plain schoolgirl baseline increases transformation contrast;
- makeover creates erotic novelty;
- social insecurity makes the pickup fantasy easier to stage;
- prolonged explicit material dominates the page count;
- the dramatic interiority supplies emotional seasoning to a work whose main commercial function remains pornography.

There is real evidence for this.

Once the private encounter begins, much of the remaining chapter is structured as explicit erotic spectacle.

The literary reading therefore cannot claim that pornographic function is secondary.

However, the skeptical reading still does not explain several features very well:

- why Saki is shown successfully forming female friendships first;
- why her mother and father are given positive recognition scenes;
- why Saki's "ordinary person" impostor anxiety is established;
- why the chapter repeatedly distinguishes social connection from sexual attention;
- why her final thought is about boyfriend status rather than simply sexual pleasure;
- why the Japanese preserves so much uncertainty about what the transformed self means.

So the current verdict is:

> **Pornographic primacy is real at the commodity/form level, but it does not exhaust the chapter's causal or psychological structure.**

# 28. Provisional hypotheses

## H1 — Recognition vulnerability
**Claim:** Saki's unstable self-worth makes external approval disproportionately powerful.

**Status:** strong prospective hypothesis.

**Support:** family praise, peer inclusion, "I might be able to like myself," pleasure at even possibly false affection.

**Falsifier:** later evidence that Saki's self-evaluation becomes independent of social recognition.

## H2 — Self-authorship versus social persona
**Claim:** the physical makeover is self-authored, but Saki's "ordinary girl" persona is partly governed by imagined social norms.

**Status:** strong.

**Support:** hobby self-editing and `普通の人を装っている`.

**Falsifier:** later evidence that Saki freely integrates her actual hobbies and no longer experiences the persona as disguise.

## H3 — Interpretive authority
**Claim:** Hayato's most important early power is his ability to define unfamiliar experiences and Saki's responses for her.

**Status:** strong.

**Support:** alcohol minimization, pill normalization, response interpretation, affection framing.

**Falsifier:** Saki consistently rejects his interpretations and retains independent meaning-making.

## H4 — Linguistic continuity
**Claim:** visual metamorphosis precedes any major linguistic metamorphosis.

**Status:** strong for Chapter 1, longitudinally unresolved.

**Support:** stable `私`, hesitation, ellipsis, self-questioning, deferential stranger speech.

**Falsifier:** later chapters show a major stable idiolect/register transformation.

## H5 — Cumulative residue
**Claim:** Chapter 1 creates conditions likely to persist rather than reset.

**Status:** prospective.

**Support:** phone contact, image capture, substance exposure, Saki's boyfriend interpretation.

**Falsifier:** Chapter 2 resets Saki to a neutral state without consequence.

## H6 — Reader-position instability
**Claim:** the chapter simultaneously preserves Saki's interior subjectivity and turns her into erotic visual object.

**Status:** strong formal observation; ethical meaning unresolved.

**Falsifier:** later formal pattern makes one of these positions clearly dominant and the other incidental.

# Final Chapter 1 freeze thesis

> **Saki's first metamorphosis is genuinely hers. The Japanese text makes that even clearer than the English first pass: she says she wants to change, seeks her mother's help, enters high school still speaking and thinking like the same hesitant girl, and discovers that the new presentation genuinely opens family and peer relationships. What Chapter 1 places in danger is not the legitimacy of transformation but the stability of authorship after transformation. Saki begins using recognition as evidence of self-worth; Hayato then demonstrates how easily someone more experienced can manipulate the conditions under which that recognition is delivered and interpreted.**
>
> **The most important Japanese-language discovery is the coexistence of outward metamorphosis with inward linguistic continuity. Saki looks transformed long before she sounds transformed. Her speech remains hesitant, self-correcting, polite toward the older stranger, and full of questions about whether she has really changed. The chapter ends not with Saki believing she has been ruined, but with her still hopeful enough to interpret an objectively asymmetric encounter as the possible beginning of a boyfriend relationship.**
>
> **At this evidentiary horizon, Saki's future remains open. Chapter 1 establishes vulnerability, impairment, and serious predatory risk. It does not establish fate.**
