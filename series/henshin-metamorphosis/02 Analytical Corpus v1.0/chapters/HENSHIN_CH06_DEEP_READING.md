# 『変身』 / *Henshin*
## Chapter 6 Deep Reading
### From being discarded to choosing a future: chemical dependency, relational disposal, homelessness, and restored strategic authorship

# 1. Scope and evidentiary boundary

This artifact analyzes **Chapter 6 only**, corresponding to printed pages **167–202** and CBZ images **JP_CBZ_IMG_0170–JP_CBZ_IMG_0205**.

The Finale is completely excluded.

No event, image, later bodily consequence, later drug development, later violence, later pregnancy outcome, or retrospective statement from the Finale may be used to interpret Chapter 6's ending.

That restriction is unusually important here because Chapter 6 ends with a decision whose meaning depends upon whether we permit the future to remain genuinely open.

The Chapter 5 freeze is inherited unchanged.

At the Chapter 5 endpoint, Saki:

- remains housed through Hayato's system;
- is commercially self-regulating on his behalf;
- has undergone one pregnancy and abortion;
- is carrying Hayato's permanent mark;
- has become capable of sourcing clients and negotiating prices;
- has begun anticipating Hayato's judgment even in his absence;
- has escalating exposure to unidentified drugs;
- has entered Obata's wider drug/sexual economy;
- has been severely intoxicated;
- and, during that intoxication, has described the altered state as:

> **「私が求めていた居場所」**

the place where she had been seeking to belong.

Chapter 5 therefore ended at:

> **`F0_NO_ARTICULATED_FUTURE`**

not because Saki expressed a wish to die, but because her preferred horizon had become indefinite continuation of an altered present.

Chapter 6 begins from that state.

The governing questions are:

1. Does the chemical `居場所` become powerful enough to compete with Hayato himself?
2. Does Hayato remain a meaningful source of care once Saki's drug use interferes with his economic interests?
3. Who owns the money Saki earns?
4. What remains of the `HAYATO` property identity if Hayato rejects her?
5. What does homelessness do to the agency taxonomy?
6. Does survival assistance arrive without extraction?
7. Can Saki still form a strategic future after reaching `F0`?
8. If futurity returns, who authors it?

Because Saki is still a high-school-age minor, the chapter's sexual exploitation is described non-graphically and only as needed to analyze agency, material survival, coercion, drug use, reader position, and causal consequence.

---

# 2. Chapter-local thesis

Chapter 6 contains two movements that initially appear contradictory.

The first movement completes the collapse of the Hayato-centered world.

Saki is:

- repeatedly discarded after severe intoxication;
- robbed;
- unable to meet Hayato's financial expectations;
- choosing drugs over urgently needed cash;
- accused by Hayato of being selfishly drug-seeking;
- expelled from his apartment;
- blocked;
- and left without money, housing, school, family, or a destination.

The second movement unexpectedly restores a form of agency that has been largely absent since Chapter 1.

After recognizing what she believes is a second pregnancy, Saki refuses the default expectation that she will terminate it.

She says:

> **「……産む」**

> I will give birth.

Then:

> **「クスリも もうしない」**

> I won't use drugs anymore.

And finally:

> **「私は……この子のために……変わってみせる」**

> For this child, I will change.

The strongest Chapter-6-only thesis is therefore:

> **Chapter 6 destroys the Hayato-authored system as a viable relationship and then reopens strategic authorship around a new future-directed commitment.**

This is not recovery.

It is not proof that Saki can realize the plan.

It is not evidence that all structural constraints have disappeared.

It is a **decision to attempt another life**.

The distinction is mandatory.

The chapter's central irony is that the strongest strategic decision Saki has made since her original makeover occurs after nearly every material condition required to realize it has disappeared.

---

# 3. Narrative reconstruction: Chapter 5's disposal becomes Chapter 6's opening reality

Chapter 5 ended with men discussing whether an incapacitated Saki could simply be left outside near garbage if she failed to wake.

Chapter 6 opens with Saki **literally among garbage bags**.

Her first thought is:

> **「…またこんな所に捨てられてた」**

> I've been dumped in a place like this again.

Then:

> **「…何回目だろ…？」**

> How many times has it been?

And:

> **「最近こんな事ばかり」**

> Lately this keeps happening.

This is one of the clearest demonstrations of the no-reset hypothesis in the entire manga.

The erotic/drug scene does not end when the magazine installment changes.

The human consequence becomes the next chapter's starting location.

The chapter does not need metaphor to describe Saki as disposable.

She has literally been discarded.

---

# 4. Saki at the chapter opening

Saki wakes:

- disoriented;
- dirty;
- among refuse;
- unsure how often this pattern has repeated;
- still preoccupied with Hayato;
- still responsible for his quota;
- and without the money she had earned.

Her immediate concerns establish the current hierarchy of dependence.

She thinks:

> **「ハヤト 心配してるかなぁ…」**

> I wonder if Hayato is worried.

And:

> **「今週のお金まだ渡してないし…」**

> I still haven't given him this week's money.

The ordering is revealing.

Even after being dumped unconscious, Saki's mind returns to:

1. Hayato's reaction;
2. Hayato's money.

The self-regulating system from Chapter 5 is intact.

---

# 5. What Saki wants at the opening

Saki wants several things simultaneously:

- to return to Hayato;
- to deliver the expected money;
- to repair the consequences of her latest intoxication;
- to avoid disappointing him;
- to replace what has been stolen;
- and to obtain drugs.

That last item becomes increasingly important.

When Saki discovers her money has been taken, she also notices:

> **「…クスリも無い」**

> the drugs are gone too.

At this point money and drugs are both urgent.

The chapter is about to force a direct choice between them.

---

# 6. What Saki knows and believes

## Saki knows

- she has repeatedly been dumped outside after intoxication;
- the money she earned for Hayato has been stolen;
- her drugs are gone;
- Hayato expects a weekly payment;
- Obata controls access to drugs and is connected to the financial system;
- she does not currently have enough money;
- she believes she caused the immediate failure by trusting/using with unsafe people.

## Saki believes

- the stolen money was earned **for Hayato**;
- Hayato remains the person whose needs she must satisfy;
- she is responsible for repairing the shortfall;
- Obata is one of the only available people from whom she can seek an advance;
- she still has to function inside the "adult world" that Chapter 4 taught her to inhabit.

She has not yet concluded that:

> Hayato's system created the conditions under which this keeps happening.

Her default explanation remains:

> **I failed.**

---

# 7. Causal development: robbery converts Chapter 5 intoxication into Chapter 6 debt pressure

Saki discovers:

> **「あぁ～お金抜かれてる～」**

The money has been removed from her belongings.

She blames herself:

> she had a bad feeling;
> she was foolish;
> she used drugs with people she did not know well enough.

Then:

> **「ハヤトのために…稼いだお金…なのに」**

> It was money I earned for Hayato.

This line is important because it preserves Chapter 4's captured economic purpose.

Saki does not conceptualize the earnings primarily as:

> my wages.

They are:

> money for Hayato.

The theft therefore creates not only material loss but relational failure.

---

# 8. Obata weaponizes the "adult world" story

Saki concludes:

> **「小幡さんに…お願いするしか…ないか」**

roughly:

> I guess I have no choice but to ask Obata.

The `しか…ない` pattern returns.

Earlier:

> only Hayato remains.

Then:

> this is the only thing I can do.

Now:

> Obata is the only available route to the needed resources.

Saki asks for an advance.

Obata refuses to treat the problem as a relationship obligation.

He tells her:

> **「咲ちゃんはもう大人なんだ……そうだろ？」**

> You're already an adult, aren't you?

Then:

> **「大人の世界はビジネスなんだよ」**

> The adult world is business.

This is a devastating callback.

Chapter 4 Saki had proudly narrated herself as having entered:

> **「大人の世界」**

Now an older man uses the same concept to explain why hardship does not generate care.

Adultness means:

> transaction.

The identity Saki once used to legitimate risk becomes the language by which help is withheld unless she provides value.

---

# 9. Saki accurately recognizes what Obata is

The Japanese gives Saki an important moment of interpretive clarity:

> **「そうだった…この男とはセックスとクスリの取引をするだけ…それだけの関係だった」**

A restrained paraphrase:

> That's right. My relationship with this man is only an exchange involving sex and drugs. That's all it ever was.

This is significant.

Saki can distinguish:

- Hayato, whom she still experiences as love;
- Obata, whom she understands as transaction.

Her ability to analyze relationships has not disappeared.

The problem is that the one relationship she exempts from the transactional model is precisely the relationship that has organized much of the transaction.

Then she thinks:

> **「やっぱり私にはハヤトしかいないんだ」**

> Just as I thought, Hayato is the only person I have.

The Chapter 3 grammar survives immediately before Hayato destroys the relationship.

---

# 10. The decisive money-versus-drug choice

Obata offers Saki a direct choice in how she will be compensated.

He tells her:

> **「どう支払って欲しいかは咲ちゃんが選びな」**

> You choose how you want to be paid.

The offered forms include:

- cash;
- the drug she has been using.

This is unusually valuable agency evidence because the alternatives are simultaneously visible.

Saki knows she needs money.

Her internal thoughts fragment around:

> **「金…金…金を取って…」**

> Money... take the money...

She knows the Hayato-centered objective.

Yet she chooses the drug.

This is the first clean prospective evidence that:

> **chemical reward can outrank the economic mission organized around Hayato.**

That is a major change from Chapter 5.

The drug is no longer merely helping Saki survive Hayato's system.

It can now redirect behavior **away from the system's stated financial goal**.

---

# 11. Saki confirms the preference after use

A man later tells Saki that at this rate she will never make the quota.

He asks:

> **「本当にクスリだけでいいのかい 咲ちゃん？」**

> Are you really okay taking only the drugs?

Saki answers:

> **「これがイイの♡」**

> This is what I want.

And describes the feeling as exceptionally good.

The evidence should be handled cautiously.

This does not by itself establish a formal clinical diagnosis.

It does establish:

- preference for the drug over cash;
- awareness that cash is necessary;
- continuation despite quota pressure;
- powerful immediate reward.

The substance ledger has crossed an important threshold.

---

# 12. Substance-use state: reward begins outranking prior goals

Chapter 5 established the drug state as:

> `居場所`.

Chapter 6 establishes behavioral prioritization.

Saki chooses the substance when:

- she has no money;
- Hayato expects payment;
- failing to pay threatens her core relationship;
- she consciously knows she should take cash.

This is stronger evidence than merely saying:

> she likes drugs.

The best disciplined formulation is:

> **Saki demonstrates severe substance prioritization: immediate chemical reward defeats a consciously recognized financial/relational objective.**

The manga still does not provide enough clinical information for a formal diagnosis.

But the dependence-like structure is now much more strongly evidenced.

---

# 13. Hayato treats Saki's earnings as his property

When Saki returns, Hayato is furious.

His first concern is money:

> **「金がねぇってどういう事だよ！」**

> What do you mean there's no money?

Saki explains:

> **「ちゃんと稼いだの…でも…盗まれて…」**

> I did earn it... but it was stolen.

Hayato answers:

> **「あの金がねぇと俺がヤバイんだよ！」**

> I'm in trouble if I don't have that money.

Then he threatens her if she has been concealing it:

> **「俺の金横取りしてたらタダじゃ済ませねぇからな！」**

> If you've been taking **my money**, you won't get away with it.

The possessive is important.

The economic arrangement has reached its ideological endpoint.

Saki performs the labor.

Hayato defines the proceeds as:

> **his money.**

---

# 14. Hayato's economic ownership is more explicit than his romantic reciprocity

Across earlier chapters, the relationship category has remained asymmetric.

Saki has called him:

- boyfriend;
- the person she loves;
- the only person she has;
- owner in a romanticized sense.

Hayato has called her:

- `セフレ`;
- the girl he found;
- someone who will do whatever he says.

Chapter 6 adds:

> **her earnings are his.**

The relationship is becoming clearer at the level of property than it ever became at the level of mutual romantic commitment.

Hayato recognizes claims upon:

- Saki's labor;
- Saki's body;
- Saki's appearance;
- Saki's money.

He does not show equivalent recognition of obligations toward:

- her safety;
- her health;
- her housing security once she fails economically.

---

# 15. Hayato discovers unauthorized drugs

Hayato searches Saki's belongings.

Saki tries to stop him because drugs are hidden there.

He finds them and asks:

> **「なに勝手にクスリ買ってんだよ？」**

> Why are you buying drugs on your own?

Then:

> **「お前 俺の金でこんなん一人でキメてたのか？」**

> You've been using my money to get high by yourself?

The wording is revealing.

The problem is not:

> drugs are dangerous.

The problem is:

> **unauthorized drugs purchased with money Hayato claims as his.**

The same substances he normalized when they facilitated sex, compliance, and labor become unacceptable when Saki acquires them independently and the spending competes with his financial extraction.

---

# 16. Saki gives the clearest work-coping explanation yet

Saki explains:

> **「仕事がキツくて…少しでも楽にできたらと思って…ちょっとだけ」**

> Work is hard... I thought it might make it a little easier... just a little.

This is a major substance-use finding.

The drug's functions have now accumulated:

- Chapter 1: externally introduced disinhibition/impairment.
- Chapter 2: repeated drug-sex pairing.
- Chapter 5: altered state as `居場所`.
- Chapter 6: **work-coping tool**.

Saki is not describing use only as hedonism.

She is describing it as an adaptation to a labor environment she finds difficult to tolerate.

That materially complicates Hayato's next accusation.

---

# 17. Hayato performs causal laundering on the drug dependence he helped create

Saki tells Hayato:

> **「でも全部ハヤトのためなの」**

> But everything is for Hayato.

She describes herself as:

> stupid;
> always failing.

Hayato replies:

> **「キメて気持ちよくなりてぇだけだろうが！」**

> You just want to get high and feel good.

Then:

> **「糞ジャンキーが！」**

> fucking junkie.

This is one of the clearest examples of **causal laundering** in the work.

Hayato's own history includes:

- introducing unfamiliar substances;
- normalizing use;
- repeatedly pairing drugs and sex;
- bringing Saki into Obata's harder-drug environment;
- facilitating drug use despite explicit risk warnings;
- managing work that Saki now says she uses drugs to endure.

When independent drug use reduces the money reaching him, Hayato deletes that history.

The resulting behavior becomes:

> Saki's personal defect.

The structure is identical to earlier interpretive dispossession:

> environment disappears;
> behavior remains;
> behavior becomes character.

---

# 18. Hayato's objection is control-based, not reliably care-based

This distinction should be frozen.

Nothing on these pages supports the claim:

> Hayato expels Saki because he has suddenly become sincerely concerned that drug use is destroying her.

His speech centers:

- missing money;
- his danger;
- unauthorized spending;
- Saki's supposed selfish pleasure;
- the possibility that she withheld his earnings.

This does not prove he has zero affect of any kind.

It does establish that the conflict is structurally about:

> **control over resources and behavior.**

Saki's drug use was useful while it served Hayato's environment.

It becomes intolerable when it competes with his claims.

---

# 19. Hayato expels Saki

Hayato says:

> **「もういいや…」**

> Enough.

Then:

> **「お前…出て行けよ」**

> Get out.

And:

> **「早く荷物まとめてどっかに消えろ」**

> Pack your things and disappear somewhere.

Saki initially cannot believe him:

> **「うそ…」**

> No...

> **「冗談だよね？」**

> You're joking, right?

She immediately shifts into repair:

> **「ごめん！私が悪かったからっ！」**

> I'm sorry! It was my fault!

Then:

> **「もっと…頑張るから！」**

> I'll work harder!

And:

> **「ダメなところ直すから…っ！」**

> I'll fix what's wrong with me!

This is one of the bleakest examples of captured interpretation in the chapter.

Hayato does not need to tell Saki:

> the relationship failed because you were defective.

Saki supplies the diagnosis herself.

---

# 20. Housing loss is non-choice

Hayato physically forces Saki out and tells her not to return.

This is not a voluntary departure.

The correct classification is:

> **`A10_NON_CHOICE`** for the immediate housing loss.

Saki does not decide:

> I should leave an exploitative relationship.

She attempts to remain.

She promises:

- better performance;
- obedience;
- self-correction.

Her material dependence is therefore directly exposed.

The Chapter 4 "refuge" was conditional upon continued usefulness.

---

# 21. The obedience-language chain reaches its relational endpoint

Outside the apartment, Saki messages and pleads.

Her language includes:

> **「ゴメンね…私がダメでハヤトに迷惑かけちゃって」**

> I'm sorry. I'm no good and caused you trouble.

Then:

> **「これからちゃんとするから…お願い 中に入れて」**

> I'll do things properly from now on. Please let me back in.

Then:

> **「なんでもハヤトの言う事聞くから」**

> I'll do anything Hayato says.

The Chapter 4–5 obedience chain continues even after the relationship ceases materially protecting her.

The most important point is not simply:

> Saki is submissive.

It is:

> **obedience remains the resource Saki believes she can exchange for belonging.**

She offers compliance as rent for relationship.

---

# 22. 「捨てられちゃった」 — bodily disposal becomes relational disposal

Saki discovers:

> **「…ブロックされてる」**

> I've been blocked.

Then:

> **「捨てられちゃった」**

> I've been thrown away / discarded.

The verb echoes the chapter opening.

Opening:

> Saki wakes after being physically dumped among garbage.

Now:

> Hayato discards her relationally and materially.

The chapter thus develops a disposal motif:

> strangers discard the body  
> → Hayato discards the partner/worker  
> → the city contains Saki without giving her a place.

This is not merely metaphorical repetition.

The same social ontology governs each scene:

> Saki is valuable while usable.

---

# 23. Another woman enters Hayato's apartment

A different woman arrives.

She apologizes for being late and says it is her first time being invited to his home.

The page strongly shows:

> Hayato can redirect sexual/relational attention elsewhere immediately after excluding Saki.

What it does **not** establish:

- the exact duration of Hayato's relationship with this woman;
- whether she becomes a girlfriend;
- whether she replaces Saki in every functional role.

Those would be speculation.

The relevant Chapter-6 fact is narrower:

> the apartment from which Saki has been excluded remains available to another woman.

That makes the supposed uniqueness of Saki's bond look even more asymmetrical.

---

# 24. The `HAYATO` mark becomes an orphaned inscription

Later, men notice Saki's tattoo and ask whether HAYATO is her boyfriend's name.

Saki replies:

> **「これは……あの…なんでも…ない…の」**

> This is... um... nothing.

And then trails toward:

> **「…もう…なんでも…」**

The body still says:

> HAYATO.

The relationship no longer does.

This is one of the strongest examples of the body carrying **historical authorship after the authoring relationship has disappeared**.

Chapter 4's mark promised permanence:

> now I will always belong to Hayato.

Chapter 6 reveals another kind of permanence:

> the mark can remain after reciprocity vanishes.

The sign of belonging becomes evidence of abandonment.

---

# 25. The city is present but not available

After expulsion Saki walks through a crowded city.

She thinks:

> **「行く宛てもない…」**

> I have nowhere to go.

Then:

> **「金もない…」**

> I have no money.

Then:

> **「私…これからどうしたら？」**

> What am I supposed to do now?

This is a crucial environmental image.

Saki is not isolated in wilderness.

Around her remain:

- crowds;
- buildings;
- shops;
- transportation;
- ordinary urban life.

The social world visibly exists.

It simply does not function as **hers**.

This is the strongest version yet of:

> **social adjacency without institutional belonging.**

---

# 26. Future horizon collapses to immediate survival again

At this point Saki has lost:

- Hayato housing;
- money;
- reliable work management;
- family;
- school;
- ordinary peer relationships.

The future-horizon metric returns to:

> **`F2_IMMEDIATE_SURVIVAL`**

and intermittently:

> **`F1_NEXT_REWARD_OR_TRANSACTION`**

The relevant questions are:

- where can she sleep?
- what can she eat?
- how can she obtain relief?
- what can she exchange?

This is not merely emotional despair.

It is material.

---

# 27. Drug reward directly weakens exit agency in public

Saki uses drugs outdoors and becomes heavily impaired.

When men approach, she internally recognizes danger.

Her thoughts include:

> **「嫌だ…逃げなきゃ…」**

> No... I have to run.

Then:

> **「…いけないのに…気持ちよすぎて…」**

> I need to... but it feels too good...

This is one of the clearest agency effects of substance use in the whole work.

Saki knows:

- the preferred action;
- the risk;
- the need to exit.

She cannot convert that knowledge into effective action because the altered state has become overwhelmingly rewarding.

This is not:

> intoxication automatically equals no agency.

It is:

> **in this specific moment, the text directly depicts chemical reward degrading exit capacity.**

---

# 28. Material survival becomes explicitly transactional

The men Saki encounters can offer only limited resources.

Their language is approximately:

> what we can provide is cold food and a hard place to sleep.

Later this becomes more explicit in the encampment:

> if Saki stays, she can get food and somewhere to sleep, but the settlement is full of men who expect sexual access.

This is survival barter.

The arrangement should be distinguished from Hayato's system.

## Hayato

Extraction was romanticized as:

- love;
- debt service;
- adulthood;
- ownership.

## Tent/encampment economy

Extraction is more openly stated:

- food;
- bed;
- access.

The lack of romantic disguise makes the arrangement easier to identify as transaction.

It does not make it autonomous.

---

# 29. Care and extraction occupy the same relationship again

The informal camp men sometimes provide genuine immediate goods:

- warm food;
- a place to sleep;
- practical information.

They also exploit Saki.

This repeats a core relational pattern.

Hayato:

> "Don't overwork" while scheduling work.

Older client:

> offers to listen to Saki's problems, then exploits her desperation.

Encampment:

> offers food and shelter while treating sexual availability as part of the arrangement.

The story repeatedly gives Saki **care bundled with claims**.

That is important because it can distort what care itself means.

If resources repeatedly arrive as:

> help in exchange for access,

then unconditional support becomes increasingly difficult to recognize as normal.

---

# 30. Eromanga grammar: homelessness becomes erotic scenario

The chapter's middle section uses recognizable degradation-eromanga conventions:

- drugged public vulnerability;
- homeless/street exploitation;
- transactional shelter;
- hygiene degradation;
- multiple male access;
- loss of private space;
- pregnancy discovery.

This is one of the work's most ethically severe formal choices.

A hostile reading can reasonably say:

> the manga converts homelessness and destitution into another fetish scenario.

That is true at the level of pornographic function.

The literary question is whether the scene also changes the state of Saki's life in ways that matter beyond erotic novelty.

It does.

---

# 31. Pornographic function versus narrative function

## Pornographic function

The middle of Chapter 6 escalates through:

- destitution;
- public vulnerability;
- drug impairment;
- survival exchange;
- bodily degradation.

The imagery remains designed for erotic consumption.

## Narrative function

The same material establishes:

- actual homelessness;
- no private sleeping space;
- no independent food security;
- no stable money;
- lack of memory around transport;
- exchange of bodily access for survival resources;
- second-pregnancy recognition.

The explicit scenario therefore produces a new material state.

The no-reset hypothesis remains strong.

---

# 32. Saki's memory gap marks severe impairment

Saki eventually wakes inside a tent encampment and thinks:

> **「…どこだろう 此処」**

> Where is this?

Then:

> **「テント村…？」**

> A tent village?

And:

> **「どうやって来たのか覚えてない…」**

> I don't remember how I got here.

This is high-confidence evidence of severe impairment.

It does not require clinical terminology.

The relevant fact is:

> Saki did not maintain continuous autobiographical awareness through the transition into the camp.

The erosion of memory adds another layer to diminished environmental authorship.

---

# 33. The camp offers survival but not autonomy

A man gives Saki warm food:

> **「温かいメシだ 食いなよ」**

> Warm food. Eat.

Another tells her:

> **「行く宛てないんだろ？」**

> You have nowhere to go, right?

Then:

> **「ここにいたら飯と寝床には困らないからよ」**

> If you stay here, you won't have to worry about food or a place to sleep.

But he adds that the camp is full of men starved for women and that Saki should stay only if she can accept that condition.

The offer is transparent.

That transparency matters.

This is not Hayato's semantic manipulation.

Saki is told the price of survival.

But transparency alone does not make the choice free.

Someone with:

- no money;
- no housing;
- no safe family;
- no school;
- severe drug vulnerability

faces an extreme material constraint.

Recommended classification:

> **`A7_MATERIALLY_CONSTRAINED`**, with individual later encounters potentially more coercive depending on immediate circumstances.

---

# 34. Saki still expresses gratitude for ordinary care

When food is offered, Saki says:

> **「ありがとう」**

This tiny line matters.

The same Saki who once responded intensely to:

- friendship;
- compliments;
- maternal support

still responds to care.

Her social orientation has not disappeared.

She is not transformed into a purely appetite-driven being.

Even at severe material collapse, simple human provision registers.

---

# 35. Pregnancy recognition interrupts survival routine

Saki smells the food and vomits before she has meaningfully eaten.

The men notice that she had not even consumed the meal.

They speculate:

> she may be pregnant.

Saki recognizes the bodily sensation:

> **「そうだ…」**

> That's right...

> **「この感覚…前にもあった」**

> I've felt this before.

Then:

> **「…妊娠…してる」**

> I'm pregnant.

This should be classified carefully.

Unlike Chapter 5, there is no pregnancy test shown on these pages.

Therefore:

> **pregnancy is Saki's high-confidence belief based on familiar symptoms, not yet medical confirmation within Chapter 6.**

That distinction belongs in the freeze.

---

# 36. The second pregnancy reopens autobiographical time

The immediate thought is:

> **「――また」**

> Again.

Then:

> **「――堕ろすのか……っ」**

> Am I going to abort again?

The word:

> again

creates temporal continuity.

The first pregnancy/abortion is no longer merely an expense in the labor system.

It becomes memory against which Saki evaluates the present.

The body is once again forcing a future question.

But this time Hayato is not present to answer it.

---

# 37. Men around Saki assume abortion

The camp men discuss the situation.

Their logic is roughly:

- Saki is very young;
- she probably does not want to have a child;
- she can earn enough money to terminate the pregnancy.

The important point is:

> the immediate social environment supplies abortion as the expected practical answer.

That makes Saki's reply especially important.

She does not simply echo the local consensus.

---

# 38. 「ダメ」「絶対イヤ」 — Saki rejects repetition before she states the new plan

Saki's internal language is:

> **「ダメ もうアレは―」**

> No. Not that again—

Then:

> **「絶対イヤ」**

> Absolutely not.

This is a strong refusal.

It precedes the affirmative choice.

The decision is therefore not merely:

> motherhood occurs to Saki.

It begins as:

> **I will not repeat the prior reproductive path.**

That is meaningful strategic differentiation.

---

# 39. 「……産む」 — the strongest strategic declaration since Chapter 1

Saki says:

> **「……産む」**

> I'll give birth.

On the next page she makes it explicit:

> **「産みます」**

> I will.

This is a remarkable agency event.

The environment around her expects another abortion.

Hayato is absent.

No romantic partner gives her the decision.

No parent gives her the decision.

No peer group gives her the decision.

No client gives her the decision.

At the level of expressed reproductive intention, this is best classified as:

> **`A1_AUTONOMOUS`.**

That does **not** mean the material circumstances are autonomous.

Her ability to realize the plan is severely constrained.

But the preference itself is not supplied by the current environment.

This distinction is central.

---

# 40. The decision is prospective, not retrospective redemption

We must not read:

> "I will give birth"

as proof that Saki has already recovered.

At the endpoint she still has:

- no stable independent housing;
- no money;
- no school;
- no family support;
- severe drug-use risk;
- an exploitative survival environment.

What she has regained is:

> **a strategic objective.**

That is different from:

> the resources to achieve it.

The correct wording is:

> **Saki decides to attempt recovery/reconstruction.**

Not:

> Saki has recovered.

---

# 41. 「見捨てたりなんか…しない」 — abandonment reverses direction

Saki thinks:

> **「もう二度と――お腹の赤ちゃんを見捨てたりなんか…しない」**

A restrained translation:

> I won't abandon the baby in my belly again.

The verb:

> **見捨てる**

means to abandon, forsake, leave behind.

This is an extraordinary lexical choice after a chapter structured by Saki being discarded.

Opening:

> strangers dump Saki by garbage.

Middle:

> Hayato throws Saki out.

Saki:

> **「捨てられちゃった」**

I've been discarded.

Ending:

> Saki promises not to **見捨てる** the child.

The chapter converts Saki from:

> abandoned object

into:

> person refusing to abandon another.

That is an ethical transformation, not merely a reproductive one.

---

# 42. The discarded person chooses non-discarding as a future principle

This deserves emphasis.

Chapter 6's most important moral movement may be:

> **Saki does not respond to being treated as disposable by deciding disposability is the rule of the world.**

Instead she says:

> I will not abandon this child.

The future project is therefore organized around a relationship principle different from the systems that shaped her:

- Hayato: usefulness determines shelter.
- Clients: money determines access.
- Obata: adult life is transaction.
- Street/tent economy: resources come with claims.
- Saki's new commitment: **I will not abandon.**

Whether she can sustain that commitment remains unknown.

But the normative content is genuinely different.

---

# 43. 「大事に…育てる」 — care becomes Saki's authored objective

Saki says:

> **「産んで…あげて… 大事に…育てるの」**

A restrained rendering:

> I'll give birth... and raise the child with care / cherish the child.

This is one of the first moments since Chapter 1 where Saki describes a future not primarily through:

- how someone will recognize her;
- what someone will pay;
- what Hayato wants;
- what drug state she wants.

The objective is:

> provide care to another person.

That reintroduces a long horizon.

---

# 44. 「クスリも もうしない」 — drug refusal becomes part of the future project

Saki then says:

> **「クスリも もうしない」**

> I won't do drugs anymore.

This is a strategic decision.

It should not be confused with evidence of successful abstinence.

Chapter 6 has just shown:

- drugs outranking money;
- drugs degrading exit capacity;
- drugs functioning as work coping;
- drugs as powerful reward;
- severe impairment.

So the promise is difficult.

The difficulty is part of its meaning.

The chapter does not tell us:

> stopping will be easy.

It tells us:

> Saki recognizes drug use as incompatible with the future she now wants.

That is a significant change in interpretive authority.

---

# 45. The drug has moved from `居場所` to obstacle

At the end of Chapter 5:

> the drug state is `居場所`.

At the end of Chapter 6:

> Saki says she will not use drugs if she is to build the future she wants.

This does not erase the earlier reward.

It creates a direct conflict:

> **chemical belonging**
>
> versus
>
> **future-oriented caregiving.**

That is perhaps the most important prospective conflict the Finale must inherit.

The question is not:

> does Saki now know drugs are bad?

The question is:

> **can a future-directed value compete with a state that has previously functioned as belonging itself?**

Chapter 6 cannot answer.

---

# 46. 「この子のために……変わってみせる」 — metamorphosis changes purpose again

The final line is:

> **「私は……この子のために……変わってみせる」**

A restrained translation:

> **For this child, I will change.**

The title-level progression is now:

## Chapter 1

> **「変わりたい」**  
> I want to change.

Purpose:

> Saki's own social future.

## Chapter 4

> **「ハヤトのためなら…いくらでも変われる」**  
> For Hayato, I can change as much as necessary.

Purpose:

> captured relationship.

## Chapter 6

> **「この子のために……変わってみせる」**  
> For this child, I will change.

Purpose:

> caregiving/future responsibility.

This is neither a simple return to Chapter 1 nor a simple continuation of Chapter 4.

The author of change is still Saki.

The **telos** has changed.

---

# 47. Does this restore self-authorship?

Partly.

The decision is not externally imposed by the current group.

Indeed, it goes against their expectation.

Saki supplies:

- the refusal;
- the goal;
- the drug decision;
- the care principle.

That is stronger strategic authorship than she has shown for several chapters.

But an important counterreading remains.

Saki does not say:

> I am worth saving for myself.

She says:

> I will change for this child.

So one could argue:

> the external object organizing self-worth has changed from Hayato to baby.

This is a serious possibility.

But it should not erase the difference between the two relationships.

Hayato's telos required:

- extraction;
- obedience;
- commodification.

The child-oriented telos, as Saki articulates it, requires:

- sobriety;
- care;
- endurance;
- non-abandonment.

So even if both are relationally organized, they are ethically and behaviorally different.

---

# 48. Motherhood-redemption counterreading

The chapter invites a conventional critical objection.

A sexually exploited and socially marginalized girl discovers pregnancy and suddenly declares:

> I will become better for my baby.

This can be read as a conservative:

> **maternal redemption**

trope.

Under that reading, the work risks implying that Saki's route back to moral purpose requires motherhood.

That critique should remain active.

But the simplest "purity restored by motherhood" version fits poorly.

Saki does **not** visually return to early Saki.

She remains:

- tattooed;
- pierced;
- light-haired;
- visibly marked by the previous chapters.

The body that says:

> I will change

is the same transformed body.

The chapter does not erase history.

It gives the historically marked Saki a new objective.

---

# 49. The body remains continuous while its future meaning changes

This matters to the title.

Chapter 1 transformation changed the body so that Saki could enter society.

Chapter 4 body modification turned the body into:

- Hayato's taste;
- ownership mark;
- market asset.

Chapter 6 does not visually undo those transformations.

Instead:

> **Saki changes what the existing transformed body is for.**

The body is no longer narrated at the endpoint as:

- an asset;
- a debt instrument;
- a client commodity.

It is:

- pregnant body;
- future caregiving body;
- body Saki intends to protect from drugs.

That is a shift in authorship without visual purification.

---

# 50. The HAYATO tattoo becomes a historical scar rather than current identity

By Chapter 6's later pages, Saki has already denied the relational meaning of the HAYATO mark:

> "nothing."

The mark remains visible.

This gives the body a layered temporal structure.

It records:

- a relationship that once organized Saki's identity;
- an ownership claim she once embraced;
- a future that has now collapsed.

The chapter does not remove the mark before Saki chooses another future.

Therefore:

> **past authorship remains materially inscribed even when present authorship changes.**

That may become important in the final full-work body analysis.

- expresses gratitude for food;
- speaks directly when a new decision becomes clear;

The chapter does not introduce a wholly new idiolect.

Earlier Saki's key words concerned:

The Chapter 6 ending shifts toward:

The voice remains recognizably Saki while the object of care changes.

# 63. Misery-machine audit

Chapter 6 actually strengthens the causal-tragedy case at the local level.

Most transitions are consequences of existing conditions.

> these events are unrelated.

> **the story's world remains suspiciously empty of protective intervention.**

# 64. Strongest interpretation

The strongest Chapter-6-only interpretation is:

> **Chapter 6 completes the collapse of the Hayato-centered world by revealing its conditions of membership. Saki's housing, relationship, and role were never secure independently of usefulness. Once powerful drug reward begins competing with the money Hayato claims as his, he reclassifies the dependence his environment helped create as Saki's private moral defect and expels her. The system then survives outside Hayato through transactional survival economies and drug-mediated vulnerability. Yet the chapter ends by giving Saki a genuinely new strategic project: she rejects repetition of the prior abortion, chooses to continue what she believes is a second pregnancy, promises to stop drugs, and defines transformation around caring for a child rather than satisfying Hayato.**

The central achievement is that the chapter does not require Saki to become the visual "old Saki" in order to author a different future.

The body remains marked.

The future changes.

> → homelessness  
> → street exploitation  
> → homeless encampment

# Final Chapter 6 freeze thesis

> **Chapter 6 first reveals the final condition of the Hayato-centered system: Saki's housing, relationship, work, and economic value are secure only while she remains useful and controllable. The chemical `居場所` discovered in Chapter 5 has become strong enough that, when Obata lets her choose between desperately needed cash and drugs, Saki chooses the drug. Hayato's response exposes the asymmetry of his earlier permissiveness. The person who introduced and normalized Saki's drug-sex environment condemns her as a `ジャンキー` only when independent use diverts money he explicitly calls `俺の金`. He then expels and blocks her.**
>
> **The chapter's disposal vocabulary makes the logic brutally coherent. Saki opens after being `捨てられてた` among garbage; after Hayato rejects her she thinks `捨てられちゃった`; the permanent `HAYATO` mark survives as an orphaned inscription whose old relational meaning she now denies to strangers. Housing, money, and ordinary institutional belonging disappear. In the encampment, food and shelter return only through another care/extraction arrangement. The world continues teaching Saki that resources come with claims upon her.**
>
> **Yet Chapter 6 does not end at that nadir. When Saki recognizes what she believes is another pregnancy, the people around her assume she will terminate it. She refuses: `絶対イヤ`, then `……産む`. She says she will not `見捨てる` the child, will raise it with care, will stop using drugs, and will change for the child. That sequence is the strongest strategic declaration of authorship since Chapter 1 because its immediate purpose is not supplied by Hayato, client, creditor, parent, or peer.**
>
> **This is not recovery. Saki remains homeless, impoverished, drug-vulnerable, outside school and family, and dependent on an exploitative informal shelter. Her pregnancy is a high-confidence self-recognition based on familiar symptoms, not yet medically confirmed on these pages. Her promise to stop drugs is an intention, not evidence that abstinence will be easy or successful. What has changed is the future horizon: after Chapter 5 reduced belonging to a chemically suspended present, Chapter 6 reintroduces months and years through pregnancy, caregiving, and the refusal to abandon.**
>
> **The final line therefore creates a third title-level form of metamorphosis. Chapter 1: `変わりたい`—Saki changes for a life she wants. Chapter 4: `ハヤトのためなら…いくらでも変われる`—change becomes captured by Hayato. Chapter 6: `この子のために…変わってみせる`—change becomes a self-declared caregiving project. Whether this is fully restored self-authorship or another externally organized telos must remain unresolved. But the difference in ethical content is real: Hayato's project demanded extraction and obedience; the child-oriented project, as Saki presently defines it, demands sobriety, care, endurance, and non-abandonment.**
>
> **Most importantly, the Finale is not allowed to rewrite this state. At the Chapter 6 horizon, a genuine alternative future has reopened. Saki may fail. The material conditions are severe. But failure is not yet inevitable, and her decision is not yet doomed. The pre-finale audit must begin from exactly that open possibility.**
