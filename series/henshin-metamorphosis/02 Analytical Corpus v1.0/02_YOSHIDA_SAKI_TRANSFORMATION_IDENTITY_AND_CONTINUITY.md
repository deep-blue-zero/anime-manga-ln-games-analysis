# 『変身』 / *Henshin*
## 02 — Yoshida Saki: Transformation, Identity, and Continuity
### The highly adaptive self

# Phase 4 language-audit revision note

This document was drafted in tandem with `05_JAPANESE_VOICE_DIALOGUE_AND_TRANSLATION_AUDIT.md`, as required by the synthesis architecture. The language pass materially sharpened—but did not reverse—the Saki thesis. The final formulation below therefore incorporates the Japanese-specific finding that Saki’s underlying speech habits remain strongly continuous while the vocabularies through which she interprets herself become increasingly supplied by external systems. In this sense, her voice is **colonized lexically more than replaced structurally**.

# 0. Executive thesis

Yoshida Saki is best understood neither as an unchanged innocent upon whom events merely happen nor as an “innocent girl” who is progressively replaced by a corrupted new personality.

She is a **highly adaptive, recognition-sensitive adolescent whose capacity for self-transformation is initially one of her greatest strengths and gradually becomes the capacity other people and systems exploit most effectively**.

The full-work character movement is therefore not:

> innocent Saki → corrupted Saki.

It is closer to:

> **self-authored adaptation → socially rewarded performance → relationally captured adaptation → economically and chemically self-regulating adaptation → material collapse → precarious recovery of strategic purpose → violent closure of a future she had begun to enact.**

The distinction matters because Saki changes enormously.

She changes:

- appearance;
- clothing and bodily presentation;
- social role;
- sexual experience;
- relationship expectations;
- economic behavior;
- drug behavior;
- vocabulary;
- relation to school;
- relation to family;
- relation to the future;
- and the practical skills by which she navigates increasingly dangerous environments.

But several things remain strikingly continuous:

- she wants somewhere she can belong;
- she is acutely sensitive to how other people evaluate her;
- she fears being boring, burdensome, troublesome, or disappointing;
- she wants to be useful to people she loves;
- she responds intensely to praise and care;
- she repeatedly tries to interpret sexual attention as relationship;
- she remains capable of gratitude;
- she remains capable of refusal;
- she remains capable of future imagination;
- and when a viable object of care reappears, she can still reorganize herself around a long-term project.

The tragedy is therefore not that Saki loses every trace of self.

It is that **the criteria by which transformation counts as successful are progressively supplied from outside her**.

Chapter 1 begins with:

> **「変わりたい」** — *I want to change.*  
> `JP_CBZ_IMG_0008 / printed p.005`

Chapter 4 culminates in:

> **「ハヤトのためなら……私 いくらでも変われるから…っ♡」**  
> *For Hayato’s sake... I can change as much as necessary.*

Chapter 6 ends with:

> **「私は……この子のために……変わってみせる」**  
> *For this child... I will change.*  
> `JP_CBZ_IMG_0205 / printed p.202`

And the imagined adult future finally says:

> **「ママはね……生まれ変われたの――」**  
> *Mama... was able to be reborn.*  
> `JP_CBZ_IMG_0244 / printed p.241`

Those four formulations are not interchangeable.

They describe four different political and psychological relations to change:

1. **self-directed transformation**;
2. **captured relational transformation**;
3. **self-declared relational responsibility**;
4. **counterfactual retrospective integration**.

The strongest final character thesis is:

> **Saki does not die because she was incapable of becoming someone else. She dies after repeatedly proving that she can become almost anything an environment requires—friend, girlfriend, worker, commodity, “adult,” addict, survivor, prospective mother—and only very late regains enough strategic authorship to ask what kind of person she herself wants those capacities to serve.**

---

# 1. Early isolation

Saki’s opening state is often remembered as “lonely bullied girl,” but the Japanese text complicates that immediately.

She has spent all three middle-school years without a friend. Yet other girls had invited her to join them. Saki refused because she anticipated humiliation. She imagined that someone like her entering those groups would be mocked.

That distinction gives her isolation an important dual structure:

- **deprivation** — she genuinely lacks friendship and social experience;
- **defense** — avoiding social participation protects her from anticipated ridicule.

As the strategy stabilizes, fear becomes contempt. Saki watches socially active classmates from a distance and tells herself their behavior is ridiculous. This is not evidence that she never wanted what they had. It is evidence that contempt has become a way to reduce the pain of wanting what she feels unable to enter.

By graduation she recognizes what the strategy has done to her. The crisis is not simply:

> nobody likes me.

It is:

> **I have become someone I dislike while protecting myself from the possibility that other people might dislike me.**

That is why `変わりたい` matters.

The first transformation begins from self-recognition, not male pressure.

Saki identifies a form of life she does not want to continue and acts.

This makes early Saki more agentic than either a corruption narrative or a pure-victim narrative permits.

Her problem is not lack of desire.

It is lack of confidence that desire can survive exposure to other people.

---

# 2. Makeover as self-authored transformation

The first makeover is one of the most important frozen facts in the entire corpus.

Nothing later is allowed to convert it retrospectively into the “first mistake.”

Saki:

- initiates it;
- asks her mother for help;
- participates actively in changing hair, makeup, clothing, and presentation;
- experiences the result as exciting;
- receives warm family recognition;
- enters high school with a concrete social goal.

The transformation is therefore best classified as:

> **`SELF_AUTHORED` with supportive maternal co-authorship.**

The body is not initially an imposed sexual object.

It is a project through which Saki tests whether presentation can help her enter relationships she previously feared.

And it works.

Her mother becomes excited to share knowledge with her. Her father responds with unexpected warmth. Female classmates approach her. They invite her out. Plans are made.

The makeover therefore produces:

- family reconnection;
- peer access;
- social confidence;
- curiosity;
- and evidence that Saki’s previous isolation was not destiny.

This is essential to the work’s anti-fatalist structure.

If transformation itself were the moral error, Chapter 1 would have made it fail.

Instead, the story makes transformation **genuinely useful** before asking what happens when a person who has discovered the power of adaptation becomes dependent upon other people to tell her which adaptations count.

---

# 3. Desire for visibility

Saki does not initially want fame.

She wants **legibility**.

She wants to be the kind of person other people can comfortably approach, invite, compliment, and include.

That means appearance functions as social technology.

The new presentation tells the world:

> I know enough of the code to participate.

But because Saki has little prior experience of ordinary peer culture, she cannot yet distinguish confidently between:

- being visible;
- being liked;
- being sexually desired;
- being socially accepted;
- being loved.

Those categories remain dangerously close together.

The Japanese captures the mechanism in:

> **「カワイイって言われるの気持ちいいし……なんか、自分が好きになれそう！」**  
> *Being told I’m cute feels good... somehow I feel like I might be able to like myself.*  
> `JP_CBZ_IMG_0013 / printed p.010`

The critical form is `好きになれそう`.

Saki does not say:

> I now love myself.

She says:

> **I might become able to.**

Self-liking has become a possibility mediated by reflected evaluation.

That creates a vulnerability without making the original desire pathological.

Recognition is genuinely nourishing for Saki.

The problem is that she begins allowing recognition to function as **evidence** about what she is.

---

# 4. First social recognition

The healthiest social recognition in the manga arrives before Hayato.

Female classmates invite Saki out.

She experiences ordinary friendship with wonder. She makes future plans. Nobody needs to sexually desire her in order for the transformed self to work.

This point is indispensable because later catastrophe otherwise creates a false retrospective memory:

> the makeover only ever attracted predators.

That is not what Chapter 1 says.

The makeover also creates:

- friendship;
- social pleasure;
- family warmth;
- ordinary adolescent possibility.

This means the work’s tragedy is not:

> visibility is intrinsically dangerous.

It is:

> **the forms of visibility available to Saki become progressively dominated by people who can extract value from being able to see her.**

Early peer recognition establishes the counterfactual norm against which later exploitative recognition must be measured.

---

# 5. Sexual curiosity and experimentation

A continuity-centered reading must not erase Saki’s sexuality in order to defend her from exploitation.

Chapter 1 makes clear that:

- she enjoys kissing;
- sexual attention can excite her;
- she experiences bodily pleasure;
- she is curious about intimacy;
- she is capable of romanticizing sexual experience.

Those facts remain compatible with:

- manipulation;
- intoxication;
- inability to refuse effectively;
- boundary violation;
- and later coercion.

This matters because a simplistic corruption model often depends upon constructing early Saki as sexually blank.

The Japanese text does not support that.

Her sexuality is not the tragedy.

The tragedy is that other people repeatedly use:

- bodily response;
- prior sexual behavior;
- drug-altered behavior;
- occupation;
- and reputation

as evidence that present consent can be presumed.

The work repeatedly forces a distinction between:

> **having sexual desire**

and:

> **authoring the conditions under which sexual activity occurs.**

Saki continues possessing the first while frequently losing the second.

---

# 6. Self-image and body image

Saki’s body becomes one of the work’s central archives of authorship.

The progression is not simply:

> modest body → sexualized body.

It is:

## Chapter 1 — self-authored body

Makeup, hair, clothing.

Purpose:

> social participation.

## Chapter 2 — socially maintained body

Status goods and fashion competence become part of belonging.

Purpose:

> remain legible among peers.

## Chapter 3 — externally interpreted body

The body is increasingly treated as proof of sexual character regardless of the conditions that produced what others saw.

Purpose supplied by others:

> evidence.

## Chapter 4 — relationship-conditioned body

Hair, tanning, piercings, tattooing, sexual presentation.

Saki says:

> **「ハヤトがその方が好きなら…なんでも♡」**

and:

> **「私をハヤトのモノにして♡」**

The body becomes a love token, ownership surface, and commercial asset.

## Chapter 5 — market body

A client explicitly tells Saki that she has lowered her own `価値`—value—through changes that Hayato preferred.

The body becomes segmented by competing male markets.

## Chapter 6 — historical body

Hayato is gone, but the `HAYATO` mark remains.

When strangers ask about it, Saki says it is essentially nothing.

The inscription outlives the relationship that gave it meaning.

## Finale — future-bearing and injured body

Pregnancy gives the body a long temporal horizon. Commercial exploitation still prices it. Final violence attacks both body and future. The imagined adult Saki then appears in a visually normalized, ordinary maternal form.

The strongest conclusion is:

> **Saki’s body is not a morality meter. It is a record of who had enough power at different times to define what change was for.**

---

# 7. Social performance

The first high-school self-introduction reveals that Saki’s new identity is not yet integrated.

She begins:

> **「趣味は……読書とゲー……」**

then corrects herself toward shopping.

She also thinks:

> **「普通の人を装っている」**  
> *presenting/disguising herself as an ordinary person.*  
> `JP_CBZ_IMG_0010 / printed p.007`

This does not mean the new Saki is fake.

It means she has an imagined model of normal girlhood and is actively editing herself toward it.

The distinction between:

- constructed;
- performed;
- false

is crucial.

All social identity requires performance.

Saki’s vulnerability lies not in performing, but in lacking enough relational security to decide which performances are optional.

Shopping, fashion, sexual confidence, adultness, commercial fluency, even drug tolerance eventually become things she can perform because she believes a valued social position depends upon them.

The performance becomes dangerous when failure appears equivalent to:

> exclusion.

---

# 8. Pleasure, risk, and ambiguity

Saki often experiences pleasure inside situations that are ethically and structurally dangerous.

The analysis must preserve both facts.

Her pleasure can be:

- physical;
- relational;
- chemical;
- social;
- economic;
- or the relief of being desired.

None automatically proves:

- free consent;
- autonomous decision-environment authorship;
- or endorsement of everything occurring around the pleasure.

The manga repeatedly dramatizes this ambiguity because its pornographic form depends upon bodily responsiveness while its narrative increasingly supplies refusal, fear, coercion, shame, and consequence.

For Saki as a character, the important point is that she does not experience herself only as a victim.

She often finds things she wants inside environments she does not control.

That is precisely why the environments can become durable.

Hayato offers:

- affection;
- sex;
- adult recognition;
- housing;
- work;
- drugs;
- a future-child promise.

Clients can offer:

- money;
- warmth;
- being held.

Drugs offer:

- relief;
- disinhibition;
- eventually `居場所`.

An exploitative system is more stable when it satisfies real needs intermittently.

---

# 9. Money and material dependence

Money’s meaning changes as Saki changes.

## Chapter 2

Money is discretionary but socially important.

It can buy:

- accessories;
- fashion participation;
- visible peer fluency.

Saki is not yet materially poor.

## Chapter 3

Her father loses his job. Saki volunteers to work and help. The family refuses the offer initially. Money now becomes imaginable as family contribution, but the chapter does not establish Saki supporting the household with sex-work earnings.

## Chapter 4

Money becomes debt service for Hayato’s world.

Commercial sex becomes organized labor.

Saki becomes increasingly competent at prices, bookings, customers, and reporting.

## Chapter 5

The abortion itself becomes an expense, producing a recursive structure:

> sex → pregnancy → abortion → expense → more need for money → more sex.

Market language enters Saki’s self-conception through `価値`.

## Chapter 6

The work economy collapses.

Saki chooses drugs over cash despite needing Hayato’s quota, is expelled, and becomes homeless.

Money reaches zero independent-security value.

## Finale

Economic authorship partially returns.

Saki saves a substantial amount and says:

> **「私のお金なの……っ！」**  
> *It’s my money.*

and that it is for the child.

This is a major identity reversal after Hayato’s earlier `俺の金`.

For the first time in several chapters, accumulated money serves a purpose Saki has chosen.

Its theft therefore destroys not just property but a materialized future project.

---

# 10. Substance-use progression

Saki’s relationship to drugs should be described developmentally and without unsupported pharmacological specificity.

## First exposure

The initial unidentified pill is introduced in a situation where Saki internally recognizes that she wanted to refuse:

> **「今断りたかったのに……出来なかった……」**

Acute impairment follows.

## Repetition

By Chapter 2 Saki actively seeks the drug while simultaneously saying:

> **「自分が自分じゃなくなるようで…怖い」**  
> *It feels as though I stop being myself... and that scares me.*  
> `JP_CBZ_IMG_0035 / printed p.032`

Desire and fear coexist.

## Work environment

Chapter 4 integrates stronger drugs into Hayato’s “adult” business world.

Saki takes a frightening substance while commanding herself:

> **「大人になれ…っ！」**

## Chemical belonging

Chapter 5 culminates in injected intoxication experienced as:

> **「私が求めていた居場所」**  
> *the place where I had been trying to belong.*

The drug now supplies more than pleasure. It supplies a temporary answer to Saki’s oldest social need.

## Priority conflict

Chapter 6 makes the dependence-like structure behaviorally explicit. Given a direct choice between urgently needed cash and drugs, Saki chooses drugs.

She later explains that work is hard and the drugs make it easier.

## Attempted recovery

Chapter 6’s child-oriented future includes:

> **「クスリも もうしない」**

The Finale proves the intention was real enough to affect behavior: Saki recognizes fetal risk, delays, reduces, and rations use.

But she cannot simply will abstinence into existence.

The strongest character conclusion is:

> **drugs move from externally authored impairment, to recurrent reward, to work adaptation, to belonging substitute, to competing master preference, and finally to the object Saki recognizes she must give up if she is to inhabit a future.**

---

# 11. Relationship dependency

Hayato matters not because he causes every catastrophe.

He does not.

His significance is that he becomes the **concentrator** of dependencies produced by several different collapses.

After Chapter 3, Saki loses or experiences as unavailable:

- school belonging;
- female friendship;
- parental protection;
- housing.

She turns to Hayato only after attempting to use the healthier maternal relationship and being refused.

Her message is:

> **「もうハヤトしかいないの」**  
> *Hayato, you’re the only person I have left.*

That line captures subjective option collapse.

Chapter 4 then ties Hayato to:

- shelter;
- love;
- drugs;
- work;
- money;
- body presentation;
- adulthood;
- future imagination.

This is why Saki’s later adoption of ownership language is psychologically intelligible:

> **「私ハヤトのモノだもんね」**

Property language feels like permanence to someone whose other relationships have disappeared.

Hayato’s eventual expulsion of Saki proves that the permanence was asymmetrical.

He can revoke what Saki experienced as belonging.

The mark remains.

---

# 12. Shame

Shame is one of Saki’s most consistent inhibitors of rescue.

In Chapter 2, her mother is still explicitly supportive. Saki cannot disclose the paid encounter because she is ashamed.

This is important because care is **available but unusable**.

Chapter 3 changes the situation.

Saki tries to seek maternal help.

She begins:

> **「お父さんが――」**

and is silenced.

The progression is therefore:

> cannot disclose → attempts disclosure → listener refuses disclosure.

Shame later becomes increasingly internalized as identity.

By the Finale, after robbery and assault, Saki does not narrate herself primarily as someone harmed by external people.

She says:

> **「私……弱い人間だから」**

The social explanations that once lived outside her have become self-description.

---

# 13. Increasing loss of alternative identities

Saki’s tragedy can be tracked as a contraction of identities she can plausibly inhabit.

## Chapter 1

Possible identities include:

- daughter;
- student;
- friend;
- reader/gamer;
- fashionable girl;
- romantic partner;
- future adult.

## Chapter 2

The visible school identity remains, but a hidden sexual/economic identity forms.

## Chapter 3

Other people begin collapsing those identities into one:

> sexually available girl.

Blackmailers, peers, father, and mother each erase different contexts.

## Chapter 4

Saki herself increasingly adopts a narrower identity cluster:

- Hayato’s woman;
- adult;
- worker;
- property.

## Chapter 5

Market identity expands but not personhood:

- client value;
- self-recruiting worker;
- drug user;
- exchangeable body.

## Chapter 6

Hayato expels her, and even the relational identity that organized the narrowed self disappears.

The `HAYATO` mark remains as an identity whose institution has vanished.

## Endpoint reopening

Pregnancy creates a new identity possibility:

> mother / caregiver.

This is the first major identity after Hayato whose current environment does not supply the meaning for her.

---

# 14. Linguistic continuity and discontinuity

The Japanese-language audit materially revises any claim that Saki becomes a wholly different person.

Her first-person reference remains:

> **私**.

More important than the pronoun alone, she repeatedly retains:

- ellipsis;
- hesitation;
- self-correction;
- fear of burdening others;
- questions about whether she is enough;
- relational gratitude;
- care language.

What changes most dramatically is the **lexicon available for interpreting herself**.

She acquires the vocabularies of successive environments:

- `普通` — normality;
- `大人` — adulthood;
- `言うこと / 言うとおり` — obedience;
- `モノ` — property;
- `価値` — market value;
- `居場所` — belonging;
- `ビジネス` — business;
- `捨てる` — disposal;
- `弱い人間` — weak person.

The strongest formulation is:

> **Saki’s voice is colonized lexically more than replaced structurally.**

Late Saki sounds like early Saki speaking through vocabularies she has learned from people and systems with greater power to define reality.

The imagined adult future is powerful because the vocabulary changes again:

- `昔` — the past;
- what mattered to me;
- Hana;
- `生まれ変われた` — I was able to be reborn.

That future voice has enough temporal distance to make earlier suffering biography rather than permanent present.

---

# 15. Body-state change

Saki’s bodily changes are extreme, but they do not form a single moral line from purity to degradation.

The body changes because different actors possess different forms of authorship.

| Phase | Body state | Dominant authorship | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ch1 | makeup, hair, clothes | Saki + mother | social possibility |
| Ch2 | fashion/status display | Saki under peer-performance pressure | maintenance of belonging |
| Ch3 | mostly same body, radically changed social reading | peers/family/abusers | evidence against Saki |
| Ch4 | Hayato-directed styling, modification, mark | relationship-conditioned + economic | love/property/market |
| Ch5 | pregnancy/abortion; client market judgments | overlapping systems | reproductive consequence and price |
| Ch6 | homeless, marked, impaired | material collapse | historical residue and disposability |
| Finale | advanced pregnancy, saved money, injury; imagined adult body | Saki’s future project, then attackers | futurity, then violent closure |

The body therefore records:

> **who has the power to make a transformation socially meaningful.**

---

# 16. Future-horizon contraction

Saki’s identity changes most profoundly when the distance into the future changes.

## Chapter 1 — `F5`

High school, friendship, weekends, possible romance, adulthood.

The future expands.

## Chapter 2 — `F4`

Still school-centered, but increasingly maintained through immediate money and drugs.

## Chapter 3 — `F2`

After expulsion:

> where can I go?

The future becomes shelter.

## Chapter 4 — `F3`

Hayato gives structure—bookings, debt, routines—but not an independently authored long future.

## Chapter 5 — `F0`

The drug state culminates in:

> **「もうずっと此処に居たい」**

The desired future becomes indefinite continuation of altered present.

## Chapter 6 — `FR`

Pregnancy reopens months and years.

Saki says:

- give birth;
- raise;
- cherish;
- stop drugs;
- change.

## Finale — `F5 → F0 → counterfactual FR`

Saki partially enacts the future:

- pregnancy advances;
- money is saved;
- drug harm reduction is attempted.

Violence closes the branch.

The imagined adult future then preserves what has become inaccessible.

Future horizon is therefore one of the clearest measures of Saki’s personhood under pressure.

---

# 17. Motherhood and future imagination

Motherhood should neither be romanticized nor dismissed as conservative purification.

The Chapter 6 pregnancy matters first because it creates a decision that Saki makes **against the immediate expectation of the people around her**.

She says:

> **「……産む」**

then:

> **「産みます」**

and:

> **「もう二度と――お腹の赤ちゃんを見捨てたりなんか…しない」**

The disposal vocabulary is crucial.

Chapter 6 begins with Saki being literally dumped.

Hayato then discards her relationally:

> **「捨てられちゃった」**

The future project reverses direction:

> **I will not abandon.**

This gives motherhood an ethical meaning beyond biological reproduction.

The repeatedly discarded person chooses non-discarding as a principle.

There is still a legitimate counterreading:

> Saki once again organizes transformation around another person rather than explicitly saying Saki herself is sufficient reason to live differently.

That ambiguity should remain.

But a child-oriented telos is not ethically equivalent to Hayato’s telos.

Hayato’s project requires:

- obedience;
- earnings;
- commodification;
- bodily adaptation.

Saki’s child project requires, as she articulates it:

- sobriety;
- care;
- protection;
- saving;
- endurance;
- non-abandonment.

The Finale proves the project was behaviorally real.

That is why later catastrophe cannot be used to say Chapter 6 was empty sentiment.

---

# 18. Final retrospective view of early Saki

The imagined adult Saki says:

> **「ママね……自分の事が嫌いだったの……」**  
> *Mama used to hate herself.*

This returns directly to the earliest wound.

The full story has supplied many possible moralizing summaries of Saki:

- she became promiscuous;
- she used drugs;
- she entered sex work;
- she abandoned school;
- she made dangerous choices.

The imagined future does not choose those as the foundational description.

It identifies:

> **self-hatred.**

Adult Saki then wonders whether she simply did not yet understand what was important to her.

That is a much less punitive retrospective model.

Early Saki was not a pure self later destroyed by impurity.

She was a lonely adolescent with an unstable theory of value.

The whole tragedy can be read as the history of other people repeatedly offering her answers to:

> what makes you valuable?

The answers become:

- cute;
- sexually desirable;
- entertaining;
- useful;
- profitable;
- obedient;
- available;
- chemically absent.

The imagined adult future finally answers through ordinary care and temporality rather than market recognition.

# 19. Strongest counterreadings

## A. Corruption narrative

The corruption reading has genuine textual support.

Saki moves from:

- socially inexperienced;
- family-housed;
- school-attending;
- sober;
- inexperienced

toward:

- commercial sexual labor;
- escalating drugs;
- body modification;
- homelessness;
- severe dependence-like behavior;
- apparent overdose death.

The manga’s eromanga structure deliberately codes many of these changes as degradation.

It would be evasive to deny that the work uses corruption iconography.

But corruption alone is too blunt because it tends to imply:

1. early Saki possessed a morally pure stable essence;
2. later sexuality or appearance is itself the corruption;
3. later Saki is less truly Saki.

The Japanese evidence undermines all three.

## B. Pure continuity narrative

The opposite reading can also become too easy.

Saying “Saki never changes inside” would erase:

- genuine drug-induced state changes;
- market vocabulary entering self-conception;
- obedience becoming generalized;
- adulthood becoming ideology;
- narrowing future horizons;
- self-blame becoming causal theory;
- changes in what she can imagine as normal.

Continuity is not stasis.

## C. Strongest combined reading

The work is most accurately described as:

> **continuous personhood undergoing radical adaptive transformation under increasingly captured authorship.**

Saki remains recognizable enough that the losses matter.

She changes enough that the word 『変身』 remains deserved.

---

# 20. Final character thesis

Yoshida Saki’s defining trait is not innocence.

It is **adaptability in search of belonging**.

She can learn:

- makeup;
- fashion;
- peer conversation;
- romance scripts;
- sexual performance;
- commercial negotiation;
- customer recruitment;
- price management;
- drug routines;
- survival barter;
- saving;
- caregiving intention.

This is an extraordinary capacity.

But adaptability is ethically neutral.

It can be:

- self-development;
- relational reciprocity;
- social camouflage;
- coerced accommodation;
- market optimization;
- dependency maintenance;
- survival.

What changes across 『変身』 is increasingly **who supplies the criterion of successful adaptation**.

Early Saki asks:

> what would let me enter the life I want?

Hayato’s system teaches her to ask:

> what would make me valuable, useful, adult, desirable, profitable, and worth keeping?

Drug dependence eventually offers:

> what if I could stop needing to answer at all?

The Chapter 6 future reopens another question:

> what kind of person would I need to become in order to care for someone without abandoning them?

And the imagined adult future supplies the final impossible answer:

> a person who survived long enough to understand the past as past.

The definitive character formulation is therefore:

> **Saki is not destroyed by change. She is destroyed in a world where her exceptional capacity to change is repeatedly made answerable to other people’s definitions of value before she develops a stable enough basis for deciding what change should be for. Her personhood survives the transformations; her authorship over their purpose does not. The late recovery branch matters because it proves that authorship can return—and that the final tragedy closes a real future rather than revealing that no alternative Saki ever existed.**
