# A path Shuro had already taken

When Kokuriko prevents Azami from threatening Shuro, Shuro has already written the story that takes her out of her expected role. Kokuriko says that if Shuro has found her own path, she need not be forced back. The act changes the immediate terms on which Shuro can continue. It also shows who can decide whether force will be used. Shuro authors the departure, Azami is in a position to threaten her, and Kokuriko restrains Azami. The three positions matter together. Permission has a real consequence for the person receiving it even when the person who grants it keeps the power to decide. [^allowance]

The earlier exchange between Shuro and Kokuriko gives that decision its weight. At the end of the first chapter, Shuro reports to Kokuriko that she failed to make the intended 百物語. She has previously claimed completion, and the city has suffered actual damage. Her admission names a failure of the intended work while leaving the harm of the attempt intact. Kokuriko accepts the failure and talks about gradually constructing another story that will burn the world. Shuro can acknowledge what she failed to do and still receive an invitation to continue. The invitation puts that tolerance alongside Kokuriko’s continuing destructive purpose. This is the earliest direct exchange between them used here. Their first meeting and the terms under which Shuro began to serve remain open. [^failure]

Kokuriko’s praise in the second chapter belongs to the same uncertain arrangement. She reports that Hyakkaryouran has been repelled and Sensei captured, and praises both Shuro and Azami. Shuro still fears being discarded. She asks why a tale on her own side attacked her and whether the gaps in her memory mean she might disappear. Praise recognizes her work in the present while offering little security against the future she fears. Her questions give that uncertainty a specific shape and leave the origin of her memory gaps and the source of the attack unresolved. [^fear]

Azami’s service has a different shape. The account puts her on the antagonist side before the travelling party meets her as an Ebisu helper. That difference between what readers know and what the travellers know makes her diversion to lodging suspect. It leaves room for the residents and produce the party encounters to be part of ordinary town life. When Azami later admits her affiliation and escort assignment, she addresses the travellers. Her work, Kokuriko’s praise, and Kokuriko’s later intervention together support an inference that Azami acts within Kokuriko’s hierarchy. The admission tells the travellers what she was doing. Her orientation toward Kokuriko remains an inference from her work and the way Kokuriko responds to it. [^affiliation]

In the mansion, the group’s search turns up a harmless private manuscript under Shuro’s pen name, 朽木修羅. The maintained account identifies a piece of work that belongs to her. The fact that others encounter it through a search gives that encounter different terms from a gift or an invited reading. The full text, genre, and intended audience remain unreceived. Shuro has written something harmless for reasons the chapter leaves with her. Its interest as part of her life does not depend on the later story’s ability to help anyone in a fight. [^manuscript]

The attack in that same stretch of the story puts limits on how much of Shuro’s conduct she can explain away as acting. Creatures directly attack her and the account represents her inward fear of abandonment. Sensei reaches her despite his reported pain. Her later boast that the course of events was staged has to be read beside the earlier panic. The person who worries that she may be discarded is also the person who later holds Sensei hostage under threat of a gun and releases the group to look for Kuzunoha. His earlier help and her later coercion belong to the same history, with the conduct and its costs still attributable to her. Fear makes her position in the group more legible. It leaves the harm she causes in view. [^fear] [^hostage]

Azami likewise has a part in the operation beyond her eventual position beside Shuro. She proposes a name for an Ayame-like figure and urges it to turn the gaze back on the public. Later she sees Hitotsume thin when Chise sings and residents hear her. She acknowledges what joy can do on that occasion and doubts the effect will last beyond the festival. Niya’s later admission that her account of an old festival law was a bluff leaves the local observation in place. The observation and warning give Azami a record of initiative within the operation. Kokuriko’s restraint later limits one particular action Azami is in a position to take. [^song]

By the time Shuro writes in front of others, the scene distributes help and authorship among several people. Niya shows her a blank vessel reconstructed from remnants of the burned book. The repair of its material is attributed to an unnamed student. Niya appeals to Shuro’s pride in writing and to her resentment of comments. The appeal is strategic and the opportunity is supplied by others. Shuro professes loyalty to Kokuriko while composing a new cat, lion, and ninja tale. The blank vessel gives her a place to put words, and the words are Shuro’s. Both the strategy that brings her to write and her decision to write belong to the occasion. [^writing]

The resulting laughter is part of what she makes possible. A doodle-like creature appears, people laugh, foes stagger, and a shot lands against a type the party had found untouchable. Izuna reports new power. These events give the new story a local practical effect. They also let people enjoy something Shuro made in a scene crowded by fear. The laughter has its own value to the people who laugh. The earlier private manuscript makes it possible to recognize a continuity in her creative activity across very different circumstances. Its harmlessness and the new tale’s humor carry meaning at their own scale. The source leaves the exact text and range of the effect open. [^manuscript] [^writing]

Kokuriko’s intervention comes after this act of writing. The close pairing of Azami’s impending threat with Kokuriko’s statement about forced return makes enforcement of an expected return a plausible reading of Azami’s position. That reading is an inference from the two acts in the scene. The account gives us the threat Kokuriko prevents and the conditional allowance she states. It leaves any prior return order and Azami’s reply unrecorded. Azami’s threatened action toward Shuro supplies the practical force that the allowance interrupts. The account gives Azami a consequential position and leaves Shuro’s response to her unrecorded. Their private relation remains open. [^affiliation] [^allowance]

In that moment Kokuriko also changes the terms of Azami’s service. The person who praised Azami’s work now prevents an action she is in a position to take. Praise and restriction are both available within the hierarchy. Shuro’s different act precedes this limit on enforcement, while her profession of loyalty is still the last explicit allegiance supplied here. The case for reading Kokuriko’s decision instrumentally is substantial. She has already accepted a failed attempt while inviting a further destructive project, and she praises people for work that helps her. A Shuro who can make something unexpected could remain useful. It is also possible that the immediate cost or inconvenience of enforcing return makes intervention attractive on this occasion. Both possibilities remain readings of the situation. The action can still count as particular restraint. A person may be spared pressure in an encounter by someone whose purposes remain hostile. Equally, the earlier manuscript and the fact of Shuro’s new writing before Kokuriko’s permission give little purchase to a story in which all of Shuro’s activity can be assigned to a single directing will.
