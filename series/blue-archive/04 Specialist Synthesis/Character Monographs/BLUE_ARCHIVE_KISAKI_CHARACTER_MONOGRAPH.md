## 1. Core thesis: accepting responsibility does not make office the purpose of a life

Kisaki's strongest sustained distinction is between being responsible for a school and being a person whose life belongs entirely to that responsibility. She says people do not live to work, finishes necessary work efficiently, enjoys exercise she believes private and sometimes wants to sleep late. She also makes actual public apologies, interrupts unsupported accusations and refuses an offered relief purchased through renewed exploitation of students. These statements and acts support a serious commitment to others without proving that she chose headship, that leadership is her only desire or that suffering is her proper reward. Her statement that she never chose to become head is especially consequential beside Rumi's chosen cooking vocation and their remembered imperfect mooncakes (BA:group:3401:u0001–0006;3402:u0001–0015;BA:event:840:004:u0091–0138;007:u0058–0077).

The monograph's thesis is that Kisaki tries to make an inherited public position answerable to lives that exceed their functions, including her own. She can conceive tradition as protection and gratitude rather than fixed repetition, want strangers to enter the school and allow personal address outside office. Yet this is a fallible, incompletely realized practice. Her members can turn a welcome into interrogation and captivity; her confidence that Teacher's presence prevents conflict is contradicted by actual confrontation; the past she recounts includes procedural shortcuts and erased records. Her own affection can press on the adult's finite availability or bodily boundaries. Wanted company, bodily pleasure and accountable public action coexist with unequal power; neither side licenses forgetting the other (BA:event:826:009:u0059–0118;839:002:u0016–0084/003:u0021–0165;840:010:u0058–0082;859:002:u0001–0032).

Ordinary evidence has standing here before it becomes an explanation of leadership. Tea with a friend, crepes, a roof's horizon, a boat's scenery, fingers warmed together, a chosen gift, watching water and wanting to be carried are ends represented as worth having. They need not restore health, solve factionalism or produce a crisis victory to count. Conversely, the fact that Kisaki enjoys or requests something does not establish that every other participant wants it. Her life is more legible when the analysis preserves both its pleasures and the exact recipients, objections and finite outcomes around them.
