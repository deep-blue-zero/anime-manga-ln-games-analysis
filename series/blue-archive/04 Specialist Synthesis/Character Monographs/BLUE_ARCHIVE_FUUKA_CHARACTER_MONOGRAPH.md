## 1. Core thesis: culinary care needs a recipient, and its maker needs a life

Fuuka gives the series a demanding account of everyday care. Preparing food matters because someone wants to cook and someone else wants to eat; the quantity produced, the provider's good intention and the recipient's gratitude do not independently settle whether a particular act respects either person. She has substantial practical knowledge, actual pleasurable meals and completed assistance. She also overprovides, makes uncertain medical explanations, briefly considers an alarming concealment, and repeatedly works amid demands that override her objections. Her strongest literary distinction is therefore the effort to make care particular: ask what this person likes, notice what this body can manage, distinguish requested help from commandeered work, and preserve the cook's own desired company, rest and recognition. That effort has real local achievements and serious failures. It is neither an empirical rule of safe food nor a declaration that her future conduct is always prudent.

The quiet sources are essential to this thesis. Fresh produce interests her before it becomes institutional supply. A soba meal ends in conversation and actual home accompaniment. Clothing and hair can be uncertain, a ride can be wanted and frightening, and peace can be wished for without being achieved. These are independent ends, not decorative proof that she deserves public rescue. The written repertoire similarly gives home cooking, lunchboxes, recipes, particular holiday foods and the wish not to need a weapon. A character reduced to exhausted cafeteria throughput would lose the distinctions that make imposed kitchen help, an interrupted holiday and a chosen meal invitation analytically legible. [F01–F04, F09–F11; `BA:bond:20022:003:scene:001:u:0028,0038`]
