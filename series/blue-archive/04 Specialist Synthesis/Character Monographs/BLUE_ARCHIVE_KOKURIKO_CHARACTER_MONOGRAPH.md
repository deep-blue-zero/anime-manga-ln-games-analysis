# Kokuriko — authority, permission and the limits of authorship

Kokuriko’s authority is most revealing where she chooses not to enforce it. She accepts Shuro’s failed work, later prevents Azami from threatening her, and says that Shuro need not be forced back if she has found her own path. The same person proposes another world-burning story, acknowledges pressure on Ayame and admits covering the Hyakka students’ sight during an imposed confrontation with their fears. Permission therefore belongs inside an account of coercion. It changes what can be said about her conduct without settling why she permits this particular divergence. The strongest reading of these encounters holds both facts together: Kokuriko exercises consequential authority, yet her treatment of another person’s course is selective rather than exhaustively possessive. That selectivity may itself serve control.[^1]

The distinction matters because the available story is unevenly known. These two maintained chapter readings and their contextual synthesis preserve represented present actions alongside Nagusa’s firsthand recollection, Kokuriko’s historical claims, Shuro’s inward fears and later reports of aftermath. Those forms do not carry equal authority. A witnessed loss is stronger than a hostile taunt, but it is not a replay of the original incident; a staged effect does not authenticate every explanation offered for it. Reading Chapter 2 after Chapter 1 enlarges the account of Kokuriko’s conduct. It does not supply an omniscient biography, a sampled private life or a demonstrated conversion. The argument concerns the choices actually represented and the differences they leave open.[^2]

Nagusa first places Kokuriko within an intimate loss. She testifies that the director of 花鳥風月部 turned Ayame into a 百物語 and took her beyond 黄昏 at the Great Snowfield. The testimony gives Kokuriko a place in Nagusa’s remembered injury and separation; it does not prove Ayame’s death or establish the identity of every later Ayame-shaped presence. Kokuriko’s subsequent affirmation that she pressured Ayame gives adverse conduct a second, differently situated warrant. It does not make Nagusa’s entire memory transparent or turn Kokuriko’s own account into a complete confession. The asymmetry is essential: Nagusa speaks from loss, while Kokuriko later speaks as someone who can acknowledge pressure and supply a location without accounting for all its consequences.[^3]

Her office helps explain this asymmetry but cannot replace it. Nagusa identifies her as director; Azami later admits allegiance and an escort assignment, and Kokuriko praises the two operatives and overrules Azami’s threatened action. That is enough to establish situated leadership. It is insufficient to make every creature, interruption or subordinate decision the execution of a single order. Her claim to a Kadeno and Hyakka past likewise belongs to the encounter, while the mansion’s diary-like account offers a possible historical connection. Neither produces an inspected charter, a complete succession record or a verified account of the earlier unnamed miko. Institutional position gives her words force without supplying the missing history that would make that force fully legible.[^4]

The first chapter’s closing exchange already joins latitude to a hostile project. Shuro admits that the intended 百物語 failed. Kokuriko accepts the failure while claiming that the twenty-year festival has been reenacted and inviting the gradual making of another story that would burn the world. Failure is neither denied nor made the end of the relation. Yet the proposed continuation is destructive, and the claim of reenactment remains hers. The exchange therefore does not reveal compassion simply because punishment is absent. It shows an authority able to accommodate failed work and preserve a future undertaking. That accommodation is a particular response to Shuro, not proof that the undertaking was secretly harmless or that the intended completed work actually existed.[^5]

Acceptance nevertheless has relational meaning. A response that leaves room after failure differs from one that treats a subordinate as disposable, even when continued usefulness could explain it. Reducing the scene to utility would discard what Kokuriko actually allows; elevating it to benevolence would invent the inward reason. The narrower claim is stronger: she does not make this admitted failure an occasion for represented rejection, and the relation continues under the terms she proposes. What Shuro can safely expect from that relation remains another question. The later account refuses to let Kokuriko’s tolerance decide it on Shuro’s behalf.[^5]

Shuro’s fear supplies that refusal. When hostile creatures attack her, her panic and abandonment anxiety are represented as her own experience; later, even amid Kokuriko’s praise, she fears being discarded and wonders whether her memory gaps mean that she could disappear. These fears do not establish that Kokuriko threatened abandonment. They do establish that praise does not exhaust the recipient’s situation. Shuro’s later claim that everything was acting is constrained by the earlier attack and panic, just as an interpretation of secure belonging is constrained by her fear. Kokuriko has influence over a person whose inward response is not reducible to that influence, and whose uncertainty cannot be converted into Kokuriko’s private wound.[^6]

The praise is itself specific. Kokuriko commends Shuro and Azami while reporting that Hyakka has been repelled and Sensei captured. The later cellar scene confirms captivity separately from her initial report. This sequence supports a leader who assesses and addresses operatives within an ongoing operation; it does not prove perfect knowledge of every event or authorship of every danger. Azami’s guide and escort activity remains Azami’s, and Shuro’s uncertainty about why an allied tale attacked her remains unresolved. Kokuriko’s competence is visible in particular interventions and in the authority to set a limit. Its reach cannot be measured by assigning to her every event that occurs on the antagonist side.[^6]

Shuro’s writing makes the limit of such assignment especially clear. The harmless manuscript found under her pen name, 朽木修羅, is hers. Later Niya appeals to that writing pride and to resentment of comments, and Shuro writes a humorous cat, lion and ninja tale while still professing loyalty to Kokuriko. The visible creature, laughter and local countereffect give the new story an achieved consequence, without establishing stable allegiance or universal author control. Kokuriko does not become its hidden author because she later permits Shuro’s path. Shuro has already written; Niya has already made her interested appeal; the spectators have already laughed. Their separate acts precede and condition what Kokuriko can choose to do about the divergence.[^7]

At that point Kokuriko stops Azami from threatening Shuro and declines forced retrieval if Shuro has found a path of her own. The interruption has a concrete beneficiary: it places a limit on the coercive response Azami proposes. The allowance also recognizes a possibility beyond immediate obedience. Its significance does not depend on a formal resignation or permanent separation, neither of which is established. Nor does an unnamed special relation entitle the reader to invent parenthood, romance or a hidden promise. A threatened enforcement is blocked, and a particular course is allowed to remain open. Those local endpoints deserve their full weight.[^1]

They also deserve the strongest instrumental objection. Letting a useful subordinate depart can preserve allegiance, avoid a costly conflict, encourage further work or express confidence that control need not be exercised immediately. The account does not determine which, if any, explains Kokuriko. Her permission is therefore compatible with a strategic controller. What it constrains is the more exhaustive proposition that every subordinate choice is her authored performance, or that departure necessarily meets forced return. Shuro’s independently represented fear and authorship already resist that proposition. The permission makes the policy of immediate retrieval less absolute without disproving manipulation or revealing a benevolent inner essence. Selective latitude is an observable feature of this authority; its ultimate purpose remains unsettled.[^1][^6][^7]

The recipients of her authority are not interchangeable. Shuro receives tolerance of failure, praise and a blocked threat. Azami receives praise but also an override. Ayame is connected to admitted pressure and Nagusa’s loss testimony. Renge, Kikyou and Yukari are made to confront fears in a setting they have not freely chosen. To call all these relations protective would make the most favored instance govern everyone else. To call them all identical domination would lose the difference between stopping a threat and imposing exposure. Kokuriko’s conduct is ethically uneven in its actual directions, and the limited good of one intervention does not cancel the costs borne elsewhere.[^3][^6][^8]

Her account of the books makes this unevenness a problem of who may define another person. She claims an original set of three: desire-mirroring 稲生物怪録, fear-mirroring 稲亭物怪録 and person-mirroring 稲生霊怪録. The third is said to have been reconstituted after losing its form twenty years before. Yukari senses a book, but the object, restorer and activation are not displayed. The distinctions among the named books matter; their proximity does not make every story form the same entity or every claimed rule true. The person-mirror claim gives Kokuriko an interpretive position from which to present other people to themselves. It does not give the reader a complete technical account of how that position is produced.[^8]

The resulting dark self-scrutiny is consequential even within those limits. Renge, Kikyou and Yukari voice fears concerning youth, harshness and substitution, and Kokuriko admits covering their sight. Their speech can matter without becoming an objective and exhaustive biography. An induced disclosure is still an exposure imposed on someone, and a true fear need not be the truth of the whole person. The chapter has already challenged accounts that turn hurt, role-performance or hidden desire into universal worthlessness. Kokuriko’s method makes the ethical question sharper: who gets to choose the occasion and terms on which another person becomes visible? Calling the encounter insight or instruction would not by itself supply consent or erase its cost.[^8]

Nagusa’s response offers a different practice without becoming Kokuriko’s intended lesson. She acknowledges weakness without trying to be Ayame and extends a hand. Elsewhere she admits that she cannot define the real Ayame completely but wants to remain beside her. These are Nagusa’s acts of relation under uncertainty, not evidence that Kokuriko orchestrated a cure or benevolently engineered self-acceptance. The apparently present right arm startles Azami; its later return does not provide a complete causal explanation of the book encounter. Kokuriko’s admission and Nagusa’s answer occupy the same sequence, but sequence alone cannot transfer Nagusa’s choice or the bodily outcome into Kokuriko’s authorship.[^9]

Sensei’s position is similarly local. Kokuriko reports the capture, and the cellar confirms physical confinement; during the later encounter she says the book does not affect Sensei. This is opposition and an asserted limit, not evidence of intimacy or a universal immunity possessed by adults. Sensei’s rescue of Shuro, Niya’s appeal, Hyakka’s actions and the festival participants’ work remain distinct contributions. Kokuriko’s treatment of an adult opponent can be considered without turning that opponent into the sole effective agent or into the measure of every student’s inward life. The represented relation offers no secure basis for a private affinity, chosen mentorship or comprehensive account of what either knows.[^6][^9]

A different absence concerns ordinary life. Within these received accounts, Kokuriko has no independently described meal, hobby, leisure routine, private message or consensual everyday companionship. That limits a portrait of her private self; it does not make her an empty character or authorize a compensating invention. Failure acceptance, praise and permission retain their particular value where they occur, but they are not a secretly sampled domestic baseline. The distinction permits a character study proportionate to the evidence: sustained attention to sparse conduct, explicit uncertainty about unrepresented experience, and no requirement that a small relational act justify itself through the scale of a crisis.[^10]

The surrounding creative life reinforces this principle. Shuro’s humorous tale matters as something she writes and people enjoy, as well as through its local effect on hostile creatures. Chise’s singing and the residents’ hearing likewise remain actual acts when Niya’s purported ancient purging law is later withdrawn as a bluff. Pleasure does not become false because a historical explanation fails, and art does not acquire its only value when useful in a fight. These acts belong to their makers and audiences. Kokuriko’s story-project can be compared with them without appropriating their enjoyment as her taste, crediting their effects to her permission, or reducing the people she encounters to material for an antagonist’s narrative.[^7][^10]

The contextual comparison gives that surrounding life further texture: wanted company, food, craft, play and rest have achieved and unfinished forms of their own. A repaired object does not grant unlimited access to its maker; later enjoyment does not retroactively authorize unwanted participation; quiet company need not become a spectacle to be fulfilled. These goods are not Kokuriko’s private possessions by proximity, and they cannot date every public, event and private aftermath against one another. They nevertheless establish what a reading concerned only with crisis function would miss. Other people have purposes beyond obedience, threat and rescue. Kokuriko’s latitude to Shuro is one local relation to that independence, not ownership of the independent life itself.[^10]

Her reported language is most securely approached through speech acts: accepting a failed undertaking, proposing another, claiming an earlier history, praising operatives, limiting a threat and describing a mirror. The recurrence of story and book vocabulary supports a reading of authority over how people are represented. It does not supply an inspected Japanese idiolect. The maintained terms retain their separate referents, while exact sentence forms, pronouns, register, audible inward speech and performed delivery remain unavailable here. This matters for interpretation: a paraphrased allowance can establish the content of a represented decision without entitling the reader to hear tenderness, irony or detachment in an unreceived performance.[^11]

A purely destructive embodiment is therefore an incomplete reading, although it has a real warrant. The proposed world-burning story and the use of other people’s fears give Kokuriko a destructive narrative function. Yet a function cannot exhaust the decisions about failed work, threatened retrieval and unequal exposure. The reverse reduction, a powerless mythic boaster, also begins from something real: her historical and mechanical claims exceed what the accounts independently authenticate. It becomes too strong when it erases her admission of covered sight, the represented coercive encounter or the authority exercised over Azami. Uncertain mechanism leaves the scale and explanation of power open; it does not make every act or harm unreal.[^5][^8]

A historical wound may eventually explain more, but it cannot be used to fill those openings now. The diary-like family and festival rupture, together with Kokuriko’s Kadeno and Hyakka self-history, make resentment or revenge serious questions. They do not establish that she was the earlier unnamed miko, supply her age or prove a private trauma that causes the later project. A desire to be recognized as author or director is also conceivable from the way she frames people and stories, but the account does not depend on diagnosing such a need. The ethical force of pressuring Ayame and exposing the juniors survives without a complete origin story; the meaning of permitting Shuro likewise survives without a settled hidden motive.[^4][^8]

What develops across the two chapters is accordingly the reader’s access to Kokuriko’s authority. A retrospective account of loss is followed by her response to failure, then by praise, selective permission, a claim about personhood, acknowledged pressure and escape. This sequence complicates the portrait rather than demonstrating reform. It also does not establish one global calendar for every private episode and festival comparison. A local account can retain an impaired arm, a later returned arm and Ayame’s continuing sleep at their own represented stages without forcing every other source into an invented before or after. Kokuriko’s changing visibility is not yet a sustained record of changed conduct in an ordinary aftermath.[^2][^9][^10]

The endpoint leaves that distinction intact. Kokuriko escapes uncaptured, and the aftermath reports her, Azami and Shuro disappeared. Ayame physically returns but remains asleep; the public enemy-created explanation is an institutional simplification, not a complete identity test for every companion, hostile speaker, shadow or castle participant. Neither returned body nor official account settles Kokuriko’s motive, every book’s fate or later responsibility. There is no represented capture, durable reform or completed reckoning that would make the earlier coercion harmless in retrospect. There is also no basis to turn disappearance into death, permanent defeat or knowledge of where the three go next.[^9]

Kokuriko thus remains particular without becoming fully known. She can accommodate failed work, allow a subordinate’s divergent course and restrain an ally, while pursuing hostile creation and imposing visibility on people who did not choose it. The allowance may be instrumental; the pressure is not absolved by the allowance. Her authority is clearest in these differences of response and recipient, and its limits appear wherever another person’s fear, writing, refusal or care remains their own. A later account of motive, ordinary life or accountability could substantially revise this portrait. At the present endpoint, the warranted conclusion is narrower: selective permission is a meaningful exercise of coercive authority, not its disappearance and not a settled explanation of its purpose.[^1][^3][^8][^9]
