## 1. Core thesis: pleasure and giving do not need to wait for a completed rescue

Hiyori is often legible through the things she lacks: a reliable address, power, water, money, safe accommodation and an expectation of welcome. Yet her private argument is just as specifically about what she wants to do with access once it is available. She wants to read with the screen bright, look at fashion, collect magazines, see the sea, wear a cute swimsuit, taste something cold and give another person a pleasant experience. These are intrinsic ends. They need not become training, useful labor or proof of rehabilitation before they matter. [20017:002/003/005;10102:002/003/005;written20017/10102.]

The strongest change is a change in participation rather than an uninterrupted rise in confidence. Hiyori can receive batteries or magazines, ask for company and bodily care, initiate a thank-you outing, insist that a gift be accepted and choose a photograph over a replaceable magazine. That is a repertoire of preference, request and reciprocity. Fear, low self-appraisal, mistakes and scarcity remain inside it. The person who asks for help can also want to contribute something without proving that all help has become unnecessary. [Complete private15;EVENT810E075;EVENT838E021.]

Care has a double task in this argument: preserve the wanted experience and remain answerable for its means. A new swimsuit really brings joy, but presenting an adult purchase as a contest victory deceives the recipient. Staying at a hospital answers a request for company, while announced confiscation of the unfinished magazine remains a contested intervention. A gift’s acceptance answers her own request; the ensuing unrequested head pat is a different act. An affirmative ending need not certify everything that led to it. [20017:005;10102:002;EVENT810E075.]

Her characteristic mistake is to convert a local difficulty into a judgment about what her whole life is allowed to contain. A failed display can mean fireworks are unnecessary in her life; another person’s expression can become evidence that she is useless; pleasure can seem forbidden or destined to disappear. Those formulations are serious self-appraisals, not the narrator’s moral law. The text’s positive answer is often small and completed: magazines bought, a first taste, fireworks actually played together, a gift accepted, photographs taken. Its scale does not reduce its analytical weight. [20017:003;10102:003/005;EVENT810E075;EVENT838.]

The governing claim is therefore finite: particular pleasures and relationships become available without making hardship unreal. A monograph that waits for total recovery before recognizing them would reproduce Hiyori’s own tendency to deny that her life may have such things. Conversely, treating each pleasure as permanent rescue would erase the conditions that give the choice its weight.
