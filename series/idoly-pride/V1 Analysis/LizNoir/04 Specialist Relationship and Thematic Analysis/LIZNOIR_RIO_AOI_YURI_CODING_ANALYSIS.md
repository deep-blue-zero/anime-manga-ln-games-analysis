Yes — from the text and visuals together, Rio/Aoi is **very strongly yuri-coded**, but its actual “flavor” is not straightforward fluffy romance. It is more like:

**old-partner yuri: rival-spouses, co-dependent stage soulmates, with domestic intimacy hidden under professional pride.**

Not “they blush and confess” yuri. More like **two women whose lives, bodies, work, grief, and self-concepts have grown around each other so deeply that romance-coded imagery becomes almost inevitable.**

## The core dynamic: Rio is weight; Aoi is freedom

Their origin begins from opposition. Rio is effort, burden, obligation, family pressure, money, survival, and proof. Aoi enters as the person who names what Rio cannot see: **Rio’s dance is “heavy.”** Aoi’s own dance is described as the same choreography but transformed into something freer and more enjoyable, and Rio immediately recognizes that Aoi has something she lacks. origin\_liz\_001\_a\_budding\_lily

That is the first romantic-adjacent structural move: Aoi is not merely “better at dancing.” She is the missing element in Rio’s body. Rio has discipline without release; Aoi has release without structure. LizNoir is born because each woman exposes the other’s incompleteness.

So the emotional flavor is not soft first love. It is **completion through irritation**. Aoi annoys Rio because Aoi is exactly the kind of free person Rio cannot be; Rio stabilizes Aoi because Rio is exactly the kind of purposeful person Aoi needs beside her.

## “Partner” is the central word, and it carries more than business

The strongest textual anchor is Aoi repeatedly naming Rio as her irreplaceable partner. Against Mana, Aoi tells Mana not to provoke Rio because Rio is **“僕のたった一人のパートナー”** — “my one and only partner.” She then says that even if her own ability may not reach Mana, **with Rio, the two of them can win**. Rio is visibly moved by this and later admits she was happy to hear it, while Aoi treats it as obvious. origin\_liz\_002\_one\_more\_dream

That asymmetry is important. Rio hears the phrase emotionally; Aoi says it as fact. Aoi’s intimacy is unguarded, almost careless, because she does not experience the bond as something that needs to be dressed up. Rio experiences it as embarrassing because she is the one who understands its emotional weight.

This is why their yuri coding feels plausible rather than imposed. The story does not need to say “romance” because it keeps using **exclusive-partner language**, bodily synchronicity, private domesticity, abandonment/waiting, and jealousy-adjacent protectiveness.

## Mana’s death turns the bond tragic

Mana interrupts Rio and Aoi at the exact point where their shared dream is intensifying. Rio disappears after Mana’s death because Mana was the rival she needed to defeat to give her idolhood meaning. But Aoi does not say goodbye. She says she will wait. origin\_liz\_002\_one\_more\_dream

That makes Aoi’s love — using the word broadly here — unusually patient. Aoi does not chase Rio, does not drag her back, and does not replace her. She simply keeps the space open. Later Rio repeatedly frames Aoi’s waiting as something she must answer or repay. That gives the relationship a very classic “separated lovers / promised return” shape, even though the text keeps it within idol-partner language.

The emotional flavor here is **widow-like without marriage**: Aoi keeps dancing with an absent partner; Rio returns carrying guilt that she left the one person who did not abandon her.

## Domesticity is not incidental; it is one of their love languages

Aoi and Rio are repeatedly written in domestic scenes: Rio cooking for Aoi, Aoi wanting Rio’s cooking every day, Rio saying she is not Aoi’s mother, Aoi answering that she does not think of her that way. origin\_liz\_004\_love\_heart

That “not your mother” line matters because it clarifies the relationship’s ambiguity. Rio often performs a caretaker role: food, health, correction, schedule, manners, rest. But Aoi resists letting that role collapse into maternal framing. She wants Rio’s care, but not as a child being mothered. The bond sits in a liminal space: partner, rival, caretaker, housemate-like intimacy, old companion.

That is exactly the texture of a lot of yuri-coded idol material: not necessarily canonical dating, but **life-partner domesticity under the cover of unit work**.

## Aoi is the only person who can puncture Rio without humiliating her

Rio is proud, image-conscious, and easily embarrassed. Many characters tease her, but Aoi’s teasing has a different quality because Aoi has earned access. Aoi can call Rio obvious, heavy, stubborn, or easy to read; she can expose Rio’s softness; she can say direct things that make Rio flustered. But it rarely feels cruel, because Aoi’s foundation is loyalty.

That is why Aoi’s teasing lands differently from Kokoro’s teasing. Kokoro throws grenades into Rio’s dignity. Aoi quietly opens doors Rio is trying to hold shut.

Aoi sees Rio’s hidden states very early: in the café, she immediately notices Rio is not well and knows the small café setting is tied to Rio’s past. She even says she has a right to hear the story, which Rio reluctantly accepts. origin\_liz\_001\_a\_budding\_lily That is intimate in a very specific way: Aoi is one of the only people who can claim access to Rio’s private history and not be rejected.

## The four-member LizNoir transition proves Rio/Aoi were almost a closed world

When Ai and Kokoro are added, Rio and Aoi initially reject it almost as a violation. Rio says LizNoir having anyone besides herself and Aoi is impossible; Aoi says LizNoir does not need anyone except Rio and Aoi, and that her feeling has not changed since Rio returned. origin\_liz\_005\_smile\_or\_perfect…

That is a huge indicator of how closed and exclusive the original duo was. Ai and Kokoro do not just join a group; they enter a relationship-system. The old LizNoir is essentially **Rio and Aoi’s shared body**: two performers, one standard, one wound, one unfinished battle.

So when the new members arrive, the conflict is not only “are they skilled enough?” It is “can LizNoir still be LizNoir if Rio and Aoi are no longer the entire unit?” That is exactly the kind of premise that amplifies yuri coding: the original pair is treated as something sacred, almost marital in its exclusivity.

## The later conflict is basically a relationship crisis

In “Black Lily in the Storm,” Rio and Aoi’s stage synchronicity collapses. Aoi says their breathing no longer matches; their dance has become heavy; the issue is not physical condition but something wrong between them. She asks Rio what they are trying to become top idols for, and whether becoming top is worth throwing away their LizNoir-ness. Rio prioritizes BIG4 and her responsibility as leader, while Aoi insists that if growth means abandoning their pride and joy, she would rather stay as they are. origin\_liz\_007\_black\_lily\_in\_th…

That reads almost like a couple’s philosophical breakup. It is not “we disagree about business strategy.” It is “our bodies no longer move together because our reasons for living this dream have diverged.”

And Rio’s inner narration immediately ties her resolve to the people who followed her: Kokoro, Ai, and especially Aoi, who waited a year and called her her only partner. origin\_liz\_007\_black\_lily\_in\_th… Rio’s leadership ambition is not cold; it is charged by obligation to Aoi’s faith.

## The “Lily” imagery is doing several things at once

I would read the lily recurrence as deliberately overdetermined.

On the surface, it is LizNoir’s flower-symbol language: elegance, nobility, bloom, darkness, refinement. “Black lily” fits LizNoir’s aesthetic: beautiful but severe, feminine but not soft, floral but gothic.

But because **yuri literally means lily**, the repetition becomes hard to treat as neutral when paired with Rio/Aoi visuals like the ones you posted: face-to-face proximity, hand-to-mouth gestures, entwined bodies, neon pink/blue lighting, secretive titles like **“secret my Lily,”** and a card pair where Rio/Aoi are framed as a matched dyad rather than merely unitmates.

The top image especially is not just “two idols posing together.” It is staged around **possession and invitation**: Aoi above/around Rio, Rio reclined and looking outward, hands near mouth/face, intense pink-blue contrast, and the word “Lily” foregrounded in the story UI. The second image pushes even further into shadowy intimacy: two women close enough that the scene is about breath, gaze, and withheld contact rather than performance.

So the lily motif seems to function on three levels:

1.  **LizNoir brand:** black lily = elegant, dark, proud flower.

2.  **Rio/Aoi pair-symbol:** lily = the closed bloom of their original two-person bond.

3.  **Yuri resonance:** lily = female-female intimacy, especially when paired with “secret,” physical closeness, and partner exclusivity.


## So what “flavor” is Rio/Aoi?

I would describe it as:

**Austere yuri, not sugary yuri.**

Or more fully:

**Rio and Aoi are a rival-partner dyad whose intimacy is built from shared performance, mutual incompleteness, domestic caretaking, abandoned-and-waited-for grief, and the bodily knowledge of having once been the only person who could make the other fully herself.**

Rio’s side of it is prideful, guilty, caretaking, and easily embarrassed. She needs Aoi because Aoi is the one person who can make her lighter without making her weaker.

Aoi’s side is freer, more openly attached, and more quietly possessive. She needs Rio because Rio gives shape, seriousness, and destination to her freedom.

It is not written like an ordinary romance route. It is written like **stage-marriage yuri**: two women whose relationship is so foundational to their art that romantic coding becomes one of the natural visual languages for expressing it.
