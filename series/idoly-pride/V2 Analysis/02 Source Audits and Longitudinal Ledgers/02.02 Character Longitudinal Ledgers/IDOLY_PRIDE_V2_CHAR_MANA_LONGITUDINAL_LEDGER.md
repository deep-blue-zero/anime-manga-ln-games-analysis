# IDOLY PRIDE V2 — MANA NAGASE LONGITUDINAL LEDGER

## P2-A1 — 長瀬麻奈 / Mana Nagase

## 0. Responsibility and authority

This is the first evidence-bearing Phase-2 longitudinal character ledger. Its responsibility is not to write a polished final character essay. It reconstructs **how the text makes Mana legible across time**, what later material preserves or changes about earlier claims, where other characters turn Mana into a symbol, and where the primary source itself limits the authority of that symbol.

The governing Phase-2 principle remains:

> **ledger first, synthesis later; chronology and disclosure boundaries must survive endpoint knowledge.**

Accordingly, this ledger separates five things that are especially easy to collapse around Mana:

1. **Mana the living person** — daughter, sister, classmate, singer, young professional, rival, and Makino's partner in work;
2. **「アイドル長瀬麻奈」** — the professional self Mana consciously constructs and repeatedly defines through singing for an audience;
3. **the public Mana myth** — “legend,” “miracle,” benchmark, perfect idol, frozen rival, and posthumous cultural object;
4. **ghost Mana** — a directly acting posthumous character within the franchise's diegesis, but not an unlimited interpreter or sovereign over survivors;
5. **later Mana inheritances** — what Kotono, Sakura, Suzu, Nagisa, Haruko, LizNoir, IIIX, Hoshimi, fans, and later aspirants choose to make of her.

These layers overlap. They are not interchangeable.

The frozen substantive inheritance entering P2-A1 is `IDOLY_PRIDE_V2_PHASE1_FINAL_BASELINE.md`. This ledger does not rewrite that baseline silently. It records transitions using `PRESERVE / STRENGTHEN / REVISE / DOWNGRADE / REJECT / OPEN`.

- anime-era loss and ghost framing make absence constitutive early;

# 3. Executive longitudinal model

The strongest Phase-2 model of Mana is **not “perfect legendary idol whose spirit guides the next generation.”** That description captures her reception but loses the causal architecture that made the reception possible.

Mana begins as a socially bright but otherwise ordinary schoolgirl whose most stable pre-professional trait is that **singing feels intrinsically alive to her**. LizNoir does not install that desire; the live makes her recognize a stage-shaped form for something already present. Crucially, she then authors two linked choices herself: *I will become an idol*, and *you, Makino, will come with me as manager*. Her career is therefore founded from the beginning on a paradox the later story keeps testing: Mana is an unusually singular performer whose singularity is made possible through relation.

Her public success progressively hardens `長瀬麻奈` into an object other people can use: miracle, legend, perfection, benchmark, dead rival, replacement template, grievance target, family wound, vocation catalyst. The text repeatedly returns to the gap between that object and the living person. The person is competitive, playful, insecure, guilty, stubborn, physically exhaustible, emotionally dependent on specific people, and capable of revising her own account of what made her strong. Her professional self is not fake; it is a deliberate ethical construction. But it can become dangerous when the duty of `アイドル長瀬麻奈` to a possibly one-time audience is treated as jurisdiction over the body of 麻奈 the person.

Death freezes her career before she can solve that problem in life. The posthumous story initially risks turning that freeze into permanent sovereignty: Sakura has Mana's heart/voice association; Kotono makes Mana's unfinished work into a personal commission; rivals measure themselves against a competitor who can no longer change; public grief elevates Mana over other dead performers. Yet ghost Mana herself becomes one of the strongest internal correctives to Mana mythology. She tells Sakura to find the song only Sakura can sing, says Mana's own song belongs to Mana, accepts her own posthumous disappearance rather than have Sakura's self-authorship arrested to preserve her, and ultimately reveals that her last wish was relational rather than monumental: she wanted Makino not to regret following her.

The mature Mana inheritance is therefore **continuity through transformation**. People may receive courage, craft, songs, objects, memories, rivalrous standards, grief, or care techniques from Mana. They remain legitimate heirs only by deciding what those inheritances mean in their own lives. Later material makes this formal: Kotono writes an *answer song*, gathers perspectives because “my sister” and “idol Nagase Mana” are not identical viewpoints, and uses a pendant whose meaning is explicitly that changed form can preserve relation. Mana persists most powerfully when she is no longer required to remain the causal sovereign of everyone who remembers her.

### Reading

Mana does not originate as an icon. The first causal fact is simply that her singing reaches both Makino and Saegusa before she has professional status. Even the famous debut is narrated with an important tense distinction: the people who **later** remember the event call it the beginning of the legend. This means “legend” is an act of reception and memorial compression. It may be accurate as a description of impact while remaining insufficient as a description of the person.

The 2026 answer-song event makes this distinction explicit from the survivor side. Kotono realizes that the Mana she has chiefly carried is `お姉ちゃん`, while fans and colleagues knew `アイドル長瀬麻奈`. Her response is not to decide one is authentic and the other false; she gathers perspectives. Mature Mana interpretation therefore requires **plural access to one person**, not a hidden “real Mana” behind a fake idol mask.

# 6. Chronological reconstruction in detail

## 6.1 Before the professional role

The earliest Mana is already socially bright. Makino notices that she occupies the center of a peer group while he imagines himself as one of the background many. This contrast becomes retrospectively tempting to read as destiny — “light and shadow” were always meant to meet — but the source also grounds it in ordinary adolescent difference. Makino is not an idol fan. Mana is not yet an idol. What crosses the boundary first is **voice**: he hears her sing and finds that it reaches him more than the professional idols he had regarded as distant and interchangeable.

Saegusa separately hears the same unaccompanied singing and recognizes unusual capability. Yet even here Mana is not passively waiting to be discovered. She treats his approach with ordinary suspicion, jokes about whether he is a suspicious person, and leaves him to explain himself. The pre-professional source preserves agency before prestige.

## 6.2 LizNoir as catalytic mirror

At the LizNoir live, Mana's reaction is not simply “I admire them.” It becomes a first-person displacement question: why is *someone else* standing there instead of me? That is a different psychological structure from fandom-as-copying. LizNoir supplies a **possible position**; Mana supplies the desire to occupy it.

The next-day decision pairs vocation with relation. She asks Makino to enter the agency and become her manager. The professional Mana/Makino story is therefore co-originating: she authors an idol project and simultaneously authors a role for the person whose perception she already trusts.

## 6.3 Training and the destruction of effortless-genius myth

Mana does have striking natural capability. The origin material nevertheless insists on double-life fatigue, scheduling strain, instruction, repeated work, promotional failure, and the need for managerial correction. Her eventual “legend” is built through the conversion of raw capacity into a durable professional practice.

This is especially visible at debut. Conventional promotion fails. A larger agency can purchase visibility Mana and Hoshimi Pro do not have. Mana's response is anger rooted in a merit claim: if this were about singing, she believes she could win. Makino turns that claim into a tactic: sing now, in public, with minimal infrastructure. The result works because **performance quality, managerial recognition of the right weapon, audience attention, user-generated circulation, and institutional follow-through** all intersect.

The legend is real as social fact. It is not self-causing.

## 6.4 Kotono: the cost enters the home

Career growth reallocates Mana's time. Kotono's grievance is concrete: meals, conversations, days out, ordinary sisterhood — things they actually used to share — have nearly disappeared. Mana initially resists the language of “taking distance” because she does not experience reduced time as reduced love. Kotono experiences the material result.

Their conflict therefore establishes a recurring IDOLY PRIDE problem: **intention and effect are both evidence**. Mana loves Kotono. Mana is also absent. Kotono's loneliness is not invalidated by proving Mana's love.

Mana's diary makes the private side visible. She knows Kotono is lonely; she feels guilty about mistakes and burdens on staff; she calls herself inadequate even amid career success. The public myth of effortless brightness is already unsustainable from her own interior record.

## 6.5 Cancellation, weakness, and the back-push ritual

When sister conflict and stage danger disrupt Mana's ability to perform, the source approaches a limit she normally avoids articulating: the possibility of quitting. She does not easily tell others that weakness. Kotono is the one who recognizes the pattern: Mana rarely says the vulnerable thing directly, which means those close to her have to notice.

The back-push request to Makino becomes a compact embodied technology of trust. Mana asks him to physically push her back before she goes on stage — an `おまじない`, but also a distribution of agency. The final step onto stage remains hers. The trusted other supplies momentum without becoming the performer.

## 6.6 The “miracle” and myth production

At the Hoshimi festival, Mana's decision to sing despite the weather is both admirable and a warning. She wants to repay her hometown, has written 「星の海の記憶」, and refuses to let a waiting audience become an abstraction. When the storm clears and a meteor shower becomes visible, participants call the event miraculous and later fold it into the Mana legend.

P2-A1 freezes two propositions separately:

- **TEXTUAL FACT:** the performance and weather event coincide; people experience and narrate it as miracle;
- **OPEN / CONFLICT-AMBIGUITY:** exact supernatural causation.

The “miracle” matters even if mechanism remains unresolved because the social meaning is undeniable. It teaches audiences and later characters to expect Mana to make impossible stages happen. That expectation becomes ethically dangerous when internalized by Mana herself.

## 6.7 Overwork and the professional-name trap

By `Goodbye to Goodbye`, the exceptional career is visibly consuming the person. Mana is exhausted, continues accepting work, fights efforts to slow her down, and collapses. A physician explicitly identifies overwork and orders rest.

The most important logic appears before and after that collapse. If fans are waiting, Mana says, `長瀬麻奈` cannot simply fail to sing. The professional name becomes a normative subject: **what “Nagase Mana” owes** threatens to overrule what Mana's body can sustain.

This is the shadow side of role continuity. The idol role is genuinely self-authored, not imposed from outside; that is precisely why its demands can be so compelling.

## 6.8 Audience singularity and manager responsibility

Mana's defense is not trivial stubbornness. She remembers a seriously ill fan and explains why “another show later” cannot be presumed. Live performance is `一期一会`: for a particular listener this may be the only encounter.

Makino's response eventually recognizes the ethical force without surrendering his own duty. He permits the opportunity only under conditions: stop if danger becomes unacceptable, then take mandatory recovery time. The source explicitly gives him both jobs — **stopping her and enabling her to sing**.

This is why P2-A1 rejects both simple readings:

- Mana is not right merely because sacrifice is passionate;
- Makino is not right merely because protection is safer.

The institutional problem is to make authored risk answerable to bodies, staff, family, and future vocation.

## 6.9 “One, but not alone”

Immediately before the final death sequence, Mana develops one of her most mature self-accounts. She names how often Makino has been beside her — correcting, protecting, comforting, noticing. She then says that `アイドル長瀬麻奈` is one person but not alone, and that this is why she can sing.

The line resolves a tension that the public myth tends to erase. Mana's performance must be irreducibly hers if it is to communicate. Yet the conditions that make “hers” possible are relational.

## 6.10 The interrupted answer

Mana asks Makino whether he will stay beside her from then on. The narrative cuts forward to her death. Makino later answers, in grief/memory, that he will remain forever.

P2-A1 does **not** collapse those moments. The missed answer is part of the tragedy. The final Hoshimi farewell can later create a new encounter; it cannot make the living Mana hear words she did not hear.

## 6.11 Death, ghosthood, and the danger of perfecting the dead

After death, Mana acquires two different forms of persistence:

1. direct ghost presence within the diegesis;
2. representational persistence in songs, bodies, objects, memories, career structures, and other people's aims.

These forms must be kept distinct. Ghost Mana can actually object to what survivors do in her name. Memory-Mana cannot.

That distinction becomes decisive because the living repeatedly over-assign Mana authority:

- Kotono turns diary evidence into a commission;
- Sakura initially carries voice/heart/identity pressure;
- rivals compare themselves to a dead benchmark;
- miho turns unequal mourning into grievance;
- fans and colleagues call Mana perfect or legendary.

The story then lets ghost Mana push back.

## 6.12 Anti-replacement as Mana's posthumous doctrine

Hoshimi 018 provides the clearest source. Mana tells Sakura that a Mana song is not automatically Sakura's own song and asks her to find the song that only Sakura can sing. Later she explicitly says her own unfinished song is hers; she does not want someone else to complete the regret by impersonating her agency.

This is not merely “be yourself” encouragement. It is a rule about **ownership of unfinished life**. A successor may inherit effects. The successor cannot retroactively become the deceased agent who left the work unfinished.

## 6.13 Willingness to disappear

Hoshimi 019 radicalizes the rule. As Sakura's own song emerges, ghost Mana appears to weaken. Mei proposes the most obvious preservation strategy: ask Sakura to return to singing in Mana's voice. Mana refuses.

The ethical hierarchy is therefore explicit:

> the living successor's authorship > preservation of the predecessor's continued presence.

Mana's reply that she is “already dead” is not nihilistic. It is jurisdictional clarity. The living should not be made to stop becoming themselves in order to keep the dead unchanged.

## 6.14 Kotono refuses the answer

Kotono's Hoshimi 020 confrontation is the sisterhood counterpart. For years she has wanted to know what Mana thought, why Mana acted as she did, and whether Kotono's proxy project was right. Once she believes Mana is actually present, she chooses to ask none of it.

She says the issue is hers to settle. Then she declares that she will no longer chase Mana, no longer stand in the final “in Mana's place,” and will instead discover the idol **she herself wants to become**.

The source later lets Kotono feel warmth like an embrace and interpret it as approval. That formal/subjective experience matters emotionally, but the logical order matters more: Kotono makes the choice **before** receiving the felt approval. Her autonomy is not produced by a posthumous command.

## 6.15 The final farewell and relational telos

Hoshimi 022 discloses Mana's “real dream.” She had asked Makino to become her manager and promised never to make him regret it. Becoming top idol would make him “the manager of a top idol”; her success would answer his trust.

After death, she cannot personally complete that route. She therefore comes to believe that seeing Sakura and Kotono become top idols under Makino can discharge the unfinished promise. This explains why her ghost attaches so strongly to Makino's continuing work.

The same farewell makes Mana's romantic state explicit: she says she is glad she fell for him and regrets that realizing it earlier might at least have allowed a kiss. This is a Mana-side textual romantic fact. P2-A1 stops there. Makino's reciprocal romantic state and any living-time relationship status are not silently adjudicated in a character ledger.

## 6.16 After farewell: inheritance no longer requires active ghosthood

Later stories show Mana continuing to matter without remaining the moving center of every plot. This is the mature state of inheritance.

Stellar gives the clearest ethical revision. Sakura repeats Mana's one-time-audience logic after collapsing from overwork. Her group does not mock the premise; they **answer it relationally**. If one person cannot perform safely, others cover. If burden exists, it is distributed. Rest is not betrayal of the audience because the unit's future and the body of the performer also count.

The 2026 answer-song event gives the clearest memory revision. Kotono does not reconstruct Mana by private intuition alone. She asks other witnesses. She does not restore `song for you`; she writes `With your songs`. She wears a pendant whose history explicitly says that form can change without abolishing relation.

This is the mature Mana principle in object form.

# 11. Professional philosophy: current Mana model

Mana's professional philosophy should not be reduced to one slogan. It contains at least six simultaneously operative commitments.

## 11.1 Singing is intrinsically desired

Before fame, ranking, or duty, Mana likes to sing. This protects the character from a purely sacrificial interpretation. Her career does not begin as duty to fans.

## 11.2 Performance is communicative

The point of high performance is not self-enclosed technical superiority. She wants songs to reach people, make them smile, energize them, and give hope.

## 11.3 Encounters are nonfungible

`一期一会` makes a specific audience encounter morally different from abstract future reach. A person who misses today's stage may not have a substitute tomorrow.

## 11.4 Excellence is competitive but relational

Mana is genuinely `負けず嫌い`. Later she recognizes that rivals make her stronger. Competition is not erased; its causal model becomes reciprocal.

## 11.5 The performer is singular but supported

`一人だけど、一人じゃない` denies the myth that authored performance requires causal isolation.

## 11.6 Vocation has a bodily and institutional limit

This is the commitment Mana herself never fully stabilizes in life. Her desire to sing can become self-endangering. Makino, staff, family, and later peer constitutions supply counter-authorities. The later corpus therefore preserves Mana's seriousness while refusing to universalize her willingness to spend herself.

### Phase-2 professional thesis

> **Mana's greatness lies not in transcending dependency, bodily limitation, or ordinary vulnerability, but in building a communicative professional identity powerful enough to reach others while repeatedly discovering that this identity can exist only through answerable relation. Her unresolved danger is that the same ethic can make the role claim too much of the person.**

This formulation is a Phase-2 ledger interpretation, not yet the polished Phase-3 character synthesis.

# 12. Death, absence, and posthumous authority

## 12.1 Death does not make every unfinished intention binding

Mana dies with:

- an unfinished competitive trajectory;
- a song/intention linked to Kotono;
- a missed living-time answer from Makino;
- an unfinished promise about making Makino's choice worthwhile;
- rivals who expected future contest;
- a public identity still accelerating.

Each survivor receives a different remainder. The existence of a remainder does not itself specify who must discharge it.

## 12.2 Ghost Mana is strongest when she gives up sovereignty

The posthumous Mana scenes systematically narrow her authority:

- Sakura must not become Mana;
- Mana's song cannot become someone else's identity duty;
- Sakura's own song should continue even if Mana fades;
- Kotono may speak or not speak;
- Kotono may decide without asking Mana what to do;
- Mana can clarify **her own** unfinished dream, but not author the whole future of Hoshimi.

This yields a durable principle:

> **the dead retain evidence, claims, love, history, and sometimes direct diegetic voice; they do not retain unlimited jurisdiction over the living.**

## 12.3 Absence becomes developmentally productive only when it stops being total rule

Kotono explicitly says that Mana's absence was paradoxically part of how she developed. This does not celebrate death. It recognizes that the self Kotono built cannot simply be discarded when access to Mana becomes possible again.

The mature story therefore refuses both extremes:

- “move on by erasing Mana”; or
- “honor Mana by remaining governed by Mana.”

The stable middle is **continuity without captivity**.

# 13. Public myth, benchmark, and the politics of remembrance

Mana's posthumous scale produces effects that the living Mana could not fully control.

## 13.1 “Perfect idol” is socially real and biographically incomplete

Haruko can call `アイドル長瀬麻奈` perfect because Mana successfully withheld visible insecurity from fans. That statement is not contradicted by the private vulnerability; it is explained by it. “Perfection” names a professional achievement of presentation, not literal absence of weakness.

## 13.2 Benchmarking can become nonreciprocal after death

Rio and others can continue measuring themselves against the remembered Mana, but Mana can no longer generate new public competitive evidence. The benchmark becomes historically frozen. Mature rivals therefore have to decide when respect for the benchmark has turned into captivity by it.

## 13.3 Public mourning is distributive power

IIIX/miho material proves that Mana's fame affects how other dead performers are remembered. Mana is not morally responsible for this distribution merely because she benefits from it symbolically. But “Mana” can become the name through which another survivor experiences institutional/public injustice.

This routes an important future theme:

> **memory is not only private grief; it is also allocation of cultural attention.**

P2-A1 records the mechanism without collapsing miho's grievance into a Mana character trait.

# 14. Counterevidence and rejected simplifications

## 14.1 REJECT — “Mana was simply born a legend/genius”

Why rejected: Mana genuinely loves singing and wants to encourage audiences. The public role is authored and selective, not necessarily deceptive.

Why rejected: ghost Mana explicitly says the opposite.
