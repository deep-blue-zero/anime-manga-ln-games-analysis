# 2. The three clocks: chronology, disclosure, validation

## 2.1 Fictional chronology

A useful Makino chronology is:

1. socially peripheral high-school life; little special interest in idols;
2. Mana’s singing breaks his assumption that idols are interchangeable;
3. Mana chooses him because he treats her normally and asks for his `フラットな目線`;
4. reluctant/novice entry into Hoshimi as Mana’s student manager;
5. learning through observation, practical work, promotion, and Mana’s rapid development;
6. explicit choice to remain in management after graduation, even when Mana encourages him to choose another future;
7. increasing responsibility for Mana’s health, opportunities, staff coordination, and professional risk;
8. sharp Mana/Makino conflict over overwork and whether the manager may refuse an opportunity;
9. negotiated model: take the stage under safety constraints, then rest;
10. Mana’s death before the Grand Prix final; grief and failure to process finality;
11. ghost Mana’s continuing presence and Makino’s post-death vocation organized partly around the lost final and promise;
12. recruitment of Kotono and Sakura; Makino becomes responsible for idols other than Mana;
13. group-formation experiments, firm boundaries, observation, delayed intervention, and Hoshimi unit differentiation;
14. NVGP progression in which living idols increasingly reject Mana-copy logic;
15. Mana’s final disclosure of her real dream and love; fixed Makino kiss-response at farewell; second disappearance;
16. Makino explicitly accepts that Mana’s continued ghost-presence would make him happy but would not be “right”;
17. final-stage professional limit: the idols decide and perform; Makino builds conditions and watches;
18. continuing post-Hoshimi game-era role across Tokyo, BIG4, Stellar, events, cards, bonds, and relationship-specific work;
19. later reciprocal-care/private-jurisdiction material in which Makino remains manager while also becoming trusted witness, desired evaluator, family-facing professional, and care recipient.

## 2.2 Disclosure order

The audience does not learn Makino in that causal order.

- The anime first presents him substantially through **loss**: Mana is already dead, the manager role already carries grief, and ghost Mana already makes his professional life strange.
- Anime Episode 1 backfills the first-step relation but preserves ambiguity about how professional identity matured between school and tragedy.
- The game’s Mana origins and Hoshimi expansion later supply detailed interior reasoning: why Mana chose him, why he stayed, how he learned to judge talent, how he handled overwork, and how early authority could become unilateral.
- Later cards/bonds/messages disclose a much wider manager: someone whose praise matters, whose own body is noticed, whose privacy obligations are tested, and whose relation to idols can become emotionally specific without role collapse.
- Rui’s late romantic recognition arrives after earlier affective self-consciousness; the missing phone keeps Makino’s response unavailable.

A later disclosure may reclassify the meaning of an earlier relation, but it may not be inserted into an earlier participant’s knowledge state without evidence.

# 3. Executive longitudinal model

The strongest P2-A2 formulation is:

> **Makino develops from Mana’s chosen “flat gaze” into an answerable professional whose authority is legitimate only insofar as it remains responsive to the person under management.**

He does **not** begin as a calling-driven producer. He begins as the comparatively unobtrusive boy who thinks he belongs to `その他大勢`, does not regard idols as especially differentiated, and is selected by the most socially luminous girl in his class precisely because he does not change himself around her. Mana wants a person who can look at her without the distortions of worship. That relational accident becomes a profession.

The profession then becomes his own. By graduation, Mana explicitly offers him an exit for the sake of his future. Makino stays because the work itself has become interesting and because he wants to see how far Mana can go. The distinction matters: **Mana is the catalyst of vocation, not its permanent owner.**

Makino’s core competence is not omniscience. It is **attention under uncertainty**. He notices that Mana is not happy about a debut everyone else celebrates. He notices fatigue behind insistence. He can tell Saegusa that he does not yet know whether Sakura has talent and still ask to take responsibility for her because letting the trajectory disappear untested would be a professional regret. He sometimes waits precisely because the performer or group needs to formulate the problem rather than receive his answer.

That competence is paired with real power. Makino can decline work, define group architecture, refuse Kotono’s solo plan, require shared living, set health conditions, negotiate with organizers, and shape access to opportunity. P2-A2 therefore rejects two easy simplifications:

- he is **not** merely a supportive servant whose ethics consist of doing whatever the idol wants;
- he is **not** a paternal sovereign whose benevolence gives him authority over the idol as a person.

The longitudinal change lies in how that power becomes answerable. Early Makino can act unilaterally for a legitimate reason and still wound the person whose interests he is protecting. Mana’s `私に相談もなしに！` matters. Later Makino increasingly explains consequences, negotiates conditions, delays intervention, protects privacy, and says directly that some answers are **not his to make**. At the Hoshimi final he can describe the manager as “powerless” at the decisive moment because `全ては本人達次第だ`—everything ultimately depends on them.

This does not make management irrelevant. It gives it a constitutional boundary. The manager creates conditions under which another person can exercise authorship.

Mana’s death puts that ethic under its hardest test. Grief initially binds Makino’s vocation to a dead-centered mandate: raise successors, return to the Grand Prix, complete what Mana could not. The mature Hoshimi arc does not resolve this by forgetting Mana. It resolves it by **removing Mana from the position of causal sovereign**. Makino supports Sakura abandoning Mana’s voice; he tells senior rivals that Sakura and Kotono may surpass Mana once they possess their own songs; he supports the living idols’ strange final-stage plan; and he accepts that Mana remaining as a ghost would make him happy but would not be right.

The result is not “moving on” in the thin sense. It is **role continuity without role captivity** and **legacy continuity without restoration**.

The later game layer then prevents Makino from remaining only a grief character. His 428-bundle occurrence footprint spans main arcs, events, bonds, and cards. Idols care for him. Rui wants his praise. Sakura feeds him when he neglects dinner. Kotono’s family recognizes him within a trust network. Kana tells him a private truth and limits its circulation. Suzu’s overwork gives him a reason to offer rest without denying her vocation. Relationship-specific intimacy grows, but the role does not disappear.

This is why the best current capstone is:

> **professional authority without personhood superiority; intervention without goal seizure; reciprocal care without role collapse; recognition without possession; inheritance without replacement.**

# 6. Chronological reconstruction in detail

## 6.1 Before management: an ordinary boy without an idol vocation

Makino’s starting point matters because it prevents the profession from being read as destiny. He places himself on the shadow side of school life while Mana occupies the socially radiant center. In the Mana origin material he thinks of himself as `その他大勢`; he also admits that idols had seemed more or less interchangeable to him.

The first crack in that assumption is perceptual rather than ideological. Mana’s singing reaches him differently. He notices that her voice affects him more than LizNoir’s even before he has a framework for why. That is the first seed of professional judgment: not a ranking system, but the recognition that two competent performances do not create identical effects.

Mana then converts that perception into a relationship. She chooses him because he is the male classmate who treats her most normally. The request is strikingly specific: come with me to Hoshimi and advise me from that `フラットな目線`.

Makino is therefore recruited as **anti-distortion** before he becomes expert.

## 6.2 Apprenticeship: learning that attention is work

The early management period is full of ordinary incompetence. Makino is young, unfamiliar with industry procedure, and explicitly dependent on Saegusa’s institutional backing. The text does not pretend that Mana’s trust magically makes him qualified.

What he learns first is to watch.

He sees Mana’s talent in singing and dance. He sees the cost of the double life. He sees her body leaning on him even while she insists she can reduce sleep. Most importantly, when the office celebrates her debut, he notices that she is not happy. Mana’s response—essentially, *you really do watch me*—marks observation becoming relational competence.

That observation then changes strategy. Makino had assumed that debut itself was the objective. Mana explains that debut is only a start and that most acts disappear. He revises his own model and commits to making her song reach more people. The low-resource street promotion that follows is not simply a manager inventing Mana’s career; it is the first clear instance of **artist desire translated into professional means**.

## 6.3 Staying after graduation: vocation ceases to be borrowed

Hoshimi 001 later makes a crucial fact explicit. Makino initially thought his work at the agency might last about half a year. At graduation Mana tells him he can leave for the sake of his own future.

He does not.

He remains because the work has become interesting and because he wants to see how far Mana goes. That is the point at which it becomes misleading to describe him as someone who is only “helping Mana.” His profession has acquired intrinsic content.

The same scene preserves the relational element. Makino’s vocation is self-authored **and** still entangled with watching Mana’s transformation. The series does not require autonomy to mean causal independence from relationship.

## 6.4 Overwork: the first constitutional crisis of authority

The Mana overwork material is the most important early stress test because both sides have ethically intelligible claims.

Makino sees bodily danger, growing scheduling pressure, and a performer who will refuse no opportunity. Mana sees a singular audience obligation: a person may have only one chance to hear her; `アイドル長瀬麻奈` exists to give hope and make people smile.

Makino first responds through unilateral protection. When a major venue opportunity arrives while Mana is hospitalized, Saegusa tells him the decision is his. He declines it before consulting Mana.

Mana’s anger is justified in one narrow procedural sense: `私に相談もなしに！` He decided what she could do without asking the person whose vocation was at stake.

But the text does not simply reverse and say Mana was right. Makino’s criticism exposes the hidden labor of her self-sacrifice: sales, publicity, venue staff, lighting, styling, management, and the many people who bear the consequences if she collapses. Mana’s audience ethic is morally serious but not self-sufficient.

The resolution is negotiated risk:

- the live happens;
- any sign of danger triggers cancellation;
- afterward Mana must take a full week off.

This becomes a model for mature Makino: **not prohibition, not surrender—constraint designed to keep the stated vocation possible.**

## 6.5 Death and the danger of memorial capture

Mana’s death occurs before the final Makino has helped her reach. The immediate response is denial shaped through logistics: he thinks in terms of recovery and rescheduling before accepting finality. The anime formalizes the loss through absence and silence, then returns Mana as a ghost in the ordinary classroom where the partnership began.

The supernatural continuation makes grief livable, but it also creates a danger. Makino can continue speaking with the dead person whose interrupted project defined the trauma.

Three years later, his work on new Hoshimi idols is partly framed through a promise to cultivate successors and win the revived Grand Prix “for Mana.” That is not false. It is also not the final form of his vocation.

## 6.6 Sakura: uncertainty, responsibility, and potential

Sakura is one of the cleanest demonstrations that Makino does not confuse talent judgment with certainty. Asked to decide whether to accept her, he says he cannot yet tell whether she has talent. He nevertheless asks to take responsibility for her because sending her away would produce professional regret.

This is important for character reconstruction. Makino is not a cold optimizer who requires validated metrics before committing. Nor is he a sentimental believer who declares every aspirant exceptional. He can say **I do not know** and still act.

The action is therefore a wager with accountability: `俺に、預からせて下さい`.

## 6.7 Kotono: authority, credibility, and the cost of saying no

Kotono arrives with a clear requested path: solo idolhood, in a place connected to Mana’s former management. Makino refuses.

He explains that he has no intention of selling the current Kotono as a solo act and tells her she may leave if that condition is unacceptable. The internal narration calls it a gamble because she might actually walk away.

This is not gentle management. It is role authority in its sharpest form.

The reason it remains analytically defensible is that Makino is not denying Kotono’s desire to become an idol. He is making a professional judgment about **the path most likely to realize the goal**. But P2-A2 also refuses to treat that as proof of infallibility. His decision materially shapes her life. That power is exactly why later answerability matters.

## 6.8 Group formation: learning to wait

Hoshimi 005 provides a more mature counterpoint. Makino has already found Mei and thinks she can supply something the serious unit lacks. Then Kotono independently says the group itself feels incomplete and begins trying to diagnose why.

Instead of immediately announcing his solution, Makino waits.

He says the group’s own discussion is not a bad thing and is willing even to delay the debut until they reach an answer. Ghost Mana notices the change and remarks that he has become more manager-like.

This is a subtle but important development: **a manager can possess a proposed answer and still decide that giving it now would make the people less capable of authoring the problem.**

## 6.9 Professional boundaries: intimacy is not entitlement

The same Hoshimi period contains an unusually explicit anti-fraternization statement. After teasing creates the appearance that he may have personal interest in Mei or others, Makino says he has enough pride as a manager never to “make a move on” a talent.

This does not make the corpus romance-free. Mana’s posthumous confession, Rui’s later attraction, and various teasing interactions exist. The importance of the line is jurisdictional: **relationship-specific warmth does not automatically authorize erotic use of professional access.**

It also explains why Rui’s late-state attraction cannot be turned into mutual romance by inference. Her feeling is hers. Makino’s category requires Makino evidence.

## 6.10 The Mana-copy crisis: protecting a successor from the prestige of inheritance

Late Hoshimi makes Makino’s memorial development unusually explicit. Sakura’s Mana-linked voice and heart make literal replacement logic tempting. Mana herself rejects it. Her song is hers; Sakura and Kotono need songs only they can sing.

Makino serves as translator, witness, and eventually participant in that revision. After Sakura stops using Mana’s voice, he tells Rui/LizNoir-side seniors that this is good: Sakura and Kotono now possess the possibility of standing beside or surpassing Mana because they have begun to acquire their own songs.

That is a major change from “complete Mana’s unfinished final.” The manager’s measure of fidelity becomes **the production of non-Mana idols**.

## 6.11 Kotono and Mana: knowing when the answer is not his

Hoshimi 020 provides perhaps the cleanest statement of Makino’s mature jurisdictional limit.

Kotono learns that Mana’s ghost exists but hesitates to speak with her. Makino realizes he had assumed confession/reconciliation was Kotono’s wish. When Sakura asks what they should do, he says:

> `俺達が、決めることじゃないと思う`

He adds that only the sisters can understand the matter and that action taken because someone else told Kotono what to do could leave an irreversible regret. Speaking or not speaking is Kotono’s freedom.

This does not make him emotionally absent. It is a deliberate refusal of relational sovereignty.

## 6.12 The farewell: love, professional success, and an incomplete romantic answer

Mana’s final disclosure reorganizes the meaning of her ghostly mission. Her “real dream” was not only her own summit. She wanted Makino never to regret following her; she wanted to make him the manager of a top idol.

Death made personal completion impossible, so she came to believe Sakura and Kotono could fulfill the relational promise by becoming top idols under him.

She then says explicitly that she is glad she fell in love with him and jokes that if he had noticed sooner, they might at least have kissed.

The next fixed narration is `（唇を重ねる）`.

P2-A2 treats this carefully. It is much stronger evidence of reciprocal romantic orientation than ordinary affection. It is not a selectable player reply. But it still does not give the project a clean proposition such as “Makino and Mana were mutually acknowledged lovers before her death.” Makino’s exact living-time self-recognition remains unstated.

The relationship ledger will adjudicate the final category.

## 6.13 Accepting the second loss

At the opening of Hoshimi 023, Mei asks whether Makino is lonely now that Mana is gone again. He says yes, but also says this feels right. If she stayed, he would of course be happy, but that would not be right.

This is grief without repudiation and continuation without restoration.

The statement is one of the strongest pieces of evidence against interpreting Makino’s mature vocation as a refusal to release Mana. He can want the ghost and still accept that his happiness is not sufficient reason for the dead to remain.

## 6.14 The final stage: operational power and existential powerlessness

The living idols devise a risky, unusual final-stage concept. Makino does not take authorship away from them. He says their idea is good, admits he cannot guarantee the audience will like it, and ultimately concludes that doing what they want is best. His job becomes the organizer-facing groundwork.

Immediately before the decisive stage he describes managers as powerless:

> `全ては本人達次第だ`

At the most important moment, he can only watch.

This closes a loop opened by Anime Episode 1. The novice once stopped at Mana’s stage threshold because that was simply how the job worked. The mature manager now understands the ethical meaning of the threshold.

## 6.15 Credit and professional maturity

The final also gives Makino public recognition. Saegusa treats him as having become `一人前`; Asakura tries to recruit him at higher pay and refers to his history managing Mana.

Makino responds by refusing a simple auteur narrative: Mana carved her own path; he did nothing.

Literally, that is false modesty. He plainly did many things. Analytically, however, the direction matters. Makino’s mature self-conception does not make the idol a work he owns.

The producer/manager is causally important **without being the causal sovereign**.

## 6.16 Later game life: the manager becomes part of a reciprocal social system

The post-Hoshimi game layer widens Makino beyond the Mana/Kotono/Sakura axis.

Rui’s bond makes his praise desired recognition. Sakura sees his skipped dinner and feeds him. miho notices late working. Kana trusts him with a private family truth and explicitly limits circulation. Kotono’s parents discuss his support and want to meet him. Suzu’s self-authored overwork gives him reason to offer rest. Haruko and later professional contexts treat him as someone with transferable experience rather than merely Mana’s former manager.

This is the mature social architecture:

> Makino is responsible for people who also retain standing to answer, refuse, care for, evaluate, and trust him selectively.

# 11. Counterevidence and rejected simplifications

## 11.1 REJECT: “Makino is only a self-insert”

The fixed biography, 34,011-utterance occurrence footprint, Mana history, non-selectable professional decisions, and authored internal narration are incompatible with a contentless-avatar model.

## 11.2 REJECT: “Makino’s job is to fulfill whatever the idol wants”

He declines opportunities, imposes conditions, refuses Kotono’s solo plan, offers rest, and explicitly claims responsibility for risk.

## 11.3 REJECT: “Makino knows what is best because he is the manager”

He repeatedly admits uncertainty, makes mistakes, revises his assumptions, and says some answers are not his to make.

## 11.4 REVISE: “Makino protects through benevolent unilateral control”

Early evidence supports the **unilateral** part in some situations, but the mature model is more constrained: explanation, negotiation, waiting, privacy, and performer authorship become increasingly important.

## 11.5 REJECT: “Mana’s death permanently turns Makino into a caretaker of her dream”

The dead-centered mandate is historically real. The endpoint explicitly shifts him toward managing living idols who may surpass Mana and whose own songs matter more than reproduction.

## 11.6 REJECT: “Grief resolution requires forgetting Mana”

Makino continues speaking to and about Mana, values her memory, and frames his vocation through what she gave him. The change is jurisdiction, not memory deletion.

## 11.7 REJECT: “Reciprocal care means the professional hierarchy disappears”

Sakura feeding him and Rui preparing for him do not make them his managers. Reciprocity concerns standing and care, not identical office.

## 11.8 REJECT: “Makino’s anti-fraternization line proves no romantic situation can ever exist”

It establishes a strong professional boundary with living talent under management. It does not erase Mana’s posthumous farewell or decide all future relationship-status edge cases.

## 11.9 REJECT: “Rui’s romantic attraction proves Makino reciprocates”

The missing call is a real formal dependency. No amount of generic warmth fills it.

## 11.10 REJECT: “Makino’s self-effacing `俺は何もしてません` means management is causally irrelevant”

The corpus repeatedly shows his decisions matter. The line is best read as non-possessive credit attribution, not literal causal nullity.
