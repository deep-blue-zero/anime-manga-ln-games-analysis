# IDOLY PRIDE V2 — GRIEF, DEATH, MEMORY, INHERITANCE, AND NON-REPLACEMENT LONGITUDINAL LEDGER

## P2-E-03 — transformed continuity without posthumous jurisdiction

---

# 0. Analytical responsibility

This is the third P2-E theme/institution longitudinal ledger.

P2-E-01 established that **causal importance does not automatically create ownership**. P2-E-02 established that professional care is most legitimate when it preserves capability without seizing another person's goal. P2-E-03 asks what happens when the person whose causal importance is at issue is **dead, absent, remembered, embodied through a transplant, preserved in an object, encoded in a song, frozen as a rival, or reconstructed by survivors who can no longer obtain ordinary correction from her**.

The central problem is not whether the dead matter. In IDOLY PRIDE they plainly do. Dead people remain:

- beloved family and friends;
- causes of vocation;
- sources of craft and care techniques;
- rivals and professional standards;
- bodily provenance;
- authors of surviving songs, diaries, notebooks, promises, and objects;
- public icons or publicly neglected figures;
- sometimes, in Mana's exceptional case, directly acting ghosts.

The problem is **what kind of authority survives death**.

This ledger therefore owns the recurrent cross-unit mechanism connecting:

- death as an irreversible change in relational structure rather than a narrative reset;
- survivor guilt and the limits of retrospective responsibility;
- memory as evidence, relation, and reconstruction;
- posthumous wishes and the limits of their jurisdiction;
- embodied inheritance and transplant provenance;
- songs, notebooks, pendants, performances, and other memory media;
- proxy continuation and successor pressure;
- frozen rivalry and memorialized benchmarks;
- private remembrance versus public memorial survival;
- transformed reperformance and answer works;
- new relationships that do not replace the dead;
- inheritance of practices without inheritance of identity;
- continuing attachment without mandatory restoration, forgetting, or closure.

It does **not** replace the Mana/Kotono, Mana/Makino, Sakura/Kotono, or miho/Yō relationship ledgers; Sakura, Mana, Kotono, Makino, Haruko, or miho character ledgers; LizNoir/Tsuki/IIIX unit constitutions; or later Phase-5 audiovisual analysis. Those remain canonical local owners of their facts and relationship/unit states.

---

# 1. Inherited boundaries from P2-E-01 and P2-E-02

P2-E-01 stabilized:

> **`ANSWERABLE_INTERDEPENDENCE_WITHOUT_JURISDICTIONAL_CAPTURE`**

and the rule:

> **causal importance creates duties of answerability; it does not automatically create ownership.**

P2-E-02 stabilized:

> **`SUPPORTED_AUTONOMY_THROUGH_DOMAIN_BOUNDED_INTERVENTION`**

and:

> **protect the capacity; do not seize the goal.**

Death changes the practical environment of both rules.

A living participant can answer, contest, withdraw, correct memory, renegotiate a promise, change a goal, forgive, refuse forgiveness, or surprise the other person. Death removes some or all of those ordinary mechanisms. The danger is therefore not merely that survivors continue to care. The danger is that **absence can be converted into unanswerable authority**.

A diary can become a command it never issued. A song can become an assignment. A donor organ can become a theory of inherited will. A dead rival can become an impossible permanent benchmark. A private memory can become a claim to own the public history of the dead. A promise can become a prison. A living successor can become a vessel.

P2-E-03 therefore asks:

> **How can a relation remain causally and emotionally real after death without the dead person's absence becoming a form of unlimited jurisdiction over the living?**

---

# 2. Governing thematic model

The strongest P2-E-03 formulation is:

> **`TRANSFORMED_CONTINUITY_WITHOUT_POSTHUMOUS_JURISDICTION`: IDOLY PRIDE repeatedly treats death as an irreversible change in the medium of relation, not as either total erasure or automatic sanctification. Love, grief, craft, rivalry, bodily provenance, promises, songs, objects, and remembered words can remain genuinely causal after death. Their legitimacy depends on preserving provenance while refusing to turn absence into an unanswerable command over the living. Mature inheritance therefore changes form: a survivor may answer rather than complete, reinterpret rather than impersonate, circulate rather than seal, receive influence without surrendering authorship, and form new relationships without retroactively replacing the dead. Even direct ghost Mana does not become a sovereign interpreter of survivors' futures; she increasingly ratifies the right of Sakura, Kotono, and Makino to continue without reproducing or retaining her.**

A complementary model is:

> **`PROVENANCE_PRESERVING_INHERITANCE_WITHOUT_PROXY_SUBSTITUTION`: inheritance is strongest when the origin remains nameable and historically specific while the living recipient is free to transform what was inherited into a new act, relation, role, or work.**

A memory corollary is:

> **`MEMORY_AS_RELATIONAL_EVIDENCE_NOT_POSTHUMOUS_COMMAND`: artifacts, testimony, remembered acts, and even fixed final wishes can establish what the dead person loved, feared, intended, or requested. They do not automatically settle every later decision made in that person's name.**

A continuation corollary is:

> **`CONTINUATION_AS_FIDELITY_WITHOUT_REPLACEMENT`: new companions, new performers, new arrangements, new institutions, and new ambitions need not dilute a dead relationship. Continuation can be the fulfillment of anti-captivity rather than evidence of forgetting.**

The shortest rule is:

> **preserve provenance; transform continuation; do not make absence sovereign.**

---

# 3. Definitions and anti-definitions

## 3.1 Grief

Grief here means the continuing affective, relational, practical, and interpretive consequences of death or irreversible loss.

It does **not** mean:

- a single acute emotional episode;
- proof that the survivor is incapable of future attachment;
- a requirement that mature development culminate in forgetting;
- a diagnostic claim about clinical bereavement;
- a moral permission to attribute any later decision to the dead.

## 3.2 Memory

Memory includes:

- personal recollection;
- testimony by other witnesses;
- diaries and notebooks;
- songs and performances;
- physical objects;
- bodily provenance;
- archival/media representation;
- public reputation and myth.

Memory is not automatically identical to complete truth. Different witnesses can hold non-identical but compatible knowledge of the same person.

## 3.3 Inheritance

Inheritance means a later person or group receives something causally descended from an earlier relation or person: a technique, value, promise, role, work, object, standard, bodily history, opportunity, grievance, or vocabulary.

Inheritance does not itself imply:

- identity continuity;
- obligation to reproduce the original form;
- ownership by the predecessor;
- successor inferiority;
- moral debt without limit.

## 3.4 Replacement

Replacement is the claim or practice that one living person, work, role, or relation can stand **as the same relational object** as the absent original in a way that erases difference or transfers the original's jurisdiction.

Replacement must be distinguished from:

- succession;
- new companionship;
- transformed reperformance;
- role inheritance;
- influence;
- tribute;
- answer works;
- practical substitution in a narrow task.

A person may take over a function without becoming the dead person.

## 3.5 Provenance

Provenance means preserving where an inherited thing came from and what relation originally gave it meaning.

Provenance is the major safeguard against both erasure and capture:

- without provenance, the dead can disappear from history;
- with provenance but without transformation, the living can become trapped in restoration;
- mature inheritance requires both memory of origin and present-tense authorship.

---

# 4. Death changes the constitution of a relationship

The strongest cross-case fact is that death is not merely “one participant is no longer present.” It changes what kinds of relational acts are possible.

Before death, another person can:

- answer;
- correct a misunderstanding;
- revise a wish;
- refuse a memorial role;
- forgive or remain angry;
- change a professional standard;
- ask for something new;
- reveal that a survivor's interpretation is wrong.

After death, those possibilities are ordinarily unavailable.

The survivor may still have:

- fixed words;
- objects;
- recordings;
- diaries;
- testimony;
- habits learned from the dead;
- remembered obligations;
- public narratives;
- imagined address.

But interpretation becomes asymmetrical.

This is why the same source can be both **highly evidentiary** and **low in ongoing jurisdiction**. Mana's diary is excellent evidence that she loved Kotono and regretted their distance. It is poor evidence for the proposition that Kotono must complete Mana's idol future. Yō's final request is excellent evidence that she wanted miho not to remain trapped and explicitly allowed new companions. It is not evidence that Yō later chose every specific IIIX action performed in her name.

The first governing distinction is therefore:

> **historical authority is not the same as continuing decisional authority.**

---

# 5. A posthumous-authority ladder

P2-E-03 requires a more precise authority hierarchy than “the dead would have wanted X.”

## 5.1 Living present-tense self-authorship

For decisions about a living person's current identity, body, vocation, and relationships, the living person's authored position remains the strongest ordinary authority.

This is why Sakura decides what Sakura sings; Kotono decides whether Mana's voice will adjudicate her future; Makino decides whether his professional life requires Mana's continued ghost presence; miho decides whether and how to continue with living companions.

## 5.2 Direct fixed words from the deceased

A recorded or remembered request can be strong textual evidence about the deceased's intention **at the time it was expressed**.

Examples:

- Tomo's notebook rejects proxy wish-list fulfillment;
- Yō says not to remain trapped, permits new companions, and keeps ambition legitimate;
- Mana's diary proves love and unfinished song intention.

The scope of the request still matters. A fixed instruction is not a blank check to govern unforeseen later circumstances.

## 5.3 Direct posthumous agency

Mana is exceptional because the diegesis allows ghost Mana to speak and choose after death.

This gives her more direct posthumous agency than Tomo or Yō. It still does not restore ordinary living reciprocity or make her sovereign.

Mana herself:

- rejects Sakura/Kotono proxy substitution;
- accepts Kotono's right not to hear her answer;
- accepts eventual disappearance;
- does not require Makino to preserve supernatural copresence.

The exceptional ghost case therefore **strengthens**, rather than weakens, the anti-jurisdiction rule.

## 5.4 Documentary and material evidence

Diaries, pendants, notebooks, archived footage, donor history, and old songs can establish provenance and perspective. They cannot speak beyond what they contain.

## 5.5 Survivor interpretation

Statements such as “I must do this for her” require separate scrutiny. They may be sincere and psychologically powerful while remaining survivor-authored.

## 5.6 Public myth

Public reputation is often the least reliable guide to a dead person's total personhood. Mana as “miracle” and Yō as nearly erased former partner demonstrate opposite distortions: myth can overconcentrate memory or delete it.

# 6. Mana / Kotono — grief becomes dangerous when evidence is promoted into commission

The Mana/Kotono relationship provides the clearest route from ordinary attachment, through unresolved death, into proxy inheritance and finally transformed continuity.

Before Mana's career, Kotono's relation to her sister is ordinary and infrastructural: shared time, music, outings, drawings, and a dense attachment world. Career acceleration creates real scarcity. Mana continues to love Kotono; Kotono nevertheless loses dinners, conversation, shopping, and ordinary proximity. The sisters repeatedly attempt or desire repair but do not secure it before death.

This matters because death freezes not a harmonious relation but an **unfinished mismatch**.

Kotono's cruel final living words are not literal indifference. They conceal a wish for Mana to remain nearby as an ordinary older sister. Death removes the possibility of ordinary correction. The survivor is left with guilt plus incomplete knowledge.

Mana's diary later repairs one epistemic error: scarcity did not mean reduced love. It proves that Mana thought about Kotono, felt guilty, and intended a song for her.

Kotono then commits the critical interpretive overreach:

> evidence of unfinished intention becomes a self-imposed duty to complete the dead person's future.

Nothing in the diary says “become me” or “stand in my place.” The proxy mandate is Kotono-authored.

This is P2-E-03's first major rule:

> **the survivor may inherit an unfinished intention without inheriting the deceased person's identity or exclusive route.**

---

# 7. Mana / Kotono — address without adjudication

Ghost Mana makes the Mana/Kotono case unusually strong because the deceased person can actually respond.

Yet the relationship matures when Kotono refuses to make that answer determinative.

In `hoshimi_020_on_my_way`, Kotono chooses a one-sided conversation:

- she will speak to Mana;
- she does not want Mana's voice relayed back;
- she will not ask the dead sister to settle the vocational question;
- she must decide what kind of idol she wants to become.

Mana is informed of those terms and accepts them.

The governing structure is:

> **I can still address you without asking you to govern me.**

This is not severance. Kotono still wants Mana to know. She still hopes Mana watches. What changes is the **jurisdictional role** of the dead sister.

The later `With your songs` event demonstrates that this release makes more memory possible, not less. Kotono can investigate “idol Nagase Mana” through other witnesses precisely because she no longer needs one totalizing answer that will dictate her own future.

---

# 8. Plural Mana — memory becomes more accurate when no survivor claims total ownership

The 2026 answer-song material creates a memory/historiography principle with series-wide value.

Kotono possesses privileged family knowledge of Mana. That privilege is real. It is incomplete.

She seeks perspectives from people who knew Mana differently:

- Suzu as admirer and later inheritor of idol possibility;
- Haruko as older professional peer/friend;
- Rio as rival;
- Sakura through the extraordinary donor-heart and successor-pressure history.

The result is not a search for the single hidden “real Mana.” It is a recognition that:

> **family Mana, public idol Mana, rival Mana, colleague Mana, loved Mana, and remembered Mana are non-identical perspectives on one person.**

Plural memory resists two opposite failures:

1. **mythic flattening** — the public icon erases the private person;
2. **possessive memory** — the closest survivor claims exclusive authority over who the dead person really was.

P2-E-03 therefore treats plural testimony as a mature memory practice when provenance remains visible.

---

# 9. The pendant — transformed form as a material theory of memory

The Mana/Kotono pendant history is one of the corpus's cleanest object-level statements.

Kotono once makes Mana a matching beaded necklace. Kotono later loses hers. Mana takes her own necklace apart and remakes it into two matching star pendants.

The original form is gone.

The relation is not.

Mana explicitly treats the changed form as compatible with continued matching.

This object gives P2-E-03 a compact model:

> **continuity does not require restoration of original form.**

The same logic later governs:

- sisterhood after death;
- `With your songs` as an answer rather than a restored `song for you`;
- `Friend Glass` as transformed reperformance rather than counterfeit Sundance restoration;
- Haruko's fulfillment of Mana-linked promises through successor participation and changed roles;
- living units whose history remains real after their founding form changes.

Transformation is not automatically fidelity. But formal change is not automatically betrayal.

# 10. Sakura / Tomo — survival debt appears before the Mana transplant problem

Sakura's later origin supplies a decisive genealogy.

Before Mana becomes donor provenance, Sakura has already experienced severe illness, mortality awareness, friendship with Tomo, Tomo's decline, and Tomo's death.

Tomo gives Sakura several things:

- permission to cry rather than continuously reassure everyone;
- a wish-list practice that turns limited time into chosen desires;
- ordinary friendship and shared experience;
- a final complicated message about what Sakura should do after Tomo's death.

At one point Tomo asks Sakura to fulfill desires in her place. Sakura takes this seriously.

But the later-delivered notebook anticipates the exact proxy impulse and corrects it: Sakura should not live Tomo's list as Tomo's substitute; Sakura should pursue Sakura's own desires.

Both statements remain textual facts. The later notebook does not erase the earlier request. It narrows the legitimate interpretation of it.

The result is the earliest explicit anti-proxy rule in Sakura's life:

> **surviving someone does not make the survivor executor of the dead person's unrealized life.**

---

# 11. Sakura / Mana — embodied provenance without identity jurisdiction

The transplant makes inheritance literal.

Mana's heart is physically part of Sakura's living body. Sakura's post-transplant voice is publicly heard as Mana-like. The public increasingly names Sakura as Mana's return. Sakura herself begins to worry that her current impulses and idol life may not fully be “hers.”

This is the hardest possible non-replacement test because the inherited object is not merely a keepsake. It is a life-sustaining organ.

The corpus nevertheless refuses the inference:

> **body history = inherited will.**

Sakura's heartbeat practice predates her knowledge that Mana is the donor. Once provenance is disclosed, Sakura initially turns gratitude into mandate: perhaps she should sing Mana's unfinished song correctly.

Mana directly rejects the premise:

- Mana's song belongs to Mana;
- unresolved regret cannot be repaired by someone else reproducing Mana's act;
- Sakura needs a song only Sakura can sing.

Sakura's mature position is not bodily purification. She does not need to deny the heart's history.

Instead:

> **provenance becomes part of Sakura without becoming sovereign over Sakura.**

This is one of the strongest cross-series instances of inheritance without replacement.

---

# 12. Survivor guilt — moral emotion does not create retrospective omniscience

The miho/Yō material supplies a second major grief safeguard.

During Yō's hospitalization, miho leaves on a professional trip believing Yō will recover. Yō sends reassuring photographs. miho returns to discover far more serious deterioration than she had understood and later experiences intense guilt for leaving.

The guilt is textual and must not be dismissed.

But guilt cannot retroactively grant knowledge the survivor did not possess.

P2-E-03 therefore distinguishes:

- **regret** — “if I had known, I would have stayed”;
- **responsibility under the information actually available**;
- **counterfactual knowledge invented by grief**.

The relation-level rule is:

> **survivor guilt may be psychologically real without proving knowing abandonment.**

This matters beyond miho because grief repeatedly encourages characters to rewrite the past into a cleaner moral ledger than the source supports.

---

# 13. Yō's final request — anti-captivity is explicit text

Yō's final request is one of the strongest posthumous-authority controls because it directly anticipates the danger of memorial captivity.

She tells miho:

- do not remain trapped by me;
- find new companions;
- reach the top.

The request preserves ambition and future attachment simultaneously.

It therefore rejects two false fidelities:

1. **relational stasis** — staying alone proves devotion;
2. **vocational retreat** — continuing ambition betrays the dead.

Yō's singularity does not require miho's future to remain empty.

This becomes crucial when IIIX later acquires real trust, dependency, and shared work even while miho refuses ordinary friendship labeling. Yō does not need to be replaced as `たった一人の親友` for her anti-captivity request to succeed.

The mature rule is:

> **a dead relation can remain singular without requiring all later relations to be shallow.**

---

# 14. Unequal public mourning — grief can identify a real social-memory injury

Yō's death occurs in the shadow of Mana's much larger public catastrophe.

miho sees widespread mourning for Mana and little public attention to Yō. She asks why nobody will grieve her friend too.

The later extremity of miho's response must not erase the original injury:

> **public memory is unequally distributed.**

Some dead people become symbols. Others become footnotes or disappear from the visible record.

P2-E-03 therefore refuses a simplistic therapeutic reading in which the correct answer to grief is purely private acceptance. Yō was an idol. Public erasure is not only an internal feeling; it concerns archive, attribution, media, and cultural memory.

The error enters when miho converts that real injury into a zero-sum memorial program.

---

# 15. miho's memorial office — zero-sum memory is survivor-authored, not Yō-mandated

miho responds to Yō's public neglect by deciding that Mana's traces should be erased in return.

The logic is psychologically intelligible:

- public memory appears finite;
- Mana occupies overwhelming memorial space;
- Yō appears absent from it;
- competitive records are actionable where death is not.

But the program fails on its own terms.

Erasing Mana cannot restore Yō's reciprocal presence. More importantly, Yō never commanded it.

The project is best classified as:

> **`SURVIVOR_AUTHORED_MEMORIAL_OFFICE`** — the survivor appoints herself administrator of what history is allowed to remember, then mistakes that office for the dead person's will.

The later remembered Sundance fan breaks the strongest premise: Yō was not literally forgotten by everyone.

That correction does not erase unequal mourning. It destroys the totalizing zero-sum model.

---

# 16. Private fidelity can fail public memory

The U.S. archive sequence makes a second problem explicit.

A promotional video presents miho's Sundance past while removing Yō, effectively making the history appear solo. The edit treats the dead former partner as inconvenient noise inside a polished public story.

miho is horrified.

Yet the source then turns the problem back toward her own strategy.

If Yō's story remains completely sealed inside miho, if Yō-associated work cannot be touched, and if public presentation never names Yō, the living public has little route by which to encounter her.

This produces a real tension:

- privacy can protect against appropriation;
- circulation can protect against disappearance.

The answer is not “everything private must be public.” It is narrower:

> **private remembrance alone cannot accomplish the separate goal of public memorial survival.**

That distinction routes directly into P2-E-04's later publicity/media/disclosure ledger.

---

# 17. `Friend Glass` — exact relational truth cannot be reproduced

`Friend Glass` is the corpus's strongest memorial-art test.

miho states that the original song was essentially **miho and Yō themselves**. Its meaning therefore depends on a specific relation that no longer exists in living reciprocal form.

That creates an impossibility:

> the old relation cannot be performed “as before.”

The false solutions are:

- pretend fran or kana is Yō;
- reproduce the old arrangement exactly and call the relation restored;
- never perform the work again because change would be betrayal.

Instead IIIX changes the work substantially so that it expresses the abrasive, living relation IIIX actually has.

The new performance preserves attribution to Sundance/Yō while refusing relational counterfeit.

The model is:

> **`MEMORIAL_REPERFORMANCE_WITHOUT_RELATIONAL_REPLICATION`**.

The changed form is not less faithful merely because it is changed. In this case change is what prevents a false claim that the original relation can be recreated.

---

# 18. `Friend Glass` — new art can send memory backward rather than overwrite it

The transformed IIIX performance creates a second memorial effect.

miho introduces the song publicly as a precious Sundance work sung with an important friend, explicitly says the old form cannot be reproduced, and asks the audience to hear the current friendship song.

After the performance:

- listeners seek the new version;
- some seek the original;
- comparisons make Sundance/Yō discoverable;
- the possibility of new Yō fans becomes visible.

The new work therefore does not supersede the old one.

It can circulate attention **backward** toward provenance.

This gives P2-E-03 a public-memory principle:

> **transformation can expand memorial circulation when the new work names rather than captures its origin.**

---

# 19. “I'm no longer alone” — continuation is not replacement

At the end of the `Friend Glass` sequence, miho imagines what she would tell Yō:

> `もう一人じゃないから、安心して`

The line would be badly misread as “I found people who replace you.”

Its actual structure is anti-captive.

Yō explicitly told miho to find new companions. miho can now acknowledge that living relationships have become consequential while still naming Yō as singular and preserving Sundance provenance.

This case clarifies non-replacement:

> **the dead person's uniqueness is not measured by how empty the survivor keeps the future.**

New attachment can be evidence that a dead person's anti-captivity hope succeeded.

---

# 20. Mana / Makino — death interrupts a future rather than completing it

Mana asks Makino whether he will remain beside her.

Death intervenes before he can answer in shared living time.

Makino later answers the question in grief.

The distinction matters because posthumous commitment is not equivalent to a mutually received living promise. The relationship remains genuinely unfinished.

The ghost period then allows new conversations, including explicit romantic recognition that living Mana and Makino never reached together.

This does not restore the lost living future.

The farewell makes the limit physical: Mana names love; Makino attempts a kiss; there is no physical sensation; Mana disappears.

The posthumous relation can therefore produce new truth without undoing death.

P2-E-03 rejects both extremes:

- “death means nothing new can happen relationally”; and
- “ghost presence restores the relationship to ordinary living reciprocity.”

---

# 21. Mana / Makino — separation without erasure

After Mana's final disappearance, Makino says that her remaining beside him would make him happy, but would not feel right.

This is not rejection of love.

It is acceptance that continued supernatural availability should not become the condition under which the relationship remains real.

Makino can still:

- miss Mana;
- love her;
- think about what she would say;
- treat their meeting as vocationally constitutive;
- regard management as a life made meaningful through that relation.

He no longer requires:

- ghost Mana to authorize each decision;
- a living idol to become Mana;
- his profession to remain an unfinished memorial task.

The mature form is:

> **separation without erasure.**

This is one of the corpus's strongest counterexamples to the idea that fidelity requires continuing access.

---

# 22. Mana as dead rival — frozen benchmarks can capture futurity

LizNoir demonstrates a different grief mechanism.

Mana's death occurs before the decisive contest can be completed. A living rival is answerable: she can improve, lose, win, change style, demand another rematch, or cease to matter. A dead rival cannot.

The benchmark freezes.

Rio and Aoi's desire to defeat Mana is not wrong because competition is wrong. The problem is temporal:

> **one impossible rematch can begin to monopolize what all future contests mean.**

This is **futurity capture**.

The dead rival becomes permanently perfect for the role the survivor assigns her because she can no longer answer or change.

The later loss to Tsuki begins breaking result sovereignty. Rio/Aoi discover that even a decisive defeat cannot retroactively make their partnership meaningless. Later LizNoir can treat Mana as formative rival without allowing the impossible rematch to own the unit's future.

This is grief through competition rather than family or friendship, but the constitutional problem is the same.

---

# 23. Haruko / Mana — promises survive by translation rather than restoration

Haruko carries unusual Hoshimi memory authority:

- her idol desire predates Mana;
- Mana becomes friend, benchmark, witness, and source of belief;
- they make stage-facing promises;
- Mana dies before literal fulfillment.

Later Haruko does not solve the unfinished promises by insisting on impossible restoration.

Instead:

- an unfinished gratitude song becomes shared current-Hoshimi work;
- successor performers can inherit its stage function;
- Haruko remains an active idol rather than retiring into memorial custodianship;
- a revived musical can cast Haruko in the role Mana once played without claiming Haruko becomes Mana;
- Haruko explicitly marks imagined copresence with Mana as imagination;
- she learns from Mana's precedent and then leads in her own way.

The governing model is:

> **`TRANSMISSION_WITHOUT_SELF_ERASURE`: a survivor may carry forward a promise by translating its relational purpose into a form available to the living.**

Literal reenactment is not the only measure of fidelity.

# 24. Nagisa and the notebook — relational succession without replacement

Mana's relationship to Kotono also produces a living relational inheritance through Nagisa.

Mana recognizes Nagisa as someone who can remain beside Kotono under conditions Mana herself cannot fully supply. After bereavement, Nagisa becomes one of the living relations that makes Kotono's survival and later self-authorship possible.

The drawing notebook becomes a crucial medium. Mana and Kotono once used it as asynchronous sister communication. Nagisa later writes herself into its “you are not alone” grammar.

This does **not** make Nagisa a replacement sister.

The inheritance is functional and relational:

- a care practice survives;
- a medium survives;
- the living relation is new;
- the original sisterhood remains historically specific.

P2-E-03 therefore distinguishes:

> **relational succession from relational substitution.**

A later person can help carry a function once associated with the dead without becoming the dead person.

# 25. Inherited practices — the most durable legacy is often procedural

Some of the franchise's most productive inheritance is neither object nor identity.

It is a **way of acting**.

Examples include:

- Mana's audience-singularity ethic inherited by Sakura, later revised by distributed care and bodily limits;
- Mana-linked care techniques passing through Haruko into Sakura/Kotono support;
- Sundance's audience-happiness telos returning inside miho's later IIIX work;
- technical craft descending from Sundance without making technique itself a grief pathology;
- Mana's ordinary-person recognition and coauthored-work model shaping Makino's later management of living idols;
- Kotono's transformed answer-song practice turning remembered relation into new authorship.

Procedural inheritance is especially resistant to replacement because it does not require the recipient to resemble the predecessor.

The mature rule is:

> **a practice can descend from a dead person while becoming genuinely owned by the living person who now performs it.**

# 26. Public memory and private memory are different goods

P2-E-03 must keep two memory problems separate.

## Private continuity

A survivor may preserve relation through:

- internal address;
- keepsakes;
- private recollection;
- personal rituals;
- bodily memory;
- trusted confidants.

## Public memorial continuity

A public figure's name and work persist only if some combination of archive, attribution, performance, media, testimony, or institutional memory makes them encounterable.

The Yō case demonstrates that private fidelity does not automatically achieve public survival.

The Mana case demonstrates the opposite danger: public memory can become so dominant that the person is flattened into myth and other dead figures become invisible by comparison.

The mature system therefore needs both:

- **protection from erasure**;
- **protection from mythic capture**.

P2-E-04 will inherit the media/disclosure side of this problem.

# 27. Non-replacement taxonomy

“Non-replacement” is too vague unless the types are separated.

## 27.1 Identity non-replacement

Sakura is not Mana because she carries Mana's heart.

## 27.2 Relational non-replacement

Nagisa is not Mana because she supports Kotono. fran/kana are not Yō because they become important to miho.

## 27.3 Artistic non-replacement

The IIIX `Friend Glass` performance does not become the same relational artwork as the Sundance original. `With your songs` is not `song for you` completed by proxy.

## 27.4 Role non-replacement

Haruko can perform a role Mana once performed without becoming Mana. Later LizNoir members can become legitimate coauthors without proving founders were interchangeable.

## 27.5 Benchmark non-replacement

A later rival can occupy a similar competitive function without erasing the historical role of Mana.

## 27.6 Memorial non-replacement

Public attention to one dead person does not have to erase another. The correction to unequal memory is additive/plural, not counter-erasure.

The common principle is:

> **same function is not same person; inherited function does not transfer total provenance or identity.**

# 28. What mature grief does not require

The frozen corpus does not support a universal “closure” doctrine.

Mature grief does not require:

- no longer crying;
- no longer missing the dead;
- removing all keepsakes;
- ceasing to address the dead imaginatively;
- denying that the dead shaped one's vocation;
- ending rivalry or aspiration because the rival is dead;
- forming a new relation in the same category;
- total public memorial success;
- certainty about metaphysical mechanisms.

Kotono can cry and love Mana after proxy duty ends. Makino can remain lonely after accepting Mana's disappearance. miho can preserve Yō as singular while becoming less alone. Haruko can carry Mana promises while remaining an active current performer.

The criterion is not emotional absence.

It is whether grief still **owns the future by default**.

# 30. Longitudinal synthesis

Across the strongest cross-unit cases, grief/inheritance repeatedly moves through a seven-stage grammar.

### Stage I — living relation

The dead person must first be restored as a person rather than a symbol: sister, friend, rival, performer, manager-partner, colleague.

### Stage II — irreversible interruption

Death removes ordinary reciprocal correction. Unresolved conflict, promises, rivalry, and desire become frozen.

### Stage III — authority inflation

The survivor promotes memory, artifact, guilt, bodily provenance, public myth, or unfinished intention into a rule for the future.

### Stage IV — contradiction

The inherited mandate begins harming living identity, relations, public truth, bodily sustainability, or the stated values of the dead person.

### Stage V — provenance recovery

The survivor distinguishes:

- what the dead actually said;
- what artifacts actually prove;
- what the survivor added;
- what other witnesses know;
- what cannot now be known.

### Stage VI — transformed continuation

The living person reauthors the inheritance through a new song, new relation, new role, new institution, new rivalry, new public circulation, or new practice.

### Stage VII — continuing relation without sovereignty

The dead remains loved, grieved, named, studied, answered, or admired, but no longer possesses the living future by default.

Not every case completes every stage by the locked snapshot. miho's grief endpoint and broad public Yō remembrance remain open. The grammar is therefore descriptive, not a claim of universal completion.

# 31. What counts as stronger evidence of mature inheritance

Stronger evidence includes:

- explicit distinction between the dead person's wish and survivor interpretation;
- explicit refusal of proxy identity;
- changed form with preserved attribution;
- new living relationships acknowledged without declaring the dead replaced;
- plural witness reconstruction;
- ability to remember without requiring adjudication from the dead;
- ability to preserve an inherited practice while revising its harmful application;
- public memorial circulation that names provenance;
- continuing grief that no longer dictates all future choices;
- acceptance that some counterfactual living future is unknowable.

---

# 32. What counts as stronger evidence of memorial capture

Stronger evidence includes:

- “I must do this because the dead person would want it” without supporting fixed evidence;
- treating a successor as the same person as the dead predecessor;
- using donor history as identity jurisdiction;
- keeping living relationships shallow to preserve singularity;
- erasing another dead person to make memorial space;
- refusing any transformation of inherited material even when exact restoration is impossible;
- allowing an unanswerable dead rival to monopolize all future competition;
- claiming sole ownership of the dead person's total history;
- making grief itself evidence that the survivor has a permanent moral debt.

# 40. Thematic capstone

P2-E-03 stabilizes a grief theory that is neither “let go” nor “keep everything unchanged.”

IDOLY PRIDE's dead remain powerful because their relationships had real content before death. The mature corpus repeatedly restores that content before asking what inheritance should mean. Mana was not born as a legend; Yō was not born as a memorial grievance; Tomo was not born as a bucket-list instruction. They were living people who could surprise, care, compete, disagree, and make ordinary requests.

Death makes them easier to simplify.

That simplification can take opposite forms:

- sanctification into a perfect benchmark;
- reduction into a duty;
- use as a replacement template;
- deletion from public memory;
- sequestration into private memory so complete that public history disappears;
- conversion of a relational work into a fixed relic;
- conversion of a survivor's guilt into retroactive omniscience.

The mature answer is **provenance-preserving transformation**.

Kotono does not erase Mana; she stops asking Mana to own her future and then remembers her more pluralistically. Sakura does not purge Mana's heart; she refuses to treat it as inherited will. Makino does not stop loving Mana; he stops requiring her continued ghost presence. Haruko does not discard shared promises; she changes how they are fulfilled. LizNoir does not forget Mana as rival; it stops allowing an impossible rematch to monopolize futurity. miho does not replace Yō with IIIX; she learns that Yō's singularity can coexist with new companions and that transformed public art may preserve Yō better than sealed private possession.

The capstone formulation is therefore:

> **`TRANSFORMED_CONTINUITY_WITHOUT_POSTHUMOUS_JURISDICTION`: the dead remain part of the causal architecture of the living, but the most faithful inheritance preserves origin while returning authorship to present-tense persons.**

And the practical rule is:

> **preserve provenance; transform continuation; do not make absence sovereign.**
