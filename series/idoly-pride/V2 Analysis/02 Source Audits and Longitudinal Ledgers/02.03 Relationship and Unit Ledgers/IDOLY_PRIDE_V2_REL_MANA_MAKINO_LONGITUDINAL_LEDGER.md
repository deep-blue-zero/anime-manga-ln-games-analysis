# 0. Responsibility, authority, and the central problem

This ledger is the current Phase-2 relationship authority for **Nagase Mana / Makino Kouhei** through `IP-V2-SNAPSHOT-2026-08-13-A`.

The relationship is unusually easy to misread because four different structures occupy the same pair:

1. a school-age boy and girl with ordinary pre-professional attraction and curiosity;
2. an idol and manager whose careers become deeply interdependent;
3. a survivor and a directly acting ghost whose posthumous co-presence permits conversations unavailable in ordinary bereavement;
4. two people whose romantic relation becomes explicit only when death has already made an ordinary living relationship impossible.

The ledger therefore refuses several common collapses:

- **professional care is not automatically romance;**
- **romantic attraction does not make the professional relationship fake;**
- **mutual reliance does not erase managerial duty or performer authorship;**
- **Makino's fixed professional boundary does not prove absence of feeling;**
- **Mana's later confession does not retroactively create a living dating history;**
- **Makino's attempted farewell kiss does not establish that he consciously knew himself to be in love before Mana's death;**
- **ghost intimacy is not an ordinary continuation of the living relationship;**
- **continued love after farewell does not require continued supernatural presence;**
- **Sakura and Kotono's success is not proof that they become substitutes for Mana.**

The strongest longitudinal formulation is:

> **`COAUTHORED_VOCATION_WITH_POSTHUMOUS_ROMANTIC_RECOGNITION_MANA_MAKINO`: Mana selects Makino at the moment she selects idolhood because his ordinary, non-reverential gaze gives her a form of recognition she wants beside her. Makino initially follows because Mana asks, but independently chooses the profession as it becomes meaningful to him. Their living relationship matures through observation, argument, negotiated authority, reciprocal care, and an explicit denial that Mana's apparently singular performance is causally solitary. Romantic attraction is present in the living record on both sides, but it never becomes an explicitly mutually known or enacted couple-state. Death interrupts Mana's unanswered request that Makino remain beside her. The ghost period then makes the missed relationship legible: Mana eventually names love, Makino answers with a fixed kiss attempt, and both lose the possibility of converting that recognition into an ordinary future. Their final maturation therefore requires separation: Makino can remain shaped by Mana, love her, and regard their meeting as vocationally constitutive without requiring Mana to remain supernaturally available or requiring living idols to become her replacements.**

The deepest paradox is:

> **their romantic truth becomes explicit only after the condition for an ordinary mutual romance has been permanently removed.**

That paradox must not be solved by rewriting the living relationship as a secret established couple.

## 2.2 Disclosure chronology

The audience does not receive the relationship in causal order.

Early Hoshimi material begins with **death already present**. Makino's grief and ghost Mana can initially make the pair look like a tragic romance whose professional past merely supplies backstory.

Mana's origins later complicate that view by restoring the actual living architecture:

- the relationship begins before professional roles;
- Makino has ordinary adolescent attraction but is not yet emotionally close to Mana;
- Mana selects him because he does **not** already treat her as an idol-object;
- the actual school “confession” is a work invitation, not a declaration of love;
- management becomes a learned joint practice;
- Makino later rechooses it after Mana explicitly gives him an exit;
- conflict over health and work is genuine, not flirtatious theater;
- Mana and Makino develop a constitution for disagreeing with one another.

The 2024 homecoming event then retroactively adds living-era romantic coding without changing living status. It shows that Mana could behave in a manner legibly directed toward Makino before death even though the final farewell later says her conscious recognition came “too late.”

Hoshimi 020 adds another crucial late disclosure: Mana had told Kotono before idolhood that she liked someone. Makino himself did **not** know that fact until after Mana's death. Endpoint knowledge therefore cannot be imported into his living choices.

Hoshimi 022 finally makes Mana's own romantic category explicit and supplies Makino's fixed kiss response. This settles more than early teasing ever could, but it settles the pair **at farewell**, not retroactively as an established pre-death couple.

# 4. Formation: ordinary gaze as the origin of exceptional trust

Mana does not choose Makino because he is already an expert manager. He is not.

She chooses him because he possesses a scarce relational resource: **ordinary sight**.

Mana is socially conspicuous before professional debut. Other people already respond to her beauty, charisma, and centrality. Makino also notices her beauty, but he does not organize his behavior around obtaining access to an idol-object. When Mana asks whom in class treats her most normally, she arrives at Makino.

That matters more than technical qualification.

Her request is roughly:

- come with me to Hoshimi;
- become the person beside my idol project;
- keep giving me the flat perspective you already give me as an ordinary classmate.

This creates a relationship architecture the rest of the series repeatedly tests:

> **Can the person professionally responsible for “Nagase Mana the idol” continue to answer to “Mana the person” rather than merely to the public object?**

At first, neither knows how to do the job. Their professional relation is therefore not manager-as-creator over raw talent. Mana supplies the original vocation. Makino supplies a gaze and then learns a profession capable of supporting that vocation.

This is why `二人三脚` is structurally apt. It should not be romanticized into fusion. Two people remain two centers of agency.

# 5. Double choosing: Mana recruits Makino; Makino later rechooses the work

A weaker reading treats Makino's entire identity as something Mana gives him.

The sources support a two-stage account instead.

## 5.1 First choice: Mana chooses him

Mana makes the initiating relational move. Her idol project includes Makino from inception.

This is not institutional fate. It is not Saegusa assigning a random employee. Mana asks for this person.

The farewell later reveals the ethical weight she attached to that request: because he came **for her**, she wanted to make the choice worthwhile **for him**.

## 5.2 Second choice: Makino chooses to stay

At graduation, Mana does something analytically important: she gives him an exit.

She does not claim that his original acceptance creates indefinite personal debt. She tells him to consider his own future.

Makino remains anyway because:

- management has become interesting in itself;
- and he wants to see how far Mana can go.

This is the first major anti-captivity hinge in the relationship.

Mana catalyzes Makino's vocation. She does not permanently own it.

The same structure later makes survival possible. If management had never become anything other than “being Mana's manager,” then her death could only terminate Makino's professional identity or condemn him to proxy repetition. The series instead lets the original relation become a durable source without remaining an exclusive jurisdiction.

# 6. Professional intimacy: observation, argument, and the back-push ritual

The relationship becomes intimate professionally before it becomes explicit romantically.

That intimacy has at least four dimensions.

## 6.1 Fine-grained observation

Makino learns Mana's deviations from baseline.

When her sister conflict affects her, he can tell that something is wrong even when obvious surface signs are absent. Mana notices this and remarks that he watches her closely.

This is not yet romance. It is high-quality relational attention.

## 6.2 Permission to disagree

Mana and Makino do not preserve closeness by avoiding conflict.

They argue about:

- workload;
- what opportunities should be refused;
- whether physical risk justifies restriction;
- what an idol owes a one-time audience;
- what a manager owes the body and future of the performer;
- who gets to decide under uncertainty.

The relationship is strongest when disagreement becomes **answerable** rather than unilateral.

## 6.3 The back-push ritual

Mana asks Makino to push her back before she goes onstage. She calls it an `おまじない`.

The image is analytically clean:

- Makino supplies force at the threshold;
- Mana crosses it;
- Mana performs;
- the audience receives Mana's song;
- Mana later thanks Makino for helping her reach the act.

Support is real without becoming authorship seizure.

## 6.4 Reciprocal care

Makino is not the only caregiver.

Ghost-era and living evidence alike show Mana noticing Makino's exhaustion, telling him to sleep, and objecting when he drives himself beyond sustainable work. This blocks a paternal reading in which the manager possesses all protective intelligence and the performer is only managed risk.

Their mature relationship is better described as **differentiated reciprocal care**.

# 7. The impossible-stage dispute: from veto to negotiated authority

The most important living professional conflict is not evidence that the relationship fails. It is evidence of what kind of relationship it becomes.

Mana's career accelerates. She accepts work aggressively. Makino sees exhaustion and argues for reduction. Mana experiences intervention as a threat to the singing through which she understands the ethical meaning of idol work.

When Mana collapses and is hospitalized, Makino refuses an important performance without consulting her.

Both positions contain truth and error.

## Makino's valid claim

He is not merely a scheduler. He has responsibility for:

- Mana's body;
- other staff who absorb emergency consequences;
- family worry;
- the sustainability of a career rather than one performance.

## Makino's procedural error

He initially treats protection as sufficient authority to decide for her.

Mana correctly objects: it is her work and her body, and the opportunity matters for reasons he has not fully understood.

## Mana's valid claim

For Mana, the audience is not an abstraction. A particular listener may have only one chance to receive a song. “Just cancel” therefore has an ethical cost that cannot be represented only as schedule optimization.

## Mana's error

She underweights the way her self-sacrifice transfers risk and labor to other people.

## The resolution

Neither side simply wins.

Makino understands more of what Mana is carrying. Mana understands more of what she has made Makino and others carry. He allows the important performance under a safety threshold and mandatory recovery period.

His mature line—stopping her and letting her sing are both his job—captures the relationship's professional constitution.

The model is:

> **professional intervention is legitimate only when it remains answerable to the performer's self-authored ends; performer autonomy is not a license to externalize unlimited risk onto the relational system that makes performance possible.**

This principle matters downstream far beyond Mana/Makino.

- his attempt to suppress hope about what she might want;

## 8.2 Mana's affect before conscious labeling

The strongest fixed evidence is layered:

- Kotono remembers pre-idol Mana saying she had someone she liked and describing a person who is ordinary/unflashy but morally clean and helpful—highly Makino-legible;
- the admitted Hoshimi homecoming flashback shows living Mana using `大好きな人` in a conspicuously Makino-directed exchange, then taking his hand while avoiding explicit naming.

The combined evidence makes “Mana only developed feelings after death” untenable.

But the final farewell also matters: `もう少し早く気付いてたら` indicates that **conscious recognition or explicit self-knowledge came too late**.

The most defensible reconciliation is:

> **Mana's behavior and attraction precede her fully articulated recognition of being in love.**

The exact moment of conscious recognition remains open.

## 8.3 Mutual knowledge before death

This is where endpoint inflation must stop.

Makino explicitly learns only after Mana's death that pre-idol Mana had said she liked someone. Hoshimi 020 marks this as new information to him.

There is no fixed evidence that Mana knew Makino's reciprocal romantic state while alive.

Therefore:

> **bidirectional attraction does not equal mutually known romantic reciprocity.**

## 8.4 Professional boundary as action constraint

Makino fixedly states that as a manager he has enough professional pride not to make romantic/sexual advances on talent he manages.

This does not prove that he feels nothing.

It establishes a boundary between:

- feeling;
- permissible professional action.

Because Makino already had attraction before management, the text itself demonstrates why those categories cannot be collapsed.

The ledger does **not** claim that this boundary is explicitly stated to be the sole reason he and Mana never date. Death, imperfect self-knowledge, workload, and simple non-confession also matter. The source supports the boundary as a real constraint, not a total causal explanation.

# 9. Mana's anti-solitary declaration: the dyad as a condition of singular performance

Immediately before death, Mana supplies one of the relationship's clearest self-descriptions.

She acknowledges that Makino has:

- stayed beside her;
- scolded her when she ran too hard;
- comforted her;
- protected her.

Then she says:

> `アイドル長瀬麻奈は、一人だけど、一人じゃないんだ`

and:

> `一人じゃないから、歌えるんだよ、私`

This does not mean Makino co-owns Mana's performance.

It means singular artistry is **relationally scaffolded**.

The performer onstage is one person. The causal architecture that makes the performance sustainable is not one person.

This is one of the strongest textual ancestors of the series-wide `ANSWERABLE_INTERDEPENDENCE` architecture.

# 10. Death interrupts the living answer

Mana then asks:

> `これからも、私のそばにいてくれる？`

The scene does not grant her an answer before death.

That gap must be preserved.

After the accident, Makino remembers her question and answers that of course he will remain beside her—`これからも……ずっと`.

Emotionally, this is a direct response.

Chronologically, it misses its recipient.

Therefore the correct claim is not:

> “Makino promised Mana while she was alive that he would always stay.”

It is:

> **Mana asked for a future; death intervened before the answer could become shared knowledge; Makino's answer exists as grief-stricken posthumous commitment.**

This missing exchange is the relationship's central rupture.

It also previews the romantic one. Later, the pair will finally understand more about what they felt, but again too late for ordinary mutual enactment.

# 11. Ghost Mana: continuation under altered ontology

Ghost Mana is not merely Makino remembering her.

Within the story she has direct diegetic agency. She can:

- speak;
- observe;
- argue;
- tease;
- desire;
- withhold;
- change her mind;
- make decisions about whether her continued presence should influence survivors.

But the ghost relationship is not a resumed living relationship.

Several asymmetries remain absolute:

- Mana's body/life cannot be restored;
- physical contact is normally impossible;
- Makino ages while Mana's appearance remains fixed at death;
- public recognition is constrained;
- ordinary shared future planning cannot proceed normally;
- Mana's continued presence is narratively tied to unresolved purpose and eventual disappearance.

The ghost period therefore creates a strange freedom.

Mana can joke that since she cannot be touched, she can bring her face close and say `キスキス`. She also says that they can finally collide in their ordinary selves.

The professional roles no longer have exactly the same practical structure they had while she was an employed living idol.

This loosens speech.

It does not erase history.

# 12. From memorial mission to non-replacement

After Mana's death, Makino initially organizes substantial professional energy around her unfinished trajectory.

That response is understandable and dangerous.

If the goal becomes:

> create another Mana / finish Mana through another girl,

then both Mana and the living idol are denied personhood.

Hoshimi eventually rejects this.

Ghost Mana is one of the rejection's strongest agents. She insists that:

- Mana's song belongs to Mana;
- Sakura needs Sakura's song;
- Kotono needs Kotono's song.

Makino correspondingly comes to treat the successors as people who may become as great as or greater than Mana **through difference**.

This is crucial for the Mana/Makino relationship itself.

Makino does not honor Mana most accurately by keeping his managerial life frozen at the moment of her death.

He honors the professional relation they actually built by continuing the part of it that was never supposed to be possession:

- observe accurately;
- protect without seizing the goal;
- argue when necessary;
- help an idol become more fully the person she is rather than more fully Mana.

---

# 13. Mana's “real dream”: reciprocity rather than possession

Hoshimi 022 radically reframes Mana's ambition.

At the school where she first recruited Makino, she recalls the promise she made when he agreed to come:

> `絶対、後悔させないから`

She had understood his acceptance as a gift.

Her “real dream” was therefore not simply:

> win the NEXT VENUS Grand Prix.

It was:

> become the top idol so that the person who agreed to follow her would become the manager of the top idol, and would know that coming with her had been worthwhile.

Mana says this explicitly as `最高のお返し`.

The relationship therefore contains **reciprocal vocational gift logic**:

- Mana asks Makino to risk a future beside her project;
- Makino says yes;
- Mana converts that yes into an ethical promise to make the risk worthwhile;
- Makino converts the original request into a profession he later chooses for himself.

Neither is simply debtor or owner.

Death prevents Mana from completing the gift through her own living career. Her attempt to route the remaining promise through Sakura and Kotono is understandable but must ultimately be constrained by anti-replacement. They cannot become Mana for the sake of satisfying Mana/Makino unfinished business.

The promise is finally fulfilled in a transformed sense when Mana believes the living idols can reach the top under Makino **as themselves**.

# 14. The farewell confession: explicit love, strong reciprocity, impossible enactment

Only after the professional/memorial promise is settled does Mana name the romantic truth directly.

She tells Makino:

> `良かった / {user}くんを好きになって`

This is the strongest relationship-category evidence in the corpus.

Makino's response is not a player option and not a branch-only flirtation.

Mana says:

> `もう少し早く気付いてたら…… / ……キスくらい、出来たかもよ`

Fixed narration follows:

> `（唇を重ねる）`

Then immediately denies physical consummation:

- there is no sensation;
- Makino passes through her;
- Mana disappears.

The correct adjudication is precise.

### Established

- Mana loves Makino.
- Mana understands that she recognized it too late for ordinary living enactment.
- Makino attempts to answer the invitation physically.
- Makino's response strongly supports reciprocal romantic feeling at that moment.

### Not established

- Makino explicitly saying `俺も好きだった`.
- exact timing of Makino's conscious romantic self-recognition.
- a living mutual confession.
- a living kiss.
- dating.
- couple status.
- any future in which the two negotiate ordinary romantic life together.

The farewell is therefore not a belated transition into dating.

It is **recognition at the boundary of irrevocable separation**.

---

# 15. After disappearance: love without supernatural retention

Hoshimi 023 supplies the mature endpoint.

Makino does not pretend he is no longer lonely.

He says:

- yes, he is lonely;
- Mana remaining beside him would make him happy;
- but that continuation would not feel right.

This is not emotional repudiation.

It is jurisdictional release.

Mana's presence had once been desired because grief could not accept absence. Later her direct presence becomes unnecessary for the relationship to remain causally and emotionally real.

Makino can still internally ask what Mana would think. He can still address his conclusions to her memory. He can still understand his career through the day they met.

But he does not need:

- Mana to remain a ghost;
- Mana to authorize every decision;
- Sakura or Kotono to become Mana;
- his own professional identity to remain “the man whose only legitimate purpose is completing Mana.”

His line that he is glad he chose this work, addressed to Mana, closes the vocational loop.

Mana promised to make him glad he came.

Makino ultimately is.

The fulfillment survives Mana's death without requiring her continued control of his future.

# 21. Final relationship model

The complete relationship can be represented as five successive constitutions.

## I. Ordinary recognition

Makino first matters to Mana because he does **not** treat her as exceptional in the socially distorting way others do.

## II. Co-authored vocation

Mana says “come with me.” Makino does. Later he stays because he chooses the work for himself.

## III. Negotiated professional interdependence

They learn how to observe, correct, protect, and argue without converting managerial responsibility into ownership or performer autonomy into immunity from consequence.

## IV. Death-interrupted mutuality and posthumous recognition

Living attraction exists, but mutual romantic knowledge never stabilizes. Death cuts through Mana's explicit request for continued adjacency. Ghost life permits delayed knowledge. Mana finally names love; Makino's fixed kiss attempt strongly answers it; physical and temporal reality prevent ordinary enactment.
