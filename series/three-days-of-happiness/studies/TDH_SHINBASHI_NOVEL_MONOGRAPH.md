# Three Days of Happiness — Shinbashi (シンバシ)

## Orientation

Shinbashi lives next door to Kusunoki, a young man who has sold most of his remaining life and goes out with Miyagi, the watcher assigned to him. Ordinary people at first cannot see her. Kusunoki remembers Shinbashi looking disturbed when he saw Kusunoki talking to her around the apartment. When three men harass Kusunoki at a pub, that same neighbor publicly greets both Kusunoki and Miyagi, and takes a harasser's seat. He later invites both of them into his circle and lends Kusunoki a car for an outing with Miyagi. His account of her changes: he first proposes an unusually persuasive mime, then doubts that mime adequately explains what Kusunoki is doing. He never claims to see her (Ch14 P0010–P0043, P0050–P0065, P0069–P0077).

The portrait's center is **willingness to act before the question of belief is settled**. Shinbashi first acts on curiosity about his own perceptions. He follows through in ways that help two people: he gives them somewhere to go together and a means of going farther. His generosity is observable whether or not he admitted a helpful motive in the pub; his reasons for intervening need not reduce to benevolence or to experiment alone.

## 1. A public intervention with a private question

Shinbashi walks up as if continuing an ordinary acquaintance. He mentions Miyagi by name before demanding the seat. The request is formally polite and, in Kusunoki's hearing, delivered in an intimidating tone. Kusunoki attributes the harasser's changed demeanor to Shinbashi's size and look; there is no recorded fight. Shinbashi introduces himself to Miyagi and supplies a reply as though she had answered, even though she has remained silent. This failed conversational alignment is Kusunoki's immediate reason for concluding that the neighbor cannot actually see her. After the men leave, the public smile drops (Ch14 P0010–P0021).

Shinbashi denies both literal belief in Miyagi and Kusunoki's suggestion that he came over simply to help. His own account is precise: he takes Kusunoki's behavior as a performance intended to destabilize other people's perceptions; he admits it has partly worked on him; and he wants to find out what happens if he actively accepts the sense of someone there. He wonders if he might eventually see her. That is his experimental question, not an observation of a change in vision. He says Kusunoki's description matches the person he had imagined. The novel gives his report, not an independently recorded image or an explanation of the match (Ch14 P0022–P0032).

When Kusunoki says Miyagi wants a handshake, Shinbashi offers his hand despite evident doubt. Kusunoki narrates Miyagi actually holding and shaking it; Shinbashi sees his own hand move and asks if that is what she is doing. Kusunoki relays her thanks. Shinbashi says he had a vague sense that she would thank him, and answers her through Kusunoki. The mediator matters: Shinbashi's compliance and response are attested, but direct touch perception and direct conversation are not. He suggests others might also have sensed something and dismissed it until they learned they were not alone. He calls that suggestion groundless and says he hopes it happens, distinguishing a social wish from a demonstrated prediction (Ch14 P0033–P0043).

## 2. Making room, then revising the explanation

Kusunoki attributes a local shift to the pub episode. People start going along with Miyagi's presence as a shared convention, not because they all believe a woman is really there. Shinbashi gives that convention a domestic setting when he invites both visitors to share leftover drinks before he goes home. His friends bring their own positions: Suzumi ventures a suggestion of presence, Asakura tests the account for inconsistency before making room for her, and Riko asks for a display of affection. Shinbashi looks on expectantly with Asakura; he does not make Riko's demand. The party is hospitable and theatrical at once. Making room for Miyagi does not erase the fact that the couple becomes an entertainment for people who still do not see her (Ch14 P0046–P0065).

A few days later Shinbashi tosses Kusunoki his car keys and offers the car while he will not need it. He imagines the couple going somewhere together. This material help persists away from the pub audience and does not depend on eliciting another public demonstration. At the door he also revisits his first explanation: Kusunoki does not strike him as merely lying, and the presence he has felt no longer seems adequately explained by mime. He proposes conditionally that Kusunoki may see a world no one else sees and that ordinary perception may take in only the share of reality its observers need. That conditional is the change: he no longer treats his original hypothesis as sufficient, but supplies no new visual testimony (Ch14 P0069–P0077).

## 3. Speech and relationships

Shinbashi's courteous address is flexible. At the pub, polite forms deliver a coercive request to the harasser; he uses respectful address for both neighbors and introduces himself to Miyagi as a social equal. In testing his hypothesis with Kusunoki he repeatedly qualifies what he knows and marks what is conjecture. During the loan his casual first person briefly shifts to *ore*; in his reflection on perception he uses *boku* again. The sample suggests registers adapted to a practical offer and a reflective explanation; it cannot establish his speech away from this circle or a stable rule for choosing pronouns (Ch14 P0010–P0018, P0022–P0043, P0072–P0075).

The Kusunoki relationship develops from a neighbor's discomfort, as remembered by Kusunoki, into an alliance with practical consequences. The Miyagi relationship begins as an enacted greeting without actual exchange and becomes an interaction he knowingly sustains through an intermediary. He includes her by name in the invitation and loan even without confirmation that he can perceive her. Her smile, handshake, and thanks are available to the reader through Kusunoki, not through direct access to either participant's inner life. Shinbashi's friends participate, but their responses are evidence about them, not interchangeable evidence of his beliefs.

## 4. Function, counterreading and limits

Shinbashi puts a public experiment in acceptance into words, and a credible motive rival is that he simply finds a compelling performance worth joining. His initial denial of rescue and his friends' enjoyment support that reading. Against reducing the episode to spectacle, he expresses a hope for broader acceptance and lends his car when the spectacle is no longer in front of him. A bounded reading accommodates both: public curiosity and private practical generosity can coexist, while the exact mix of motives at the bar remains unknown.

Kusunoki's chapter moves from coerced visibility in a hostile room to voluntary recognition by neighbors, and then to an outing made possible by an ordinary loan. The ensuing report of town-wide convention is Kusunoki's causal attribution, not an independently surveyed response. In Chapter 15 others actually describe seeing Miyagi; Shinbashi has already gone home and is not shown there. The reunion cannot retroactively confirm that Shinbashi saw or heard her during the pub scene, or tell us his eventual judgment. The text does not provide his family history, life elsewhere, or his response to learning of either final sale.
