# Three Days of Happiness — The substitute watcher

## Orientation

The novel does not name the man who watches Kusunoki for two days while Miyagi is off. Kusunoki has sold most of his lifespan. His watcher must accompany him; Miyagi ordinarily holds that assignment. Her substitute enters as a hostile critic of Kusunoki's leisure, but has a reason to ask what he has done to her. The man handled the purchase of Miyagi's time when she was ten, knows her records, and thinks her situation pitiable. He comes to treat Kusunoki with less hostility after skimming her notebook, points him toward a confrontation about the initial sale price without naming the true figure, accompanies him to the later sale, and, according to Miyagi, contacts her and handles her subsequent transaction (Ch12 P0004–P0049, P0080–P0101; Ch14 P0155–P0162; Ch15 P0090–P0105).

What distinguishes him is **selective intervention**. He wants to prepare for his own death by coming near the deaths of others. At work he acts on information gathered through an institution and, sometimes, on personal concern. He can press hard for an answer while refusing to give one. His care for Miyagi is visible in what he asks and does, but does not establish intimacy or a settled general ethic.

## 1. From contempt to qualified attention

Kusunoki first describes him through an unflattering, anticipated contrast with Miyagi, not through a neutral camera. The substitute says her absence is a scheduled break and that he will replace her for today and tomorrow; when asked whether everything will return to normal, he answers that this is the plan. He scrutinizes the vending-machine album, dismisses it, and criticizes the paper cranes and how Kusunoki uses his remaining life. Kusunoki is less offended than might be expected: overt criticism can be easier to bear than watchful silence. The early antagonism is mutual in register, not a one-sided injury in the narrator's account (Ch12 P0004–P0023).

After lunch the substitute asks if Kusunoki has caused trouble for Miyagi, calling her *ano ko* (“that girl”). Asked if they are close, his tone softens: he says watchers cannot see each other, they have corresponded in writing only two or three times, and he studied her records because he handled the purchase of her time. He speaks with emphasis about her misfortune, says she could not have judged normally at ten, and makes his own treatment of Kusunoki sound conditional on the answer. Kusunoki takes the concern as evidence of common ground and likes him more. Concern is a strong inference from his repeated question and explicit sympathy; closeness is not, and he expressly denies it (Ch12 P0025–P0041).

Kusunoki confesses hurting Miyagi and describes how close he came to physically forcing her. In Kusunoki's account, the man looks about to grab him but does not. Kusunoki gives him her observation notebook, which she left behind, unread because he fears an institutional penalty for her. The substitute quickly turns through it, says little, and thereafter rarely picks at Kusunoki. Neither Kusunoki nor the reader learns its contents. Kusunoki infers it contained a favorable account, but the observation is the substitute's subsequent difference in treatment, not the notebook's verdict. The substitute later says he did not read it in much detail. The sequence supports responsiveness to new information, while leaving his reason for softening and the notebook's content open (Ch12 P0042–P0049, P0080–P0083).

## 2. A choice to work around mortality

On a night walk near the apartment, Kusunoki asks whether the substitute watches because he too sold his time. After a sarcastic question about whether he would get sympathy, the man says he sold neither time, health nor lifespan. He does this work by choice. He denies finding it fun, compares it to visiting others' graves, and says that encountering many deaths now may make it possible to accept his own death later. His reply to the jab about old people's thinking is plain acquiescence. This is an unusually direct statement of purpose from a man who otherwise tends to question, mock, qualify or withhold; it is still his self-report rather than evidence that his plan works (Ch12 P0061–P0070).

The account matters for his institutional place. Miyagi's assignment reflects a sale; his testimony supplies a different path into watching and does not show that the two watchers perform identical duties in every circumstance. The novel gives no employment history, evidence of his own sale beyond his denial, or independent view of the deaths he says he wants to encounter.

## 3. Questions, disclosures and the limits of care

The substitute says he asks every subject what they did with the proceeds. Kusunoki says he distributed most of the money in individual banknotes after the intended recipient escaped him. The man laughs, but twice distinguishes his reason for laughing or mocking from the interpretation Kusunoki supplies. He asks whether Kusunoki really believed his lifespan was worth the money he accepted and directs him to ask Miyagi when she returns, refusing to say more. The question leads Kusunoki to infer a false valuation; confirmation and the actual numbers come from Miyagi's later testimony, not from the substitute. The man can deliberately bring a fact into question without vouching for what the recipient should finally conclude (Ch12 P0080–P0103, P0147–P0157, P0163–P0173).

When he accompanies Kusunoki to the shop for his second sale, the substitute is surprised by the valuation and asks whether Kusunoki expected it. The woman who performs the appraisal, not the substitute, gives the account of an alternative future in art. Afterward, Miyagi says the substitute tells her that Kusunoki has sold down to the limit and paid most of her debt. In her report, she has already decided on her own sale by the end of their exchange; he handles its procedure. She covers the remaining debt with proceeds of her own. This is her report of his action, not a witnessed scene of him persuading her. Neither his surprise nor his facilitation establishes whether he approved of either sale. Nor does the text expose his knowledge of her plan before she decided, any fee or procedural details, or the degree of discretion his work afforded (Ch14 P0155–P0168; Ch15 P0089–P0105).

A useful counterreading is that routine duties explain much of this conduct: looking at records, replacing an absent watcher, accompanying Kusunoki to the shop and processing a sale. Duties do not explain away his explicit concern or his conditional warning, and his interest in his subject's spending may be personal practice rather than institutional mandate. Conversely, concern does not make him an unqualified guardian. He judges Miyagi's first sale harshly yet is described as handling her last one; the text leaves that tension rather than providing a conversion, sanction or private endorsement.

## 4. Speech, relationships and function

With Kusunoki he speaks in blunt, colloquial contractions and familiar, often abrasive address. The delivery shifts within a single relationship: a grudging qualification about Miyagi's return, sharp contempt for the album, the quieter explanation of his and Miyagi's limited contact, then unexpectedly candid reflection about his own death. In the price exchange he shifts between ridicule and a precise limit on what he will say. Situational modulation is evidenced; his spoken register to Miyagi, the language of their correspondence, and how he talks to other subjects are not (Ch12 P0007–P0040, P0063–P0070, P0080–P0101).

The characterization is relationally asymmetric. To Miyagi he is a remote colleague with privileged documentary access and, on her testimony, an effective procedural intermediary. To Kusunoki he is first a critical substitute, then a limited confidant and a link to evidence Kusunoki cannot independently obtain. What changes is observable treatment and the kind of information exchanged, not a demonstrable friendship. Narratively the man widens the institution beyond the pair: he knows a different history of Miyagi's sale, affords a second perspective on its rules, and links the two final transactions without being identified as their appraiser or either participant's decision-maker. His admission about death offers a foil to Kusunoki's conduct: one man chooses proximity to other people's deaths to prepare for his own, while the other, faced with imminent death, discovers reasons to remain attentive to life. That is a structural comparison, not proof of what either man ultimately learns.
