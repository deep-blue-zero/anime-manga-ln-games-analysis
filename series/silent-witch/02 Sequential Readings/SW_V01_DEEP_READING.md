## 3. Current-volume story synopsis

The volume opens with Isabelle Norton, not with Monica Everett. In the east of the Ridill Kingdom a black dragon has appeared in the Worgan range, a calamity recorded only twice before. Isabelle, the fifteen-year-old daughter of Count Kerbeck, refuses to abandon the family house while her father commands the knights. A stable boy bursts in with a report that the mage sent from the capital, one of the kingdom’s Seven Sages, has driven the dragon off alone and without a single casualty. Before anyone can believe it, more than twenty wyverns that had gathered around the dragon descend on the house. A gate of light opens in the sky, a wind spirit king’s breath becomes lances that pierce each wyvern between the eyes, and the bodies are lowered to the ground as lightly as leaves. The caster is a small hooded figure with a staff taller than itself and a black cat at its feet: the Silent Witch. Isabelle knows her rescuer only by that title; chapter 1 identifies the Witch as Monica Everett. Isabelle watches, enraptured. So, from a distance, does an unnamed blue-eyed man, who says in a voice like someone falling in love that he has finally found something to be absorbed in.

Three months later the legend’s owner is asleep at a desk in a broken-down mountain hut, a pen still in her hand. Monica Everett is in her seventeenth year, underfed, hollow-eyed, living alone with a talking cat called Nero and with heaps of calculations she has begged from the other Sages. Annie, the village girl who delivers her food, chatters about the heroic Silent Witch while Monica’s stomach knots. The narrator then explains what the epithet conceals. Humans can only work magic by chanting; Monica alone can cast without a chant; and she learned to do it because she could not speak in front of people. Faced with failing her practical examinations at the mage academy Minerva, she solved the problem by removing the speech rather than the fear.

Her fellow Sage Louis Miller, the Barrier Mage, arrives with his contracted wind spirit Lynn and an order she cannot refuse. The king has secretly charged him with guarding the second prince, Felix, without Felix’s knowledge. A protective and tracking brooch Louis built was shattered the day after Felix received it, and Felix claims he broke it by accident. Louis, a conspicuous man and the leading figure of the first prince’s faction, cannot enter Serendia Academy, which is controlled by the second prince’s grandfather, Duke Clockford. So Monica will go in his place for the year until Felix graduates. Louis argues that nobody would take so plain and so obviously amateur a girl for a Sage, and that her magic makes no sound. Louis grips her shoulder; she nods before she can stop herself. Her cover is lifted from a popular romance novel: she is Monica Norton, an orphan adopted by the late dowager countess of Kerbeck and now the despised attendant of the count’s daughter, Isabelle.

In the capital Louis’s wife Rosalie, a physician, examines Monica on sight and prescribes food, sleep and a bath. Louis discovers that his colleague cannot name the ranks of the peerage and does not remember where she put the patent that makes her a peer of count rank. Isabelle arrives, performs a venomous villainess, then drops the act and reveals herself as the Silent Witch’s most devoted admirer, delighted to bully Monica as cover. At the academy Isabelle uses staged contempt to secure exactly what Monica wants, a solitary attic room. On the same day the student council, led by Felix, expels its treasurer Aaron O’Brien for embezzlement. Aaron insists he had an accomplice but finds that he cannot recall the person’s face, voice or name.

Monica’s first school day is a sequence of small defeats and small kindnesses. She cannot utter a self-introduction. A classmate, Lana Colette, scolds her country braids and then re-plaits them and leaves a ribbon in her hair. Looking for somewhere empty to eat a pocketful of nuts, she wanders into a closed garden and is seized as an intruder by the council secretary Elliott Howard, who is guarding a blond student sitting as bait; Monica does not yet know that he is Felix. He releases her, picks up her nuts, and shields her when a flowerpot falls from above; she diverts it with a silent gust and faints. She dreams of a drunken man raising a bottle over her and of the counting song she used to retreat into. That night Nero, taking human form to help her practise saying thank you, terrifies her simply by being an adult man; and from the attic window she and Nero see a hooded blond figure leaving the boys’ dormitory.

The next day she manages to thank Lana and asks to be taught the braids. Summoned to the council room, she learns that yesterday’s kind student is Felix himself, and responds by asking why a prince was out of his dormitory at night. When he denies it, she proves it on a blackboard from the proportions of his head and body, which approximate the golden ratio. Felix and Elliott explain that a signboard was dropped at Felix the day Aaron was condemned, that the flowerpot was a second attempt, and that Elliott suspects Monica. She is told to clear herself by finding the culprit. She reconstructs the pot from its shards, computes the balcony it fell from, and on the way is knocked down a staircase during a quarrel between Lana and Caroline, a count’s daughter. To hide her magic she fills the steps with an invisible barrier and so turns the stairs into a slope, down which she rolls into the vice-president, Cyril Ashley. When Caroline blames Lana, Monica forces herself to speak, and lies that she merely slipped. In the music room she finds an upturned pot used as a footstool. The culprit is her classmate Selma Karsh, the class health monitor, Aaron’s fiancée, who confesses to everything, including crimes she did not commit, to save him. Monica solves the case from fragments and cannot understand the motive at all.

Then she sees the council’s ledgers. Asked to check five years of accounts, she falls into them so completely that she checks every year the council has existed, does not hear the officers arrive, and does not react when the secretary Bridget Graham strikes her with a fan. Felix, who had dropped his keys beside her as a test and set his contracted spirit Wildianu to watch, finds the keys untouched. He kisses her swollen cheek; she takes it for Nero’s paw. He appoints her treasurer on the spot, and afterwards tells Wildianu that taming this frightened squirrel and making her confess her purpose will be a game to pass what he calls the remainder of his life before the succession is decided.

The council receives her coldly. Cyril, set to train her, is harsh in manner and scrupulous in substance, and he has quietly investigated the staircase incident and disciplined the true offender. Monica, who assumed rank would decide the matter, is astonished. That evening Cyril notices that the falsified figures of recent years are in a left-handed slant he recognizes. The narration blanks out where he goes next; he comes to himself holding a key, with a headache and no memory. The following night his body, which absorbs magical power beyond its capacity, runs out of control because the brooch that drains the excess has begun feeding it back. He staggers into the forest. Monica, brought by Lynn, lets Nero snatch the brooch, then meets Cyril’s frenzy without a word, dissolving each spell and shielding his body from his own cold until he is exhausted, which is the treatment. She repairs and improves the brooch, made carelessly by another Sage, because she does not want him to think of his constitution as a curse. Cyril loses consciousness believing he has seen a silent monster.

Monica now understands the pattern. Aaron, Selma and Cyril all show the after-effects of mind-interference magic, a restricted art. She takes down her hood and stops the council’s adviser, her homeroom teacher Victor Thornley, as he removes the reviewed ledgers at night. The falsification became skilful five years ago, when he took the post. He seizes her and casts a memory spell, boasting of its perfection. It falls apart against her. The one thing that makes Monica angry is a botched formula; she unpicks his second, mind-destroying spell and reweaves it into its completed form, a swarm of white butterflies, which seals him for twenty-four hours into a dream of the counting song’s endlessly multiplying pigs. Lynn carries him to Louis.

A week later the newspapers credit Louis with the arrest. Monica is practising braids with Lana and speaking to her in plain form. Felix braids her hair himself and sends her to introduce herself to some twenty club presidents. Cyril, who has secretly studied her small ink-calloused hand and appears to connect it with the hand that reached for him in the forest, though he says nothing, offers to go with her because her work has earned it, and calls her by her office for the first time. She answers as loudly as she can.

The secret episode then moves three pieces. Monica’s report, a few lines of congratulation and understatement, is burned by Louis, who remembers that at their selection she wept, panicked and beat him completely, and who has also planted a disciple of his own in the school. He reads the king’s order as surveillance of Felix, whom he finds uncanny, not as protection. And Felix, who destroyed Louis’s brooch because he could read its tracking formula, tells Wildianu that if a Sage must watch him he would rather it were the one he adores: the Silent Witch. He does not know that she is Monica.

## 6. Sustained diagnostic close reading

The argument of this section can be stated in advance. Volume 01 takes a comic premise, Monica Everett, a girl so afraid of speaking that she taught herself magic that needs no voice, and arranges the whole book around the gap between three things: what the kingdom says about the Silent Witch, what each person who meets Monica decides she must be, and what the narration shows of her from inside. No character holds all three. The reader holds more than anyone in the story, and the novel uses that surplus as its main instrument, for comedy and for dread alike. Two formal habits carry it. First, Monica’s power is almost always narrated through someone else’s eyes, while her own viewpoint narrates fear, embarrassment and small domestic uses of magic. Second, the book repeatedly prints a silence: a watcher left unnamed, a destination blanked out of a sentence, the object of a wish elided, the content of a dream withheld.

Observation numbers in the form “obs. 007” point to the register in section 6.14, which gives each record’s locator, speaker and focalizer, evidence class, rival reading and uncertainty.

### 6.1 The legend seen from outside

The prologue never enters Monica’s mind, never lets her speak, and never names her. It is told through Isabelle Norton, whose knowledge of magic is that fire or wind flies straight at a target, and whose standard is therefore easy to surpass. Everything the reader first learns of the Silent Witch is filtered twice. The dragon is dealt with off the page and arrives as a stable boy’s excited report, hedged with a reported-speech ending and doubted at once by his sister (S10:E000084–E000096; obs. 001). The verb throughout is that the dragon was repelled, not killed. Only the wyverns are destroyed in view, and what Isabelle registers is not force but control: no death cries, bodies settling like leaves into one heap, magic that is 「恐ろしく静かで、正確な魔術」 (S12:E000018; obs. 002). The silence of the epithet is first presented as an aesthetic property of the spellwork, a quality an admirer can fall in love with, before the book reveals what it costs.

The figure itself is small, hooded, faceless, and identified by an institution rather than by a person: the narration explains that staff length marks rank and that only seven people may carry a staff as tall as themselves (S12:E000041–E000044). The illustration set into the prologue makes the same choice. Isabelle fills the left foreground in close detail; the Witch is a tiny shape with its back to the viewer on a far hill (S11:E000004; obs. 125). Reputation, in other words, is something that happens to this character at a distance.

The prologue’s last five blocks change viewpoint to an unnamed man with blue eyes who says, using the pronoun 僕, that he has finally found 「夢中になれるもの」 (S13:E000012; obs. 003). The object is a thing, もの, not a person, and the narration adds that his voice had warmth as though he had fallen in love. The scene is a planted question. Section 6.11 argues that the volume answers it in its final line without ever stating the answer.

One qualification belongs here. A reader of the physical or electronic book does not actually meet the Witch as a blank. The colour frontispiece that precedes the prologue is a character card that prints her name, calls her the youngest Sage ever chosen, and draws her with her hands clasped at her mouth, blushing and anxious, with a cat on her hood (S02:E000004; obs. 124). The prose withholds what the packaging has already disclosed. The effect of the prologue is therefore not a mystery about who the Witch is; it is a demonstration of how she looks to people who do not know.

### 6.2 The explanation that deflates the title

Chapter 1 begins with a cat’s paw on a sleeping face and a hut whose chief merit, for its occupant, is that it is 「静かで人がいないのがいい」 (S15:E000047; obs. 004). The mirror a visitor forced on her shows a girl she describes without self-pity and without interest: worn robe, straw-like braids, a body pale as a corpse’s. When the delivery girl Annie asks whether Monica can use magic, Monica cannot produce an answer of any kind, and the secret is kept only because Annie answers her own question: a real mage would be making a career in the capital, not hiding on a mountain (S15:E000143–E000152; obs. 005). This is the first appearance of a pattern the volume will repeat with Isabelle, with the count, with Felix and with Elliott. Monica’s concealment rarely depends on her lying well. It depends on other people’s confidence that someone like her could not be what she is. Annie’s cheerful retelling of the dragon legend then lands on its subject as physical pain: Monica presses her stomach and sweats through a cool morning (S15:E000211; obs. 006).

Only after Louis has greeted her by her full title does the narrator step out of Monica’s viewpoint for a page of explanation (S15:E000357–E000383; obs. 007). Its content is the premise of the series; its manner is what matters. Humans must chant to shape power, even skilled mages need seconds, and one girl made the impossible possible. Then the cause: she was so shy that in front of strangers her throat would seize, and at worst she would vomit or collapse, so she failed her practical examinations because she could not chant in front of an examiner. Where an ordinary person would have worked on the shyness, the narrator says, her thinking shot off at an angle and she simply learned to cast without speaking. The passage ends on two deliberately anticlimactic judgments: she mastered the art for 「これっぽっちも感動的ではない理由」 (S15:E000382) and the whole thing was the end point of 「斜め上の努力」 (S15:E000383).

The reading this invites is that Monica’s silence and her genius are one mechanism, not a strength paired with a weakness. The chantless casting is an avoidance that succeeded so completely it became a title. That matters for everything after it, because it means the volume cannot cure the shyness without touching the thing the kingdom admires, and it means that every use of her magic at school is also a reminder of what she could not do. The same passage states a limit that later chapters respect: she can cast roughly eight in ten known formulas without a chant, not all of them (S15:E000365; obs. 008). Chapter 4 adds the corollary that makes her situation at school genuinely constrained rather than merely awkward. Because of her stage fright she can only cast silently, so any magic she shows will identify her (S18:E000295–E000296; obs. 033).

The narrator’s comic framing should not be taken as the book’s whole account of the cause. It is the account available at the end of chapter 1. Sections 6.4 and 6.6 collect the evidence, supplied later and never summarized by the narrator, that her habit of not speaking is older and darker than an examination.

### 6.3 An order she cannot refuse and a plot borrowed from a novel

Louis Miller’s scene is built on register. He speaks an elaborate, old-fashioned polite style, addresses Monica as 同期殿, and smiles throughout. The politeness never drops; what changes is the grammar underneath it. Pressing her to agree that a Sage should do a Sage’s work, he runs a single verb down a staircase from invitation to bare command within one line (S15:E000643), and the narrator notes the final form drily. The chapter title calls what he asks 無茶, an unreasonable demand, and the prose agrees: when he explains that no one would suspect so drab a girl, the narrator’s whole comment is 「暴言である」 (S15:E000595; obs. 010).

Monica’s consent is narrated as something her body does. She offers the objection that she is only a 「補欠合格」 Sage (S15:E000645), is told that the king left the choice to Louis and that she therefore has no veto, has her shoulder gripped, and nods; the narration immediately repeats the verb in the regretful form 「頷いてしまった」 (S15:E000647; obs. 011). Louis then mentions that failure might mean execution. That statement is his, made to someone he is coercing, and the secret episode shows him telling Lynn that he thinks it unlikely (S27:E000251). At the end of chapter 1 it functions as a threat, not as an established rule of the world.

Two things in this scene show Monica as more than its victim. She reads the brooch’s formula at a glance and names what Louis has politely not said, that it is a device for watching the prince rather than for protecting him (S15:E000496–E000513; obs. 009). His reply is a motto, delivered with a hand on his chest like scripture: 「バレなきゃ良いんですよ」 (S15:E000531). And she privately judges his real motive to be wounded pride over the broken device. Her fear of him does not stop her from seeing him clearly.

The cover identity is the chapter’s best joke and one of the volume’s organizing ideas. Louis has taken it from a current romance by a novelist Nero also reads: a count’s daughter torments a poor relation who catches a prince’s eye (S15:E000663–E000725; obs. 012). The count’s real daughter is delighted to play what she calls a fashionable villainess. From here on the story is conscious that it is borrowing a genre. Isabelle rehearses her laugh daily, quotes the latest volume while beating a stuffed toy against a wall for the benefit of the corridor, and keeps a stock of cracked teacups to smash on hard floors for the sound (S22:E000081–E000131). A council officer is nicknamed after a character in the same books. Felix, it turns out, has read them all. The plot the novel inside the novel promises, a despised girl noticed by a prince, does then happen, and the volume’s irony is in how: the real prince, Felix, notices her as a suspect and a pet.

Chapter 2 opens with the first instance of the mechanism that chapter 7 will name. Flying to the capital, Monica feels like the livestock in a children’s riddle-song about a farmer whose pigs are sold in growing numbers each winter, and she retreats into computing the series far past the point the song asks for; the narration calls this 「半ば現実逃避」 (S16:E000031; obs. 014). The recurrence is the one in which each term is the sum of the two before it, and it will return three more times in the volume, each time doing different work.

The same chapter sets out the three-layer structure in the open. Isabelle declares that Monica is shy but braver and stronger than anyone. Monica’s response is 「一体それは誰のことだろう」 (S16:E000345). The narrator then supplies the causes Isabelle cannot know: Monica went to the dragon because Louis dragged her out of the hut, and she left without attending the feast out of shyness, not modesty, and she is not eloquent enough to correct the misunderstanding, which Louis intends to exploit (S16:E000354–E000361; obs. 018). Admirer’s image, self-image, and causal account sit side by side in ten lines.

Two further details in chapter 2 repay attention. Rosalie Miller’s examination is the only assessment of Monica’s body by someone qualified to make one: pale inner eyelids and nails, dry skin, underweight for her age, signs she reads as malnutrition and anaemia, against which Monica’s own statement that she is healthy carries no weight (S16:E000112–E000129; obs. 016). The neglect the hut scene played as comedy is given a clinical description by a character with standing to give it. And the peerage lesson establishes that Monica is herself a noble, holder of a count-equivalent title given to every Sage, who has mislaid the patent and the ring somewhere among her papers (S16:E000220–E000226; obs. 017). She will spend the rest of the volume being despised as a commoner by people she outranks or equals.

Chapter 3’s first scene is the volume’s first move outside Monica altogether, into the mind of the embezzling treasurer, Aaron O’Brien. It introduces Felix as the only smiling person in the room, a man whose single word 「さて」 changes the air (S17:E000063; obs. 020), and whose gentlest sentences are the cruellest. Aaron, cornered, screams that the prince is royal in name only and the duke’s dog; Felix, sentencing him, picks the insult up and hands it back as the description of who has beaten him (S17:E000181; S17:E000241; obs. 023). The scene also plants the plot’s central device as an experience before it is a rule: Aaron reaches for the accomplice who will save him and finds a hole where the face, the voice and the name should be (S17:E000155–E000166; obs. 022). Monica hears the rule that evening, explaining to Nero why she may not simply rewrite the memories of anyone who suspects her, and connects it to the raving student she passed in the corridor, then dismisses the thought with 「まさか、ね」 (S17:E000576; obs. 027).

### 6.4 The smallest speech act

The title of chapter 4 calls a self-introduction the greatest trial, and the joke is exact. A Sage who has faced a black dragon stands at the front of a classroom, is told by her teacher to greet the class, opens and closes her mouth, and is dismissed to her seat without having said her own name (S18:E000105–E000131; obs. 030). The narration likens the classroom to a tribunal and her to the accused. Louis had coached the minimum formula; even that is beyond her.

What follows is the book’s first sustained piece of ordinary social life, and it is carefully two-sided. Lana Colette interrogates Monica’s braids, her lack of cosmetics, her old shoes; Monica’s admission that she knows no other hairstyle exposes that she has no maid, which the class reads as a sign of an irregular background. Then the class turns on Lana, audibly, as the daughter of a baron who bought his title. Lana ends the conversation with the verdict that Monica is boring, and the narration stops on the word: 「つまらない」 is something Monica is used to being called and believes (S18:E000218; obs. 031). Yet the scene closes with Lana seizing the braids, re-plaiting them in a fashionable style, telling her to learn to do it herself, and leaving behind a ribbon. The kindness arrives in the grammar of irritation. The illustration places the viewer in Monica’s chair, with Lana leaning down and two classmates whispering behind a fan in the background (S18:E000195).

The middle of the chapter gives the first block of history. Monica is commoner-born and never attended a town school; her father, described as very learned, taught her everything; after his death and unspecified complications she was adopted by a person who had been his pupil, and entered Minerva; and she had no friends there except one, with whom she finally broke (S18:E000277–E000293; obs. 032). The sentence about the friend is given a pause and a correction of itself, as if the narration had tried to say “none” and could not.

In the same passage she reasons about her mission with a clarity her speech never has. If guarding were the aim, Louis would have placed her in Felix’s year, or sent a man who could enter the boys’ dormitory. Louis is overbearing and has a ruinous character, she thinks, but he is able; the plan has too many holes to be what it claims (S18:E000302–E000306; obs. 034). The secret episode will confirm that she is right to doubt, and she is never told.

Her first use of magic at school is neither protective nor planned. Seized from behind in the old garden by Elliott, who takes her for a hired intruder, she panics, and the panic is printed as a run of two repeated words; a gust throws dirt in his eyes (S18:E000367–E000368; obs. 035). She then trips over her own feet. The second use is the one that matters to the mission: the unnamed blond student pulls her close as something falls, and she deflects it with wind; he remarks on the lucky gust (S18:E000439–E000453). Whether he believes in the luck is not stated. The narration later has Monica judge the save a near thing that depended on noticing in time (S18:E000518), which is a limit on her ability worth recording against the legend.

The student is Felix, though Monica will not learn it until the next day, and his first gestures are worth isolating because they are the ones the volume will keep. He silences Elliott by putting a finger to his lips, the same gesture the cover gives to the Witch. He kneels to gather a stranger’s scattered nuts. And he names her, on no acquaintance, a 「子リス」 who has strayed in (S18:E000422; obs. 036). The animal name is affectionate, diminishing, and permanent.

Unconscious in the infirmary, Monica dreams (S18:E000468–E000490; obs. 037). A large black shadow flickers as if lit by a candle. She thinks, in a child’s resigned idiom, that the man she calls 「おじさん」 has been drinking today (S18:E000476). The shadow shouts. She knows the rule for such times, that one must not say anything unnecessary, and so she shuts her mouth, looks down, and thinks about the pigs in the song, counting the series; a memory of her father praising her for noticing a property of neighbouring terms passes through. The shadow brings a bottle down toward her. The shattering turns out to be the flowerpot. She wakes remembering only that the dream was frightening.

This is the most important passage in the volume for understanding the title, and it must be read for exactly what it is. It is a dream, triggered by a real impact, and the dreamer retains almost none of it. It does not say who the man is, when this was, or how often. What it does establish, in the narration’s own presentation, is an association in Monica’s mind between an angry adult man, being struck, a rule of silence, and the counting song as the place to go. The chapter then corroborates the association twice in waking life. When Nero takes the form of a man in his twenties to help her practise, she already knows he can do this and has seen it before, and her body nevertheless locks; she curls up and covers her head with both hands (S18:E000637–E000651; obs. 040). And her explanation of why she could thank Nero but not the people who were kind to her is the plainest sentence she speaks in the chapter: 「ネロは人間じゃない」 (S18:E000617; obs. 039). Her fear is of persons.

Against the dream the chapter sets its thesis sentence, in Monica’s viewpoint as she lights a candle without a word: people call this magic a miracle, but for her an ordinary school life is far harder than casting it (S18:E000564; obs. 038). And it closes on a comic rehearsal that is also her first completed thanks. Nero, in the form that frightens her, demands praise instead of apology; she squeezes her eyes shut, clenches her fists and gets the sentence out, then parrots his self-praise until he remarks that he feels like a villain brainwashing an innocent (S18:E000704–E000710; obs. 041). The joke is sound, and so is the progress.

### 6.5 Numbers as shelter and as sight

Chapter 5 opens on the earliest memory the volume supplies. At about five Monica asked her father for a tape measure so that she could find the areas and volumes of the things around her. A visiting friend of his was taken aback; her father heard her reason, smiled, and gave it to her, and she measured the furniture and both their hands and feet until the markings wore away (S19:E000011–E000035; obs. 043). Three sayings of his are quoted across the chapter, each introduced by a dash rather than quotation marks, as remembered voice: that 「世界は数字に満ちている」 and the human body too (S19:E000023); that if one strips away what is superfluous the numbers that remain are simple (S19:E000125); and that 「困難な挑戦ほど楽しい」 (S19:E000151).

These sayings explain how Monica perceives. She does not remember faces; she remembers the angles and widths that compose them, and chapter 4 presented this as a practical trick she adopts because a bodyguard must recognize people: 「数字で覚えてしまえばいい」 (S18:E000016; obs. 028). She recognizes Thornley by his jaw and Elliott by the slope of his eyes. She identifies the hooded figure in the dark by the ratio of the back of his head and the lengths of his torso and legs (S18:E000743–E000767; obs. 042). In chapter 6 she reconstructs the size, shape and weight of a pot from a handful of shards and is puzzled that Nero cannot: surely anyone can tell from the pieces (S20:E000082; obs. 054). She does not know that her way of seeing is unusual.

The same faculty is her shelter, and chapter 5 shows her beginning to notice its limit. Drinking coffee made in her father’s pot, she turns his second saying over and finds that she cannot apply it: an equation yields its answer, but what counts as superfluous in a person’s heart does not. The word she worries at is 「無駄」 (S19:E000129; obs. 044). On the desk are Lana’s ribbon and the nuts a stranger picked up for her. Until yesterday she would have called a ribbon waste and skipped a meal without regret; this morning she eats the nuts slowly, tasting them. Nero’s contribution, quoted from his favourite novelist, is that life is full of waste and should be enjoyed for it, and that her praise is not waste to him. She decides to try something difficult, and the third saying returns.

What she tries is speech. She has attempted Lana’s braids herself and produced something the narration calls avant-garde. She gets the whole sentence of thanks out, to the end, and when Lana refuses the returned ribbon as 「もう流行ってない」 (S19:E000191), the narration marks the moment she would ordinarily have retreated and records that she does not. She asks to be taught, and the narrator’s entire comment on the mangled final syllable is the one word 「嚙んだ」 (S19:E000196; obs. 045). The exchange that follows is the foundation of the volume’s one friendship. Lana, combing, falls silent and then asks whether her talk is boring, since everyone thinks a new-money family vulgar. Monica answers that she is told the same thing, because she only talks about numbers (S19:E000244–E000260; obs. 046). The word that wounded her the day before becomes the thing the two have in common.

Lana also gives the book its counter-principle. When Monica starts analysing the braid as positions, angles and ratios of hair, Lana tells her that this kind of thing is learned not by numbers but by hand: 「手で覚える」 (S19:E000279; obs. 047). It is the first statement in the volume of a knowledge that is not her father’s, and by the epilogue Monica is submitting a braid for judgement on both terms at once, ratio and angle and whether it holds.

The golden-ratio scene uses the same faculty for comedy and for characterization at once. Learning that the kind student is Felix, the prince she must guard, Monica’s first thought is not awe; it is a question about why he was outdoors at night (S19:E000435; obs. 048). When he deflects with courtly indirection, the narration says something ignites in her, 「証明したい」, the scholar’s reflex (S19:E000453). For the length of the proof her sentences are long, exact and unbroken; she lectures a prince on the proportions of his own skull and navel and tells him a tape measure would confirm it (S19:E000461–E000481; obs. 049). The stammer returns only when she hears herself. Felix checks his tailor’s figures and finds she is right.

Two details in the aftermath deserve separate notice. Monica’s attempt to excuse herself links the ratio to the riddle-song’s number series, and Elliott, a count’s son, has never heard of the song, while Felix, a prince, knows it and says so with the pleasure of someone who has just understood an old puzzle (S19:E000519–E000521; obs. 050). The volume offers no explanation for a prince’s familiarity with a street rhyme. And Felix describes two attempts on his life with unbroken serenity, which Monica finds stranger than the attempts: she wonders whether royalty simply grows used to it (S19:E000653–E000657; obs. 052).

The chapter ends with the first task she takes on by decision. Elliott’s challenge to find the culprit is a taunt, Felix’s acceptance of it is unreadable, and she would rather be shut in her room; but she is the prince’s guard, and she says yes, biting the word (S19:E000789–E000793; obs. 053). Compare the nod in chapter 1.

### 6.6 The staircase

Chapter 6 puts the three parts of Monica’s character into a single minute. Pushed off a landing in someone else’s quarrel, she thinks at great speed: wind indoors would expose her; a barrier around her own body would make the fall look wrong; so she lays an invisible barrier over the steps and fills them in. The narrator pauses to say that this is the finest control of magical power in the country, spent without reserve on a staircase, and then lets the physics arrive. A slope is faster than steps. She rolls the length of it and down the corridor, howling (S20:E000293–E000316; obs. 056). The chapter title names the result. The genius is real, the application is instant, and the blind spot belongs to the same mind.

She lands on Cyril Ashley, the vice-president, whom she does not yet recognize. He reaches for her head to look at her forehead, and before she knows who he is or what he wants she has covered her head with both hands, because she thought she would be hit (S20:E000346; obs. 057). It is the second time in two chapters. What he actually does is move her fringe aside, ask where it hurts, and ask twice more. His fingertip is cold, and Monica, even now, forms a technical hypothesis about why.

Then the offender, Caroline, a count’s daughter, tells Cyril, as a council officer, that Lana pushed Monica for fun, and invites him to prefer an old family’s word to a merchant baron’s. The narration gives Monica’s knowledge as knowledge, not opinion: when a person of higher rank says that you are the one at fault, that becomes the truth, and in this school 「身分が全て」 (S20:E000434; obs. 058). She concludes that nothing she says can matter. The next line is the single word 「それでも」 (S20:E000443). If Caroline had only denied everything, Monica would have accepted it. But she has put the blame on Lana, and the narration sets the reason on a line by itself, behind a dash and between blank lines: Lana will be punished on a 「冤罪」 (S20:E000448). Monica begs her own throat to move.

What she manages to say is false. She slipped; nobody is to blame; she is sorry; and she runs (S20:E000461–E000484; obs. 059). She does not accuse the guilty party, because she believes that is impossible. She makes herself the cause, because that is the one statement a person of no standing is always allowed to make. On the stairs afterward she steadies herself with a sentence that repeats the rule from the dream almost word for word: if she endures and says nothing unnecessary, it will all come out smoothly (S20:E000492).

The volume does not explain why a false accusation, of all wrongs, is the one she cannot let stand, nor where she learned what rank does to truth. Both are stated as settled facts about her and both point outside the book. They are recorded in section 10 as open, and they are the strongest evidence, with the dream, that the examination-room story of chapter 1 is not the origin of her silence but only its most recent chapter.

The scene’s resolution is postponed for two chapters and is one of the volume’s quiet turns. In chapter 8 Cyril mentions, as an administrative aside, that he questioned the students who were present, established what happened, and has reprimanded Caroline and required a written apology. He is annoyed with Monica, not for causing trouble, but for filing a false report that cost him time. Her reaction is 「こういう人も、いるんだ」 (S22:E000928; obs. 091). An expectation she held as a law has met its first counterexample.

### 6.7 Solving a crime without understanding it

The flowerpot case is a fair-play puzzle and Monica solves it with nothing but measurement and observation. The shards give a large round pot, clean, therefore empty or washed; the spread and fineness of the fragments on soft ground, the still air, and the resistance of her own gust give a height and a balcony (S20:E000074–E000110). On the balcony she finds one filthy pot upside down beside the high rail, and her glove picks up its dirt (S20:E000533–E000544; obs. 060). In the council room she adds the inference that wins the case: Selma, who tended her in the infirmary, had bare hands, though gloves are part of the uniform, and today wears brand-new gloves; a soiled glove embroidered with Selma’s initials lies in a nearby powder-room bin (S21:E000110–E000121). The culprit was small and weak and needed a footstool.

Monica explains this in long, steady sentences and is disappointed only when Felix tells her to skip the equations. She can talk about calculation indefinitely; that is one of the conditions under which her voice works, and the volume shows it consistently.

Selma Karsh is given the chapter’s first viewpoint, and it makes her motive legible to the reader before the trial. She keeps companions who use her as a foil and an errand girl because the title of friend saves her from being a person with nothing; her fiancé does not love her and is said to prefer the beautiful council secretary; and she must save him because only she can, as someone told her: 「あの人も言っていた」 (S21:E000033; obs. 064). The phrase is the single clue in her section that someone else has shaped her thinking, and it is never resolved by name. Under accusation she confesses to the pot, the signboard, the embezzlement itself and to having led Aaron astray, asking only mercy for him, her face twisted into a smile and her eyes unfocused (S21:E000137–E000153; obs. 065). The confession overreaches what she could have done; Felix answers that Aaron’s guilt is already established.

Monica’s response is the clearest statement in the volume of her limits as a reader of people. She could find the culprit from broken earthenware, but she cannot understand wanting so badly to be loved by someone; she asks herself 「どうして、そこまで他人に期待できるんだろう」 (S21:E000189), watches with no expression, and later decides that what frightens her about Selma is precisely the devotion others might admire. She has attachments, she reflects, but only to equations and formulas: 「人には執着できない」 (S21:E000216; obs. 066). The sentence is a self-description and should be held as one. Within this very volume she keeps a dead father’s coffee pot as her only treasure (S17:E000529–E000533; obs. 026), tastes nuts because a stranger gathered them, refuses to undo braids because a classmate made them, and risks exposure for a boy who insulted her. The volume is building a gap between what she says she cannot feel and what she does.

The stakes of detection are put to her bluntly. Asked what will happen to Selma, the prince answers that an attempt on royalty means death for her and her family, and Monica trembles at the thought that her deduction will have killed them; the narration states the lesson as the meaning of being a royal guard (S21:E000193–E000197; obs. 067). Felix then softens: the case cannot be made public, and Selma will withdraw citing illness. He adds that her readiness to throw everything away for someone dear moves him, and his eyes seem to be looking somewhere far off: 「思うところがある」 (S21:E000201). Whether the death sentence was ever intended, or was said to see what Monica would do, the text does not decide. The remark about devotion is one of several moments in which Felix seems to speak from a private history the volume declines to supply.

### 6.8 Ten viewpoints and one kiss

The chapter titled for the prince’s secret is the most fragmented in the book, passing through ten viewpoints, and the fragmentation is its argument. No one in it sees what the reader sees.

Monica, shown the ledgers, comes alive. She spots three discrepancies at a glance, speaks to the prince without lifting her eyes, and states the accounting conventions she can read as plainly as a professional (S21:E000233–E000253; obs. 068). The narration explains that she loves beautiful equations as others love artworks, and that an error in a ledger is a stain on a masterpiece. Felix offers her five years of accounts, and the line that follows is the funniest and saddest summary of her in the book: she had been 「数字に飢えていた」 (S21:E000263).

Felix’s next scene is the first of three in his own viewpoint, and it overturns the impression of his kindness. Leaving her, he drops his keys on her path on purpose, summons a white lizard that is his contracted spirit, and sets it to watch whether she uses them on any cabinet but the accounts; his smile in that moment, the narration says, is a hunter’s (S21:E000305–E000327; obs. 069). He then lists three explanations for her: a minder from the duke, a minder or guard from the king, or an assassin. He dismisses the first two because she is 「無能すぎる」 for either to have sent (S21:E000352), and the third because she did not know his face and did nothing when she had him alone at night. The reader knows the second explanation is the true one. Felix considers it and rejects it for the exact reason Louis gave for choosing her (S15:E000624). Louis’s design works against the cleverest person in the school in the first test it faces.

The next two viewpoints are borrowed from girls who barely matter to the plot and who are used to show Felix from outside. Caroline, the count’s daughter from the staircase, believes he will certainly be king and that every girl wants to marry him; Bridget Graham, a marquis’s daughter and the only girl Felix himself appointed to the council, walks past her without a glance (S21:E000390–E000459). Then Bridget finds a stranger in the archive surrounded by emptied shelves, is ignored three times, and strikes her across the face with a closed fan (S21:E000515–E000522; obs. 070).

At this point the book does something it does only once: it gives a few lines of Monica’s interior in hiragana, broken by dashes, in the grammar of a small child. It hurts; hurting is frightening; frightening is painful. The narration then states the mechanism the earlier chapters had shown without naming: the more pain or fear she meets, the deeper her mind sinks into numbers, because while she is thinking about them she does not have to feel; the beautiful world of numbers never says cruel things or hurts her (S21:E000528–E000538; obs. 071). The phrase 「いたいは、こわい」 (S21:E000530) belongs to the only passage in which the narrator explains Monica from below the level of her comic self-presentation. Nero, watching through a locked window, confirms it from his side: striking her makes it worse, and the only way back is the touch of a paw (S21:E000557–E000563; obs. 072).

Felix arrives, finds the keys exactly where he dropped them, and finds that she has checked not five years but every year on record, annotating each error. He is surprised and wonders when anything last surprised him (S21:E000657). She brushes his hand away. Cyril begins a spell. Felix stops him, and with a small curiosity that the narration names, kisses her swollen cheek. She stops, eyes still on the page, and tells Nero to wait a little longer (S21:E000682–E000689; obs. 073). She has taken the kiss for the paw.

The kiss should be described exactly. It is given to Monica while she is unaware of her surroundings and has just been struck, by a prince who is testing her, in front of witnesses; it is narrated in his viewpoint as curiosity, not desire, and in hers it does not exist. Later the same day she tells Nero, in solemn confidence, that she has discovered the prince’s great secret, 「肉球があるの」 (S21:E000940), and Nero, very seriously, tells her to forget the whole thing (S21:E000940–E000949; obs. 076). The person most affected does not know what happened, the one witness on her side chooses to keep it from her, and the scene is played for laughs. Section 8.5 considers how far the comedy governs the evaluation.

The appointment follows the same logic. Asked for her honest opinion, Monica says the sums of money are astonishingly large and the management astonishingly careless, and Cyril explodes. Felix turns to his council: this is the reality of past councils, even he could not detect Aaron’s fraud at once, and so he names her treasurer (S21:E000737–E000748; obs. 074). In Felix’s last viewpoint scene that night, Wildianu observes that he had in fact known about the fraud from the beginning and let it run for a year to make the expulsion certain (S21:E000994–E000995; obs. 077). His public statement to his own council was untrue.

What he says next is the closest the volume comes to his self-description, and it is spoken in a different voice. Publicly Felix uses 私. To Wildianu, alone, he calls this a game, the game of taming a frightened squirrel until she confesses her design; he says what amuses him is that she has no interest in him at all, that she finds a children’s song about pigs more delightful than him; and then, in the pronoun 僕, that his life is a 「余生」, a remainder to be lived out until the next king is chosen, so he may as well enjoy it (S21:E001000–E001027; obs. 078). The narration adds that Wildianu knows Felix’s wish and bows, and does not say what the wish is (S21:E001035). Finally Felix concedes, to himself, that the night outing Monica saw was not a decoy operation at all but an excursion he kept even from Elliott, and that the explanation he gave was a cover (S21:E001056–E001060; obs. 079). He flicks a white pawn off the chessboard; the narration compares it to Monica falling off the bed.

The chapter’s title is therefore misleading in a precise way. Felix has several secrets: a contracted upper spirit, the private pronoun, the remainder-of-life, the unexplained wish, the foreknowledge of fraud, the nocturnal absences, and the test. Monica learns none of them. The secret she believes she has discovered is a cat’s paw.

Felix’s public face has a further feature that the volume plants without comment. Thinking privately about why trouble must be handled quietly, he reflects that whatever he is called, he cannot go against the duke, and the narration doubles the adverb that closes the thought: 「絶対に」 (S20:E000662; obs. 062). In the next sentence he says aloud that it would be awkward if Felix Arc Ridill were thought too incompetent to handle so small an incident, naming himself in full in the third person (S20:E000663). The secret episode shows that the two men he expelled were both the duke’s placements (S27:E000331; obs. 120). Felix, who believes he cannot defy his grandfather, is engaged, carefully, in removing his grandfather’s instruments.

### 6.9 Cyril

Cyril Ashley enters as comedy, a vice-president who shouts that every slight to the prince deserves ten thousand deaths, freezes miscreants’ feet, and insists he is always calm (S17:E000485). The volume then gives him more interior than anyone except Monica and Felix, and makes him the character through whom it thinks most directly about recognition.

His first viewpoint scene is a small study of conscientiousness. Staying late to prove that the new girl’s review must be wrong, he finds it flawless and catches errors he would have missed; he concedes her ability and still mutters that he dislikes her, because she ignored the prince (S22:E000022–E000038; obs. 080). Then he notices that the errors increase from a certain year and are written in a left-handed slant he recognizes: 「右下がりの数字」 (S22:E000050). The narration follows him out of the room and then stops printing the sentence: where he went is replaced by dots, and he comes to himself at the door holding a key instead of the papers, with a headache, deciding he must be tired (S22:E000046–E000075; obs. 081). The reader is shown the act of erasure as an erasure in the text. Readers who had noticed Thornley holding his watch in his left hand on the first day (S18:E000049; obs. 029), crying the first warning when the signboard fell (S19:E000619; obs. 051), and serving five years as the council’s adviser now have the culprit; no character does.

Cyril’s second appearance as instructor turns his severity into something Monica can use. He makes lists, answers questions, and fills a glass with ice by short chants between explanations, telling her each lump is for her mouth when she errs; she notices that the cold that leaks from him stops while he does it (S22:E000751–E000770). When she apologizes for causing extra work by checking too many years, he rejects the premise: necessary work is not extra. Then he asks why she cringes, why she is servile when the prince has trusted her (S22:E000816–E000830; obs. 089).

The narration answers with three remembered voices, each a different register, each saying the same thing: why so servile, you should be proud of your talent, if you belittle yourself what does that make those beneath you (S22:E000825–E000827). Their speakers are not named. Monica’s reply is the volume’s most exposed sentence of self-description: she cannot be proud of herself; 「できないん、です」 (S22:E000838). The narration then gives the history behind it. At Minerva one boy was her friend; he looked after her and practised chanting with her; when she learned to cast without chanting and was praised as a genius, the friendship broke, and he accused her of having privately looked down on him all along; they never reconciled (S22:E000839–E000862; obs. 090). Someone once said, she recalls, that talent can become a curse; for her it always is, because it takes what she wanted, and the narration names two losses on a line of its own: 「父も、友人も」 (S22:E000888). How talent took her father is not said anywhere in the volume.

Cyril answers that he hates the word “cannot.” The next night shows why. His viewpoint gives a medical rule, a constitution that keeps absorbing magical power past the body’s capacity until it poisons itself, so that the excess must be vented; and a biography. He was a poor distant relation adopted into a marquisate because the marquis had only a daughter and he was the most able candidate; his adoptive sister proved far more learned than he; he asked what he had been adopted for; he drove himself at magic until he developed the condition; and the marquis gave him the brooch that drains the excess, which he received as permission to belong (S23:E000262–E000320; obs. 095). His thought at the edge of collapse is that he wants to be expected of by someone, and the text elides the name: the object of the wish is printed as dots (S23:E000328; obs. 096).

In delirium he begs the brooch back from Nero, whom he sees only as a cat, as his foster father’s gift and then speaks past everyone to his mother, asking why she will not acknowledge him: 「お母様」 (S23:E000486). The flashback that follows supplies the wound. His father was of the marquis’s blood but idle and overbearing, and drank himself to death; Cyril took his mother’s side; she could not look at his face because it was his father’s; when the adoption came and he rejoiced that he could ease her life, she said with a sigh that he was a noble’s child after all. He wanted to answer 「私は貴女の子です」 and could not say it (S23:E000531–E000536). Two characters, then, carry a sentence they could not speak to a parent. The volume makes the silence of its heroine a shared human condition rather than a quirk.

The fight is the first time the reader sees Monica’s full power, and it is seen from Cyril’s side (S23:E000592–E000644; obs. 098). She never speaks. She does not look at him; she studies the brooch. His ice chains fall apart without a word from her. Every one of a spray of arrows meets a small flame of its own, and none of the flames touches the trees; to anyone trained, the narration says, the showy serpent of fire is nothing beside those small precise fires. When he pours out all his cold, she turns it aside with wind and at the same time shields his body from his own frostbite. He reasons that she must have hidden allies, and then that if she does not, she is a monster: 「バケモノ」 (S23:E000644). He faints repeating that “cannot” is not allowed and that he must meet expectations. The last thing he sees is the monster running toward him with a hopelessly clumsy gait (S23:E000664; obs. 099).

The scene is a treatment disguised as a duel. Monica explains afterward that the quickest remedy for early poisoning is to make the patient spend his power to exhaustion (S23:E000676–E000678). The brooch had been made without the protective formula such tools normally carry, which is why a strong magical attack on its wearer could reverse it; its maker’s mark names another Sage, whom Louis calls a greedy petty villain (S23:E000695–E000716; obs. 100). The reader can join this to the blanked sentence of chapter 8: the strong attack was the memory spell. Monica does not yet.

She rewrites the brooch, restrains herself from improving it too much, and gives the reason to Nero. She envies Cyril a little, for being able to take pride in being recognized and to work for it; his constitution, managed well, is an advantage some mages court deliberately; and she did not want him to 「呪いだと思ってほしくなかった」 (S23:E000750; obs. 101). The narration completes the thought: she cannot help thinking of her own talent as a curse, and so she wanted him at least to be proud, on her behalf too. It is the first act of care she initiates for anyone in the book, it is anonymous, and it is an explicit projection.

### 6.10 The perfect formula

Chapter 10 begins with two non-human or half-hidden beings measuring each other. Nero, in human form, delivers the unconscious Cyril to Felix at a crack in the dormitory wall that generations of students have used, and gives the name of his favourite novel’s hero as his own. Felix recognizes the reference and says he has sampled every entertainment the kingdom offers, novels, games and plays; Nero finds the smile hollow and thinks him 「気持ちの悪い人間」 (S24:E000093), a prince born to everything with the empty eyes of someone who has nothing (S24:E000087–E000094; obs. 102). Felix has slipped Wildianu into Nero’s robe to spy; Nero pulls the lizard out by the tail. Leaving, Nero makes a silent promise about Monica: if the bored prince breaks Nero’s favourite, Nero will eat him head first (S24:E000172). Felix, for his part, concludes that the man, whose real name he does not know, was not human and probably not a spirit either, 「人外」 (S24:E000195), and lets him go. Monica knows none of this exchange.

Monica’s deduction is then given in her own viewpoint, as a chain: the brooch failed because its wearer was struck by strong magic; Cyril’s frenzy resembled mind-interference after-effects more than poisoning; Selma knew of Aaron’s disgrace though it had been hidden from students, so someone told her and drove her to act; Aaron, Selma and Cyril all fit (S24:E000263–E000291). She does not take the case to the council. She takes down her hood and walks up to Thornley in the dark (S24:E000295; obs. 104).

Her speech in this scene is unlike any other in the book. Her first word to him is a single bare demonstrative; her second sentence asks what he means to do with the papers; her third tells him not to bother substituting them because she remembers every figure she has read. The narration says the expression leaves her face as it does when she faces numbers: 「表情が抜け落ちる」 (S24:E000326). She states that the council’s accounts were chaotic for decades, that the concealment became skilful and the sums larger five years ago, that this was the year he became adviser, and that he was Aaron’s accomplice (S24:E000316–E000350). There is no stammer. The condition for her voice here is not a non-human listener or a mathematical proof; it is that she is facing a broken formula.

Thornley’s boast is that his research needs money, and that a dull girl could never understand its worth. He grips her wrist and her face and casts a memory spell, inviting her to admire its perfection (S24:E000377–E000396). It dissolves before it lands. The narration then gives the one rule for Monica’s anger. She almost never becomes angry; when she is mocked as slow and useless she agrees. But defiling a perfect formula she will not allow, and his spell, like the falsified ledgers, is a botched one. Her verdict is 「全然、完璧じゃない」 (S24:E000427; obs. 105), followed by a technical critique and a turn of her own self-deprecation into a weapon: a spell blocked by someone like her can hardly be perfect (S24:E000453).

His second spell is designed to destroy a mind. She reaches into it, an act the narration calls wildly improper for anyone but the far stronger party, unpicks it like knotted thread, and instead of dispelling it reweaves it, more intricate and more beautiful, until the scattered light becomes white butterflies (S24:E000459–E000467; obs. 106). A thin image of butterflies on white marks the change of viewpoint to Thornley (S24:E000474). From his side, the old grimoires said that the completed form of this art takes the shape of a butterfly; he committed crimes to reach it and failed; a shabby girl has done it without a word. Then he understands. Two years ago a girl of fifteen published a formula more advanced than one he had spent twenty years on and wrecked his pride (S24:E000516–E000524; obs. 107). His last attempted sentence breaks off at the epithet. The narration calls her a monster in a girl’s shape, and the sentence in which she names his dream is itself cut: twenty-four hours exactly, and he will dream of something the text replaces with a dash and dots (S24:E000542).

Two features of the dream deserve weight. First, it is the culprit’s own testimony and is the only place his crimes are confirmed from inside: he led Aaron on, erased Aaron’s memory of him, wiped Cyril when he noticed, and in the dream plans to brainwash the prince next, calling him the duke’s puppet (S24:E000555–E000573; obs. 108). Selma is not mentioned. Her manipulation rests on Monica’s inference and later on Monica’s report, not on his own account. Second, the dream is the riddle-song. Pigs appear and multiply by the same rule, two, three, five, eight, thirteen, until they cover the horizon, and a cart’s wheels are heard (S24:E000581–E000594; obs. 109). The song that was Monica’s refuge in the dream of chapter 4 is the instrument of her one punishment. Asked by Lynn what he is dreaming, she answers, shyly, that it is a dream of a very beautiful number sequence: 「数列の夢」 (S24:E000661).

The scene’s comedy turns on her immediate panic that she has gone too far, 「やりすぎた」 (S24:E000607), and on her uncertainty whether her use of the restricted art was lawful. The narration states the law: such magic is permitted only for interrogating grave criminals or in national emergency, with authorization from the mages’ guild or the Seven Sages. She thinks a Sage’s special status probably covers her and is not sure (S24:E000610–E000614; obs. 110). Lynn answers by quoting Louis’s motto. The legal question is left exactly as uncertain as she leaves it.

### 6.11 The hand in memory and three recontextualizations

The epilogue’s title names a hand in someone else’s memory, and its central scene is Cyril’s. Finding the new treasurer asleep over the ledger, he does not shout. He looks at a plain, oversized white glove on a hand as small as a child’s and remembers the hand reached toward him in the forest, small and carrying a callus from years of holding a pen. He takes the pen from her fingers, covers her hand with his own as if measuring it, and hooks a finger under the edge of the glove (S25:E000091–E000097; obs. 111). Felix’s voice stops him. Half-awake, Monica smiles at him and says she is glad he is no longer cold: 「ひんやりしてない」 (S25:E000117). His hand goes to the brooch; his mouth opens; Felix puts a biscuit in Monica’s mouth. Twice in one page Felix’s intervention lands at the moment Cyril comes closest to knowing. The text does not attribute intent.

The rest of the epilogue measures change. A week later Monica asks Lana to judge a braid she has made, describing it as her best work in ratio and angle, in plain form without polite endings, and Lana pronounces it 「上出来」 (S25:E000237–E000243; obs. 112). Felix re-plaits the same braid in under two minutes when it comes loose, observing that a prince must be able to do everything perfectly (S25:E000334; obs. 113), and Monica thanks him without being prompted. Then he sends her to introduce herself to some twenty club presidents, her worst conceivable task. Cyril takes the list, says he will accompany her because a week of watching her work has convinced him she deserves to be introduced as treasurer, and asks whether she means to say she cannot. She remembers him saying “cannot will not do” as he fainted, thinks that he is remarkable, and that someone remarkable is recognizing her. She stammers that she will try, he calls her by her office, 「ノートン会計」 (S25:E000440), and she answers as loudly as she can manage (S25:E000401–E000442; obs. 114). The main story ends on a role title attached to a false surname, and on her loudest voice in the book.

The secret episode is placed after this ending and alters three things the reader thought they understood.

The first is Louis. Monica’s report, reproduced in full in the book, is a few lines long, begins with congratulations on his wife’s pregnancy and a proposal to give the baby an introductory mathematics book, reduces her appointment to the council to 「色々あって」 (S27:E000017), summarizes Thornley’s crimes in plain sentences, and ends with an apology for going a little too far and a resolution to keep trying (S27:E000012–E000041; obs. 115). It is written without a single stammer and without any order. Louis reads it, groans that the only praiseworthy line is the first, complains that her scholarly papers are lucid and her reports chaotic, and calls her 「褒められ下手」 (S27:E000061), bad at taking credit. Then he burns it. In the same reflection he notes, as an aside, that he had already placed 「うちの弟子」 (S27:E000069), his own disciple, in the school as a precaution, and that Monica’s results still exceeded anything he had expected (obs. 116). Monica does not know there is another agent at the academy. The volume gives no hint who it is.

Asked by Lynn why he gave the task to Monica, Louis answers that bullying the strong is more fun than bullying the weak: 「強い者をいじめる方が、楽しい」 (S27:E000191). He does not regard her as weak. He recounts the selection: one vacancy became two when an elderly Sage retired ill; the examination was an interview and a duel using attack magic only; Monica collapsed in the interview, which is why she thinks she was a makeweight; but the examiners never said which candidate was better (S27:E000204–E000216). In the duel she wept, sniffled, panicked, and shut him out entirely with spells of a power, range and complexity that should have required long chants (S27:E000225–E000239; obs. 117). His verdict, as a self-declared genius, is 「アレはバケモノです」 (S27:E000242). He dragged her to the dragon to give her some confidence, and she ran home; her servility, he thinks, makes his own defeat look ridiculous (S27:E000247–E000249; obs. 118).

This revises chapter 1 in the reader’s understanding without revising any fact in it. Monica’s belief that she is the reserve candidate was always marked as a belief she held without being told (S15:E000836; obs. 013); now an involved party remembers the opposite outcome. Louis’s coercion in chapter 1 remains coercion. But his motive now includes something like investment in her self-respect, mixed with pride, and the volume lets both stand.

The second recontextualization is the mission. Louis does not take the king’s order at face value. He thinks its real meaning was 「監視せよ」 (S27:E000253): watch the second prince. He describes Felix as accomplished, charming and backed by the most powerful noble in the kingdom, and also as 「得体が知れない」 (S27:E000264), with something he senses moving under the smile that slips away whenever he probes it (S27:E000251–E000272; obs. 119). Orthodox methods will not catch so slippery a prince, so Louis chose the most incongruous instrument he had. This confirms the doubt Monica voiced to herself in chapter 4. It is Louis’s interpretation of the king; the king’s intent is not shown.

The third is Felix. His last scene confirms that the duke appointed both the dismissed treasurer, Aaron, and the arrested teacher, Thornley (S27:E000331), that Felix knew of Thornley’s guilt and wished to deal with him personally, and that he attributes the arrest to Louis, whom he believes has been sniffing around him (S27:E000331–E000340; obs. 120). That belief is wrong: the arrest was Monica’s, and the public credit was arranged by Louis’s leak to the press. He also reveals that he read the tracking formula in Louis’s brooch and had Wildianu destroy it at once, and the narration adds that publicly he is supposed to know nothing of magic (S27:E000361–E000367; obs. 121). The broken brooch of chapter 1, which launched the whole mission, was not an attack he was hiding. It was his own act.

He infers that a Sage is watching him and, warming visibly, wishes it had been another: the hero who repelled the Worgan dragon, the only chantless caster in the world, a genius of a thousand years, the Silent Witch. The final words of the book’s narrative are his name for her, 「レディ・エヴァレット」 (S27:E000393; obs. 122), spoken as one speaks of someone beloved.

The prologue’s watcher was a blue-eyed man who used 僕 and whose voice sounded as if he had fallen in love with the magic he saw. The watcher’s eyes are called 碧 (S13:E000004), and Felix’s eyes receive the same colour word again and again (S17:E000237; S18:E000405; S21:E000074; obs. 024); he uses 僕 only in private; and here the Witch is the object of the same rapt attention. The volume never says that the watcher was Felix, and this reading records the identification as a strong inference rather than a stated fact. If it holds, the book ends on a triple irony. The watcher he wishes for is already watching him. He has classified her as too incompetent to be anyone’s agent. And he gives her rival the credit for her work.

### 6.12 The visual channel

The illustrations do not simply decorate the prose; they redistribute what the reader knows and when.

The cover shows the hooded girl with a finger raised to her lips, the cat on her shoulder, the staff across her body (S01:E000004; obs. 123). The gesture asks for silence and promises a secret at once, and the volume later gives the same gesture to Felix when he silences Elliott in the old garden (S18:E000397). The colour frontispieces then disclose, before the prologue, the name the prologue withholds; the hut scene of chapter 1 with Louis’s remark about wasted talent; and the climax of chapter 9, a ring of fire around the hooded Witch with Cyril’s thought that a single caster doing all this would be a monster (S02:E000004–S04:E000004; obs. 124). A reader of this edition therefore meets the night fight’s key sentence as a caption before meeting Monica at all.

Inside the book the illustrations follow the prose’s distribution of power. The prologue picture makes the Witch tiny and turned away (S11:E000004; obs. 125). The council is introduced as a formal group portrait with name plates, in which only Felix smiles (S17:E000112; obs. 126). Lana is drawn from the height of Monica’s chair (S18:E000195). Monica waiting at the council door is drawn from above, small, one hand at her chest and the other gripping her skirt (S20:E000674; obs. 127). Comic beats get comic deformation: Nero convulsing over coffee is drawn in a squashed, cartoon style quite unlike the rest (S19:E000108). Isabelle’s picture joins her adoring face and her villainess face with a double arrow, a diagram of a switched persona (S22:E000233; obs. 128).

The one interior image of Monica using her full power comes at the end of chapter 10. She is in profile with her hood down, a butterfly alighting on her raised finger, and her hair is in the braids Lana taught her (S24:E000514; obs. 129). It is the only picture that puts both of her identities on one body: the Silent Witch in the middle of her most dangerous spell, wearing the schoolgirl’s ribbon.

Caption plates sometimes name characters before the prose does. Lynn’s full name appears on the plate in chapter 1 (S15:E000438) and enters the prose only in chapter 9 (S23:E000109). Felix’s and the other officers’ names are given on the group portrait during a scene in which Monica has not yet learned them.

Chapters 7 and 9, the epilogue and the secret episode carry no story illustration apart from the secret episode’s heading. Felix’s private scenes, Louis’s confession of his defeat and Felix’s final declaration are left entirely to prose.

The character reference at the back is titled as a list of the characters “so far” and is spoiler-careful. It omits Felix’s spirit, Nero’s nature, Thornley, Selma and Aaron, and summarizes each person in a few comic lines. Several of its statements occur nowhere in the narrative, and section 7.7 keeps them apart (S29:E000004–S31:E000004; obs. 130). The jacket copy frames Monica as unaware of her own talent and the book as a brisk fantasy in which a reclusive genius secretly punishes wrongdoers around the prince (S33:E000004; obs. 131). That is marketing paratext; the narrative itself is more careful, showing Monica as someone who knows exactly what she can do with magic and does not believe it makes her valuable.

Five observations follow from the table.

First, Monica’s fluency is conditional, and the conditions are consistent across the volume. She speaks without breaking when the listener is not human (Nero throughout); when she is explaining a calculation, a proof, a ledger or a medical condition (S19:E000473; S21:E000253; S23:E000390); when she is angry on behalf of a formula (S24:E000316–E000350); and in writing (S27:E000012–E000041), where the sentences are whole but the order of events is lost. She breaks when she must speak about herself, to a person, in front of attention, and especially when she must thank or refuse.

Second, her movement in V01 can be traced as a sequence of speech acts rather than as a change of mood: a failed greeting, a drilled thanks to a cat, a completed thanks to Lana, a request, a thanks to Felix, an acceptance, a false confession to protect someone, a completed self-introduction under a false name (S22:E000658), a refusal of pride to Cyril, an accusation, plain speech to a friend, and a loud answer to her title. Section 7 records the stages as state changes.

Third, Felix’s pronouns are the most consequential linguistic fact in the book. 私 is his public self; 僕 appears only with Wildianu and in the prologue’s unnamed watcher (S13:E000012; S21:E001027). The volume never comments on the shift; it lets the reader notice.

Fourth, address terms track relationships. Cyril moves from 貴様 and the full name to the office title; Lana moves from 貴女 to the given name; Isabelle uses an affectionate kin term for a woman older than her whom her role requires her to call aunt; Felix replaces Monica’s name with an animal.

Fifth, the narrator speaks too, in a recognizable voice: deadpan one-line corrections set as their own paragraphs, such as the remark that the impressive one was Isabelle, not villainesses in general (S17:E000376), or that Monica’s human rights had just been casually removed (S22:E001001). These lines do the evaluative work the characters cannot, and they keep the reader one step outside every character, including Monica.

## 7. Character, directional relationship, world and formal development

Romanized names below are analyst conveniences for readability, not official translations. Japanese forms appear in section 6.13 and in the master ledger.

### 7.1 Monica Everett

**Entering V01 state (chapter 1).** In the year she turns seventeen; commoner by birth, a Sage and a count-rank peer for two years; living alone with a familiar; working on calculations rather than on the duties of her office; underfed. She avoids people as a preference and fears them as a reflex. She believes she owes her seat to a vacancy and to pity. She does not inhabit her rank, has never looked at the faces of the king or the prince, and remembers people by measurement.

**What V01 reveals rather than changes.** Much of what the reader learns about Monica in this volume is old information disclosed late, which the ledger records as revealed rather than developmental: a learned father who taught her and is dead; an adoption by his former pupil; Minerva and a single friendship that ended in accusation; a dream of a drunken adult man, a bottle, a rule of silence and a counting song; reflexive head-covering when a hand is raised; terror at an adult male body even when it is her own familiar’s; the knowledge that rank makes truth; an absolute refusal to let someone else be falsely accused; and the belief that her talent took her father and her friend. None of these is explained. Together they show that the comic origin story of chapter 1 is not the root of her silence.

**What changes.**

- *Speech.* By the end she can thank, request, accept a task, complete a self-introduction, accuse, and speak in plain form to a friend. The conditions under which her voice works are stable and specific (section 6.13). The change is in the number of situations she will attempt, not in a cure.
- *Initiative.* In chapter 1 she nods because a hand is on her shoulder. In later chapters she decides: to ask Lana to teach her, to take on the search, to speak against an injustice, to repair Cyril’s brooch, to confront Thornley with her face shown. Each is narrated as a choice made against fear.
- *Knowledge.* She learns that some people in authority investigate instead of deferring to rank (S22:E000902–E000928). That is a change in what she believes about the world, not yet in what she expects of herself.
- *Role.* She has acquired a false name, a school, a council office and the public mark of it, and through the office a sanctioned reason to stand near the prince.
- *Attachments.* She has one friend, Lana, and something like admiration for Cyril. Her stated self-understanding, that she cannot be attached to people, is contradicted by her conduct.

**What does not change.** Her self-assessment. She says to Cyril that she cannot take pride in herself, and nothing in the volume moves that sentence. The ending gives her recognition from others; it does not give her the ability to accept it as true. Her report to Louis, which reduces the most important events to “various things happened,” shows the same habit in writing.

**Public, private and attributed selves.** At exit she has at least four: the Silent Witch of legend, a hero whom Felix adores in absentia; Monica Norton, the shabby girl the school despises and the council tolerates; the person her few confidants know, scared, kind, exacting; and the person seen by those who fought her, a monster. Each observer holds one or two of these. The reader holds all four.

### 7.2 Felix Arc Ridill

V01 shows Felix almost entirely through other people until chapter 6, and then gives him three scenes of his own. What is observable: a public manner of perfect courtesy whose single word silences a room; a private pronoun and a private vocabulary of games, taming and a life lived as a remainder; an upper spirit he hides even from his closest officer; a literacy in magic he conceals; nocturnal outings he lies about; a year-long trap for an embezzler; a test set for Monica; a kiss given out of curiosity; and a rapt admiration for the Silent Witch whom he does not recognize in the girl he calls a squirrel. His relation to Duke Clockford is stated as one he cannot defy, and the narration in his own viewpoint doubles the word for “absolutely” (S20:E000662), while his acts remove the duke’s placements.

What is withheld: his wish, the reason his life is a remainder, the destination of his nights, the source of the duke’s hold, why he hides his abilities, and why he knows a commoners’ nursery rhyme. Several readers inside the volume call him empty or uncanny: Nero (S24:E000093), Louis (S27:E000264). Monica never does; she finds him tiring and too close. This reading records his interior as genuinely undetermined at V01, and section 10 keeps it open rather than resolving it.

### 7.3 Louis Miller

Chapter 1 presents a beautiful, polite, violent man who coerces his junior and smiles while threatening her; he pinches her ear when she will not answer, and Nero thinks him a demon. Chapter 2 gives him a wife he adores and a modest, homely house. The secret episode adds that he was beaten by Monica in their selection, regards her as a monster in power, dragged her to the dragon to give her confidence, finds her humility an insult to his own defeat, and has placed a disciple of his own in the school. He also feeds her sweets when she flags (S25:E000050). The volume does not ask the reader to choose between the bully and the patron. It shows a man who uses people and invests in them, often in one act.

### 7.4 Cyril Ashley

Cyril is loud, loyal to Felix to the point of violence, and unfailingly correct in procedure. He checks a stranger’s injury before asking whose fault it was, investigates an incident instead of deferring to rank, and changes his public judgment of Monica when her work earns it. His interior gives the volume its second study of recognition: an adopted son who needs to be wanted, who heard his mother call him a noble’s child and could not answer, who drove himself into illness and treats a defective brooch as proof of belonging. At exit he has been attacked with memory magic without knowing it, treated by Monica without knowing it, and holds an unverified suspicion about her hand. He does not know the treatment was a treatment; he remembers a monster.

### 7.5 The supporting cast

The ledger keeps thin characters explicitly thin.

- **Nero.** A familiar in cat form who reads adventure novels, senses magic, takes the shape of a black-haired, gold-eyed man, is stronger than a human, dislikes cold, and is described by Felix as non-human and probably not a spirit (S24:E000195). Toward Monica he is teasing, comforting, practical about her fear responses, possessive, and willing to hide things from her. His nature is not disclosed.
- **Isabelle Norton.** Brave in the prologue, theatrical at school, devoted to Monica, her idol, and an effective tactician who uses staged cruelty to win Monica what she needs. Her maid Agatha is the prologue’s maid and her co-conspirator.
- **Lana Colette.** A new-money baron’s daughter who is herself mocked for her family, scolds and helps in the same breath, worries when Monica disappears, apologizes for her part in the staircase incident, and becomes the first person Monica speaks to in plain form.
- **Lynn.** Louis’s wind spirit, literal-minded, bookish, without reverence for her master, the volume’s courier and Monica’s transport.
- **Elliott Howard.** Watches Felix’s back, sets traps, despises commoners who forget their place, and has not accepted Monica at exit.
- **Bridget Graham.** Felix’s childhood friend, the only girl he appointed, gifted in languages, cold to Monica, strikes her with a fan, and has not accepted her at exit.
- **Neil Clay Maywood.** Kind, polite even to his own year, and the one council member who welcomes her.
- **Victor Thornley.** Accomplished, contemptuous, wounded by Monica’s published work without her knowledge, the embezzlement’s organizer and a user of restricted magic. Removed to Louis’s custody; his licence will be revoked and exile is expected (S27:E000077).
- **Selma Karsh and Aaron O’Brien.** Removed from the school; Aaron guilty of embezzlement and a victim of memory erasure; Selma guilty of two attempts on the prince and, on Monica’s inference, manipulated into them.
- **Rosalie Miller.** Physician, whose pregnancy is announced in chapter 9, the first adult to treat Monica’s body as something that matters.

### 7.9 Form and evaluation

Five formal patterns are promoted to the cumulative ledger because they recur and will matter later. Power is narrated through external focalizers. Silence is printed as typographic elision. The narrator corrects characters in one-line deadpan paragraphs. The genre of the romance novel is a lens the characters themselves use. The animal and pet lexicon attached to Monica governs how the powerful speak to her. A sixth, the riddle-song’s number series, recurs four times with a different function each time: escape, memory, explanation, punishment.

## 8. Alternative readings, absence and contradiction

### 8.1 “The volume is a light comedy about shyness, and the trauma reading over-reads it”

The comic register is real and dominant, and the narrator’s own explanation of her silence in chapter 1 is comic. Against that, the volume repeatedly supplies material the comic explanation cannot carry: a dream of an adult man with a bottle and a rule of silence; two reflexive head-coverings; terror at an adult male shape she knows to be friendly; a rule that rank decides truth stated as her knowledge; a sentence linking her talent to her father’s loss. None of it is named, diagnosed or explained, and this reading does neither. The claim is narrower: V01 establishes that something in Monica’s past shaped her silence before Minerva, and leaves what it was unresolved. What would weaken the claim is a later disclosure that reframes the dream as unrelated fantasy; what would strengthen it is a disclosure of who the man was.

### 8.2 “Felix is kind to Monica” against “Felix is toying with her”

His acts toward her are mostly kind in form: he frees her, gathers her nuts, shields her, feeds her, braids her hair, stakes his office on her. His viewpoint scenes frame the same acts as a test, a game of taming, and amusement at a creature uninterested in him; he lies to his council and to his friend; he set a trap with his keys. Both readings fit the observable acts. What separates them is motive, and his motive is the volume’s most deliberately withheld fact. The reading therefore records him as a strategic actor whose kindness and calculation coincide, and refuses to promote either into his essence. No romantic interest on Monica’s side is shown in V01; on his side, his only language of devotion is spent on the Silent Witch in the abstract.

### 8.3 “Louis is simply a bully”

Chapter 1 supports the charge, and the narrator records it with the line that his remark is abuse. The secret episode complicates it without withdrawing it: his coercion coexists with respect, with a wish for her confidence, and with his own pride. Lynn’s two labels for him, one replaced by the other, are the volume’s joke about how hard he is to summarize.

### 8.4 “Monica does not know how talented she is”

The jacket copy says so. The narrative does not. Monica knows exactly what she can do with magic and numbers: she diagnoses, forensically reconstructs, proves, rewrites a Sage’s tool, and judges a senior mage’s spell inadequate in technical terms. What she does not believe is that these abilities make her worth anything, or that they are achievements rather than a curse. The difference matters for any later model of her: she is not unaware of her competence; she is unable to value it.

### 8.5 Normative evaluation: comedy, coercion and touch

Criterion: whether the narration registers the effect of an act on the person subjected to it, independently of the comic register in which it is presented. Louis’s coercion is registered: the narrator calls his words abuse, and Monica’s fear is shown at length. Felix’s pet lexicon is registered in one deadpan line about her human rights. Bridget’s blow is registered from Monica’s side as the trigger of a dissociative retreat. The kiss is the hardest case. It is given to someone not aware of her surroundings, it is never known to her, and her one ally decides she should not know. The narration presents it as Felix’s curiosity and plays the aftermath as a joke about paw pads. The volume does not evaluate it; this reading records that it is non-consensual in form, that its recipient remains ignorant, and that the comedy depends on that ignorance. Whether later volumes revisit it is a question, not a prediction.

Monica’s own use of a restricted art against Thornley also needs a criterion. He was attempting to destroy her mind; her response sealed him in a twenty-four-hour nightmare and delivered him for interrogation. She herself doubts its legality and fears she went too far. The volume treats the punishment as poetic justice and the doubt as comedy; it does not settle the law.

### 8.6 The afterword and the web serial

The author states that the web serial was conceived as one sixteen-chapter story, that this book corresponds to its first three chapters with substantial additions and revisions, and that the revisions are on the whole kinder to Monica (S32:E000012–E000038; obs. 132). This is author paratext about witness relationships. No web text was read or compared in this transaction, and nothing in this reading depends on the claim. It is recorded in the source map as a future comparison lead.

### 8.7 Meaningful absences inside V01

- Monica never chants. Given the stated constraint (S18:E000295), the absence is consistent across every scene in which she casts.
- The king, the first prince and the third prince never appear. Every statement about the succession is a character’s belief.
- Monica’s father is never named, nor the man in the dream, nor the pupil who adopted her, nor the Minerva friend. These are withheld identities, not absent persons.
- Felix’s mother is mentioned once, as the source of his looks (S27:E000255); nothing else is said.
- No scene shows Monica performing a Sage’s formal duties beyond the dragon, which is itself narrated only as report.
