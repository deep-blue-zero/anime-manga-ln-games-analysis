## Causal story synopsis

The first public view of the Silent Witch comes through Isabelle Norton, daughter of the count responsible for a dragon-stricken eastern territory. Isabelle and her maid Agatha choose to remain rather than abandon their home and people. News that a single royal magician has repelled the legendary black dragon seems to promise safety, until the displaced wyverns arrive. A small hooded magician summons a spirit king's wind, kills the dragons precisely and lowers their bodies without collateral destruction. Isabelle is overwhelmed by the grace and saving force of the magic; a separate blue-eyed observer discovers something that absorbs him completely.

Three months later, seventeen-year-old Monica Everett lives in a mountain hut, working on numerical problems amid papers that have displaced her bed. Her familiar Nero speaks, reads adventure stories and provides company. Annie, a village girl who delivers supplies, admires the famous witch without recognizing her in the hesitant, unkempt person before her. Monica can move papers with extraordinary exactness but cannot comfortably answer ordinary questions. Her unchanted magic developed when she could not speak during observed practical tests; it earned her a place among the Seven Sages at fifteen without resolving that difficulty. She believes she was admitted only through an extra vacancy.

Her fellow sage Louis Miller arrives with his contracted wind spirit Lin. The king has ordered him to guard the second prince Felix secretly. Louis's first-prince connections and public fame impede entry to Serendia Academy, where Felix studies under the influence of his grandfather, Duke Clockford. Louis's protective and tracking brooch has been broken, and Felix denies anything happened. Louis therefore compels Monica to infiltrate the academy, threatening dire consequences for failure. Her anonymity, student age, unguardlike behavior and unchanted magic fit the task. A fictional history makes her Monica Norton, an unwanted adopted relative abused by Isabelle. The count and Isabelle cooperate out of gratitude for the dragon rescue, while Isabelle enthusiastically rehearses the fashionable villainess part. At Louis's home his physician wife Rosalie addresses Monica's poor nourishment, sleep and hygiene. Monica discovers how little she knows about aristocratic ranks, including her own title.

At the academy, treasurer Aaron O'Brien has been caught embezzling. His family's expensive purchase of Clockford's patronage reduced the allowance he wanted for pleasure; he stole repeatedly with a collaborator whose identity he now cannot remember. Felix threatens the consequences for his family, and vice-president Cyril Ashley restrains Aaron when he lashes out. Elsewhere Isabelle obtains Monica's wished-for attic room by pretending it is a degrading punishment, exploiting the headmaster's deference to her family. Monica treasures her father's coffee pot there and tries to prepare for classes. Her failure to introduce herself draws Thornley's contempt. Lana Colette criticizes her appearance but arranges her hair and leaves a ribbon; Lana herself is mocked as a wealthy newcomer's daughter. Monica wants to express thanks but struggles to speak.

Seeking a solitary place to eat nuts, Monica enters the old garden. Elliott Howard, secretly guarding Felix as bait for an attacker, mistakes her for an intruder and restrains her. She escapes with wind but falls; Felix intervenes, gathers her scattered food and shields her when a pot drops toward them. Monica's hidden wind saves them both, and the accumulated pressure makes her faint. A disturbing dream links the falling object to an uncle's violence; Selma Kirsch visits her in the sickroom. That evening Nero's human-shaped practice helps Monica force out thanks, although his appearance itself frightens her. They also observe a cloaked figure outside the boys' dormitory.

The next morning Monica thanks Lana and asks to learn the hairstyle. Their shared fear of being called boring gives the exchange a different intimacy from fashionable small talk. Summoned by Elliott, Monica learns the kind youth is Felix and identifies him as the nighttime figure through his proportions. Challenged, she launches an absorbed explanation of the golden ratio. Felix's apparent acceptance that he was baiting a criminal conceals that he was actually going out for recreation. He and Elliott explain that an earlier sign had fallen toward Felix after Aaron's disgrace. Elliott suspects Monica of helping lure Felix beneath the pot and tells her to solve the attacks to prove her innocence.

Monica reconstructs the pot's trajectory and identifies the music-room balcony. On the way, Caroline Simmons's taunting of Selma and confrontation with Lana end in a shove: Lana collides with Monica, who falls downstairs. Monica secretly fills the steps with barriers, rolls at excessive speed and hits Cyril. He checks her injuries. Assuming rank will protect Caroline, Monica takes blame to prevent Lana being accused. She continues investigating, notices the high balcony rail and an overturned pot used as a stool, and finds Selma's discarded dirtied gloves near the makeup room. The room booking corroborates her conclusion. Selma confesses to both attacks and even the embezzlement, trying to save an unloving fiancé she feels she cannot lose. Felix refuses to absolve Aaron, but keeps Selma's case quiet and allows withdrawal rather than public capital prosecution. Monica finds Selma's total dependence frightening; Felix expresses a more sympathetic recognition.

Noticing errors in Felix's accounts, Monica eagerly accepts an audit. He secretly tests her with accessible keys and spirit surveillance, but she ignores everything except the records. Absorption becomes deeper when Bridget Graham hits her for not responding. Felix's kiss, mistaken for Nero's paw, finally draws her back. Having corrected seventy-four years of accounts in hours, Monica is appointed treasurer despite her distress. Felix privately treats discovering and taming her hidden purpose as a game, while describing his own remaining life as bounded by the royal succession. Cyril distrusts Monica's manners but verifies her work. His subsequent training is precise; he also reveals that he investigated the stair incident and censured Caroline, surprising Monica with an exception to her expectations of rank. He cannot understand why she will not take pride in recognized talent. Her memory of a friendship broken by that talent makes the exhortation painful.

Lin arrives with Louis's delighted announcement that Rosalie is pregnant, then requests a written mission report. An unusual surge of cold takes Monica, Nero and Lin to Cyril, whose magical aid has begun returning excess power to his body. His frantic attachment to the brooch reflects its meaning as his adoptive father's acceptance and his unresolved wish to be recognized by his mother. Nero takes it; Monica neutralizes Cyril's attacks, protects his body and the forest, and lets him spend the dangerous surplus. She repairs and improves the brooch because she wants him to retain pride in what she experiences in herself as a curse. Nero carries Cyril back and encounters Felix, detecting his hidden spirit scout. Felix learns the stranger is nonhuman, but not that he is Monica's familiar. Cyril recalls the small hooded rescuer as a terrifyingly strong “monster.”

Suspecting that mental interference damaged the brooch, Monica connects Cyril's condition to Aaron's missing memories and Selma's inexplicable knowledge of the concealed fraud. She confronts Thornley carrying away accounts. He tries to erase her knowledge, then destroy her mind. His defective formula offends her mathematical and magical pride; she dismantles and remakes it as butterflies, confining him to a twenty-four-hour dream of proliferating pigs. She then worries she overreacted and asks Lin to deliver him to Louis. A week later his arrest is public. Monica continues struggling with hair and introductions, but Lana remains her friend and Cyril, after a week observing her work, offers to accompany her to the club leaders and addresses her as treasurer. Recognition produces a hesitant, pleased effort rather than a sudden cure.

The supplemental **沈黙の魔女の報告書** revisits these events after the handover and closes gaps in the reader's knowledge. Louis is impressed despite the report's vagueness; he had also planted an unidentified disciple as backup. His recollection establishes that the crying fifteen-year-old Monica defeated him completely in the selection combat, undermining her reserve-admission account. His theory that the king wants surveillance remains a theory. Felix privately reveals that he recognized the brooch's tracking function and ordered Will to break it; he also already suspected Thornley. He longs for the Silent Witch as the agent who might have been sent to him, without recognizing her in the new treasurer he is manipulating. This final irony leaves protection, surveillance, admiration and concealed identity unresolved beyond the solved local crime.

## Whole-volume argument and consequential rivals

V01 makes recognition difficult by separating the domains in which a person is legible. Staff and students infer breeding, merit, danger or desire from dress, fluent speech, rank, facial expression and physical proximity. Those cues are not always false: Monica really lacks social training, Cyril really does show anger, Selma really attempts an attack. The error is to infer a whole person or an authoritative truth from one channel. Monica's own strongest judgments can fail too. Her account of reserve selection is contradicted by Louis's combat recollection; her expectation of rank-bound justice is corrected by Cyril's witness work; her belief that Lana will reject a new office is tested against renewed practical care. This is not an opposition between a truthful private self and a false public mask. Private explanations are partial, and behavior has different costs and meanings for different observers.

The strongest developmental reading is therefore modest: belonging becomes possible through small reciprocal uses of attention before Monica can feel proud of genius. Lana learns that her supposedly vulgar expertise can be desired; Monica acquires something worth attempting badly. Cyril's recognition is earned within accountable work and may also be affected by an unconfirmed memory of rescue. Isabelle sacrifices proximity to supply the room Monica actually wants, although she still misunderstands the rescue's original motive. None gives Monica a complete new self. The ending's continued errors, stammer and dependence on accompaniment are positive constraints on a total-transformation reading. Conversely, treating every success as someone else's coercion misses her own request for instruction, concern over false accusation, rescue beyond the minimum mission, and final effort to answer loudly.

Formal silence is similarly plural. The famous silence allows unusually fast, covert magic and invites awe. A classroom silence hides the intended greeting that will not come; absorbed silence can make a slap counterproductive; silence can be practiced concealment, constrained refusal or withheld knowledge. Louis and Felix often speak with polished gentleness while reducing others' options. The reader receives the reverse of their public legibility through moving focalization, then must correct even apparent insider explanations. Aaron's and Cyril's interrupted memory are literally rendered as missing continuity; Selma's interior needs challenge a simplistic confession-based solution. The extra's disclosures do not just add facts: they reveal how several earlier explanations were calibrated to recipients. The solved mystery ends with more precise questions about the guardians, not knowledge of the next volume.

Technical beauty is both a saving practice and an ethically unstable standard. Controlling the dragons' bodies and protecting Cyril from his own cold show care through accuracy; repairing an aid lets another person preserve pride. Yet the impeccably remade dream terrorizes its subject, and the comic narrative promptly returns Monica to fear of authority. The rival claim that technique functions simply as benevolent self-expression cannot accommodate that turn. Nor does the novel equate all restraint with violence: witness checks, careful explanation, food, teaching and accompaniment offer different ways to respond to vulnerability. Their coexistence with pinching, hitting, threats and pet metaphors makes any blanket moral endorsement or blanket condemnation too coarse for the actual tonal and causal variation.
