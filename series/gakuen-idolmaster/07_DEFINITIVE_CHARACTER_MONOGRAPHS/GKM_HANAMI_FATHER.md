# The Hanami sisters’ father

When Hanami Ume asks her father whether she has disappointed him by choosing to be an idol, he says he never expected anything of her in the first place. Ume’s dismay makes sense. Her parents are famous retired athletes. The family runs a training center. Her father knows her competitive habits well enough that she thinks she recognizes his judgment even when someone else repeats it. Yet he follows the apparent dismissal with a distinction. He has expectations of himself and of people working toward an interest they share. What he does for Ume and her sister Saki is cheer them on. Ume offers to change the arrangement. As an idol, she says, meeting people’s expectations is precisely her job. He answers with advice from someone whose competitive career has already ended. Ume notices that advice still sounds rather different from an expectation. Their conversation matters because neither has to pretend the difference has gone away. (Ume Dear 003 L273–285 and 026 L714–787.)

## What the family business lets other people see

The sisters grew up with both parents often away and their grandparents looking after them. Saki says she and Ume spent much of that time together. The father’s influence can therefore be considerable even where he was an occasional presence. When the Producer develops a training plan for Ume, he visits the parents in Aichi after booking through their website. Both parents supply the form they want him to use and review the plan he developed with a trainer. Saki recognizes the format at once. The Producer says the parents called Ume a slow starter. Ume guesses the remark must have come from her father. Her confidence about who would say it tells us how well she recognizes his outlook. The original comment was delivered to the Producer, away from the girls. (Saki Dear 010 L281–302; Ume Dear 002 L281–321 and 003 L106–128, 273–285.)

A school support story gives him a smaller request that sounds unmistakably his. Saki boasts to him about the academy’s training equipment. He asks her to film it and turn her observations into a report. Ume, seeing Saki get the camera ready, jokes that the two of them are making work for themselves again. One daughter enjoys having an expert to impress. The other knows exactly what a casual mention of gym equipment can unleash. Saki credits him with this particular commission. The mother’s qualifications as a trainer and her forceful massage have their own sources in Ume’s story. Their daughters tell the difference between their parents’ contributions. (Support 3-0023 part 2 L86–104; Ume Dear 021 L337–379.)

When Ume brings classmates Shinosawa Hiro and Kuramoto China to the training center, their host introduces himself as Ume’s father and invites them to train as freely as if they were at home. China wonders whether he could be an older brother. He thanks her, says he hears that often, explains his family’s care for maintaining a body in peak condition, and offers them his books. An unexpected compliment takes only a moment to become an opportunity to teach and advertise. Hiro’s response is different. This, she thinks, is recognizably *Saki’s* father. The resemblance she notices follows his manner of meeting a compliment. No narrator needs to certify how young he looks. (Ume Dear 025 L328–373.)

The same pride makes him a vulnerable spokesman. Ume points out that none of the younger athletes from his old sporting circle has joined the center and reports that people online call it a factory for making cyborgs. She adds that Nagoya residents laughed at a menu made with a well-known café. Ume wants a new public image for a business she plainly knows from inside. He has claimed that younger athletes admire him. She asks what that reputation has actually done for recruitment. Her teasing and the later remark about his cooking reveal how readily people can make a local celebrity into a joke. The episodes give Ume’s report and his own reaction. Neither is a survey of public opinion. (Ume Dear 025 L442–493 and 026 L695–714.)

## The contest he agrees to enter

It is the Producer who first proposes that Ume represent the center as its publicity ambassador. Her father objects that he is better-looking and more popular, so the job need never reach her. Ume replies that an idol would make the obvious representative of an idol-training facility. Then he can debut as an idol himself, he says, and asks for the Producer’s help. Ume is the one who tells us his eyes look serious. He is joking competitively with her and also willing to risk a real result. The Producer then proposes choosing the ambassador in a competition and inviting the idols already visiting the facility. The prospect of a national broadcast is shared aloud. The father welcomes a viewer vote and the chance to unveil the center’s stage. His grounds include good publicity. Hiro and China volunteer to enter, giving Ume two more people whose response matters to her. (Ume Dear 025 L381–431, 494–581.)

The publicity calculation survives the warmth of this scene. Ume tells the Producer before the event that the ambassador’s title had held little interest for her; who was going to compete made it exciting. Her father gets a worthy public occasion for his business even if he loses. He also gives his daughter a chance to beat him in public. The Producer works with him as Ume’s rival, and afterward congratulates Ume on winning the appointment. She can make her father’s facility look good through a result neither his role as president nor his celebrity could simply give him. His own ranking and the details of his stage act are unstated in this material. (Ume Dear 026 L350–365; 027 L15–54, 64, 400–437.)

## What a retired athlete can offer an idol

Alone with Ume that evening, he notices she looks unusually happy even though she is away from Saki. She wants to talk about being an idol. He says he has studied the subject for less than a year. It is Ume who argues that someone remembered as a superhero by an older generation has something to tell her. He answers that seeing the young performers makes him feel his age. Among the people of Aichi, he says, he is only a retired athlete and local personality, handsome but apparently hopeless at food. Ume realizes he has looked himself up online, something the others had tried to spare him. The father who offered strangers his books can also read a room badly, get stung by other people’s words, and need family members to make allowances for him. His job title gives him considerable power; his daughter can still see where his confidence runs out. (Ume Dear 026 L659–714.)

The distinction between expectation and encouragement follows from this moment. He has enough experience to know that retirement can leave regrets after a good career. He cannot promise his daughter a life without them. He can ask her to give a challenge everything she has, so that at its end she can at least put on a brave face and call it a good one. Then he tells her to treasure her opponents and enjoy overtaking and being overtaken. Ume promptly reminds him that he counts as one of the opponents now. The advice makes room for a daughter who wants a different occupation and might win at her father’s expense. It also leaves her free to point out that he still has not offered the particular kind of expectation she asked for. (Ume Dear 026 L722–787.)

An earlier scene shows Ume putting another lesson to her own use. When her Producer blames himself after a loss, she describes her father’s practice of asking whether he could do better if he rewound time. If the answer is no, he tries to stop wasting time on regret. Ume expects that question to comfort the Producer. Instead he sees a possible better result and says that the choice it would take is one he still refuses to make. She declines to ask what it was. The lesson creates the conditions for an honest conversation even though her father’s hypothetical does not produce the answer she expected. Later she interprets a private consultation between him and the Producer as concerning herself and Saki, and says their interventions felt like a reminder. That is Ume’s retrospective understanding. We never hear a private agreement that would entitle either man to claim authorship of her decision. (Ume Dear 023 L93–123, 141–189; 027 L454–486, 547–586.)

## How the father is present in the recording

The registered Ume Dear 21–27 recording, SRC-0012, gives him a speaker label, 花海社長, “President Hanami.” It gives Ume the face the viewer can watch. At 26:00 his welcome begins in the dialogue box while she stands in the gym. At 28:15 his approval of the viewer vote is printed beneath her. At 36:28 the box names him as the person saying he supports both girls. Ume is the visible figure with folded arms. At 36:48 the father’s remark about retirement appears below Ume standing with her hands on her hips. At 36:59 his advice about enjoying a rivalry is printed while she holds up her fists. The shots let an audience observe what it is like to receive these words as Ume. They give the father's language a presence without assigning her gestures to him.

Two earlier observers independently identify the training-room introduction as an unvoiced character’s appearance and place the later father–daughter exchange in the lounge. Their compact recording has the historical recording’s complete encoded audio at the same clock. The frame observations above come from the historical file itself. In the locked scripts the 28 dialogue boxes naming him across these two episodes occupy intervals without a registered ADV voice take. The scene conveys his changes of inflection in writing through word choice, an aborted retort, and the abrupt descent from boasting to self-mockery. The daughter’s reactions supply a second expressive register. This edition supports no description of his personal vocal timbre, and the competition’s visible stage performance in the later chapter is Ume’s. (Ume Dear 025 L333–539 and 026 L659–775; SRC-0012, prior W4 S20 blind/challenger observations over 25:58–29:00 and 35:08–37:16.)

The father we can describe here is proud of remaining fit, easy to bait where local recognition is at stake and quick to make his expertise available. Neither altruism nor promotion cancels the other in his invitation to compete. The clearest measure of his support comes when Ume asks whether her career choice has disappointed him. He will care about what she does, enter a contest against her, and live with her winning. He gives her a reason to value the opponent she overtakes, including the one she calls Dad.
