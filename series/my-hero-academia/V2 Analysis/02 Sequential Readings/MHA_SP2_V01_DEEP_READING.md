## Heroism before power, recognition before legitimacy, and the social machinery that decides who is allowed to dream

# 1. Central thesis and volume role

Volume 1 does considerably more than introduce a powerless boy, give him a superpower, and admit him to hero school.

Its deeper structural movement is:

> **a child whom nearly every ordinary social mechanism has taught to treat his heroic dream as illegitimate is repeatedly re-evaluated when his conduct forces other people and institutions to revise their judgment.**

The first-pass thesis — **Midoriya is heroic before he is powerful** — is correct. The second pass makes that thesis more precise.

The volume does not merely contrast “good-hearted quirkless boy” with “powerful people.” It builds a sequence of recognition failures and recognition revisions:

1. Midoriya is born into a society where ability is immediately socially legible through **個性 / quirk**.
2. Medical diagnosis converts his difference into the category **無個性 / quirkless**.
3. His mother responds to his dream with grief and apology rather than the affirmation he wants.
4. His middle-school environment treats hero aspiration as an extension of quirk status; Bakugo is validated as plausible, Midoriya is publicly ridiculous.
5. All Might — Midoriya’s supreme moral authority — initially gives him essentially the same answer as everyone else: dream, but look at reality.
6. The sludge-villain crisis produces a contradiction. The licensed heroes possess power and professional status but are momentarily unable or unwilling to act effectively; the quirkless boy moves.
7. All Might revises his judgment because Midoriya’s conduct reveals something that ability testing could not.
8. One For All is offered not as random fortune but as **entrusted power after observed moral action**.
9. U.A.’s entrance examination first appears to quantify hero potential through combat points, then reveals an unannounced rescue metric that validates action undertaken without visible reward.
10. Aizawa immediately prevents this validation from becoming sentimental complacency: heroic intent that destroys the user’s body is professionally inadequate.

The result is a sophisticated opening argument:

> **Heroic worth cannot be read directly from power, but heroic worth also cannot remain merely inward. It must become embodied, sustainable, socially effective action.**

Volume 1 therefore establishes three problems that the entire series will keep revisiting:

- **recognition:** who sees heroic possibility before institutions certify it?
- **power:** what makes power legitimate, usable, and morally meaningful?
- **social responsibility:** what happens when everybody can see danger but waits for someone more authorized or more suitable to act?

With full-series hindsight, the first volume is striking because the ending’s mature social answer is already present in miniature. The decisive Chapter 1 image is not simply Midoriya running into danger. It is a crowd and multiple professionals **watching** while one socially disqualified child moves.

The final epilogue’s emphasis on earlier ordinary intervention does not appear from nowhere. Its moral seed is on page 47.

# 2. Narrative architecture

## Movement I — A society of visible difference: pages 5–24

The series opens not with All Might but with a proposition about equality:

> **「人は生まれながらに平等ではない」**

The statement is visually grounded in children displaying powers and in young Midoriya learning that he does not share them. This matters because *MHA* begins by naturalizing inequality at the level of bodies before it later asks what institutions do with that inequality.

The opening does not initially condemn quirks themselves. It establishes that **difference becomes classification**, and classification becomes expectation.

Middle school then demonstrates the social consequence. Hero aspiration is treated almost like a prestige career track whose eligibility is informally determined in advance by visible ability. Bakugo’s ambition is socially ratified. Midoriya’s is exposed to the room as comic impossibility.

The teacher does not protect Midoriya from this hierarchy; he helps stage it.

That is the first institutional finding of the reread:

> **Hero Society’s sorting mechanisms precede hero education. U.A. does not create the hierarchy from nothing; children arrive already trained to understand quirks as signals of future social rank.**

Bakugo is therefore not merely an independently arrogant child. His arrogance is constantly rewarded by an environment that treats his impressive quirk as evidence of destined exceptionalism.

Midoriya’s notebook habit forms under the opposite condition. Lacking an ability that makes him legible as a future combatant, he becomes an observer. The first pass recognized his analytical intelligence, but the second pass should stress that analysis is partly an adaptation to exclusion: if he cannot participate bodily, he participates cognitively.

## Movement II — The ideal says no: pages 25–43

Midoriya’s encounter with All Might first fulfills a fantasy and then destroys it.

All Might’s private body is the volume’s first major symbolic rupture. The public Symbol of Peace is revealed as a wounded man with a strict temporal limit. His enormous heroic form is not a permanent biological truth but a maintained public state.

This is important before One For All is even explained.

The Symbol of Peace is already a **double body**:

- public body: huge, smiling, apparently inexhaustible;
- private body: damaged, coughing blood, time-limited, physically precarious.

All Might explains that his smile itself has a strategic and psychological function. He saves people smiling, but that smile also helps him deceive the fear and pressure within himself. The series therefore introduces the Symbol of Peace as emotional labor and self-management, not merely muscles.

Midoriya then asks the question that his entire childhood has prepared:

> can a person without a quirk become a hero like All Might?

All Might does **not** initially give the answer the series is famous for.

He tells Midoriya that professional hero work is life-threatening, points out other socially valuable occupations such as police work, and tells him that although dreaming is not wrong, he must look realistically at his circumstances.

This is crucial.

The story’s moral authority initially reproduces society’s realism.

All Might is not cruel. His answer is professionally intelligible. But structurally, he becomes the last and most painful participant in Midoriya’s failed-recognition chain.

The famous later affirmation matters precisely because it is a **revision**, not because All Might possessed mystical insight from first glance.

## Movement III — Watching versus moving: pages 44–60

The sludge-villain recurrence is the volume’s moral experiment.

The scene is carefully constructed around professional mismatch. Heroes are present. They are not indifferent monsters. They are assessing compatibility, waiting for the right quirk, trying to prevent escalation, and recognizing the danger of an uncontrolled intervention.

That prevents the scene from becoming a childish “adults bad, kid good” binary.

Yet the social result remains paralysis.

Bakugo is visibly suffocating.

The crowd watches.

The professionals hesitate.

All Might, constrained by his spent transformation time, watches with guilt.

Midoriya initially joins the spectators. Then he recognizes Bakugo’s face.

The decisive wording is not a philosophical speech. It is perception:

> **「君が救けを求める顔してた」**

The act begins from recognition.

His body moves before he can justify it. He later asks himself why he came out and what he is doing. The point is not that he calculates himself into heroism. His prior years of obsessive observation and moral aspiration have become embodied reflex.

Visually, page 47 is foundational. Midoriya’s terrified face dominates the foreground while the crowd fills the background. He is not visually presented as stronger than the spectators. He is presented as the one who has crossed the line between **seeing** and **acting**.

That is the first mature version of the series’ bystander problem.

All Might is moved in turn. Midoriya’s action does not solve the physical crisis. It changes the person who can.

That causal distinction matters:

> **Midoriya’s first successful heroic act is not defeating the villain. It is breaking another hero out of paralysis.**

This anticipates the entrance exam’s later statement that his action moves people.

All Might’s intervention then becomes both rescue and moral confession. His later judgment of Midoriya is rooted in the recognition that, without the boy’s action, All Might himself would have become only words.

The chapter ends with the famous affirmation:

> **「君はヒーローになれる」**

But on reread, the important word is not only “hero.” It is **can**.

Midoriya has not yet become one. A previously foreclosed possibility has been reopened by recognition.

## Movement IV — Worthiness becomes embodied preparation: pages 61–86

Chapter 2 immediately refuses magical wish fulfillment.

All Might’s offer is phrased through moral and relational vocabulary:

- Midoriya is **worthy** of inheriting the power;
- All Might asks him to **receive** it;
- the quirk is something that can be **passed on**.

One For All’s social form therefore precedes its mechanics in the reader’s understanding.

At this point in publication order, we do not yet know All For One, the vestiges, Yoichi, or the history of coercive accumulation. We should not retroactively pretend that Volume 1 explains the full metaphysics.

What it does establish is sufficient:

> **this power is not self-created and not simply discovered. It exists through voluntary transfer.**

Even before the name One For All receives its later philosophical depth, its first meaning is relational.

The ten-month training sequence is equally important.

All Might does not merely increase Midoriya’s strength. He gives him a public-service task: clear the accumulated trash from Dagobah Municipal Beach Park.

This is not incidental training scenery.

Before Midoriya receives superhuman power, his preparation for heroism literally consists of **restoring neglected common space through ordinary labor**.

The work is exhausting, unglamorous, repetitive, and publicly useful.

That makes Chapter 2 a quiet ethical bridge:

> heroic power must be carried by a body built through service.

The cleaned beach also provides a visual before/after model of agency. Midoriya transforms an environment before OFA ever transforms a villain.

His overtraining reveals the earliest form of a long-running defect: he does not merely work hard; he exceeds the prescribed plan because he treats self-expenditure as proof of seriousness. All Might has to tell him that collapsing before the exam defeats the point.

This is the first warning against the Dark Deku logic, decades of publication time before the solo arc.

The hair-ingestion gag deliberately punctures the sacredness of inheritance. OFA is grand in ethical structure and ridiculous in transfer mechanism. That tonal mixture keeps succession physical rather than abstract.

## Movement V — U.A. tests what cannot be openly scored: pages 87–130

The entrance exam introduces institutional heroism.

Its surface metric is combat points.

Present Mic explains villain robots, point values, and the zero-pointer whose “merit is none.” The visible logic rewards destruction.

Midoriya, already socially conditioned to assume his worth must be proven through performance, becomes trapped by the score logic and fails to accumulate points.

The zero-pointer then creates a situation where the stated incentive says: leave.

Uraraka is endangered.

Midoriya moves.

This repeats Chapter 1 but under institutional observation.

The crucial difference is that this time he possesses enormous power. Yet the moral sequence is unchanged:

1. someone is in danger;
2. there is no apparent reward for intervening;
3. he acts before optimizing for himself;
4. the action destroys his body;
5. another person must save him in return.

Uraraka’s role must be emphasized more strongly than in the first pass.

She first interrupts Midoriya’s nervous isolation before the exam. During the zero-pointer crisis she physically prevents his fatal fall. Afterward, when she believes he may have received zero points, she attempts to give him some of hers.

Her question — whether her points can be shared with “that person” — is important because she does not yet know Midoriya well. She is responding to witnessed conduct.

In other words, the volume repeats its recognition structure:

> **Midoriya acts → another person revises what he means to them → that person acts because of him.**

U.A.’s hidden rescue points then institutionalize that process.

The institution’s moral message is admirable but not uncomplicated.

On the positive side, U.A. explicitly rejects the idea that hero value can be reduced to combat output. A candidate can enter the country’s most prestigious hero school through rescue action.

But U.A. still converts unquantified virtue into a score after the fact. The exam therefore reveals an institutional tension that will recur throughout the series:

> **heroism resists measurement, yet institutions need measurements in order to select, rank, license, and distribute opportunity.**

The rescue-point system is the first benevolent example of that problem.

The acceptance message’s formulation — **「君の行動は人を動かした」** — is one of Volume 1’s most important lines.

It defines Midoriya’s power before OFA mastery as social causation.

His action moves All Might.

His action moves Uraraka.

The institution notices that capacity.

Later the whole series will move toward distributed heroism; Volume 1 already defines a hero partly as someone whose conduct makes other people act.

## Movement VI — Moral impulse meets professional discipline: pages 131–170

Admission is not the ending.

Aizawa’s quirk-apprehension test immediately strips away ceremonial triumph.

His pedagogy is severe and deliberately anti-romantic. He treats “potential” as something that must become observable professional viability. His threat to expel the student with the lowest performance is presented as part of U.A.’s freedom and as a way of imposing rational pressure.

Midoriya’s problem becomes obvious: if using OFA disables him, then the power cannot yet function as hero power.

Aizawa’s objection is therefore deeper than “you scored badly.”

He reads self-destruction as operational failure.

That is one of the earliest and strongest corrections to the idea that sacrifice automatically equals heroism.

Midoriya initially repeats the entrance-exam model: break the body to produce one decisive act.

Aizawa forces adaptation.

The finger throw is still self-injury, so we should not exaggerate it into mature mastery. But it marks a conceptual shift:

> **heroic intent begins learning proportionality.**

Midoriya asks not simply how to produce maximum force but how to preserve enough of himself to continue acting.

This is why Aizawa matters philosophically from his first appearance. He represents a form of care that feels harsh because it refuses to romanticize an unsustainable rescuer.

All Might is emotionally certain Midoriya deserves a chance.

Aizawa asks whether the boy can remain functional after using that chance.

Both are necessary.

Bakugo’s response to Midoriya’s newly visible power then converts an old social hierarchy into personal crisis. From his perspective, the supposedly quirkless “Deku” has concealed or acquired something powerful enough to threaten the status order on which Bakugo built his self-conception.

This is not yet the mature guilt later volumes reveal, but the first volume already shows that Midoriya’s existence violates Bakugo’s equation:

> strong = superior = heroic legitimacy.

## Movement VII — Naming, school identity, and the threshold of professional heroism: pages 171–189

Chapter 7 is transitional, but it should not be treated as disposable setup.

First, the class social environment begins forming.

Uraraka’s response to “Deku” performs an important act of linguistic re-authoring. The word has functioned as Bakugo’s diminutive insult, linked to uselessness and incapacity. Uraraka hears it differently, associating it with something like “頑張れ” — effort, doing, encouragement.

The semantic history of the word does not disappear.

What changes is who has the authority to assign meaning to it.

That will matter enormously when Midoriya later adopts “Deku” as a hero name.

Second, the introduction of battle costumes marks the transition from students with quirks to students beginning to author professional heroic identities.

Costume, name, body, and quirk will become a recurring *MHA* grammar. The first volume ends almost exactly at the threshold where private aspiration is being converted into public role.

That is structurally appropriate.

Volume 1 begins with a child watching heroes on a screen.

It ends with that child walking toward hero training in costume.

But the intervening chapters have made clear that the transformation is not fantasy fulfillment alone. It requires recognition, labor, institutional selection, bodily discipline, peer relationship, and the painful revision of what “power” means.

# 3. Character-state ledger

## Midoriya Izuku / 緑谷出久

### Desire
To become a hero who saves people with the reassuring joy he associates with All Might.

### Fear
That quirklessness is not merely a physical limitation but proof that his dream is socially and practically illegitimate.

### Self-conception
At the beginning he thinks of himself as someone whose heroic desire may be childish fantasy. He has internalized low rank sufficiently that Bakugo’s contempt can still organize his emotional posture. By the end, he has received external evidence that his conduct is genuinely heroic, but he has not yet built a stable identity around that evidence.

### Relation to power
Initially, power is what he lacks and studies from the outside. He admires power because of what All Might does with it, not because domination itself attracts him. After OFA, power becomes entrusted responsibility — but still something external, explosive, and physically alien.

### Current contradiction
He possesses an unusually strong rescue reflex but also a dangerous tendency to treat his own body as disposable evidence of sincerity.

### Important change
The volume transforms the question from:

> “Can a quirkless boy be a hero?”

into:

> “Can a boy whose heroic instinct has been recognized learn to embody extraordinary power without destroying himself?”

### Full-series hindsight
The opening volume already contains both halves of his entire arc:

- the virtue: he moves when someone needs help;
- the danger: he believes moving is worth any cost to himself.

Dark Deku does not invent the martyr complex. It radicalizes a structure visible from Volume 1.

---

## All Might / オールマイト

### Desire
To preserve public reassurance and continue saving despite a body that can no longer sustain the role indefinitely.

### Fear
Failure of the Symbol; becoming unable to meet the expectation attached to his image; allowing fear to become visible.

### Self-conception
He understands himself as someone who must not visibly yield to evil. His heroic identity is public and performative without therefore being false.

### Relation to power
Power is rescue, deterrence, reassurance, and burden. His body reveals that power’s cost even when his speech still treats the Symbol as necessary.

### Current contradiction
He tells Midoriya to face reality, then is forced by Midoriya’s action to admit that his own professional realism had missed the quality he claims heroism fundamentally requires.

### Important change
He moves from idol to mentor, and from evaluator to person capable of revising his evaluation.

### Full-series hindsight
The Symbol of Peace’s fragility is present before the political consequences of that fragility become visible. Volume 1 establishes the hidden bodily and emotional cost; later volumes will reveal the social cost of concentrating so much public security in one body.

---

## Bakugo Katsuki / 爆豪勝己

### Desire
To confirm that he belongs at the top of the hierarchy and is destined to become an elite hero.

### Fear
At this point it is not articulated as fear, but his behavior suggests extreme sensitivity to any evidence that someone he ranks beneath himself may possess moral or practical superiority.

### Self-conception
Exceptional by nature. His powerful quirk, praise from classmates, and educational expectations all reinforce this.

### Relation to power
Power means proof: proof of rank, superiority, future legitimacy, and personal identity.

### Current contradiction
He wants the social role “hero” but already violates one of the volume’s central heroic virtues: the ability to recognize value in someone weaker and to accept help without interpreting it as humiliation.

### Important change
Midoriya’s acquisition of visible power destabilizes a hierarchy Bakugo had treated as natural.

### Full-series hindsight
The later apology becomes more meaningful because Volume 1 shows that Bakugo’s cruelty is not merely temper. It is organized around a theory of worth in which weakness should know its place. His eventual capacity to name that distortion is a genuine philosophical reversal.

---

## Uraraka Ochako / 麗日お茶子

### Desire
Not fully developed yet, but she wants entry to U.A. and is socially open in ways Midoriya’s prior environment was not.

### Relation to heroism
Her first major function is reciprocal rescue.

She helps Midoriya before she has reason to gain from it. She later tries to share points because she believes his action deserves institutional recognition.

### Important change
She becomes Midoriya’s first peer-level counterexample to the social world represented by Bakugo’s middle-school circle.

### Full-series hindsight
Her later question — who saves heroes? — has a miniature prototype here. Midoriya saves her, she saves him, and then she tries to save his future by reallocating points.

---

## Iida Tenya / 飯田天哉

### Initial state
He mistakes Midoriya’s anxious muttering and attempted contact with Uraraka for unseriousness and possible interference.

### Change
During the entrance exam, he concludes that Midoriya perceived the “true nature” of the test before he did.

### Significance
Iida participates in the volume’s recognition-revision pattern. Formal seriousness initially causes misrecognition; observed action corrects it.

This is a valuable early warning against equating rule-consciousness with complete moral perception.

---

## Aizawa Shota / 相澤消太

### Desire
To produce students capable of functioning under the extreme demands of hero work.

### Relation to power
Power is only meaningful when usable under operational constraints.

### Pedagogical philosophy
Anti-sentimental, high-discretion, “rational” pressure.

### Important function
He is the first adult after All Might to challenge Midoriya from the opposite direction.

All Might says:

> your conduct makes you worthy of power.

Aizawa says, effectively:

> worthiness does not excuse incompetence in using it.

The complete series needs both propositions.

---

## Midoriya Inko / 緑谷引子

Inko receives relatively little page time, but her emotional function is major.

After the quirkless diagnosis, young Izuku replays footage of All Might and implicitly asks for confirmation that he can still become like him. Inko’s response is grief:

> **「ごめんね出久」**

Midoriya later remembers that what he wanted was not an apology but affirmation.

This should not be read as maternal cruelty. Inko is frightened for her child and responding to the social reality she believes he faces.

But it is still a recognition failure.

The person who loves him most cannot yet imagine a path in which his desire remains viable.

With series hindsight, that scene becomes one of the cleanest early examples of *MHA*’s claim that love alone is not sufficient. A person can care deeply and still fail to see what someone needs to hear.

# 4. Power philosophies

| Character | Power means | Power permits | Power obligates | Current contradiction |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Midoriya | the capacity to save | intervention he previously could only imagine | service, responsibility, self-discipline | he still spends his body as though sacrifice proves worth |
| All Might | rescue + reassurance + deterrence | becoming a public pillar | never visibly yielding to fear/evil | the pillar is physically unsustainable |
| Bakugo | superiority, rank, proof | social dominance and future hero legitimacy | winning | he confuses strength with moral entitlement |
| Uraraka | practical capacity to help | reciprocal aid | using ability for people, not only score | still underdeveloped in V1 |
| Iida | ordered competence | legitimate action inside systems | discipline, seriousness | procedure can initially misread people |
| Aizawa | usable professional capability | high-risk hero work | efficiency, survivability, adaptability | rationality can become severe sorting |
| U.A. | measurable potential | access to professional formation | reward both combat and rescue | heroism resists the metrics used to select it |

Volume 1’s most important power argument is that **possession is not legitimacy**.

Bakugo possesses power but has not yet developed the relational ethics the volume treats as heroic.

Midoriya lacks power but demonstrates those ethics.

All Might possesses vast power but momentarily fails to act because his body and professional situation constrain him.

Uraraka’s modest quirk becomes lifesaving because she uses it at exactly the right moment.

The volume therefore refuses a simple power ranking almost immediately.

# 5. Hero Society / institutional analysis

Volume 1 already contains more social-system material than the first pass preserved.

## 5.1 Quirk society converts bodily difference into expectation

The opening says people are not born equal, but the relevant inequality is not only biological. It becomes socially meaningful because institutions and peers interpret powers as forecasts of future value.

The middle-school classroom is the clearest example.

Students publicly display quirks, discuss hero tracks, and treat Bakugo’s trajectory as plausible because his quirk is spectacular.

Midoriya’s U.A. application is exposed and mocked.

The teacher participates in the atmosphere instead of neutralizing it.

This suggests that **hero labor-market sorting begins culturally long before formal licensing**.

## 5.2 Professional heroism is a labor system, not generic altruism

All Might’s initial refusal distinguishes wanting to help from being able to survive professional hero work.

He points to police work as another honorable path.

This matters because the story does not claim that only licensed heroes perform socially valuable work. “Hero” is a specific professional-social role with exceptional bodily demands.

## 5.3 The sludge scene shows a coordination failure, not simple cowardice

The pros hesitate partly because their quirks are poorly matched.

That is a realistic institutional problem:

- specialization increases capability;
- specialization also produces situations where everyone waits for the appropriately specialized responder.

The bystander problem is therefore partly created by professionalization.

When help becomes a profession, ordinary people may come to assume that intervention belongs to professionals.

Volume 1 does not articulate that thesis politically, but the image is already present.

## 5.4 U.A. encodes competing theories of merit

The entrance exam appears combat-meritocratic.

Rescue points reveal a hidden normative correction.

Aizawa’s test then adds another correction: noble rescue that makes a student unusable is not enough.

So U.A. is not ideologically simple.

It simultaneously values:

- destructive effectiveness;
- prosocial action;
- adaptability;
- survivability;
- and measurable performance.

Later arcs will reveal the problems created by hero education, but Volume 1 does not support a blanket “U.A. is corrupt” reading. It presents a demanding institution sincerely trying to select and develop heroic capability, albeit through opaque and severe methods.

# 6. Recognition and failed rescue

This is arguably the most important second-pass lens for Volume 1.

## 6.1 Failed recognition: diagnosis and family

The doctor correctly identifies quirklessness.

The factual diagnosis becomes existential because the social world treats quirk possession as part of hero eligibility.

Inko’s apology confirms that interpretation emotionally.

The child is not only told what his body lacks. He learns what society believes that lack means for his future.

## 6.2 Failed recognition: school

Bakugo and the class can see Midoriya’s fascination with heroes, but they interpret it as delusion rather than vocation.

This is not invisibility. It is **misrecognition**.

They see the desire and classify it as ridiculous.

## 6.3 Failed recognition: All Might

All Might initially sees Midoriya’s body and professional constraints more clearly than he sees Midoriya’s heroic potential.

Again, this is not malicious neglect.

It is an incomplete reading of a person.

## 6.4 Recognition through action: sludge crisis

Midoriya recognizes Bakugo’s face as a request for help.

This is especially significant because Bakugo is not verbally asking Midoriya to save him.

Recognition precedes invitation.

Midoriya’s intervention then causes All Might to recognize Midoriya.

Recognition becomes contagious.

## 6.5 Recognition through action: entrance exam

Uraraka and Iida both revise their perception of Midoriya after seeing what he does.

U.A. converts this into formal recognition through rescue points.

The volume therefore repeatedly stages the same sequence:

> **social label → misrecognition → crisis → observed action → revised recognition**

This is far more central to Volume 1 than the first pass made explicit.

# 7. Villain formation, rescue, and accountability

Volume 1 contains little basis for a developed villain-formation theory.

The sludge villain is primarily a crisis mechanism rather than a psychologically elaborated antagonist.

Important findings are therefore mostly about **rescue conditions**, not villain ontology.

The villain’s function is to expose:

- professional specialization limits;
- public spectatorship;
- Bakugo’s vulnerability despite power;
- Midoriya’s rescue reflex;
- All Might’s bodily limit;
- and the difference between seeing danger and moving toward it.

**Hypothesis 5 — villains are often shaped by failed recognition before criminalization — receives no meaningful evidence here.**

That absence should be preserved rather than forcing later villain themes backward onto an antagonist who does not support them.

# 8. Japanese language, character voice, and key vocabulary

## 8.1 個性 and 無個性

The social vocabulary of the series is immediately classificatory.

**個性** literally has the ordinary meaning of individuality/personality, but in this setting it is institutionalized as the word for superhuman ability.

That creates an important semantic irony:

> Midoriya is **無個性** in the technical sense while being extremely distinctive in moral temperament.

The series does not need to state the irony explicitly for it to matter.

At the same time, we should avoid overreading: within the setting, 無個性 is simply the established category for a person without a quirk. The literary resonance becomes meaningful because the narrative juxtaposes classification with character.

## 8.2 救ける / 救け

Horikoshi repeatedly uses rescue language at the decisive moral moments.

The sludge scene gives the unusually concentrated formulation:

> **「君が救けを求める顔してた」**

What matters is not only danger but the perceived request embedded in another person’s face.

The series’ rescue ethic is therefore facial and relational before it becomes doctrinal.

## 8.3 受け継ぐ / 受け取る / 値する

All Might frames OFA through a cluster of succession words:

- **受け継ぐ** — inherit/carry on;
- **受け取る** — receive;
- **値する** — be worthy/deserving of.

This is strong evidence for the “entrustment” side of the OFA hypothesis.

However, Volume 1 alone does not establish the full OFA/AFO opposition, because AFO’s philosophy has not entered the text.

## 8.4 夢 and 現実

All Might’s initial response to Midoriya creates a recurring tension between dream and reality.

He does not say dreaming is itself wrong. He says reality must be faced proportionately.

The irony is that the sludge incident changes what reality contains.

Midoriya’s heroic potential was real before anyone had a category for it.

## 8.5 見込み

Aizawa’s pedagogical vocabulary centers potential/prospect — **見込み**.

That term links school selection to forecasting.

The teacher’s job is not only to measure what students are now but to decide who is worth further institutional investment.

That logic will matter greatly in later education and hero-labor analysis.

## 8.6 デク

“Deku” begins as Bakugo’s imposed diminishment.

Uraraka’s reinterpretation at the end of the volume demonstrates linguistic agency: a stigmatizing name can be re-authored through relationship.

This is not complete reclamation yet. Midoriya remains startled by the alternative meaning.

But the process begins here.

## 8.7 Voice contrast

Midoriya’s speech is heavily marked by hesitation, muttering, self-correction, and analytical overflow.

Bakugo’s is imperative, insulting, and explosive.

All Might’s public/heroic voice is declarative and theatrical; his private diminished form is looser, more candid, and more physically vulnerable.

Aizawa’s speech is clipped, evaluative, and anti-theatrical.

The series begins building professional philosophies through voice before those philosophies become explicit themes.

# 9. Visual and formal analysis

## 9.1 The opening visual grammar: unequal bodies

Page 5 does not present “inequality” abstractly.

Young Midoriya is small, tearful, and surrounded by children confidently displaying visible quirks.

The page establishes the future protagonist as a spectator within his own generation.

The final panel’s narration frames this as social reality.

That visual idea — the protagonist on the edge of other people’s power — makes the later inheritance feel structurally different from a normal power-awakening story.

## 9.2 Crowds as moral environment

The most important recurring visual motif in Chapter 1 is the crowd.

At school, Midoriya is socially isolated against a collective that finds his ambition ridiculous.

At the sludge incident, a crowd gathers behind professional responders and watches Bakugo suffocate.

Page 47 turns this into a moral composition: Midoriya’s face and forward movement cut across the static mass behind him.

The later series’ interest in spectatorship, public opinion, civilian dependency, and ordinary intervention is visually seeded here.

## 9.3 Eyes as rescue signals

Bakugo’s eye through the sludge is given disproportionate visual attention.

Midoriya’s recognition comes through the face before any explicit request.

That establishes a recurring *MHA* grammar in which seeing the person inside the crisis is part of heroic action.

With later hindsight, this resonates strongly with Tenko/Shigaraki, Eri, Todoroki, and Toga — but the volume itself only establishes the underlying visual logic, not those later applications.

## 9.4 All Might’s double body

The transition from monumental All Might to skeletal Toshinori is visually comic and disturbing.

His public body occupies space confidently.

His private body is narrow, wounded, and often framed with blank space emphasizing depletion.

The Symbol is therefore visually constructed as something a vulnerable body must produce.

This is early evidence for the later critique of singular symbolism.

## 9.5 Page 59: recognition as vertical monument

The page dominated by **「君はヒーローになれる」** transforms a private conversation in a rainy alley into a monumental image.

The phrase is visually larger than the characters.

This is exactly what Midoriya has needed from childhood: not simply power but authoritative recognition.

The page gives affirmation the scale usually reserved for a finishing move.

That is an important formal statement about what the story treats as consequential.

## 9.6 The beach as before/after body

Dagobah’s garbage field mirrors Midoriya’s unprepared body: overloaded, unusable, requiring slow restructuring.

The cleaned beach and trained body emerge together.

The transformation is not instantaneous and not glamorous.

The montage grounds inherited power in labor.

## 9.7 Zero-pointer spectacle versus rescue intent

The zero-pointer is enormous, designed to dominate the page and the examinees’ attention.

Midoriya’s punch is correspondingly spectacular.

But the ethical center is visually elsewhere: Uraraka trapped beneath rubble.

The spectacle is justified by relation.

The large action image matters because a small human need produces it.

## 9.8 Falling and reciprocal rescue

After Midoriya’s punch, the visual joke becomes moral structure: the rescuer is helplessly falling.

Uraraka touches him and removes gravity.

The sequence prevents one-way savior imagery.

His heroism creates the conditions for somebody else to rescue him.

This is one of the earliest formal prototypes of the series’ eventual rejection of solitary martyrdom.

## 9.9 Aizawa’s capture weapon and institutional restraint

Aizawa’s scarf repeatedly enters panels as a line of control.

Where All Might’s visual language is expansion and impact, Aizawa’s is binding, interruption, and redirection.

His visual role matches his pedagogy: stop wasteful motion, suppress dangerous power, force thought.

## 9.10 Costumes and authored public bodies

The closing pages’ costume reveal turns quirks into designed identities.

Bakugo’s aggressive silhouette, Uraraka’s rounded suit, Iida’s armor, and Midoriya’s emerging costume language make professional heroism visibly mediated.

A hero does not merely possess a body and quirk. The hero becomes a public visual object.

That will later matter for branding, symbolism, masks, exposure, and identity.

# 10. Body, embodiment, family, and inheritance

Volume 1 is extraordinarily bodily.

## Midoriya’s quirkless body

His body initially functions as an exclusion criterion.

The doctor’s toe-joint explanation gives the social judgment medical form.

That diagnosis is factual within the setting, but its narrative function is to show how a body becomes destiny through interpretation.

## All Might’s wounded body

All Might’s injury reveals that even the ideal heroic body is finite.

The public symbol is built on concealment of damage.

The body pays for social reassurance.

## OFA as bodily inheritance

The transfer through DNA makes succession material.

Power is not an abstract title; it enters flesh.

But Midoriya’s body cannot yet contain it safely.

That mismatch establishes one of the series’ central embodiment problems:

> **receiving an inheritance is not the same as being ready to live inside it.**

## Training and bodily authorship

Midoriya spends ten months changing his body before receiving the power.

That matters ethically because his worthiness is not a substitute for effort.

All Might’s choice opens a path; Midoriya must materially build the capacity to walk it.

## Self-injury

The entrance exam and Aizawa test reveal the danger of converting bodily destruction into proof of heroism.

Aizawa’s critique is correct: a one-use hero is not operationally sufficient.

The series will spend many volumes teaching Midoriya that preserving his body is not selfishness.

## Family inheritance versus chosen inheritance

Inko gives Midoriya life and love but cannot give him the quirk or social validation his dream seems to require.

All Might gives him a chosen inheritance unrelated to blood.

This establishes a major series distinction very early:

> inheritance can be biological, but it can also be voluntary, ethical, and relational.

# 12. Secondary-character findings

This section produces some of the clearest second-pass gains.

## Uraraka is not merely the first friendly girl

Her Volume 1 function is philosophically specific.

She is the first peer who treats Midoriya’s awkwardness without contempt, physically rescues him, and then attempts to use her own institutional capital — exam points — to protect his future.

That makes her an early model of **reciprocal heroism**.

## Iida is not merely comic rule enforcement

His misreading and revision establish a contrast between formal seriousness and moral perception.

He can be wrong because he follows visible procedure too literally, but he is capable of revising himself quickly when evidence changes.

That capacity matters to his later growth.

## Inko is an early failed recognizer

Her love does not automatically generate the right answer.

This is important because *MHA*’s later social ethic is not “good people always know what to do.” It is that people must become willing to see and act differently.

## Aizawa is an early anti-martyr mentor

His harshness makes more sense in full-series context.

He is not attacking Midoriya’s moral instinct. He is challenging a form of heroism that cannot survive repeated use.

That distinction becomes extremely important later.

# 13. Prospective versus retrospective reading

## At this point in publication order

A careful reader can reasonably conclude:

- Midoriya possesses heroic instincts before possessing power.
- All Might sees those instincts and chooses him as successor.
- OFA is a transferable quirk based on voluntary inheritance.
- Midoriya must build a body capable of carrying it.
- U.A. values rescue as well as combat.
- Aizawa values practical sustainability over dramatic self-sacrifice.
- Bakugo’s power-centered arrogance makes Midoriya uniquely threatening to him.
- Uraraka and Iida are beginning to provide Midoriya with a healthier peer environment.
- hero work is socially institutionalized, physically dangerous, and highly competitive.

A reader **cannot yet** responsibly conclude:

- the full OFA/AFO moral opposition;
- the historical vestige architecture;
- Shigaraki/Tenko as the central failed-rescue case;
- the full political consequences of the Symbol of Peace;
- the final shift toward distributed heroism;
- the HPSC’s covert violence;
- heteromorph discrimination;
- or the ending’s ordinary-intervention thesis in its mature form.

## With full-series hindsight

Volume 1 becomes much denser.

The sludge scene looks like the primitive version of the entire late-series social argument:

> a vulnerable person is visible; authorized actors are constrained; spectators wait; one person moves; that movement changes what everyone else can do.

All Might’s hidden wound becomes the first sign that singular symbolism consumes the symbol-bearer.

Aizawa’s criticism becomes an early rejection of the martyr logic Midoriya will later take to dangerous extremes.

Uraraka’s rescue of Midoriya becomes an early prototype of her later insistence that heroes themselves must be allowed to receive protection.

Bakugo’s hostility becomes the beginning of a very long dismantling of the belief that strength confers superior human worth.

OFA’s language of receiving and inheriting becomes the first half of a later opposition between **entrustment and possession**.

The crucial retrospective gain is therefore not that Volume 1 “secretly contains the entire ending.”

It is that the ending radicalizes moral structures that were already present in simple form.

# 14. What the first pass missed or underweighted

The first pass got the single most important macro-thesis right:

> **Midoriya is heroic before he is powerful.**

That remains strongly supported.

The second pass, however, reveals several important omissions or compressions.

## 14.1 Underweighted: All Might first says no

The earlier synthesis tended to remember All Might primarily as the adult who recognizes Midoriya.

Volume 1 is more interesting.

All Might first fails to recognize the possibility Midoriya needs affirmed. He gives a professionally reasonable but emotionally devastating answer.

His later affirmation is therefore an evidence-based moral revision.

This makes All Might a better mentor and a more human character than an always-correct oracle.

## 14.2 Underweighted: recognition is the real structural engine

The volume is built from repeated judgment revisions:

- Inko cannot see a path;
- school sees delusion;
- All Might sees impracticality;
- All Might revises;
- Uraraka revises social meaning;
- Iida revises his judgment;
- U.A. formally recognizes rescue;
- Aizawa revises from “hopeless self-destruction” to “possibly adaptable.”

The series’ later recognition thesis is already structural here.

## 14.3 Missed/underweighted: Hero Society’s hierarchy begins before U.A.

The middle-school classroom is not neutral background.

It is a social sorting institution that publicly links quirk quality to future prestige.

Bakugo’s worldview is socially cultivated as well as personally chosen.

## 14.4 Underweighted: All Might’s smile is already a burden technology

Page 38 gives one of the earliest and clearest statements that the Symbol’s smile is used to manage internal fear and external expectation.

The Symbol of Peace is therefore already a psychologically costly performance.

## 14.5 Underweighted: the sludge scene is about coordination and spectatorship

The professionals are not simply cowards.

Their quirks are badly matched, and they wait for the right responder.

That makes the scene more institutionally interesting: specialization and professional responsibility can create paralysis.

## 14.6 Underweighted: Uraraka’s point-sharing request

Her attempt to transfer her points is an important act of recognition.

Midoriya’s action has already changed another person’s conduct before he is even admitted to U.A.

## 14.7 Underweighted: rescue is reciprocal from the beginning

Midoriya saves Uraraka from the zero-pointer.

Uraraka saves Midoriya from the fall.

The rescuer requiring rescue is not a late-series invention.

## 14.8 Underweighted: Aizawa’s critique of self-sacrifice

The first pass correctly identified Midoriya’s self-destructive OFA use, but Volume 1 gives it a sharper institutional judgment than the broad synthesis preserved.

Aizawa does not praise noble sacrifice. He treats incapacitating power use as a serious defect.

## 14.9 Underweighted: Dagobah cleanup as pre-power hero labor

Midoriya’s first sustained “heroic” labor after being chosen is not combat.

It is cleaning a public beach.

That should be preserved as a small but meaningful counterweight to reading *MHA* entirely through battles.

## 14.10 Refinement: Midoriya’s lack of hatred should not be romanticized

The earlier analysis emphasized that Bakugo’s bullying does not become revenge.

That remains true.

But Volume 1 also shows how deeply Bakugo’s treatment has conditioned Midoriya:

- he flinches;
- he self-minimizes;
- he expects contempt;
- he interprets social situations through low status;
- and “Deku” has been imposed on his identity.

The absence of revenge is morally important, but it should not be mistaken for absence of injury.

# 16. Revised interpretation

The mature second-pass reading of Volume 1 is:

> **Volume 1 is a story about how heroic possibility becomes legible.**

Midoriya’s core virtue is indeed prior to power, but the volume’s real architecture is not internal virtue alone. It is the interaction between a person and a series of social judgments.

His dream passes through:

- medical classification;
- family fear;
- peer ridicule;
- educational hierarchy;
- professional realism;
- crisis recognition;
- mentorship;
- bodily labor;
- institutional testing;
- peer reciprocity;
- and professional discipline.

That sequence matters because *MHA* does not treat “believe in yourself” as sufficient.

Midoriya needs somebody to tell him he can become a hero.

But then he needs to train.

He needs power.

But then he needs to learn not to destroy himself with it.

He needs U.A. to recognize rescue.

But then he needs Aizawa to tell him that rescue without sustainability is not enough.

The volume’s ethic is therefore neither pure inspirationalism nor pure meritocracy.

It is closer to:

> **A humane society must recognize forms of worth that existing metrics miss — and then give those forms of worth the discipline, resources, and structure necessary to become effective.**

That is a much richer foundation for the series than “quirkless boy gets lucky.”

- Midoriya is medically classified as quirkless.

- Midoriya trains for ten months before receiving OFA.
- Midoriya attacks the zero-pointer to save Uraraka despite the robot carrying no score value.
- Uraraka saves Midoriya from his fall.
- Uraraka asks whether she can give Midoriya some of her points.
- U.A. awards rescue points.

- Uraraka reinterprets the sound/meaning of “Deku” more positively.

## Strongly supported inference

- Midoriya’s analytical habit is partly an adaptation to exclusion from embodied participation.
- Bakugo’s superiority complex is socially reinforced by the school environment, not generated only from private personality.
- All Might’s famous recognition is more meaningful because it is a revision after initially misreading Midoriya’s potential.
- The sludge scene is an early example of professional specialization contributing to bystander paralysis.
- U.A.’s hidden rescue points reveal tension between institutional measurement and unquantifiable heroic motive.
- Aizawa functions as an early corrective to Midoriya’s martyr tendency.
- Uraraka establishes reciprocal rescue as part of the series’ moral grammar from the beginning.
- The volume repeatedly uses observed action as the mechanism by which people revise social labels.

## Plausible interpretation

- Dagobah cleanup deliberately frames civic maintenance as a pre-superpower form of hero labor.
- The opening use of 無個性 creates a meaningful irony between technical “quirklessness” and Midoriya’s strong personal distinctiveness.
- The large visual scale of “君はヒーローになれる” formally treats recognition as an event comparable in importance to combat spectacle.
- The volume’s spectatorship compositions are the earliest visual seed of the epilogue’s ordinary-intervention ethic.

## Speculative / unresolved

- How far Horikoshi had already conceptualized the final distributed-heroism endpoint when staging the Chapter 1 crowd cannot be established from Volume 1.
- Volume 1 alone cannot establish whether the Symbol of Peace is socially destabilizing rather than merely personally costly.
- Volume 1 alone cannot establish the later OFA/AFO metaphysical opposition beyond the voluntary-transfer side of OFA.

# 20. Forward continuity watchlist

The reread should actively test the following after Volume 1:

1. **Midoriya’s self-sacrifice:** Does he learn that preserving himself is part of saving others, or merely find more efficient ways to injure himself?
2. **Bakugo’s hierarchy:** When does he begin to distinguish victory from superiority and help from insult?
3. **All Might’s symbol:** How does the private cost established here become a social/institutional problem?
4. **OFA vocabulary:** How do 受け継ぐ, 託す, 与える, 奪う, and related terms evolve once AFO and the vestiges enter the text?
5. **Recognition before certification:** Which later characters are misread because their power, appearance, family, or social category makes them illegible?
6. **Spectatorship:** How often does the series repeat the structure “someone suffers visibly while others wait”?
7. **Uraraka reciprocity:** Does her early rescue of Midoriya develop into a distinct philosophy of protecting rescuers?
8. **Iida and procedure:** When does formal duty help him, and when does it fail to contain emotion or revenge?
9. **Aizawa’s pedagogy:** Is his severe rationality consistently protective, or does it reproduce harmful institutional sorting?
10. **Deku as name:** At what point does Midoriya fully author the insult into a chosen public identity?
11. **Hero metrics:** How do rankings, points, licenses, scouts, and professional prestige continue the tension between measurable ability and moral worth?
12. **Ordinary labor:** Does the series continue to value noncombat maintenance, care, support, evacuation, and social repair alongside spectacular fighting?
13. **Bodies as inheritance sites:** How do injury, scars, quirk mismatch, inherited bodies, and support equipment complicate the idea that power can simply be “given”?
14. **Recognition as predation:** When the series introduces AFO, compare All Might’s evidence-responsive recognition of potential with predatory recognition of vulnerability.

# 21. What this volume changes in the current MHA model

Volume 1 **strengthens** the first pass’s central thesis but **changes its explanatory center**.

The earlier model was:

> Midoriya proves that heroism precedes power.

The second-pass model should now be:

> **Midoriya proves that heroism precedes power, but Volume 1 is equally about whether people and institutions can recognize that heroism before conventional markers of ability make it obvious.**

That refinement matters because it connects the protagonist’s origin directly to the series’ eventual social argument.

The problem is not only that some people lack power.

The problem is that societies build shortcuts for deciding:

- who has potential;
- who is worth investing in;
- who should intervene;
- whose dream is realistic;
- who deserves protection;
- and whose visible suffering is somebody else’s responsibility.

Midoriya’s first great heroic act breaks one of those shortcuts.

He has no suitable quirk.

He has no license.

He has no tactical solution.

He does not even successfully rescue Bakugo by himself.

But he **sees**, he **moves**, and his movement changes what a stronger person does next.

That is the deepest Volume 1 foundation for the complete series:

> **Heroism begins not when one possesses enough power to solve everything, but when another person’s need becomes sufficient reason to move — and mature hero society must learn how to recognize, support, discipline, and eventually distribute that impulse without turning any one rescuer into a disposable symbol.**
