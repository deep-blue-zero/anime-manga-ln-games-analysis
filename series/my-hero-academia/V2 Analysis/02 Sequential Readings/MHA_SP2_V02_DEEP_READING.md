# 1. Central thesis and volume role

Volume 1 asked whether heroic possibility can be recognized before conventional power makes it socially legible. Volume 2 assumes that recognition has occurred and asks the harder professional question:

> **Once a person has power, status, and permission to act, what makes the use of that power heroic rather than merely forceful?**

The volume's answer is **judgment**.

Heroism is no longer tested only through willingness to move. It is tested through the ability to determine:

- when action is necessary;
- when escalation is excessive;
- what risk to another person is acceptable;
- whether winning actually means performing the task well;
- when one should lead and when one should defer;
- how to preserve one's own future usefulness;
- how to coordinate different people rather than centering oneself;
- how to distinguish simulated danger from real lethal danger;
- how dangerous Quirks should be ethically directed;
- and whether power can remain protective when institutional systems fail.

The deepest structural movement is therefore:

> **heroic disposition → professional judgment → real crisis**

This is why the indoor battle trial and USJ belong in the same volume. The first half gives students artificial hero/villain roles inside a bounded exercise. The second half places them in an artificial disaster facility intended for rescue training, only for literal villains to invade it. The manga moves from **role-played villainy inside a simulation** to **actual lethal enemies occupying the simulation itself**.

The progression exposes whether the students have learned anything deeper than genre posture.

Midoriya's Volume 1 lesson was that heroic worth precedes power. His Volume 2 lesson is that good intent does not make power self-justifying. His desire to save must be translated into controlled risk, tactical reasoning, bodily preservation, and collaboration. Bakugo's parallel lesson is harsher: combat superiority does not equal professional superiority, and a person whose identity depends on being naturally above everyone else must decide what to do when reality supplies evidence against that belief.

The volume also makes the All Might problem systemic for the first time. Volume 1 showed the bodily cost of being the Symbol of Peace. Volume 2 explicitly states that society is drawn toward evil if All Might disappears, shows his available activity time shrinking, lets his compulsion to keep rescuing interfere with his scheduled teaching role, and then stages a villain attack designed around his expected presence. The Symbol is genuinely necessary — and that necessity is itself a vulnerability.

A concise second-pass formulation is:

> **Volume 2 is about whether heroic intention can become hero-quality judgment before the role stops being pretend.**

# 2. Narrative architecture

## 2.1 The volume is built from progressively less protected simulations

The volume's three major social spaces form a deliberate sequence.

### A. Indoor battle trial

Students are assigned the signs of heroism and villainy before they possess the corresponding experience. Bakugo and Iida are called the villains; Midoriya and Uraraka are called heroes; a fake nuclear weapon supplies an artificial objective. The exercise is not merely a fight. It is an attempt to make power answer to scenario, rules, role, and objective.

The most important fact is that the nominal winner is not automatically the best performer. Yaoyorozu later identifies Iida as the strongest participant because he most faithfully understood the scenario and acted as though the weapon and defense obligation were real. Midoriya and Uraraka win, but their victory contains tactical looseness and luck. Bakugo can dominate combat exchanges and still perform badly as a professional-in-training.

This is an early institutional refusal to equate:

> **victory = competence = heroism**

### B. Classroom / cafeteria leadership

The next simulation is social rather than combat-oriented. The class must choose a representative. Bakugo immediately wants the role. Iida insists that a vote is the legitimate procedure. Midoriya unexpectedly wins.

Then a false crisis occurs when the press breaches the school perimeter. Formal title is tested against actual capacity. Iida is the person who can turn panic into order. Midoriya notices this and voluntarily gives him the position.

The structure therefore repeats the battle-trial lesson:

> **formal outcome does not necessarily reveal functional competence.**

Midoriya has the votes; Iida demonstrates leadership. Midoriya's response is not to protect his status but to align authority with demonstrated function.

### C. USJ rescue simulation

Thirteen's facility is designed to simulate disasters so students can learn to use Quirks for rescue. Her lecture explicitly names the moral problem: many Quirks can kill people easily. Superhuman society appears stable because their use is licensed and regulated. The point of training is to learn how dangerous capacities can serve human life.

Then Kurogiri opens a portal and literal villains enter.

This is the volume's decisive formal rupture. The word `敵` ceases to be a classroom role and becomes a lethal category. The artificial flood zone becomes an actual combat environment. The students' Quirks cease to be graded demonstrations and become survival tools. The same professional principles remain relevant, but error no longer costs points.

## 2.2 A recurring distinction: status versus function

Volume 2 repeatedly disaggregates titles from actual functions:

- Bakugo has the strongest self-image but not the best battle-trial performance.
- Midoriya wins class presidency but Iida is the better crisis leader.
- All Might is the symbolic center of hero society but cannot physically occupy every role that depends on him.
- Aizawa is a homeroom teacher whose real-crisis competence includes solo combat, command, evacuation planning, and protective sacrifice.
- Thirteen is an elite rescue specialist whose Quirk is also lethally destructive and whose combat matchup has limits.
- Iida's heroic contribution at USJ is not to remain and fight; it is to run away from the battlefield fast enough to bring help.

The mature social model beginning to emerge is not “everyone becomes All Might.” It is:

> **heroic systems work when different people recognize the function that the situation actually requires and perform it reliably.**

## 2.3 The ending preserves, rather than resolves, the Symbol contradiction

The volume spends much of USJ showing why All Might's absence cannot be solved by simply waiting for All Might:

- Aizawa fights.
- Thirteen evacuates and commands.
- Iida is entrusted to obtain outside help.
- Uraraka supports that task.
- Midoriya, Tsuyu, and Mineta construct a local escape plan.
- Todoroki, Momo, Jiro, Kaminari, Bakugo, and Kirishima solve different battlefield problems.

Yet the final pages restore the classic formula:

`もう大丈夫`

`私が`

`来た`

All Might's arrival immediately changes the emotional and tactical field. The manga does not debunk the Symbol. It demonstrates that the Symbol is real, powerful, and socially meaningful while simultaneously showing why no functioning society can safely make him its only reliable answer.

Shigaraki's response — `コンティニューだ` — keeps the contradiction open. For everyone else, All Might's arrival means reassurance. For Shigaraki, it means the game continues.

# 3. Character-state ledger

## 3.1 Midoriya Izuku

### Current state

Volume 2 moves Midoriya from “worthy recipient of One For All” toward **active authorship of inherited power**.

The clearest linguistic marker occurs after the battle trial. Midoriya tells Bakugo that One For All was received from another person, refuses to reveal the giver, and says that one day he will properly make it his own and surpass Bakugo with `“僕の力”` — **“my power.”**

This is a significant transition. He does not reject inheritance. He tries to convert inheritance into embodied ownership through responsibility and mastery.

### Perception and attention

Midoriya's analytic attention broadens from Quirk mechanics to multi-event inference.

At USJ he links the previous media/security incident to the villains' knowledge of the training schedule. In the water zone he reasons about enemy positioning, his team's abilities, and the need to preserve his own arm. Later, after an initial local victory, he explicitly recognizes the risk of overgeneralizing: his team's success against one group had created the illusion that their power broadly “worked” against the enemy force. At the plaza he questions the logic of Shigaraki simply declaring `ゲームオーバー` and retreating if killing All Might was truly the central objective.

This is a useful modeling development: Midoriya is not merely observant. He is especially sensitive to **mismatch between stated objective and observed behavior**.

### Decision policy

The most important new line is his refusal to treat an arm as expendable in the water-zone plan:

> `腕は犠牲に出来ない！`

This is a direct behavioral response to Volume 1's Aizawa problem. Midoriya remains willing to expose himself to danger, but he is beginning to distinguish courageous expenditure from strategically stupid expenditure. He needs his body for the rest of the crisis.

His final charge at Shigaraki/Nomu shows that the rescue reflex remains dominant when another person is in immediate danger. The difference is that the reflex now coexists with a stronger analytical loop.

### Authority and leadership

Midoriya does not appear intrinsically attached to formal status. He is surprised that classmates vote for him as class president. When Iida demonstrates superior crisis leadership, Midoriya immediately argues that Iida should take the role.

This is early evidence for a later-important tendency:

> **Midoriya seeks efficacy and rescue more strongly than positional authority.**

### Relationship with Bakugo

The rivalry becomes more explicit and more reciprocal. Midoriya says he wants to defeat Bakugo **because Bakugo is impressive**, not because he despises him. He also feels compelled to tell Bakugo a partial truth about his Quirk despite the secrecy burden. Bakugo's interpretation matters to him unusually strongly.

That is not healthy relational equality yet. Fear, admiration, injury, and status asymmetry remain. But the relationship can no longer be modeled as simple bully/victim hostility.

### Japanese voice

Midoriya retains hesitation and explanatory overflow in interpersonal conflict, especially around Bakugo and One For All. Under tactical danger, his language can become compressed and internally rapid. Tsuyu's comment that his intense analytical demeanor is a little unsettling is useful low-level social evidence: the habit other characters experience as “muttering” or intensity is not just narrator convenience; it is perceivable behavior.

## 3.2 Bakugo Katsuki

### Current state

Volume 2 is the first major correction to a shallow “Bakugo is angry and arrogant” model.

His aggression toward Midoriya is extreme, but it is **relationship-conditioned**. It is driven by a status theory in which Midoriya was supposed to be beneath him and by a sense that Midoriya's hidden power means he has been deceived or patronized.

He repeatedly demands that Midoriya use the Quirk. His goal is not to defeat a handicapped opponent. He wants to crush `全力のてめェ` — Midoriya at full power — because only that can restore the hierarchy he believes should exist.

### Childhood self-model

Midoriya's recollection is unusually explicit. Bakugo was already the type who could do things well and was already rough and dominant. Quirk manifestation then accelerated those qualities “in a bad direction.” Childhood praise produces a comparative self-model:

> `俺がすげーんだ 皆俺よりすごくない！`

The important point is causal restraint. His Quirk does not create his entire personality. It interacts with preexisting competence, confidence, peer praise, and a social environment that reads powerful Quirks as future status.

### Risk tolerance

The grenade exchange provides a high-value evidence atom. When All Might asks whether Bakugo intends to kill Midoriya, Bakugo answers:

> `当たんなきゃ死なねぇよ！`

This should not be paraphrased as “Bakugo wants Midoriya dead.” It reveals something more useful for modeling: **very high risk tolerance combined with confidence that his own control is sufficient to make the risk acceptable**. He is willing to impose danger because he believes he can manage the lethal boundary.

That is professionally defective, but it is different from homicidal intent.

### Response to defeat and criticism

Bakugo's post-trial breakdown shows an ego that is brittle but not evidence-proof. He admits:

- Midoriya beat him in the exercise;
- Todoroki made him think he might not be able to measure up;
- Yaoyorozu's critique made sense to him.

He cries. The shame is real.

But his recovery style is neither withdrawal nor denial. It is furious recommitment:

> `こっからだ!!`

> `俺はここで 一番になってやる!!!`

This is a core predictive datum. When his self-concept is destabilized by credible evidence, his characteristic repair strategy is **competitive escalation and renewed work toward superiority**.

### Crisis behavior outside Midoriya-specific conflict

At USJ, Bakugo is materially more strategic than his battle-trial behavior would predict if anger were treated as a global trait. He identifies Kurogiri as a high-leverage logistical target and attacks him. He recognizes that the enemies sent against the scattered students appear to be lower-tier. He is still abrasive, aggressive, and independently minded, but he is capable of cool battlefield prioritization.

This proves that his worst dysregulation in the battle trial cannot simply be generalized to all danger contexts.

### Authority/status

In low-stakes classroom life he immediately wants to be class president. Status desire is therefore not confined to combat. He wants formal recognition of being “top” as well as demonstrated superiority.

## 3.3 Iida Tenya

Volume 2 transforms Iida from procedural classmate into an early model of **legitimate and functional leadership**.

He insists that class president should be determined by vote because the class has too little mutual trust for a more informal process. His formalism is not merely comic fussiness; it is a theory of legitimacy.

His admiration for Ingenium explains his ideal:

> a hero who values discipline and **guides people**.

When the cafeteria crowd panics, Iida does exactly that. He asks Uraraka to float him toward a visible position, then deliberately formulates a message that is short, direct, reassuring, and bold. He turns himself into an information signal.

Midoriya sees this and yields the presidency.

At USJ, Iida receives another leadership test. His highest-value heroic action is to leave everyone else behind, run to the school, and bring help. He accepts being entrusted with the group:

> `皆を…任された！クラスを!! 僕が!!!`

This gives him unusually strong early evidence across procedure, low-stakes leadership, false crisis, and actual lethal crisis.

## 3.4 Uraraka Ochako

Uraraka's Volume 2 role is quieter but corrects a purely warmth-centered model.

She rapidly supports Iida's cafeteria plan, and at USJ she reasons that Kurogiri must have some tangible body or vulnerability if he needs to assume a form. She helps create the opening for Iida's escape.

Her emerging pattern is **supportive tactical responsiveness**: she is socially warm, but her care expresses itself through quickly making another person's useful plan possible.

## 3.5 Aizawa Shota

Volume 1 established Aizawa as the teacher who rejects self-destructive heroism that leaves the student unusable. Volume 2 prevents that from being misread as a doctrine of bodily safety at all costs.

At USJ he enters combat against overwhelming numbers because the students need protection. He delegates evacuation and communication tasks immediately, understands that his own Quirk is not sufficient against every opponent, and continues acting after catastrophic injury. Most strikingly, while Nomu is crushing him and Shigaraki reaches for Tsuyu, Aizawa still activates Erasure to prevent Decay from killing her.

The corrected principle is:

> **Aizawa is not anti-sacrifice. He is anti-waste.**

Professional self-endangerment is justified when it buys concrete protection for another person; routine self-destruction caused by poor technique is not.

## 3.6 All Might

Volume 2 expands All Might from ideal/mentor into a **systemically overloaded institution embodied in one person**.

Recovery Girl can rebuke him for failing to stop the Midoriya/Bakugo escalation and tells him that having this power carries responsibility to learn how to guide. He privately acknowledges that transferring One For All and continuing to overextend himself have shortened his activity window.

He is also emotionally imperfect as a teacher. During the battle trial he knows he should stop the escalation yet also does not want to deprive the boys of the confrontation. His attachment to Midoriya contributes to pedagogical indulgence.

His heroism remains authentic. The problem is not hypocrisy. The problem is that the same reflex that makes him rescue people constantly makes it difficult for him to occupy all the social roles that have accumulated around him.

## 3.7 Tsuyu Asui

Tsuyu receives one of the strongest new secondary-character baselines.

She explicitly tells Midoriya that she says whatever she thinks and asks him to call her `梅雨ちゃん`. This bluntness persists under danger rather than disappearing. She notices Midoriya's Quirk resemblance to All Might, analyzes the villains' likely objective, rescues Midoriya physically, helps execute the water-zone plan, and after victory immediately prioritizes reaching the exit rather than celebrating.

Her early model is:

- blunt social expression;
- low embarrassment about stating observations;
- strong situational calm;
- practical rescue behavior;
- quick return to objective after success.

## 3.8 Yaoyorozu Momo

Yaoyorozu's post-trial critique is philosophically important. She evaluates **process and scenario fidelity**, not only outcome. She can say that the nominal winners performed imperfectly and that Iida, on the nominal losing team, was the best participant.

Her Creation Quirk later makes cognition materially part of her power: she can make nonliving objects only through sufficient knowledge of their structure.

Her emerging character model therefore already links:

> analytic rigor → preparation/knowledge → material capability.

## 3.9 Todoroki Shoto

Todoroki enters the ledger as a high-capability, low-disclosure figure. His battle-trial performance is efficient enough that classmates immediately perceive a different level of power. At USJ he remains composed, freezes large groups, and interrogates the mismatch between the supposed anti-All-Might plan and the apparent quality of some attackers.

This is not yet enough for a broad personality model, but it establishes:

- high confidence backed by competence;
- low emotional display in combat;
- tendency to assess opponent quality and plan coherence;
- willingness to use intimidation during interrogation.

## 3.10 Shigaraki Tomura

Volume 2 supplies a crucial prospective baseline that later revelations must not overwrite.

At first appearance Shigaraki is grotesque, impatient, and game-coded. He is willing to discuss killing children instrumentally to draw out All Might. He frames failure as `ゲームオーバー` and All Might's arrival as `コンティニュー`.

But “immature gamer villain” is already insufficient. He closely observes Aizawa, notices the timing of Erasure, recognizes accumulating fatigue, infers that Aizawa is better suited to short one-on-one engagements than prolonged group combat, and understands that Aizawa entered anyway because students needed him.

He can therefore be simultaneously:

- emotionally immature;
- casually lethal;
- game-metaphorical;
- tactically observant;
- capable of modeling another person's professional motive.

When reinforcements become likely, he accepts retreat rather than pursuing suicidal persistence. His frustration is displaced toward Kurogiri, whom he threatens despite Kurogiri's obvious operational importance.

## 3.11 Kurogiri

Kurogiri's baseline is markedly different from Shigaraki's. His language is formal and polite even while announcing murderous intent. His role is logistical: dispersal, transport denial, matchup evaluation, and operational support.

He assesses Thirteen as a rescue specialist with less direct combat experience and turns her own Black Hole against her through Warp Gate. This is disciplined role analysis rather than theatrical cruelty.

## 3.12 Mineta, Kaminari, Jiro, Kirishima, and Nomu

These characters receive useful but narrower evidence.

- **Mineta** panics dramatically when confronted with real death and initially assumes his Quirk is useless, but participates successfully once Midoriya supplies a plan. Do not promote “cowardice” into a total model from one first mortal crisis.
- **Kaminari** possesses large-area offensive capacity that is dangerous to allies and carries a temporary overload cost. His effectiveness therefore depends strongly on team positioning.
- **Jiro** acts pragmatically within that team configuration and helps turn Kaminari into a controlled area weapon.
- **Kirishima** is eager to help other students and is surprised by the relative calm/tactical focus Bakugo displays at USJ.
- **Nomu**, prospectively, is best treated as an engineered anti-All-Might instrument rather than as a psychologically modeled person. Volume 2 does not yet supply evidence for autonomous interiority.

# 4. Power philosophies

## 4.1 Thirteen states the professional ethic explicitly

The most important philosophical speech in Volume 2 belongs not to Midoriya or All Might, but to Thirteen.

She explains that her Black Hole can reduce almost anything to dust. She then generalizes:

> many students also possess powers that could easily kill people.

She says superhuman society appears to function because Quirk use is licensed and strictly regulated, but that this apparent stability should never obscure the lethality of what people carry in their bodies.

Then she synthesizes the pedagogical purpose of the previous U.A. exercises:

- Aizawa's physical tests taught students what their powers can do.
- All Might's battle trial taught the danger of directing those powers against people.
- Rescue training should teach how to apply them for human life.

Her conclusion is explicit:

> `君たちの力は 人を傷つける為に あるのではない`
>
> `救ける為にあるのだ`

This is foundational because it turns what might otherwise look like Midoriya's personal moral preference into a professional doctrine of legitimate power.

### Refined power proposition

Volume 1:

> power should answer another person's need.

Volume 2:

> **power is inherently morally underdetermined and often physically dangerous; heroism is the learned judgment that directs dangerous capacity toward life.**

## 4.2 Bakugo: power as comparative proof

Bakugo still treats power as evidence of rank. Midoriya's hidden Quirk is intolerable because it retroactively destabilizes the hierarchy through which Bakugo has understood himself.

His repeated insistence that Midoriya use full power reveals that “winning” only proves identity if the opponent is genuinely strong. This is why Bakugo's drive cannot be reduced to easy bullying. He needs worthy resistance in order to prove superiority.

The moral defect is that another person's safety becomes secondary to that proof.

## 4.3 Midoriya: inherited power becoming authored responsibility

`“僕の力”で君を超えるよ` is the key transition. Midoriya cannot ethically remain a passive container for All Might's gift. The task is to make the inherited capacity his through discipline, judgment, and personal style.

This is a first step toward the later difference between:

> **possessing an inheritance**

and

> **authoring a life through it.**

## 4.4 All Might: power as social infrastructure

All Might's power does not merely stop criminals. His continued existence organizes expectations. He himself says that if he disappears, superhuman society will be pulled toward evil.

This is both an argument for his extraordinary importance and evidence of dangerous concentration. A single body has become a stabilizing institution.

## 4.5 Shigaraki: overwhelming force as status erasure

When Shigaraki tells Aizawa that even an excellent Quirk becomes, before overwhelming power, effectively `無個性`, the wording is striking.

Prospectively, this is not yet a full ideology. But it is a meaningful lexical seed: the category that defined Midoriya's childhood social exclusion is repurposed by a villain to mean **powerlessness produced through superior force**.

The line should remain open rather than overtheorized at this stage.

## 4.6 Nomu: power engineered as instrument

Nomu's first function is to be `対平和の象徴` — an anti-Symbol weapon. Its body is presented less as a self-authored person than as a deliberately built answer to All Might.

Without importing later information, Volume 2 already gives us the unsettling image of a humanoid body whose apparent purpose is entirely instrumental.

That is an early personhood/toolhood problem, even though its full ethical content is not yet available.

# 5. Hero Society / institutional analysis

## 5.1 U.A. teaches that professional evaluation is not a scoreboard

The battle-trial debrief is one of the strongest institutional moments in the early series.

Yaoyorozu's critique establishes that:

- assignment fidelity matters;
- reckless force inside a building matters;
- defenders should treat the protected object as real;
- a nominal victory can still expose poor professional judgment;
- a nominal loser can still demonstrate the best role performance.

This complicates any reading of U.A. as simply a meritocracy of combat strength.

The school is attempting to measure something harder:

> **whether power can be made operationally trustworthy.**

## 5.2 Licensing is not decorative bureaucracy

Thirteen's speech gives a strong justification for regulation. In a society where ordinary citizens can possess body-integrated lethal capacities, a licensing system is one mechanism by which use is made socially legible and constrained.

The manga does not present regulation as proof that society is fully healthy. Thirteen pointedly says the system **appears** to function. But she does establish why hero professionalism cannot be reduced to private virtue.

A person may mean well and still be unsafe.

## 5.3 U.A.'s fame creates exposure

The press/security breach reveals that a prestigious, visible hero institution is also an intelligence target. The villains can use ordinary media chaos as cover to obtain scheduling information.

This is an early version of a larger Hero Society contradiction:

> **public visibility creates trust, prestige, and recruitment — but also predictability and attack surface.**

## 5.4 All Might as single point of failure

This is the volume's largest institutional finding.

All Might is simultaneously:

- active frontline rescuer;
- Symbol of Peace;
- U.A. teacher;
- One For All mentor;
- strategic target of villains;
- psychological guarantee of public safety.

His body cannot indefinitely support all these functions.

At USJ the scheduled three-person teaching structure is reduced because All Might used up time performing outside rescue work. The villains, meanwhile, built their plan around the expectation that he would be present.

This should not be moralized into “All Might irresponsibly abandoned class.” The stronger point is structural:

> **a society that makes one heroic person indispensable creates cascading vulnerabilities whenever that person's finite body cannot occupy every role assigned to the symbol.**

## 5.5 U.A. adults are not interchangeable

Volume 2 differentiates professional specialization:

- Aizawa: suppression, close combat, crisis command;
- Thirteen: disaster rescue, hazardous-force management;
- All Might: overwhelming combat/reassurance;
- Recovery Girl: bodily sustainability and professional accountability.

The later series-level movement toward distributed heroism already has an institutional grammar here: different competencies have to cover each other's limits.

# 6. Recognition and failed rescue

Volume 2 is less centered on the Volume 1 image of visible suffering while bystanders freeze. Instead, it asks **who can correctly recognize what kind of help a situation requires**.

## 6.1 Midoriya recognizes Iida's leadership

This is one of the cleanest low-stakes recognition scenes. Midoriya has formal status and relinquishes it because he recognizes someone else's demonstrated competence.

Recognition here is not “you can become a hero.” It is:

> **you are the person who can perform this function better than I can.**

That is a mature form of social recognition because it redistributes authority.

## 6.2 Iida is entrusted with leaving

At USJ, heroism could superficially look like staying and fighting. Instead, Iida is explicitly entrusted with escape and communication. Thirteen tells him to use his Quirk **to save**.

The scene recognizes that a person's value may lie in performing a task that does not look spectacular from inside the fight.

## 6.3 Midoriya refuses Mineta's self-disqualification

Mineta believes his Quirk is useless in the immediate crisis. Midoriya instead looks for an application that makes it useful in combination with the environment and the team.

This is a small but important continuity from Midoriya's own history. He is inclined to ask:

> **what can this person/power do in this situation?**

rather than accept the person's current low self-assessment as final.

## 6.4 All Might's arrival and recognition at scale

The ending returns to the opposite form of recognition: everyone immediately recognizes what All Might's presence means.

His symbolic power is not abstract propaganda. It changes what frightened people believe is possible.

The problem, again, is concentration rather than falsity.

# 7. Villain formation, rescue, and accountability

## 7.1 Do not retrospectively backfill Shigaraki's origin

Volume 2 does not yet explain the chain of childhood failure that later becomes central to Shigaraki/Tenko. A second-pass reader knows that history, but the prospective artifact must preserve what is actually available here.

At this point Shigaraki is:

- anti-All Might;
- instrumentally willing to kill children;
- associated with All Might-focused planning;
- emotionally volatile;
- tactically observant;
- dependent on Kurogiri/Nomu and a wider organization;
- inclined to game metaphors.

The volume does **not** yet justify an origin-based failed-recognition thesis for him.

## 7.2 Villainy is organized rather than merely spontaneous

The USJ attack matters because it shows villains performing intelligence gathering, role matching, communications denial, battlefield dispersal, and target-specific engineering.

The enemy is becoming an institution too.

This is important for later series architecture: Hero Society does not face only isolated bad actors; it can face alternative networks that learn, coordinate, recruit, and build specialized assets.

## 7.3 Shigaraki's accountability problem is already severe

Whatever later causal understanding may add, Volume 2 leaves no ambiguity about present agency. Shigaraki proposes murdering students as a means to draw out All Might and personally attempts lethal violence.

Future explanation must therefore never be allowed to erase current accountability.

The later series' rescue question will have to coexist with this early fact:

> the person who may once have needed rescue is now deliberately creating victims.

## 7.4 Nomu and instrumentality

Nomu is introduced as a purpose-built answer to the Symbol of Peace. Even before later revelations, this is the inverse of Thirteen's ethic.

Thirteen says dangerous power must be learned and directed toward human life.

Nomu appears as a body directed entirely toward a target.

That contrast should remain on the continuity watchlist.

# 8. Japanese language, character voice, and key vocabulary

## 8.1 `勝つ / 負ける / 超える / 一番`

Volume 2 makes rivalry vocabulary central.

For Bakugo, these words are identity-bearing. Being first is not a pleasant outcome; it is the state required by his self-concept.

For Midoriya, `超える` operates differently. Surpassing Bakugo is tied to admiration and self-authorship rather than proving that Bakugo is worthless.

## 8.2 `力`

Two uses are especially important.

Midoriya:

> `“僕の力”で君を超えるよ`

Recovery Girl:

> `この力を持った者の責任`

Together they make “power” both personal and obligated. Midoriya wants to make it his; ownership does not erase responsibility.

## 8.3 `責任`

Responsibility becomes a bridge term linking:

- One For All secrecy;
- teaching/guidance;
- bodily management;
- entrusted roles;
- professional use of Quirks.

## 8.4 `導く`

Recovery Girl tells All Might to learn the position of one who guides. Iida describes Ingenium as a hero who values discipline and guides people.

The recurrence matters. Heroism is beginning to mean not only doing things **for** people but orienting other agents so they can act well.

## 8.5 `自尊心`

All Might explicitly names Bakugo's self-respect/pride as important. The manga does not treat Bakugo's pride as something to simply annihilate. It is dangerous in its current form but also a potential engine of professional striving.

## 8.6 `敵`

The semantic shift is formal and dramatic. In the battle trial, students play `敵`. At USJ, Aizawa states that the intruders are actual enemies.

The same lexical category moves from pedagogy to death.

## 8.7 `救ける / 救う / 人命`

Thirteen turns rescue vocabulary into professional doctrine. The students' Quirks are for human life. Iida is told to use his Quirk to save by escaping.

This broadens “rescue” beyond direct physical extraction.

## 8.8 `資格制 / 規制`

These institutional terms matter because the manga explicitly recognizes that dangerous embodied powers require governance. Heroism is not purely private morality.

## 8.9 `平和の象徴`

The phrase becomes an enemy target rather than only a heroic title. Shigaraki's group is organized around killing the social symbol itself.

## 8.10 `無個性`

Shigaraki's use of the word for Aizawa under overwhelming force is notable because it converts the social category that defined Midoriya's childhood into a generalized condition of powerlessness.

Evidence level: **plausible thematic seed**, not yet a settled ideology.

## 8.11 `ゲームオーバー / コンティニュー`

Shigaraki encodes strategic states through game language. This is not merely a catchphrase. It appears at the exact moments he decides an operation should terminate or restart.

For reconstruction, the important fact is functional:

> game vocabulary is part of how early Shigaraki conceptualizes unfolding conflict.

# 9. Visual and formal analysis

## 9.1 Enclosed school corridors versus open disaster architecture

The battle trial uses narrow interiors, doors, floors, and an object to defend. Power is constrained by architecture and rule.

USJ then opens into a large artificial geography of disasters — water, fire, collapse, terrain zones — designed to make students think ecologically about rescue.

Villain arrival turns that pedagogical geography into a battlefield.

## 9.2 Kurogiri as visual rupture

Thirteen's bright, explanatory rescue lecture is interrupted by an expanding black portal. Form and content align: the controlled institutional simulation is visually invaded by something that does not belong to its rules.

## 9.3 Shigaraki's first close visual grammar

Hands obscure his face; one eye remains violently legible; the body is decorated by parts that read more as grotesque attachments than a conventional costume.

Prospective reading: alienation, concealment, bodily grotesquerie.

Retrospective reading: the hand motif becomes vastly more specific later, but that later knowledge must not be smuggled into what Volume 2 itself explains.

## 9.4 Bakugo crying

The page refuses a simple strong/weak binary. Tears and aggressive speech occupy the same breakdown. Vulnerability does not soften his verbal style; it intensifies the recommitment.

This is valuable modeling evidence because affective display and aggression coexist rather than alternate cleanly.

## 9.5 Iida as visible signal

In the cafeteria crisis, Iida physically moves upward into everyone's sightline. Leadership is visualized as creating a common point of attention so information can reorganize the crowd.

## 9.6 Environmental rather than bodily Smash

In the water zone, Midoriya uses force on the environment to create a vortex instead of simply shattering enemy bodies. The page-scale spectacle remains, but the target has changed.

This is an early visual sign that power can become strategic rather than sacrificially direct.

## 9.7 Aizawa surrounded

Panels repeatedly place one teacher inside a mass of enemies. On a smaller scale, the image reproduces the institutional problem surrounding All Might: too much protective responsibility concentrated in one body.

Unlike the All Might symbol, however, Aizawa's survival depends visibly on coordination and limits.

## 9.8 Nomu as body made into function

Nomu's exaggerated musculature, exposed brain, and nearly expressionless physicality make the body read as engineered capability before personality.

Even without later exposition, the form supports the volume's recurring question:

> what happens when power is detached from self-authored purpose?

## 9.9 `もう大丈夫 / 私が / 来た`

The final arrival sequence uses oversized text and All Might's body to reoccupy the page. The Symbol of Peace is not merely discussed as social infrastructure; the manga **performs** reassurance through scale, pacing, and recognition.

That makes the structural critique more convincing, not less. The reader feels why dependence on him emerged.

# 10. Body, embodiment, family, and inheritance

## 10.1 Bakugo's Quirk is bodily and accumulative

Explosion is produced through sweat from his palms; support equipment stores that bodily output. Increased exertion increases available explosive capacity.

This is important because his power is not detached from affect/effort. His body literally produces the material through which his high-output fighting style escalates.

## 10.2 Midoriya begins treating his body as a continuing resource

The line `腕は犠牲に出来ない！` is one of the volume's most important embodiment changes.

Volume 1: bodily sacrifice proves heroic disposition but exposes incompetence.

Volume 2: Midoriya begins reasoning that a destroyed arm reduces his ability to continue helping during a multi-stage crisis.

This is the earliest strong step from:

> **body as expendable price of heroism**

toward

> **body as a resource that must remain available for repeated heroism.**

## 10.3 All Might's body is a public institution with a shrinking operating window

His available transformation/activity time is not merely a private health issue. It determines what classes can be staffed, which rescues receive him, and whether villains believe they can act.

The body/society linkage is now explicit.

## 10.4 Aizawa's body refines the sacrifice problem

Aizawa is badly injured while protecting students and still spends remaining capacity to stop Shigaraki from touching Tsuyu.

The volume therefore rejects a simplistic “never sacrifice your body” rule.

The real ethical distinction is:

- **avoidable self-destruction caused by poor control** is professionally irresponsible;
- **deliberate bodily risk that concretely protects another person when alternatives are absent** can be heroic.

## 10.5 Chosen inheritance begins becoming self-authorship

Midoriya's `僕の力` formulation gives chosen inheritance a bodily endpoint: the gift must become something his own body and judgment can actually operate.

## 10.6 Iida's inheritance is relational rather than biological power

Ingenium supplies an ideal of discipline and guidance. Iida inherits a model of heroic conduct before the story asks him to inherit anything else.

This expands “inheritance” beyond Quirks.

# 13. Prospective versus retrospective reading

## 13.1 What a first-time reader can responsibly infer by Volume 2

A reader can infer that:

- Midoriya and Bakugo's relationship is built from childhood admiration, hierarchy, injury, and rivalry rather than pure hatred.
- Bakugo's pride can be destabilized by evidence and criticism.
- Midoriya wants to make inherited power genuinely his own.
- All Might's body/time limit is beginning to create institutional problems.
- U.A. trains ethical control, not only combat.
- Iida has real leadership potential.
- Thirteen's rescue philosophy is a core professional norm.
- Shigaraki is both emotionally immature and tactically observant.
- the villains are organized enough to gather intelligence and execute role-specific planning.
- Nomu is an anti-All-Might engineered/modified asset of some kind.

A reader cannot yet responsibly infer:

- Shigaraki's childhood history and the full failed-rescue causal chain;
- Kurogiri's deeper identity;
- the full ontology/personhood of Nomu;
- the later One For All / All For One inheritance architecture beyond what All Might has already disclosed;
- the eventual social-collapse implications of All Might's retirement;
- later reconciliation/apology arcs.

## 13.2 What full-series hindsight adds

Hindsight makes several Volume 2 seeds much more important.

### Bakugo

His capacity to absorb evidence without surrendering his ambition becomes crucial. The later character is not created from nothing; Volume 2 already shows the mechanism by which he can change: humiliation destabilizes him, observation penetrates, and ambition survives.

### Midoriya

`僕の力` is an early self-authorship statement. Later technical and ethical ownership of One For All can be traced back to this desire not merely to borrow All Might's strength.

### All Might

The USJ staffing problem is a small-scale preview of the larger social problem that appears when the Symbol can no longer cover the entire system.

### Aizawa

His later willingness to pay enormous bodily costs for students is already coherent with the distinction established here between wasteful self-harm and protective sacrifice.

### Shigaraki

Later revelations profoundly change our understanding of why he exists, but Volume 2 must remain evidence that he is already an accountable lethal agent with meaningful tactical intelligence.

# 14. What the first pass missed or underweighted

The first pass grouped Volumes 1–3 into a broad early-series synthesis. That captured the “heroism before power” thesis but flattened several Volume 2-specific structures.

## 14.1 Volume 2 has an independent thesis

The book is not merely continuation of Midoriya proving himself. Its central problem is **judgment after recognition**.

## 14.2 Bakugo's ego is evidence-sensitive

The first pass correctly read power as identity/rank/proof/superiority, but underweighted the fact that Bakugo can internalize evidence against himself. Todoroki frightens him; Yaoyorozu's critique convinces him; Midoriya's victory wounds him. He does not simply deny reality.

The developmental problem is how he metabolizes the evidence.

## 14.3 Bakugo's risk policy is not homicidal intent

`当たんなきゃ死なねぇよ！` should be read as reckless confidence in personal control, not desire to kill Midoriya. That distinction is important for any serious behavioral model.

## 14.4 Midoriya's desire to beat Bakugo is admiration-based

His statement that Bakugo is impressive and therefore someone he wants to beat makes rivalry a positive aspiration distorted by an abusive history, not revenge.

## 14.5 `僕の力` makes self-authorship explicit early

The first pass emphasized entrusted inheritance. Volume 2 already shows Midoriya's desire to transform inheritance into ownership without denying its origin.

## 14.6 The Symbol problem is systemic by Volume 2

All Might's social necessity, shrinking time, and USJ absence together provide much stronger early evidence for institutional overconcentration than the first pass credited.

## 14.7 Recovery Girl is philosophically important

She is not only a healing utility. She supplies adult accountability, bodily sustainability, and the concept that power-bearers must learn to guide.

## 14.8 Yaoyorozu establishes process-based professional evaluation

Her debrief prevents the training arc from becoming a scoreboard morality.

## 14.9 Iida's leadership is not just procedural comedy

Voting, cafeteria crisis management, and messenger duty form a coherent early leadership philosophy.

## 14.10 Thirteen provides a foundational power ethic

The explicit statement that Quirks can kill and must be used for human life is one of the clearest early texts for the series' philosophy of legitimate power.

## 14.11 Tsuyu deserves longitudinal weight immediately

Her bluntness, calm, rescue behavior, and prioritization are already sufficiently patterned to seed a real model.

## 14.12 Aizawa's position requires revision

Calling him merely “anti-martyrdom” is too broad. He opposes incompetent, unsustainable self-destruction; he personally accepts severe sacrifice when it concretely protects students.

## 14.13 Shigaraki is tactically sharper than his surface coding suggests

His observation of Aizawa is strong enough that “immature gamer villain” is already an incomplete description.

## 14.14 Bakugo's aggression must be relationship-conditioned

His Midoriya-specific training rage and his live-crisis analysis of Kurogiri are too different to average into a single “hotheaded” trait.

## 14.15 Nomu is an early person-as-instrument image

The full ethical meaning is not yet available, but the visual/function architecture should have been placed on the personhood/toolhood watchlist.

# 16. Revised interpretation

After Volume 1, the working model was:

> **Midoriya proves that heroism precedes power, and the story is equally concerned with whether people and institutions can recognize heroic worth before conventional ability makes it obvious.**

Volume 2 adds the next clause:

> **Recognition is only the beginning. Once power is entrusted, licensed, or socially authorized, heroism depends on judgment: whether force can be controlled, whether risk is proportional, whether another person's competence can be recognized, whether one's own body can be preserved for future action, and whether dangerous capacities can be made to serve life.**

This changes the early-series model in three major ways.

First, **Midoriya's central developmental problem is already shifting from moral qualification to professional reliability**. His rescue reflex remains the core virtue, but `腕は犠牲に出来ない！` proves he is beginning to understand that a hero's body cannot be treated as disposable in every encounter.

Second, **Bakugo's arc is already structurally developmental rather than static**. The key fact is not merely that he is proud. It is that evidence can enter his model, produce shame, and be converted into renewed striving. That is the psychological mechanism later growth can work through.

Third, **the manga is already moving away from a one-person theory of heroism even while demonstrating why All Might became indispensable**. Midoriya yields leadership. Iida leaves the battlefield to save everyone. Teams make powers usable through combination. Aizawa and Thirteen occupy specialized roles. Yet the final `私が来た` still lands as genuine salvation.

The central Volume 2 paradox is therefore:

> **A great hero can be indispensable, but a healthy heroic system cannot depend on any one person being indispensable.**

# 21. What this volume changes in the current MHA model

Volume 2 changes the current series model more than its reputation as an “early training + first villain attack” volume would suggest.

## 21.1 Heroism is now explicitly a discipline of dangerous power

Thirteen's lecture establishes a professional ethic that can organize much of the rest of the series:

> **Quirks are capacities, not moral credentials. Many can kill. Heroism consists partly in learning to direct dangerous capacity toward human life.**

That is a stronger and more institutionally grounded proposition than the generic idea that “good people use powers for good.”

## 21.2 Midoriya's martyr problem is already beginning to change

He does not solve it, but the water-zone decision proves he has begun to reason about bodily continuity. A living, functioning hero is more useful than a boy who spends an arm on the first local problem.

## 21.3 Bakugo must be modeled dynamically and relationally

Volume 2 supplies the mechanism of future change:

> **contradictory evidence can wound him deeply without destroying his ambition.**

It also proves that his ugliest dysregulation is not a uniform crisis personality. Midoriya activates a specific status wound.

## 21.4 Iida becomes the first strong proof that distributed heroism is not diminished heroism

His best action at USJ is to run away from the fight because everyone else needs him to bring the system back online. Midoriya recognizes his leadership before the institution formally does.

## 21.5 Aizawa clarifies the ethics of self-sacrifice

The series is not advocating simple bodily preservation. It is distinguishing **wasteful damage** from **protective cost**.

## 21.6 All Might becomes a system problem as well as an ideal

The ending still makes his arrival magnificent. That matters. The second pass should resist the temptation to retroactively reduce the Symbol of Peace to a mistake.

The more accurate model is:

> **All Might is genuinely extraordinary enough to justify society's trust, and that success is precisely what allows too much responsibility to accumulate around one finite body.**

## 21.7 Shigaraki's first model must preserve both immaturity and intelligence

Later trauma does not erase early accountability, and early gamer coding does not erase tactical perception.

The current prospective Shigaraki model should therefore remain deliberately double:

> **emotionally immature, casually lethal, symbol-fixated — and already capable of careful opponent modeling.**

## Final V02 formulation

> **Volume 1 establishes that heroism can exist before power. Volume 2 establishes that power does not become heroic merely because a heroic person possesses it. It has to be judged, controlled, directed, shared, and sometimes withheld. The first true crisis then asks whether those disciplines survive when there is no longer a teacher grading the exercise.**

And the larger architectural change is:

> **My Hero Academia is already moving from a story about becoming powerful enough to save toward a story about building people and institutions capable of making good decisions with power — especially when the Symbol who once made those decisions for everyone cannot be everywhere.**
