Done — I’ll treat **My Hero Academia Vols. 20–21** as incorporated into the working corpus.

Current coverage:

## The big shift: heroism after rescue becomes heroism as restoration

Volume 20 completes the Eri emotional arc that began in the Overhaul material. The fight with Gentle is not a world-ending battle, but for Midoriya it is ethically significant because the thing he is protecting is **Eri’s first chance at happiness**.

That makes the conflict unusually intimate. Gentle is not Muscular or Overhaul. He is not a monster in the same category. He is a failed aspirant, someone whose desire to be remembered curdled into theatrical criminality. Izuku can understand that pain, but he cannot allow Gentle’s wounded self-mythology to destroy Eri’s fragile recovery.

So this arc sharpens something important:

> **Midoriya’s heroism is not only about saving lives from immediate death. It is about protecting the conditions under which a rescued person can begin to live.**

That is a more mature form of rescue. It is less spectacular than “Infinite 100%,” but arguably more thematically important. The School Festival makes joy itself into something worth defending.

## Gentle and La Brava: failed recognition versus chosen devotion

Gentle is a useful mirror for the failed-hero version of Izuku. He once wanted to be a hero, failed, became socially humiliated, and tried to replace legitimate heroism with notoriety. His desire is still partly heroic-shaped — he wants to matter, to leave a mark, to have someone see him — but it has been warped by shame.

La Brava deepens that. Her devotion to Gentle is not purely comic or villainous; it is a form of rescue too, but one that locks both of them into an unhealthy performance. Gentle gives her a place to belong. She gives him emotional fuel and power. They are mutually saving each other, but in a way that leads them away from responsibility.

That contrasts with Midoriya and Eri. Izuku also receives power from the person he saves, but he uses it to liberate her. Gentle receives power from La Brava, but uses it to continue his self-dramatizing crime.

So the story is drawing a subtle line:

> **Being needed by someone does not automatically make your path righteous.**

That is important for a series so invested in bonds, rescue, and inspiration.

## The School Festival: All Might’s smile becomes personal

The festival payoff with Eri smiling is one of the clearest examples of Izuku internalizing All Might’s ideal without simply imitating All Might’s public symbolism.

All Might’s smile was society-scale. It reassured everyone at once. It was heroic charisma as infrastructure.

Izuku’s version here is smaller: he helps create a moment where one abused child can smile for the first time. That does not replace All Might’s Symbol of Peace, but it shows a different path forward.

> **If All Might’s smile held society together, Midoriya’s emerging heroism begins by restoring a smile to one person who lost the capacity for joy.**

That is very consistent with his growth. He is not ready to become a national pillar, but he is already able to enact the moral substance of the symbol at human scale.

## Endeavor: power without trust cannot simply inherit the symbol

The Endeavor/Hawks material is the largest non-Izuku development in this batch, but it matters enormously for understanding the world Izuku is growing into.

Endeavor becomes No. 1 by ranking, but not by love. He has the position, but not the social meaning All Might carried. That distinction is central.

All Might was trusted before he arrived. Endeavor has to prove himself after arriving.

That makes Endeavor a counter-model for Midoriya. He shows that being powerful enough to win is not the same thing as being morally trusted enough to reassure people. Endeavor can defeat High-End, but his victory is strained, bloody, terrifying, and ambiguous in public meaning. It does not produce the same effortless peace All Might did.

So the post-Kamino world is defined by a gap:

> **The office of No. 1 Hero still exists, but the symbol has become unstable.**

This is the social vacuum into which Midoriya will eventually have to grow.

## Endeavor’s family: heroism judged at home

Endeavor’s family scenes are especially important because they bring hero ethics back into private life.

A person can be a great public hero and a catastrophic husband/father. That is not a side issue; it is one of *MHA*’s deepest critiques of hero society. Public achievement can hide private damage. Rankings can measure combat and rescue performance, but they cannot measure whether a hero has destroyed the people closest to him.

This also complicates Midoriya’s hero worship. Early Izuku loves heroes through their public acts, costumes, names, quirks, and rescues. By this point, the series is teaching him — and us — that the moral reality of heroism cannot be read only from public image.

Endeavor’s arc asks:

> **Can someone become a better hero after having been a terrible person where it mattered most?**

The story does not absolve him here. It places him at a starting line, not a finish line.

## Hawks: heroism as compromise, speed, and infiltration

Hawks adds a very different model of heroism. He is informal, calculating, media-savvy, and comfortable operating in morally gray institutional space. If All Might was transparent public reassurance, Hawks is opaque strategic prevention.

His stated desire for a world where heroes have too much free time is actually a powerful ideal. It sounds lazy, but it means:

> The best hero society would be one where heroes are almost unnecessary.

That is very different from Endeavor’s achievement-driven intensity or Midoriya’s rescue-first urgency. Hawks is thinking structurally. He wants threats prevented before they become public disasters.

This broadens the series’ hero taxonomy:

-   **All Might**: hero as symbol.

-   **Endeavor**: hero as overwhelming power and belated accountability.

-   **Hawks**: hero as intelligence, speed, compromise, and prevention.

-   **Midoriya**: hero as rescue, restoration, and inherited ethical will.


## The vestige dream: One For All becomes historical consciousness

The vestige dream in Volume 21 is a major escalation in Midoriya’s relationship to One For All.

Before this, One For All could still be understood mostly as inherited power: All Might’s strength passed to Izuku. But the dream makes it feel older, stranger, and more collective. Izuku is not just holding All Might’s embers. He is connected to a chain of people, wounds, choices, and resistance going back to All For One’s origin.

That changes the meaning of the quirk.

> **One For All is not merely stored strength. It is inherited memory, accumulated will, and historical opposition to domination.**

This makes Izuku’s burden much less private. He is not just All Might’s successor. He is the current living point of a multi-generational struggle.

It also deepens the contrast with All For One. All For One treats power as something to take, distribute, possess, and subordinate. One For All survives because people pass it forward voluntarily, even when they cannot finish the fight themselves.

So Izuku’s power is becoming morally and historically dense. It is not only “my quirk.” It is closer to:

> “I am carrying the unfinished will of people who resisted possession.”

## Shinso: society’s moral reading of quirks is unreliable

Shinso’s return in Joint Training is another important piece of worldbuilding. His quirk looks villainous. It invites suspicion. But his desire is heroic.

That makes him a strong parallel to early Midoriya. Izuku was excluded because he had no quirk. Shinso is marginalized because his quirk is socially misread. Both show that hero society’s first impressions are morally unreliable.

Midoriya’s likely relationship to Shinso is therefore thematically natural: he should understand, maybe better than most, that a person’s heroic worth is not determined by how their power looks.

This also reinforces the series’ broader power philosophy:

> **A quirk is not an ethic. The person’s use of it is the ethic.**

That idea has been present since the beginning, but Shinso expresses it very cleanly.

## Joint Training: heroism as adaptation among peers

The Joint Training arc starts as a lower-stakes school exercise, but after Kamino, Overhaul, and Endeavor, “lower stakes” does not mean unimportant. It is where the students can test growth without immediate catastrophe.

The key theme is adaptation. Class A has experience against real villains. Class B has developed differently, often with less public trauma but more structured peer competition. Shinso’s participation adds another axis: someone trying to cross into the hero course from outside the default pipeline.

For Midoriya, this arc is beginning to set up the next stage of One For All: not just making his body survive the power, and not just making the power his own style, but understanding that the quirk itself may contain more than he knew.

## Updated Midoriya thesis

Through Volume 21, I would describe Izuku this way:

> **Midoriya Izuku is a rescue-first heir to One For All whose heroism has expanded from emergency intervention into restoration, protection of joy, and historical inheritance. He is learning that power is not only something to control in his body, but something carrying memory, social consequence, and unfinished moral struggle.**

The key change from these volumes is that Midoriya’s world has become larger in three directions:

First, **downward into the intimate**: Eri’s smile shows that saving one child’s joy matters.

Second, **outward into society**: Endeavor and Hawks show that heroism after All Might is politically unstable and institutionally complicated.

Third, **backward into history**: the vestiges reveal that One For All is not just All Might’s gift, but a chain of resistance against All For One.

So Izuku’s burden is no longer merely:

> “Become strong enough to save people.”

It is becoming:

> “Learn what kind of hero can restore individual lives, survive public instability, and carry a power whose history is larger than you.”
