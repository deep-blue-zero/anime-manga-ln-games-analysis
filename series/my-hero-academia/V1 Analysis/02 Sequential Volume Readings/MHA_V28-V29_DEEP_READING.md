Done — I’ll treat **My Hero Academia Vols. 28–29** as incorporated into the working corpus.

Current coverage:

## The big shift: Midoriya’s rescue ethic collides with apocalypse

Volumes 28–29 are where the war stops being an operation and becomes a catastrophe. The heroes do not merely fight an enemy force; they fail to prevent Shigaraki from becoming a walking disaster. Once he awakens, the scale changes instantly.

Before this, Midoriya’s heroism was often framed around identifiable people:

> Bakugo.
> Todoroki.
> Iida.
> Kota.
> Eri.
> Gentle and La Brava, indirectly.

Now he is dealing with a villain whose very existence threatens cities. Shigaraki’s Decay is not just an attack; it is disaster as a quirk. The “person in front of me” model of rescue suddenly has to coexist with mass evacuation, triage, and containment.

This is the largest stress test yet for Izuku’s ethics:

> **Can a rescue-first hero still think in human terms when the threat has become civilization-scale?**

## Shigaraki becomes the anti-All Might

Shigaraki’s awakening is terrifying because he finally becomes a negative symbol. All Might’s arrival meant society could relax: the Symbol of Peace was here. Shigaraki’s awakening means the opposite: everything stable begins dissolving.

All Might’s body carried reassurance.
Shigaraki’s body carries collapse.

That makes him more than a strong villain. He becomes an anti-symbol, the point around which fear reorganizes society. His presence changes the meaning of space itself: the ground, the city, the hospital, even escape routes become unsafe.

That is important for Midoriya because he is now facing not merely an opponent, but the counter-answer to All Might’s social role.

## One For All becomes a beacon and a curse

When Shigaraki begins pursuing One For All, Izuku’s inherited power becomes a strategic liability. This is an important reversal.

Earlier, One For All was the power that let Izuku save people. Here, it also attracts the enemy. Midoriya has to pull away from civilians because his presence near them increases danger.

That is a painful development:

> **The power meant to save people can endanger them simply by being present.**

This makes Izuku’s burden much more adult. He can no longer think of One For All only as heroic capacity. It is also a target, a secret, a historical weapon, and a magnet for catastrophe.

## Midoriya and Bakugo: the secret becomes shared responsibility

Bakugo’s role in these volumes is excellent. He is not just “the rival tagging along.” He understands the One For All situation, understands Midoriya’s self-destructive tendencies, and recognizes the strategic implications faster than most people could.

This is a major change from early Bakugo. He once saw Deku’s movement toward danger as an insult or challenge. Now he sees it as a dangerous pattern that must be managed.

Bakugo’s growth is not that he becomes sweet. It is that his aggressive clarity is now pointed toward protecting the fight, protecting the mission, and eventually protecting Midoriya himself.

This turns the rivalry into something much richer:

> **Bakugo is becoming the person who can see Midoriya’s heroism clearly enough to fear what it will do to him.**

That is why “Katsuki Bakugo Rising” lands. It is not a random sacrifice scene. It is the culmination of Bakugo slowly understanding the part of All Might that Deku embodied from the beginning: the body moving before thought.

## Aizawa becomes the fragile line between society and collapse

Aizawa’s importance in this fight is enormous. His Erasure is basically the thin thread keeping Shigaraki from becoming unstoppable. That makes him symbolically powerful in a very different way from All Might.

All Might protected society by being overwhelming.
Aizawa protects society by denying catastrophe its full expression.

That difference matters. In the post-All Might world, no single hero can simply punch the problem away. Survival depends on coordination: Endeavor’s firepower, Ryukyu’s restraint, Manual supporting Aizawa, Rock Lock protecting the line, Gran Torino’s mobility, Deku and Bakugo’s intervention, and so on.

Heroism becomes collective infrastructure.

That is also a quiet answer to Shigaraki’s destructiveness. Shigaraki dissolves structures. The heroes survive by becoming structure.

## Gran Torino introduces the darkest challenge to Midoriya

Gran Torino’s harsh suggestion that killing can be a form of salvation is one of the most important moral pressures on Izuku so far.

This directly attacks Midoriya’s rescue-first worldview. Until now, even when villains were dangerous, the story often preserved the idea that heroes save, stop, capture, and protect. But Shigaraki’s scale makes that harder. If he cannot be stopped without killing him, what does rescue mean?

The cruel question is:

> **Is saving Shigaraki compatible with saving everyone else?**

This is where Midoriya’s future moral conflict becomes visible. He has seen enough of Shigaraki’s pain to recognize that there is a human wound under the monster. But he is also watching Shigaraki destroy lives in real time.

That tension is much harder than the Eri arc. Eri was an innocent child who needed rescue from a monster. Shigaraki is the child nobody rescued who has become the monster.

## Float is perfect symbolic inheritance

Nana Shimura’s **Float** manifesting here is one of the strongest symbolic beats in the war.

Technically, it lets Midoriya move the fight into the air and reduce Shigaraki’s contact with the ground. Symbolically, it means Nana’s heroic will is now being used against the catastrophic legacy created by her family tragedy.

That is brutal and elegant:

> Nana’s quirk helps Midoriya fight Nana’s grandson.

This is not just new utility. It ties Midoriya directly into the Shimura wound. One For All is no longer abstract inheritance; it is history returning through the body.

## Midoriya regresses under pressure — and that matters

One of the best things about these volumes is that they do not let Midoriya’s technical growth solve his deeper flaw.

Endeavor’s training helped him become more disciplined. Blackwhip is better controlled. Float gives him a new tactical layer. He is no longer the same boy who broke his fingers in the Sports Festival.

But when Shigaraki threatens everyone, Midoriya starts burning himself up again. The old martyr logic returns:

> If my body is the price of stopping this, then I will pay it.

That is heroic, but it is also dangerous. It shows that Izuku’s self-destructive tendency was never merely a lack of technique. It is moral and psychological. He believes so deeply in rescue that he will spend his future if the present demands it.

Bakugo’s horror at this is important because he sees the pattern: Midoriya is becoming terrifyingly competent at destroying himself for others.

## Gigantomachia and the students: children against historical disaster

The Gigantomachia material gives the student side its own war-scale test. Yaoyorozu, Kirishima, Mina, Ashido, Class 1-A, Class 1-B, and the others are not fighting in an exam simulation anymore. They are trying to stop a walking calamity tied to the League’s rise.

This is where U.A.’s premise becomes morally disturbing again. These students are brave and capable, but they are still teenagers being asked to intervene in a battle that even pro heroes can barely contain.

Kirishima’s moment is especially strong because it pays off his earlier origin material. His fear of freezing, his admiration for Crimson Riot, his desire to become someone who moves when it matters — all of that comes back under real war conditions. His courage is not natural ease; it is constructed courage finally proving itself.

## Updated Midoriya thesis

Through Volume 29, I would describe Izuku this way:

> **Midoriya Izuku is a rescue-first heir to One For All whose inherited power has now become both society’s best hope and a catastrophic target. His central conflict is no longer only mastering power, but preventing his own heroic self-erasure from turning him into another sacrificed body in One For All’s history.**

The key change is that Izuku is no longer just learning to become a hero.

He is becoming a battlefield fulcrum.

One For All draws Shigaraki.
Float ties him to Nana’s tragedy.
Blackwhip lets him restrain disaster.
His body becomes the tool he is willing to break again.
Bakugo becomes the person who understands the danger of that willingness.

So the most important question now is not simply:

> Can Deku defeat Shigaraki?

It is:

> Can Deku stop Shigaraki without losing the part of himself that still wants to save the child buried inside him — and without destroying himself in the process?
