Done — I’ll treat **My Hero Academia Vols. 16–17** as incorporated into the working corpus.

Current coverage:

## The big shift: saving becomes reciprocal

The Overhaul/Eri material pushes Midoriya’s rescue ethic into its most important form so far.

Earlier, Izuku’s pattern was:

> See someone in danger → move first → spend his body → save them.

But with Eri, the story complicates this. She is not just a person to be carried away from danger. She has been so thoroughly abused that she has internalized the idea that her existence hurts others. Overhaul’s control over her is not only physical; it is psychological and moral. He has trained her to believe that escape itself is selfish because other people will suffer for her sake.

So the challenge is not merely “defeat Overhaul.” It is:

> **Can Izuku save someone who has been taught that she does not deserve saving?**

That makes Eri one of the strongest mirrors for Izuku’s own self-sacrificial flaw. Izuku throws himself away too easily because he believes saving others is worth his own damage. Eri accepts being hurt because she believes others are worth more than herself. Both are forms of distorted self-erasure, but Izuku’s is heroic-coded while Eri’s is abuse-conditioned.

That is why their connection matters.

## Infinite 100% changes the meaning of One For All

The “Infinite 100%” moment is not just a power-up. It is a symbolic reversal of Izuku’s entire bodily problem.

Until now, One For All has been too much power for his body. His heroism keeps exceeding his physical limits. Every major rescue has risked turning him into a broken vessel for All Might’s legacy.

But with Eri on his back, his overuse of One For All is constantly rewound. For the first time, he can use All Might-level power without immediately destroying himself.

That could have been written as a simple shonen transformation, but thematically it is more interesting:

> **Izuku reaches his highest current power not by becoming self-sufficient, but by being helped by the person he is saving.**

This matters because it interrupts the martyr structure. Izuku is not saving Eri as an isolated savior-god. Eri’s will to be saved becomes part of the rescue. Her power, which Overhaul treated as raw material, becomes a force used against him.

So the ethical contrast is extremely sharp:

-   **Overhaul** uses Eri’s body as a resource.

-   **Midoriya** lets Eri’s will and power become part of her own liberation.


That is the moral difference between exploitation and rescue.

## Overhaul is the purest anti-care villain so far

Overhaul is horrifying because he is not chaotic like Shigaraki or ideologically theatrical like Stain. He is clinical. His evil is bureaucratic, experimental, sanitary, and instrumental.

He looks at Eri and sees production capacity.
Midoriya looks at Eri and sees a child.

That sounds simple, but it is the core ethical opposition of the arc.

Overhaul’s worldview is:

> Vulnerability creates usability.

Midoriya’s worldview is:

> Vulnerability creates obligation.

That makes Overhaul the most direct philosophical enemy to Midoriya so far. All For One corrupts inheritance. Stain corrupts moral purity. Shigaraki embodies cultivated resentment. But Overhaul attacks the foundation of care itself: the idea that a weak person has value apart from what can be extracted from them.

## Mirio complicates Izuku’s legitimacy again

Mirio’s role is crucial because he remains the “better candidate” problem in human form.

He is disciplined, emotionally steady, charismatic, physically trained, and able to turn an absurdly difficult quirk into graceful heroism. His fight against Overhaul and his willingness to lose his quirk for Eri are a devastating demonstration of heroic worth.

This keeps pressure on Izuku. The story does not let him escape the question:

> Did All Might choose the person with the best heroic heart, or the person best prepared to bear One For All?

Mirio proves those are not the same thing.

But the arc also shows why Izuku still matters. Mirio is an astonishing hero, but Izuku’s special role is not merely technical competence. It is the refusal to let a doomed future remain fixed. When Nighteye’s fatalism begins to dominate the emotional logic of the arc, Izuku becomes the character who insists through action that the future can still be broken open.

## Nighteye versus Midoriya: determinism versus rescue

Sir Nighteye’s foresight creates a philosophical problem for the series.

If the future is fixed, then heroism becomes tragic professionalism: reduce damage, endure loss, and accept that some things cannot be changed. Nighteye’s worldview is disciplined, rational, and emotionally wounded by All Might’s fate.

Midoriya’s worldview is almost the opposite:

> A hero acts because the person in front of him still needs saving, even if the future says failure is certain.

This could become naïve if handled badly. But the arc grounds it in Izuku’s entire pattern. He has always moved before the world gave him permission: toward Bakugo, toward Uraraka, toward Todoroki, toward Iida, toward Kota, toward Eri. The Overhaul fight is the grandest form of that impulse.

His power is no longer merely physical. It becomes the power to contest despair.

## Kirishima and Tamaki broaden the heroism map

These volumes also do good work with other hero types.

**Kirishima** shows heroism as constructed courage. He is not fearless by nature. He is someone ashamed of having hesitated, who builds an identity around becoming the kind of person who will stand firm next time. His hardness is psychological before it is physical.

**Tamaki** shows another kind of heroism: anxious competence. He is socially fragile and self-doubting, but in combat he is creative, disciplined, and deeply loyal to Mirio. His insecurity does not cancel his heroism; it coexists with it.

This is useful because it prevents Midoriya from being the only model of goodness. The series is increasingly saying that heroism can come from many deficits:

-   Midoriya must learn not to erase himself.

-   Bakugo must learn that winning must serve saving.

-   Kirishima must learn to move despite fear.

-   Tamaki must learn that anxiety does not erase competence.

-   Mirio must endure losing the very power through which he became heroic.


## Updated Midoriya thesis

Through Volume 17, I would now describe Izuku this way:

> **Midoriya Izuku is a rescue-first heir to One For All whose heroism is evolving from self-sacrificial intervention into reciprocal liberation. He still wants to save the person in front of him, but the Eri arc teaches that true rescue is not simply carrying someone away from danger; it is helping them recover the will to be saved.**

That is the major development.

Earlier Izuku’s ethic was:

> I will spend myself so others can live.

Now the better version emerging is:

> I will reach you, protect you, and help you believe your life is worth protecting.

That is a more mature and more emotionally powerful version of his heroism. It also makes his relationship to power less solitary. One For All is inherited through others; Full Cowl made it livable in his body; Shoot Style made it his own; and Eri makes his highest current expression of power possible through trust rather than isolation.
