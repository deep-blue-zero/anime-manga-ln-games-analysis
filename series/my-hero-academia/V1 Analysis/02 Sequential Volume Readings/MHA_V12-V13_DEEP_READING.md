Done — I’ll treat **My Hero Academia Vols. 12–13** as incorporated into the working corpus.

Current coverage:

## The big shift: heroism becomes professionalized

Volumes 12–13 are deceptively important. They are not as apocalyptic as Kamino, but they ask a more mature question:

> **What happens after heroic instinct has to become licensed, standardized, evaluated, and made publicly reliable?**

The Provisional Hero License Exam reframes heroism as a profession rather than a dream. Midoriya’s rescue-first instinct still matters, but now it is being tested under institutional criteria: battlefield awareness, coordination, emotional composure, public reassurance, and the ability to protect civilians while other combatants and schools interfere.

This is a major development from the entrance exam. Back then, “rescue points” validated Midoriya’s heart. Here, the licensing system demands something harsher:

> A good heart is not enough. A hero must be operationally trustworthy.

That is the story moving from **moral worthiness** to **professional competence**.

## Shoot Style: Midoriya stops trying to become All Might’s body

The most important Midoriya development is **Shoot Style**.

Until now, Izuku has been imitating All Might at a deep physical and psychological level. One For All came to him through All Might, so he instinctively imagined power through All Might’s grammar: fists, punches, smash attacks, upper-body force, overwhelming impact.

But his body cannot survive that. His arms are already medically endangered. So Shoot Style is not just a tactical adjustment. It is a psychological break from imitation.

> **Midoriya begins to understand that inheriting All Might’s power does not mean reproducing All Might’s body, fighting style, or symbolic image.**

That is huge.

He has to become All Might’s successor without becoming an All Might copy. The power is inherited, but the style has to be authored. This is one of the first times One For All feels like it is becoming **Izuku’s quirk**, not merely All Might’s quirk temporarily housed in Izuku’s body.

## The hero costume upgrade matters symbolically

The costume revision with Mei Hatsume is also important. Midoriya’s gear now exists to protect his body from the consequences of his own ethics.

That sounds simple, but it connects directly to his core flaw. Izuku’s danger is not laziness, cowardice, sadism, domination, or revenge. His danger is that he will destroy himself to save someone. The braces, soles, and support gear are narrative acknowledgments that his body needs external boundaries.

So the costume is not just equipment. It is a physical argument against martyrdom:

> **If you want to save people repeatedly, you need a body that can survive repeated saving.**

## The license exam exposes U.A.’s vulnerability

The exam’s anti-U.A. dynamic is excellent worldbuilding. Because U.A. students were publicly visible during the Sports Festival, other schools know their quirks, habits, and tendencies. Fame becomes a tactical liability.

That tells us something important about hero society: public recognition is both currency and exposure. Heroes need visibility to gain trust, ranking, and work, but visibility also makes them predictable.

This is especially relevant for Midoriya because he has inherited the most secretive quirk in the world while entering the most public profession in the world. His life is becoming a contradiction:

> He must become publicly heroic while concealing the private truth of his power.

That tension is going to matter more and more.

## Rescue is no longer just “save the person”

The rescue portion of the exam broadens the meaning of saving. The fake civilians are not just objects to carry away. They judge how the students speak, move, prioritize, reassure, and manage fear.

That is a major ethical refinement for the series.

Earlier Midoriya’s heroic instinct was bodily: run, intervene, protect. Here, rescue becomes social and communicative. A hero must not only prevent death; he must keep panic from spreading, make victims feel safe, coordinate with others, and understand that frightened civilians are not props in a heroic tableau.

This is where Bakugo and Todoroki failing makes sense. Both are extremely powerful. Both are genuinely heroic in their own way. But both fail a part of heroism that is not reducible to combat ability.

Bakugo lacks reassurance.
Todoroki lacks interpersonal flexibility under pressure.
Midoriya, despite being physically less stable than either, understands the civilian-facing side of heroism more intuitively.

## Todoroki and Bakugo fail for opposite reasons

Todoroki’s failure is tied to unresolved emotional history, especially his conflict with Endeavor and his clash with Inasa. He has immense power, but old resentment interferes with cooperation. That mirrors Iida’s Stain arc in a lighter but still meaningful way: personal emotion can deform professional duty.

Bakugo’s failure is more obvious but equally important. He still treats civilians as if they should simply shut up and be saved properly. That is funny, but it is also a real professional defect. Bakugo wants to be a hero, but his model of heroism is still too victory-centered and too contemptuous of weakness.

This reinforces one of the series’ strongest distinctions:

> **Power can defeat villains, but heroism has to be bearable to the weak.**

Midoriya understands that better than Bakugo.

## All Might and All For One: inheritance as psychological warfare

All Might’s visit to All For One in Tartarus deepens the succession theme. All For One is not merely physically dangerous; he understands inheritance as a psychological weapon.

The revelation around Shigaraki’s lineage is cruel because it turns All Might’s heroic past into a wound. All For One is effectively saying: even your mentor’s legacy can be corrupted, weaponized, and turned against you.

That sharpens the contrast between the two inheritances:

**One For All** preserves will through trust.
**All For One** corrupts will through grooming, dependency, and spite.

Midoriya and Shigaraki are not just young hero and young villain. They are heirs to two competing theories of power.

## Mirio and Nighteye challenge Midoriya’s legitimacy

The introduction of Mirio and Sir Nighteye is very important for Midoriya’s psychology.

Until now, the story’s basic moral premise has been: All Might chose Izuku because Izuku was already heroic. That remains true. But Nighteye introduces an uncomfortable institutional question:

> Was Izuku the best successor, or was he All Might’s emotionally chosen successor?

Mirio makes that question dangerous because he is not a straw man. He is not arrogant, corrupt, or unworthy. He is charismatic, experienced, physically trained, emotionally stable, and already much closer to the professional hero ideal than Izuku.

That means Midoriya’s insecurity now has a sharper form. It is no longer just:

> Can I become a hero?

It becomes:

> Did I receive a power that someone else could have used better?

That is a much more painful burden. Izuku’s legitimacy is being tested not by villains, but by the existence of a genuinely admirable alternative.

## Bakugo’s suspicion brings the secret inward

The opening of Bakugo’s confrontation over Midoriya’s quirk is also a major psychological pressure point.

Bakugo is uniquely positioned to notice the contradiction: Deku was quirkless, then suddenly gained a power connected somehow to All Might. Bakugo may not know the full truth yet, but he knows enough to feel that reality has been rearranged behind his back.

This matters because One For All’s secrecy is not abstract anymore. It is damaging relationships. Midoriya’s inherited power isolates him because he cannot fully explain himself to one of the people most defined by his existence.

## Updated Midoriya thesis

Through Volume 13, I would describe Izuku this way:

> **Midoriya Izuku is a rescue-first heir to an ethical power who is beginning to transform inheritance into authorship. His central challenge is no longer simply proving that he deserves One For All, but learning to make it his own: technically, physically, professionally, and morally.**

The key movement in these volumes is from **imitating All Might** to **becoming a hero who can survive being All Might’s successor**.

He is still defined by care. He is still rescue-first. But now the story is forcing him to professionalize that care:

> Save people without destroying yourself.
> Use inherited power without becoming a copy.
> Become public without exposing the secret.
> Carry the symbol without being crushed by comparison.
> Prove that All Might’s choice was not merely sentimental.
