Done — I’ll treat **My Hero Academia Vols. 30–31** as incorporated into the working corpus.

Current coverage:

## The big shift: hero society loses the story

Volumes 30–31 are not just the end of a battle. They are the collapse of the public narrative that made hero society function.

Until now, hero society had obvious cracks: Stain’s critique, Endeavor’s family abuse, Hawks’ covert work, the League’s origins, the Meta Liberation Army, the commercialized hero ranking system. But the public could still believe in the broad structure.

After this war, that becomes much harder.

The heroes technically prevent total annihilation, but the public meaning of the battle is disastrous:

> Cities are destroyed.
> Heroes die or resign.
> Shigaraki survives.
> All For One returns.
> Dabi exposes Endeavor.
> Hawks is revealed as a killer and a product of hero-system grooming.
> Prisoners escape en masse.
> The public no longer knows what “hero” is supposed to mean.

That is the crucial setting change. The war does not merely injure bodies. It injures legitimacy.

## Dabi’s reveal: private abuse becomes public catastrophe

Dabi’s broadcast is one of the strongest payoffs to the Endeavor material. It weaponizes a real moral truth: Endeavor’s public greatness was built alongside private familial damage.

Dabi is not morally trustworthy. His framing is malicious, theatrical, and designed to destroy. But the core accusation has truth in it. That makes it much more dangerous than a simple lie.

This is a recurring *MHA* pattern:

> Villains often expose real social rot, but their answer to that rot is monstrous.

Dabi does not want justice for the Todoroki family. He wants spectacle, revenge, and symbolic destruction. But the society he attacks has given him real ammunition. Endeavor’s abuse was not invented by villain propaganda. It was hidden under hero achievement.

For Midoriya’s broader understanding of heroism, this matters because it finally breaks the childhood assumption that public heroism can be read from public performance. A hero can save strangers and destroy his own family. That contradiction can no longer remain private.

## Hawks: the dirty hero becomes publicly contaminated

Hawks’ role also becomes much more morally complicated.

The Twice killing was preventive heroism at its ugliest: Hawks identified a catastrophic threat and stopped it before it could multiply beyond control. Strategically, he was probably right. Morally, the optics are devastating, especially once Dabi turns it into public narrative.

Hawks represents a version of heroism that All Might’s age could keep hidden:

> infiltration, manipulation, assassination-like prevention, covert state grooming, and the suppression of threats before they become visible.

That makes him one of the series’ most interesting adult heroes. He wants peace, but he is not clean. His dream of a world where heroes have free time is beautiful; the methods he uses to protect that dream are morally dirty.

This is why the Hawks/Jinwoo comparison remains strong, but with a caveat. Jinwoo also neutralizes threats quietly, but he operates as a private sovereign. Hawks operates as a compromised instrument of a public system that still needs to look morally pure.

## Best Jeanist and Lemillion: symbols return, but they cannot restore the old world

Best Jeanist and Mirio/Lemillion returning is emotionally powerful because they represent two different kinds of hope.

Best Jeanist represents professional hero composure: elegance, restraint, control, and public trust.

Mirio represents almost impossible personal resilience: someone who lost his quirk, kept his spirit, and returned to the field.

But even their return cannot undo the collapse. That is important. The story refuses the easy answer where beloved heroes show up and the old order is restored. They can help win moments, but the system-level wound is too deep.

The Symbol of Peace era is gone. Hero society now needs more than brave individuals arriving dramatically.

## Mr. Compress and the League: villain bonds become historical inheritance too

Mr. Compress’s reveal and sacrifice deepen the League as more than a random pack of criminals. By this point, the villains have their own inherited myths, attachments, and legacies.

That matters because *MHA* is increasingly structured around competing inheritances:

-   **Midoriya** inherits One For All, All Might, Nana, and the will to save.

-   **Shigaraki** inherits All For One, Nana’s family wound, and the urge to destroy.

-   **Dabi** inherits Endeavor’s ambition and family damage as hatred.

-   **Spinner** inherits Stain’s influence and then attaches himself to Shigaraki.

-   **Compress** inherits a lineage of anti-heroic criminal romanticism.

-   **Hawks** inherits the hero commission’s covert utilitarianism.

-   **Todoroki** inherits both Endeavor’s power and the damage Endeavor caused.


The story is no longer about individual power alone. It is about what people do with the past that made them.

## Deku’s vestige conversation: the decisive ethical turn

The most important Midoriya development in Vol. 31 is his conversation with the One For All vestiges.

This is where his Shigaraki problem becomes explicit. The previous users are not wrong to ask whether Shigaraki may need to be killed. From a battlefield perspective, Shigaraki is a mass-casualty threat. Gran Torino already raised the same horrible possibility.

But Midoriya’s answer is crucial: he saw something inside Shigaraki. Not an innocent villain, not an excused murderer, but a crying child.

This does **not** mean Deku naively thinks Shigaraki can simply be talked down. It means his heroism refuses to let the monster completely erase the abandoned child inside the monster.

That is probably the clearest statement of Izuku’s mature rescue ethic so far:

> **A hero must stop the villain’s harm, but should not lie to himself that the person causing harm was never someone who needed saving.**

That is a very difficult position. It is morally richer than “never kill” as a simplistic rule, and richer than “kill him because he is too dangerous” as a purely utilitarian answer. Midoriya is holding two truths at once:

> Shigaraki must be stopped.
> Tenko Shimura was a child nobody saved.

That tension is now the center of his character.

## One For All becomes a moral tribunal

The vestige space is also important because One For All now functions almost like a council of history. Midoriya is not merely using inherited power. He is being judged by inherited wills.

The previous users are not passive batteries. They are people with memories, regrets, strategies, and limits. Their presence makes One For All feel less like a superpower and more like a historical burden.

All Might gave Izuku the power because he saw heroic instinct. The vestiges now ask whether that instinct can survive the ugliest version of heroism: the possibility that saving one person may endanger everyone else.

Midoriya’s answer is not to reject their experience. It is to insist that One For All should remain a power for saving, not merely a weapon for killing All For One’s successor.

That is a huge philosophical stake.

## Deku leaving U.A.: self-sacrifice mutates into isolation

Deku’s decision to leave U.A. is understandable, but it is also dangerous.

Strategically, it makes sense. One For All attracts Shigaraki and All For One. Staying near classmates and civilians puts them at risk. Midoriya knows he is now a target on a scale the school cannot fully contain.

But psychologically, it is the return of his oldest flaw in a more mature and frightening form:

> **I will carry the danger alone so others do not have to be hurt.**

That is heroic. It is also self-isolating and potentially self-destructive.

This is the same boy who ran toward Bakugo, broke himself for Todoroki, shattered his body against Muscular, and burned himself up against Shigaraki. The difference is that now he has the power, knowledge, and social collapse around him to turn martyrdom into a lifestyle.

So Vol. 31 sets up a darker Deku:

> not evil, not corrupted, but increasingly willing to erase his own ordinary life in order to function as society’s last moving shield.

That is a major psychological shift.

## The postwar world changes the meaning of heroism

Before the war, heroism was still institutionally legible. You went to hero school, got licensed, joined agencies, climbed rankings, rescued civilians, and worked inside a broadly trusted system.

After Vol. 31, that world is broken.

Heroism now has to operate amid:

-   public distrust

-   prison collapse

-   mass villain activity

-   hero resignations

-   Endeavor’s disgrace

-   Hawks’ exposure

-   All For One’s return

-   One For All becoming central to the final conflict

-   civilians losing faith in official protection


That means Midoriya is no longer preparing to become a hero inside a stable society. He is trying to act as a hero while the social meaning of heroism is disintegrating.

## Updated Midoriya thesis

Through Volume 31, I would describe Izuku this way:

> **Midoriya Izuku is a rescue-first heir to One For All whose heroism has matured enough to see the abandoned child inside Shigaraki, but whose self-sacrificial instinct now threatens to isolate him from the very relationships that make his power ethical.**

The key movement is this:

Earlier Deku asked:

> How do I become a hero?

Then:

> How do I use One For All without destroying myself?

Then:

> How do I save someone like Eri?

Then:

> How do I stop Shigaraki?

Now the question becomes:

> **Can I stop Shigaraki without giving up on Tenko — and can I do it without turning myself into a lonely weapon?**

That last part is crucial. One For All is a power built on connection, inheritance, trust, and being chosen by others. If Deku tries to carry it in total isolation, he risks misunderstanding the very nature of the power he inherited.
