Done — I’ll treat **My Hero Academia Vols. 4–5** as incorporated into the working corpus.

Current coverage:

## Updated read of the story

These two volumes sharpen *MHA* considerably. The first three volumes established that Izuku is heroic before he is powerful. Vols. 4–5 ask a harder question:

> **What does heroic power mean once it enters public competition, family trauma, professional evaluation, and personal ambition?**

The Sports Festival is not just a tournament arc. It is a labor-market spectacle. U.A. students are being watched by pro heroes, ranked, scouted, and converted into future career prospects. That changes the meaning of power. A quirk is not only a personal ability; it is a public asset, a brand signal, a recruitment variable, and a social ranking mechanism.

That matters for Midoriya because his heroism does not cleanly translate into professional desirability yet. He can inspire, analyze, and act heroically, but his power is still visibly unstable and self-destructive. The industry can recognize Todoroki’s and Bakugo’s combat value more easily than it can recognize Izuku’s moral instincts.

## Midoriya’s biggest development: saving becomes psychological

The Midoriya vs. Todoroki fight is the key material here.

Izuku is no longer just trying to save someone from physical danger, like Bakugo from the sludge villain or Uraraka in the entrance exam. He is trying to save Todoroki from a psychological captivity: the belief that using his fire means submitting to Endeavor.

That is why the “it’s your power” idea is so important. Midoriya sees that Todoroki’s relationship to power has been colonized by his father. Todoroki technically possesses overwhelming ability, but he does not fully own it. His quirk is biologically his, but emotionally contaminated.

So Izuku’s rescue ethic expands:

> **A person may need saving not only from villains, but from the meanings imposed on their own power.**

That is a major philosophical step for him.

## The problem: Izuku’s heroism is still reckless

The counterargument is that Midoriya’s intervention is not cleanly “wise.” He pushes himself into Todoroki’s trauma in the middle of a public fight and uses his own bodily destruction as emotional pressure. It works, but it is still reckless.

This is why early Izuku is compelling rather than merely saintly. His moral instincts are excellent, but his method is crude:

> He saves by breaking himself.

That pattern is heroic, but it is not sustainable. Vol. 5 makes this unavoidable. He cannot keep treating One For All like a stockpile of sacrificial artillery. Even his finger-based workaround is still self-harm with better targeting.

So his next developmental need becomes clear:

> **He has to stop proving his heroic will through injury and learn how to make power livable inside his own body.**

That is the bridge from “worthy successor” to actual professional hero.

## Todoroki, Bakugo, and Midoriya as three power models

These volumes make the three-way contrast very sharp.

**Todoroki** represents inherited power as trauma. His strength is immense, but his selfhood is divided. He treats half of himself as Endeavor’s property.

**Bakugo** represents power as ego, victory, and personal dominance. But he is not a simple coward or bully in combat terms. His fight with Uraraka shows he takes opponents seriously enough not to patronize them. His problem is not lack of effort or courage; it is that he equates being a hero with being the strongest person in the room.

**Midoriya** represents power as entrusted responsibility. He wants to win, but winning is not his highest value. Against Todoroki, he risks his own tournament success to restore Todoroki’s agency.

That makes his heroism morally beautiful but strategically costly.

## Ochako and Iida also matter more now

Ochako’s fight with Bakugo is important because it prevents the story from making her merely “the girl Midoriya protects.” She wants to stand on her own, earn money for her family, and be taken seriously. Midoriya’s instinct to help her with strategy is kind, but her fight shows that respect sometimes means letting someone struggle under their own agency.

Iida’s material is the ominous turn. The attack on Ingenium by Stain sets up a different corruption of heroic motive: not domination like Bakugo, not inherited trauma like Todoroki, but **revenge disguised as duty**. That will matter a lot for comparing Iida’s ethics to Midoriya’s rescue-first idealism.

## Updated Midoriya thesis

Through Volume 5, I would now describe Izuku this way:

> **Midoriya Izuku is a rescue-first idealist whose heroic instinct extends beyond physical rescue into the liberation of others from fear, shame, and imposed meanings — but he still understands power through self-sacrifice more than sustainable mastery.**

That makes him very different from Jinwoo or Keyaru. Jinwoo’s power is autonomy and protection. Keyaru’s power is retaliation and anti-objectification curdled into domination. Midoriya’s power is **entrusted service** — but these volumes show the danger inside that ideal: if he does not learn control, his goodness will keep asking his body to pay the bill.
