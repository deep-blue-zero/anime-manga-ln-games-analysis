Done — I’ll treat **My Hero Academia Vols. 1–3** as working context going forward.

Current coverage:

## Working read of early *My Hero Academia*

These first three volumes establish *MHA* as a story about **heroism before power**.

Midoriya’s defining trait is not that he receives One For All. It is that he already behaves like a hero before he has any usable power at all. His body moves to save Bakugo from the sludge villain even when every adult and pro hero is frozen by practical calculation. That scene is the moral key to early Izuku:

> **He does not become heroic because he gains power. He is given power because he was already heroic.**

That makes him very different from protagonists whose power emerges from resentment, survival escalation, revenge, domination, or self-assertion. Izuku’s relationship to power begins as **entrustment**. One For All is not just a weapon or a private advantage; it is a moral inheritance.

## Midoriya’s psychology

Early Izuku is not simply “nice.” He is a mixture of:

-   obsessive hero-worship

-   intense analytical intelligence

-   low self-worth from being quirkless

-   deep moral reflex toward rescue

-   fear of being useless

-   admiration for power without worshiping domination

-   a strong need to prove that his dream was not delusional


His notebooks matter. Before he can fight, he studies. His quirklessness forces him into observation, pattern recognition, and humility. Once he receives One For All, that analytical habit does not disappear; it becomes the thing that prevents him from being merely a boy with borrowed super-strength.

He is also not emotionally simple. Bakugo’s bullying has clearly damaged him, but Izuku does not convert that damage into hatred. That is one of the most important early distinctions. He wants recognition, but he does not want revenge. He wants strength, but not superiority.

## His philosophy of power

Izuku’s early view of power is basically:

> **Power exists so that someone can be saved.**

That sounds simple, but the first three volumes keep testing it.

The entrance exam asks whether he will chase points or save Uraraka. He chooses rescue. U.A. then validates that choice through rescue points, meaning the institution itself recognizes that heroic value cannot be reduced to combat score.

The quirk apprehension test and battle trial complicate this. Izuku has noble instincts, but his power is self-destructive and uncontrolled. So the story immediately rejects the fantasy that good intentions plus overwhelming strength are enough. If he cannot control One For All, then his desire to save people may itself become reckless.

That is a crucial early ethical lesson:

> **A hero must be willing to sacrifice himself, but not addicted to self-sacrifice.**

He has not learned that fully yet, but the finger-smash adaptation shows the beginning of it. He starts moving from “throw my whole body into the heroic act” toward “use power precisely enough to survive and keep helping.”

## The early contrast characters

Bakugo is the most important foil. For Bakugo, power is identity, rank, proof, and superiority. Midoriya’s continued existence as someone “weaker” who still reaches toward heroism feels like an insult to Bakugo’s worldview. That is why Midoriya’s courage enrages him: it implies that heroism is not reducible to strength.

All Might is the ideal and the warning. He embodies heroic power as reassurance — the Symbol of Peace — but his damaged body shows the cost of turning oneself into a public pillar. Midoriya inherits not only All Might’s strength, but also the burden of becoming someone others can depend on.

Todoroki, introduced more sharply around the Sports Festival setup, adds a different power problem: inherited power as family project, trauma, and coercive ambition. His power is biologically his, but psychologically contested. That makes him a major contrast to Izuku, whose inherited power is chosen, entrusted, and aspirational.

Ochako and Iida also matter early. Ochako gives Izuku social warmth and reframes “Deku” from insult into chosen name. Iida represents rules, procedure, and formal seriousness; Izuku represents instinctive rescue ethics. Both are heroic, but they approach heroism from different angles.

## Current Midoriya thesis

Through Volume 3, I would describe Izuku this way:

> **Midoriya Izuku is a rescue-first moral idealist whose worthiness precedes his power, but whose early arc is about learning that heroic intention must become controlled, disciplined, embodied competence.**

He is not a combat power-fantasy protagonist in the Jinwoo sense, nor a revenge-power protagonist in the Keyaru sense. His power is not autonomy from the world or retaliation against it. It is a burdened gift that forces him to become physically, tactically, and ethically worthy of the heroic impulse he already had.
