Done — I’ll treat **My Hero Academia Vols. 10–11** as incorporated into the working corpus.

Current coverage:

## The big shift: Kamino turns inheritance into burden

Volumes 10–11 are a major structural turning point. Until now, One For All has mostly been Midoriya’s impossible gift: the thing that allows a quirkless boy to enter the heroic world. Kamino reframes it as something much heavier.

> **One For All is not just a power Midoriya received. It is the mechanism by which All Might’s era ends and Midoriya’s burden begins.**

All Might’s final battle against All For One makes the cost of symbolism visible. The Symbol of Peace is not an eternal god. He is an injured man burning the last embers of an inherited power. For Izuku, watching this is almost unbearable because it means the ideal he worshipped is mortal, exhaustible, and now dependent on him.

All Might pointing toward the camera after his victory is brilliant because it has two meanings. Publicly, it reads as a warning to villains: “You’re next.” Privately, Izuku understands the real message: **you are next.** That is not triumphal wish fulfillment. It is terrifying succession.

## Midoriya’s rescue ethic matures: he does not have to be the hand

The Bakugo rescue is one of Izuku’s most important ethical developments so far.

Earlier Izuku’s pattern was direct bodily intervention: run toward danger, break himself, save the person in front of him. But Bakugo cannot be saved that way. If Izuku personally reaches for him, Bakugo’s pride and their history may cause hesitation or rejection.

So Izuku does something more mature: he designs a rescue where **Kirishima** is the one who reaches out.

That is a huge step.

> **Midoriya learns that saving someone does not require centering himself as the savior.**

This is a more sophisticated form of care. He understands Bakugo’s psychology well enough to know that the correct rescue is not “Deku saves Kacchan,” but “Kacchan accepts the hand of someone he can meet without humiliation.”

That matters because it partially answers the problem I raised in earlier volumes. Izuku’s danger is martyrdom: he tends to make his own body the price of rescue. Here, he saves through planning, delegation, timing, and relational intelligence. It is one of the first times his heroism looks less like self-destruction and more like professional judgment.

## Bakugo becomes more tragic and more central

Bakugo’s kidnapping also changes his function in the story. He is not merely Midoriya’s bully/rival anymore. He becomes the person villains misread.

The League assumes that Bakugo’s aggression, pride, and violent temperament make him recruitable. But they misunderstand him. Bakugo’s personality is ugly in many ways, but his heroic aspiration is real. He wants victory, status, and superiority, but he does not want villainy.

That sharpens the contrast between Bakugo and Shigaraki. Both are angry, intense, and socially malformed in different ways. But Bakugo’s ideal is still All Might. Shigaraki’s ideal is destruction under All For One’s grooming.

So Bakugo is not “good because he is nice.” He is good because, beneath his cruelty and ego, his value structure still points toward heroism.

## Shigaraki and Midoriya become parallel heirs

Volumes 10–11 also clarify the deeper symmetry:

**Midoriya** is All Might’s successor.
**Shigaraki** is All For One’s successor.

That gives the story a much cleaner mythic structure. This is no longer just student hero versus villain organization. It is two forms of inheritance.

One For All is inherited through trust, sacrifice, and chosen succession. All For One’s legacy is inherited through grooming, resentment, manipulation, and dependency. Both successors are young, unfinished, and shaped by powerful adult mentors. But the moral structure of those mentorships is opposite.

Midoriya receives power so he can protect others. Shigaraki receives validation so he can destroy the society that failed or disgusted him.

This also makes All For One much more frightening than Stain. Stain had ideology. All For One has historical patience. He does not merely attack society; he cultivates successors, institutions, loyalties, and symbolic counterweights.

## The doctor’s warning and Inko’s objection change the ethics of heroism

The warning about Izuku’s arms is not just a medical detail. It is the story again saying:

> **Good intentions do not exempt the body from consequences.**

But Inko’s reaction is even more important. She is not being cowardly or anti-heroic. She is asserting a moral claim that hero society often suppresses: children are not symbols; they are someone’s children.

Her objection forces a different ethical problem onto Izuku:

> Does his duty to save strangers override his duty not to destroy the people who love him?

That is a more mature moral conflict than “Should I be brave?” Izuku already knows how to be brave. The harder question is whether his bravery can coexist with responsibility to his own family.

All Might bowing to Inko is therefore essential. He is not only asking permission to train Izuku. He is accepting accountability for the human cost of turning a boy into the next Symbol of Peace.

## U.A. dorms: safety, control, and institutional escalation

The move into dorms looks lighter after Kamino, but it matters. U.A. is no longer just a school. It becomes a protective institution under crisis conditions. The students are being gathered, monitored, and shielded because villains have made ordinary student life impossible.

That changes the setting. Hero education is no longer a prestigious career pipeline. It is becoming wartime preparation.

There is a faintly unsettling edge to this: the students are safer, but also more enclosed. Hero society’s failure outside the school produces tighter control inside the school. That is not presented as evil, but it is a sign that the world is becoming less stable.

## Updated Midoriya thesis

Through Volume 11, I would now describe Izuku this way:

> **Midoriya Izuku is a rescue-first heir to an ethical power who is slowly learning that heroism is not only the willingness to spend oneself, but the judgment to save through others, survive for future rescues, and carry a symbol without mistaking himself for the symbol.**

The Kamino material makes him more interesting because it removes the innocence from inheritance. One For All is not just “you get to become like All Might.” It is also:

> Your mentor’s era is ending.
> Society will need a new pillar.
> Your body may not survive your instincts.
> Your mother is right to be afraid.
> And sometimes the most heroic thing you can do is not throw the punch yourself.
