Done — I’ll treat **My Hero Academia Vols. 8–9** as incorporated into the working corpus.

Current coverage:

## Updated read of the story

Volumes 8–9 shift *MHA* from controlled educational trials into a more dangerous register. The Sports Festival and final exams were public, rule-bound systems. Even Stain, though lethal, was encountered in a bounded urban incident. The forest camp attack changes the feeling: the students are now children being hunted by ideologically mobilized villains.

That matters because the series stops asking only:

> Can these students become heroes?

and starts asking:

> What happens when hero society’s children are forced to carry hero society’s war before they are ready?

The answer is ugly: Izuku survives because his instincts are heroic, but his body is still the payment mechanism.

## Midoriya’s relationship to power: from “borrowed cannon” to “body technique”

The final-exam arc completes the Full Cowl transition. Against All Might, Izuku cannot simply admire the Symbol of Peace. He has to fight him, read him, and escape him. This is psychologically important.

Earlier Izuku’s relationship to All Might was reverential. He received One For All as a miracle and treated All Might as the living proof of what a hero should be. But the final exam forces a more adult step:

> **The ideal must become something he can oppose, analyze, and survive — not merely worship.**

That is also why Bakugo matters so much here. Bakugo’s instinct is to win even when the situation demands escape. Izuku’s instinct is to calculate around survival. Neither is complete by itself. Bakugo needs to learn that heroism is not just victory; Izuku needs to learn that respect for power cannot become paralysis.

## Midoriya and Bakugo: two distorted inheritances of All Might

The All Might exam makes the Midoriya/Bakugo contrast sharper than before.

Izuku inherited All Might’s power, but also inherited an almost crushing reverence for his symbolic role.

Bakugo did not inherit One For All, but he inherited All Might’s victory-image. To Bakugo, All Might means the hero who always wins. That is why “running away” feels intolerable to him even when the rules define escape as a valid victory condition.

So both boys misunderstand All Might in different ways:

-   **Izuku** sees All Might as too sacred.

-   **Bakugo** sees All Might as victory incarnate.

-   **All Might** is actually both symbol and exhausted human being.


That last point is becoming more important. The story keeps stripping away the fantasy that symbols are costless.

## Shigaraki’s mall encounter: villainy as failed socialization

The mall conversation with Shigaraki is one of the most important villain scenes so far. Shigaraki is still immature, but he is no longer just a chaotic gamer-brat villain. He is trying to understand why Stain moved people and he did not.

What makes the scene unsettling is that he uses Izuku almost like an unwilling moral mirror. Midoriya is forced into conversation rather than combat, and Shigaraki starts articulating his disgust with All Might’s society: smiling heroes create a world where people assume someone else will handle danger.

That critique is not identical to Stain’s, but it rhymes with it. Stain attacks false heroes. Shigaraki attacks the social complacency enabled by heroes.

The story does not endorse him, obviously. But it does reveal that villain ideology is no longer random violence. The villains are learning how to narrate society’s contradictions.

## Kota and the evolution of Midoriya’s rescue ethic

Kota is a major test for Izuku because he rejects heroes from below rather than opposing them as a villain. His parents died as heroes, and from his perspective, the adult world’s heroic language is just a way to beautify abandonment and death.

This is a much more painful critique than Stain’s because Kota is a child, not an ideologue.

Izuku cannot answer him with slogans. “Heroes are cool” means nothing to a kid whose parents died doing hero work. So Izuku’s rescue ethic has to become quieter and more patient. He does not convert Kota through argument. He protects him.

That is a recurring *MHA* idea:

> **Heroism becomes real when someone who distrusts the word “hero” is saved anyway.**

The Muscular fight is the brutal proof.

## Muscular: power without moral imagination

Muscular is one of the cleanest anti-Midoriya villains so far.

He does not have Stain’s ideology or Shigaraki’s evolving resentment. He is appetite, violence, and pleasure in domination. He kills because he enjoys overpowering others.

That makes him a perfect opponent for early Izuku’s power philosophy. Against Muscular, Midoriya’s inherited strength is not being used to win a tournament, impress scouts, or pass an exam. It is being used in the most primal heroic situation:

> A child will die unless he stops the monster in front of him.

But the fight also exposes the cost of Izuku’s ethic. He wins by going beyond safe limits again. Full Cowl improved his relationship to power, but under extreme pressure he returns to catastrophic self-sacrifice.

So the pattern is not solved. It has evolved:

-   Early Izuku breaks himself because he has no control.

-   Full Cowl Izuku learns control.

-   Forest-camp Izuku breaks himself anyway because the situation morally demands everything.


That distinction matters. It is no longer mere incompetence. It is the tragic side of his rescue-first ethics.

## Updated Midoriya thesis

Through Volume 9, I would describe Izuku this way:

> **Midoriya Izuku is a rescue-first heir to an ethical power whose deepest instinct is to protect the vulnerable before protecting himself. His growth is not just learning to become stronger, but learning how to make inherited power survivable without losing the willingness to spend himself when no one else can pay the cost.**

The danger in him is not arrogance, domination, or revenge. It is martyrdom.

That makes him very different from Jinwoo or Keyaru. Jinwoo’s power fantasy is autonomy: become too strong to be coerced, then quietly eliminate threats. Keyaru’s power is anti-victimhood turned into domination and retaliatory permission. Izuku’s power is entrusted service — but these volumes show the dark edge of that ideal:

> If power exists to save others, then Izuku will keep offering his body as collateral until someone teaches him that a living hero saves more people than a broken one.
