Done — I’ll treat **My Hero Academia Vols. 26–27** as incorporated into the working corpus.

Current coverage:

## The big shift: hero society enters wartime conditions

Volumes 26–27 move the story out of “training toward crisis” and into **organized social war**. The heroes are no longer responding to isolated incidents: USJ, Stain, Kamino, Overhaul, Gentle, High-End. They are launching a coordinated preemptive operation against an enemy organization with mass manpower, ideology, money, political reach, and Shigaraki as a catastrophic center.

That matters because the students’ position changes. They are still children in training, but the system now needs them operationally. Midoriya and his classmates are not just learning heroism; they are being folded into a national security emergency.

So the question becomes harsher:

> **Can a hero education system still pretend these are students when the world already needs them as soldiers?**

That tension was always present, but Vol. 27 makes it unavoidable.

## Endeavor Agency: heroism as automaticity

Endeavor’s teaching in Vol. 26 is one of the most useful technical lessons Midoriya has received.

Endeavor is morally compromised, but he is an excellent instructor on professional embodiment. His lesson is not “try harder” or “believe in yourself.” It is:

> Break the task into pieces.
> Repeat each piece until thought is no longer required.
> Then layer the pieces together under real conditions.

That is exactly what Midoriya needs after Blackwhip. His problem is no longer only output percentage. He now has to coordinate Full Cowl, Air Force, Blackwhip, movement, situational judgment, rescue priorities, emotional regulation, and eventually Nana’s Float.

This is a very good corrective to Izuku’s old martyr pattern. Early Midoriya solved impossible situations by spending his body. Endeavor teaches him a different model:

> **A professional hero survives by making the correct action automatic before the emergency arrives.**

That is one of the cleanest technical answers the story has given to Midoriya’s self-destructive heroism.

## The Ending incident: the trio’s growth becomes visible

The villain Ending is not a world-class threat, but the incident is very important because it demonstrates that Midoriya, Bakugo, and Todoroki are becoming operationally distinct heroes.

Midoriya’s role is especially telling. He does not win by smashing harder. He analyzes, moves, coordinates, uses Blackwhip in controlled fashion, and participates in a rescue with precision rather than catastrophic self-sacrifice.

That is the developmental payoff:

> **Midoriya is becoming someone who can save quickly without breaking himself.**

Bakugo’s growth is also visible: he saves Natsuo while still framing everything through speed and victory. Todoroki’s growth is more emotionally fraught: he acts while his father freezes. The scene is a miniature of the trio’s relationship to Endeavor. They can learn from him, but they also have to exceed his limitations.

## Endeavor’s family makes heroism morally non-transferable

The Todoroki family material continues one of the series’ strongest critiques: public hero achievement cannot launder private harm.

Endeavor wants to atone, but the story does not let “I am trying now” erase the accumulated damage. Natsuo’s anger is treated as morally serious. Fuyumi’s desire for family repair is understandable, but not automatically binding on everyone else. Shoto’s guarded openness is not forgiveness yet. Rei’s absence remains a wound.

This matters for Midoriya’s broader understanding of heroism. He grew up admiring heroes through public imagery: saves, smiles, rankings, costumes, battle records. But Endeavor forces the story to ask:

> **What is a hero worth if the people closest to him were destroyed by his pursuit of greatness?**

That question does not directly indict Izuku, but it matures his world. Heroism cannot just be judged by public outcomes.

## Shirakumo/Kurogiri: villainy as violation of personhood

The Kurogiri/Shirakumo reveal is one of the darkest pieces of worldbuilding so far.

It makes Nomu creation feel less like monster-making and more like **the desecration of memory, body, and identity**. Shirakumo was a person. Kurogiri is not simply “a villain with a mist quirk.” He is a person’s remains repurposed into a tool.

That matters thematically because it makes All For One’s evil even more coherent. His power philosophy is not just “take quirks.” It is:

> Persons can be reduced to functions.

That is the opposite of One For All’s moral grammar. One For All preserves will across generations. All For One and the doctor turn people into usable parts.

This also gives Aizawa and Present Mic tragic weight. Their old friend was not merely killed; he was converted into infrastructure for villainy.

## One For All becomes strategic curriculum

The discussion of the previous One For All users also changes Midoriya’s development. Now All Might is not simply saying “master my power.” He is helping Izuku study a chain of inherited quirks and decide what can be trained next.

The mention of **Float** is particularly important because it belongs to Nana Shimura. That means Izuku’s next stage is not just technical utility. It is emotionally and historically charged.

The irony is sharp:

-   Shigaraki is Nana’s biological legacy twisted into catastrophe.

-   Midoriya is Nana’s heroic legacy carried through One For All.

-   Float is the next piece of that inheritance.


So if Midoriya eventually uses Float against Shigaraki, the symbolism will be brutal: Nana’s abandoned family wound and Nana’s heroic will colliding through two successors.

## The war arc changes the meaning of “saving”

Once the hospital and mansion raids begin, rescue is no longer intimate in the Eri sense. It becomes mass-scale evacuation, triage, intelligence, containment, and risk management.

Midoriya’s role at this stage is not glamorous. He is part of the evacuation side, not the central assault. But that actually fits the story’s deeper ethics. A hero is not only the person fighting the final boss. A hero is also the person moving civilians away before the catastrophe hits.

The problem is that Midoriya’s inherited burden is far larger than his assigned role. He is still a student doing evacuation work, while the power inside him marks him as one of the only people who might eventually answer Shigaraki.

That gap creates the tension:

> **Midoriya is being used like a student while fate is preparing him like a final line of defense.**

## Hawks vs. Twice: preventive heroism reaches its moral breaking point

Hawks’ confrontation with Twice is one of the strongest moral collisions in the series so far.

Twice is sympathetic, loyal, damaged, and genuinely loves the League. He is also catastrophically dangerous. His quirk, once psychologically unsealed, could turn the war into a numbers nightmare. Hawks understands this faster than almost anyone else could.

That is Hawks’ heroism at its most consequential and most morally ugly:

> He does not wait for Twice to become unstoppable.
> He identifies the future disaster and tries to prevent it now.

This connects directly to the Hawks/Jinwoo comparison from earlier. Hawks and Jinwoo would agree that the best rescue may be the threat neutralized before the public even understands the danger. But Hawks is doing this inside a heroic institution that still claims public moral legitimacy. That makes the ugliness harder to hide.

Hawks tries to spare Twice if possible. But when Twice chooses the League and becomes an imminent mass threat, Hawks kills him.

That is not All Might-style heroism. It is not Midoriya-style rescue. It is **dirty prevention**.

## Twice’s death makes the League emotionally real

Twice’s final moments are devastating because they prove the League is not merely a collection of villains with compatible goals. For Twice, the League is family. Toga and Compress are not abstractions; they are the people who gave him a place to belong.

This is what makes the hero-villain conflict morally harder. Twice is not innocent. But he is not emotionally hollow either. The story is asking us to hold both truths:

> Twice is a mass-casualty threat.
> Twice is also a wounded man whose love for his friends is real.

That is exactly the kind of conflict Midoriya’s rescue ethics will eventually struggle with. What does it mean to “save” someone whose deepest attachments are tied to people who will destroy society?

## Dabi exposes the rot under hero image

Dabi’s confrontation with Hawks is not fully resolved in these volumes, but it already sharpens one of the story’s ongoing critiques: hero society relies on image, secrecy, and managed narratives. Hawks is a public hero, a covert agent, a manipulated child soldier of the system, and now a killer who acted to prevent catastrophe.

Dabi attacks that contradiction gleefully. He does not need to be morally pure to expose real hypocrisy. That is a recurring *MHA* villain pattern: villains often identify genuine social rot, then respond to it in monstrous ways.

## Updated Midoriya thesis

Through Volume 27, I would describe Izuku this way:

> **Midoriya Izuku is a rescue-first heir to One For All who has begun converting inherited power into disciplined professional technique, but who is now entering a war where the meaning of rescue is no longer simple, intimate, or clean.**

The crucial development is that Midoriya’s growth has become technically healthier just as the world has become morally more contaminated.

He is learning to save without destroying himself.
But the war is producing situations where no choice is clean.
He is learning inherited quirks one at a time.
But Shigaraki is being prepared as a society-ending weapon.
He believes in saving people.
But Twice shows that some people who need saving may also be immediate threats to everyone else.

So the next major test for Midoriya is not simply strength. It is whether his rescue ethic can survive contact with a war in which villains are both victims of social failure and agents of catastrophic harm.
