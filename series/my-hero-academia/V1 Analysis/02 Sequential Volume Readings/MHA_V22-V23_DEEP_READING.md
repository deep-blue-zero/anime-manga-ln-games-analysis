Current coverage:

## The big shift: One For All stops being “super strength”

Volumes 22–23 are one of the biggest changes in Midoriya’s relationship to power so far. Until now, One For All has mostly functioned as inherited physical force: too much strength for his body, gradually made survivable through Full Cowl, Shoot Style, support gear, and better output control.

Blackwhip changes that completely.

Now One For All is not simply:

> All Might’s power passed down to Midoriya.

It becomes:

> A living archive of prior users, their quirks, their wills, and their unfinished struggle.

That is a massive psychological burden. Izuku is no longer merely trying to master a dangerous inherited strength. He is discovering that the inheritance is more complex than even All Might fully understood. He is not just successor to All Might; he is successor to a chain of people whose powers may erupt through him.

## Blackwhip exposes the danger of emotion

The key detail is that Blackwhip does not emerge because Izuku calmly decides to use it. It erupts through emotional agitation. His desire to win, protect, respond to Monoma’s provocation, and control the match all overload into a power he cannot safely handle.

That matters because Izuku’s problem has always been that his heroic impulse outruns his body. Now the problem becomes even more dangerous:

> **His heroic emotions can trigger powers he does not yet understand.**

Earlier, recklessness meant broken bones. Now recklessness could mean collateral damage to classmates. That changes the stakes of his self-control.

This is why Shinso’s role is so important. Izuku is stopped not by brute force, but by someone else interrupting him psychologically. Midoriya’s growth here is not triumphant autonomy. It is a humbling lesson:

> He cannot master One For All alone.

That fits the whole ethical structure of the quirk. One For All is inherited through others; it should not be mastered through isolated ego.

## Midoriya’s power becomes communal, not solitary

The Joint Training arc makes Izuku’s development less individualistic. Uraraka, Shinso, Monoma, Bakugo, All Might, and the vestige all become part of his power-learning process. Even the fact that he panics and has to be helped is important.

This is not like Jinwoo’s leveling system, where isolation and self-reliance are central. Izuku’s power development repeatedly depends on:

-   mentors interpreting the burden

-   peers grounding him

-   rivals exposing weaknesses

-   predecessors communicating through vestiges

-   support gear and tactics making strength livable

-   emotional relationships preventing power from becoming uncontrolled violence


So Midoriya’s power philosophy is becoming increasingly anti-solitary:

> **Power is entrusted, interpreted, stabilized, and made ethical through relationships.**

That is very different from “I become strong enough that no one can stop me.”

## Bakugo’s match matters more than it first seems

Bakugo’s Joint Training match is one of his clearest growth moments. He wins decisively, but not through lone domination. He protects his teammates, coordinates, adapts, and makes victory compatible with rescue.

That is the synthesis All Might wanted from both boys:

> Win to save. Save to win.

For Midoriya, this matters because Bakugo is no longer merely the rival who embodies the part of heroism Izuku lacks. Bakugo is starting to integrate rescue into victory. That puts pressure on Izuku to integrate victory, control, and emotional steadiness into rescue.

Their rivalry is becoming healthier because both are now correcting each other’s incomplete inheritance of All Might.

## Shinso reinforces the “quirk is not morality” theme

Shinso’s presence remains one of the best worldbuilding elements in this part of the story. His quirk is socially coded as villainous, but his aspiration is heroic. That directly challenges the lazy moral grammar of hero society.

Midoriya was once excluded because he lacked a quirk. Shinso is marginalized because his quirk looks wrong.

Both expose the same flaw:

> Hero society claims to judge heroic will, but it often pre-judges people through quirk aesthetics.

This matters thematically for Midoriya because One For All now contains quirks that may look strange, dangerous, or uncontrollable. The heroic question is not what the power looks like. It is whether Midoriya can give that power ethical form.

## The villain turn reframes the whole setting

The opening of *My Villain Academia* is important because it starts showing the League not merely as antagonists, but as another ecosystem of rejected, warped, or socially failed people. The Meta Liberation Army also introduces a new ideological axis: quirk liberation, self-expression, anti-suppression, and mass mobilization.

That is a different kind of threat from Overhaul.

Overhaul wanted to control, extract, and purify.
The Liberation Army wants to unleash quirk identity as politics.
Shigaraki wants destruction, but is also being pushed toward leadership, succession, and historical self-understanding.

For Midoriya’s story, this matters because the villain world is becoming more philosophically crowded. “Save people from villains” is no longer enough as a social theory. Hero society is producing enemies with competing stories about freedom, repression, resentment, recognition, and the meaning of quirks.

## Updated Midoriya thesis

Through Volume 23, I would now describe Izuku this way:

> **Midoriya Izuku is a rescue-first heir to One For All whose central challenge has shifted from controlling inherited strength to ethically integrating inherited history. He must learn not only how to use power without destroying himself, but how to remain himself while multiple past wills and powers begin emerging through him.**

The new danger is not only bodily damage. It is loss of mastery.

Earlier question:

> Can Izuku survive One For All?

New question:

> Can Izuku become the conscious moral author of One For All before One For All overwhelms him?

That is a much more interesting burden.

## Hawks and Jinwoo: where they would agree

Yes — Hawks and Sung Jinwoo would probably have significant agreement, more than Jinwoo would have with someone like All Might.

The overlap is that both understand heroism less as public glory and more as **threat prevention**.

Hawks’ ideal is not “heroes should be worshiped forever.” His dream is almost anti-heroic on the surface: a world where heroes have too much free time. That means his ideal society is one where danger has been neutralized so thoroughly that heroes do not need constant dramatic intervention.

Jinwoo is similar. He does not crave public symbolism. He does not need parades, rankings, speeches, or ideological legitimacy. His preferred mode is quiet protection: identify the threat, remove it, prevent harm to the people he cares about, and often let others misunderstand or never know the full story.

So they would agree on several things:

> Heroism is not about looking heroic.
> The best rescue is often the disaster that never happens.
> Public recognition is secondary to actual safety.
> Sometimes the person protecting society has to operate unseen.
> A hero who needs applause too badly is already compromised.

Hawks would probably respect Jinwoo’s efficiency, calmness, and lack of performative ego. Jinwoo would probably respect Hawks’ speed, strategic intelligence, and willingness to dirty his hands for prevention rather than waiting for a dramatic rescue moment.

## Where they would disagree

The main difference is institutional.

Hawks is still a **professional hero inside a public system**. Even when he lies, infiltrates, or manipulates appearances, he is doing it as part of a broader hero-society apparatus. He thinks in terms of public order, institutional stability, intelligence networks, rankings, media, and prevention at scale.

Jinwoo is much more sovereign. He is not fundamentally a servant of the hunter system. He cooperates when useful, but his real authority comes from himself. His deepest instinct is:

> I will protect what matters, whether or not institutions understand me.

That means Hawks might worry about Jinwoo’s lack of accountability. A being that powerful, operating privately, is a nightmare from Hawks’ institutional perspective even if Jinwoo is benevolent.

Jinwoo, meanwhile, might see Hawks as trapped inside a compromised public system. He would understand the necessity of infiltration and secrecy, but he would likely be less emotionally invested in preserving the legitimacy of hero society as an institution.

So the cleanest comparison is:

> **Hawks is a covert institutional protector. Jinwoo is a private sovereign protector.**

They would agree on outcomes and prevention. They would differ over whether protection should remain tied to public institutions, law, and social legitimacy.

## Final read

Hawks is probably one of the MHA characters closest to Jinwoo’s mature view of power, because both are suspicious of theatrical heroism and both care more about neutralizing danger than being seen as pure.

But Jinwoo’s heroism is more absolute and personal. Hawks’ heroism is more systemic and compromised.

Hawks asks:

> How do I keep society peaceful from inside the machinery?

Jinwoo asks:

> What threat needs to disappear so the people I protect can live?

That gives them real common ground — but not identical moral worlds.
