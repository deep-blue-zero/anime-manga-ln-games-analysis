Done — I’ll treat **My Hero Academia Vols. 32–33** as incorporated into the working corpus.

Current coverage:

## The big shift: Deku almost becomes a lonely weapon

Volumes 32–33 are among the most important Midoriya volumes in the series because they take his oldest virtue and show its pathological endpoint.

Since Chapter 1, Izuku’s defining trait has been:

> his body moves before he thinks when someone needs saving.

That trait made him worthy of One For All. But after the war, after learning that Shigaraki/All For One are hunting him, after seeing society collapse, and after deciding to leave U.A., that same trait mutates into something frightening:

> **Deku tries to become a one-man shield for everyone else.**

He is not corrupted. He is not arrogant in the ordinary sense. He is still acting from care. But his care has become isolating, compulsive, and self-erasing. He is trying to remove risk from everyone around him by removing himself from ordinary human bonds.

That is the dark side of rescue-first heroism.

## The Muscular rematch shows how far he has come

The Muscular rematch is a useful benchmark because it contrasts directly with the Kota fight.

In the first Muscular fight, Izuku wins by catastrophic bodily sacrifice. He has heart, but his method is desperate. He breaks himself because there is no other way.

In the rematch, he is far more controlled. He uses multiple quirks, reads Muscular’s body, identifies structural weakness, and resolves the fight with much less self-destruction. This is not the same Deku anymore. His technical growth is real.

But the irony is that even as he becomes more technically competent, he becomes psychologically less sustainable. He can defeat Muscular more cleanly, but he is living in a way that is destroying him.

So Vol. 32 separates two kinds of growth:

> **Deku has become better at using power.
> He has not yet become better at allowing himself to be supported.**

That distinction is crucial.

## Lady Nagant attacks the hero system from inside

Lady Nagant is one of the best postwar additions because she embodies the underside of professional hero society.

Stain criticized false heroes from outside.
Dabi exposes Endeavor’s household rot.
Hawks reveals the morally dirty side of prevention.
Nagant shows the state-sanctioned assassination layer beneath the heroic public image.

She is not simply “a fallen hero.” She is someone who was used by the system to preserve hero society’s clean surface. That makes her a dark mirror to Hawks, and a warning to Deku.

Her arc asks:

> **What happens when a hero is asked to keep society peaceful by doing things society cannot admit heroes do?**

That matters for Deku because he is also being pushed toward extra-institutional heroism. He is not assassinating people, but he is drifting toward the same danger zone: lonely, secretive, body-destroying work carried out for the sake of an unstable society.

## Deku’s response to Nagant shows his mature compassion

Deku’s reaction to Nagant is important because he does not treat her as irredeemable. He sees that she is disillusioned, morally damaged, and complicit, but he also sees that her desire for a better world has not completely died.

This is the same ethical pattern from Shigaraki, but in a more recoverable form:

> **Deku refuses to reduce a person to the worst thing they have become.**

That is becoming his mature heroic signature. He is not naïve because he denies harm. He is heroic because he insists that harm-doers often still contain a buried wound, a buried ideal, or a buried self that must be acknowledged if heroism is to be more than containment.

The danger, of course, is that this compassion can become unbearable if he tries to carry every broken person alone.

## All Might and Deku: the mentor bond fractures under self-sacrifice

Deku distancing himself from All Might is one of the saddest parts of this arc.

All Might once pulled Izuku into heroism. Now Izuku is trying to protect All Might by leaving him behind. On the surface, this is concern. Underneath, it is the same isolating logic:

> The people who love me are safer if they are not near me.

This is exactly where Deku begins misunderstanding One For All. The power is built on inheritance, trust, succession, and others reaching out. But Deku’s solo phase turns that inheritance into a private burden. He carries the wills of others while refusing living support from the people still beside him.

That is why this arc is not merely “Deku becomes Batman.” It is more tragic than that:

> **He is trying to become All Might’s lonely symbol without realizing that All Might’s loneliness was part of what broke the old system.**

## Class 1-A saving Deku is the necessary correction

Class 1-A’s intervention is not just friendship power. It is the story correcting Deku’s mistaken interpretation of heroism.

Deku thinks leaving them behind is responsible. His classmates answer:

> You do not get to decide unilaterally that our love, training, agency, and risk belong outside your mission.

That is very important. They are not merely asking to be protected. They are asserting themselves as heroes who also choose danger. Deku’s self-sacrifice becomes subtly paternalistic: he treats his classmates as people whose safety he must manage, rather than as moral agents who have the right to stand beside him.

This is why the confrontation works emotionally. Each classmate reminds him of a specific bond, lesson, or piece of shared growth. They are not trying to defeat him. They are trying to restore him to the network of relationships that made his heroism human.

## Bakugo’s apology is a major moral payoff

Bakugo’s apology is one of the most important character moments in the series.

It matters because it is not framed as “I was secretly nice all along.” Bakugo names the actual wrong: he looked down on Deku because Deku’s weakness and moral courage threatened his own sense of superiority. He admits that Deku’s selflessness disturbed him because it exposed something lacking in himself.

That is a real apology because it does not erase harm, does not excuse him through childhood insecurity, and does not demand immediate forgiveness.

For Deku, it is also profound because Bakugo has always been part of his psychological wound around power. Bakugo represented the world that said weakness means worthlessness. Hearing Bakugo finally acknowledge that history helps bring Deku back from becoming a faceless weapon.

Bakugo’s growth reaches a beautiful inversion:

> The boy who once told Deku to disappear becomes one of the people who insists Deku must come home.

## Uraraka’s speech shifts rescue from individual to social

Uraraka’s speech outside U.A. is one of the clearest statements of the postwar theme.

Deku has spent the solo arc saving civilians and fighting villains, but he himself has become someone who needs saving. Uraraka asks a question the series has been building toward for a long time:

> **Who protects the heroes when they are hurting?**

This is a direct challenge to All Might-era symbolism. Society treated heroes as public utilities: people who arrive, smile, save, and absorb fear. But if heroes are human beings, then society cannot only consume their protection. It has to recognize their vulnerability.

That is why bringing Deku back to U.A. matters. It is not only about resting him. It is about forcing society to see the cost of demanding a perfect savior.

## Stain and All Might: heroism after the symbol

The All Might/Stain encounter is fascinating because Stain, despite being a murderer and fanatic, recognizes something real about All Might’s heroism. But the encounter also shows that All Might himself is struggling to understand what remains of him after losing power.

This connects directly to Deku. Both All Might and Deku have to learn that being a hero cannot mean being an inhuman symbol who carries everyone’s fear alone.

Stain’s mistake was always purity through violence. But his useful function here is that he reminds All Might that heroism was not merely the muscle form. The traces of All Might’s heroism remain in the people he saved and inspired.

That is a softer, more distributed idea of legacy:

> The symbol is not only the man.
> The symbol is also what the man awakened in others.

## Updated Midoriya thesis

Through Volume 33, I would describe Izuku this way:

> **Midoriya Izuku is a rescue-first heir to One For All who nearly turns himself into a lonely instrument of protection, but is pulled back by the living relationships that prove heroism cannot be sustained as solitary martyrdom.**

The major development is not that Deku becomes stronger. He already is strong.

The development is that the story rejects his private-savior interpretation of strength.

Earlier Deku needed to learn:

> I can become a hero.

Then:

> I can use One For All without destroying my body.

Then:

> I can save someone’s future, not just their life.

Then:

> I can see the crying child inside Shigaraki.

Now he has to learn:

> **I am also someone who must be saved.**

That is the emotional center of Vols. 32–33. Izuku’s greatest danger is no longer that he lacks power. It is that he has so much power, responsibility, and compassion that he may erase himself unless others force him to remain a person.
