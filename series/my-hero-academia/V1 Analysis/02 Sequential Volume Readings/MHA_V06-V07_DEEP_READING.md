Done — I’ll treat **My Hero Academia Vols. 6–7** as incorporated into the working corpus.

Current coverage:

## Updated read of the story

Volumes 6–7 deepen *MHA* from “boy earns heroic power” into a story about **what kind of society produces heroes, villains, symbols, imitators, and ideological fanatics**.

The Stain arc is the first major ideological stress test. Until now, the opposition has mostly been personal or situational: Bakugo’s domination complex, Todoroki’s family trauma, Shigaraki’s destructive immaturity, U.A. combat trials. Stain changes the field because he is not just violent; he has a theory of heroism. A poisonous one, but a theory.

That matters because *MHA* is now asking:

> If hero society rewards fame, money, rankings, spectacle, and branding, what separates a true hero from a licensed celebrity with a quirk?

The story does **not** simply validate Stain. He is a murderer and a fanatic. But it does let his critique land hard enough that villains rally around it. That is the setting becoming more serious: hero society has moral weaknesses, and villains are beginning to exploit those weaknesses rhetorically.

## Midoriya’s biggest development: Full Cowl

Gran Torino’s training is one of the most important shifts in Izuku’s relationship to power.

Before this, One For All is basically a borrowed cannon. Izuku fires it at decisive moments and pays for it with broken bones. It is heroic, but crude. He is still treating All Might’s power like an external miracle he can summon.

Full Cowl changes that.

Now the lesson is:

> **Power cannot remain a special sacrificial event. It has to become livable, distributed, habitual, and embodied.**

That is a huge symbolic step. Izuku is no longer merely “using All Might’s power.” He is beginning to make One For All part of his own nervous system, movement, judgment, and physical identity.

This also corrects his earlier self-destructive heroism. His first instinct is still rescue. But rescue cannot depend on destroying himself every time. If Vols. 1–5 show that he is worthy of One For All, Vol. 6 starts showing what it means to become technically worthy of it.

## Stain and Iida: heroism versus vengeance

Iida’s arc is the dark mirror of Midoriya’s rescue ethic.

Iida is not selfish in an ordinary sense. His brother is attacked, his family legacy is wounded, and his anger is understandable. But understandable anger still corrupts him. He uses the language of duty and heroism while pursuing revenge.

That makes Iida’s fall more subtle than Bakugo’s arrogance. Bakugo wants victory and superiority. Iida wants justice, or thinks he does. But his grief narrows his moral field until the person in front of him becomes less important than punishing Stain.

Midoriya’s intervention matters because he is not only saving Iida physically. He is saving him from becoming the kind of person who uses heroic power for private retaliation.

So Izuku’s ethic expands again:

> **A hero saves people from danger, but also from the moral collapse that pain can cause.**

This is where Midoriya begins to feel less like a mere “good boy protagonist” and more like the moral center of the series. He sees the endangered person even when that person is his friend making a terrible choice.

## One For All versus All For One

The All Might exposition in Vol. 7 gives the series its deeper mythic structure.

One For All is not simply a strong quirk passed down through generations. It is a historical answer to All For One. That makes the two powers philosophically opposed.

**All For One** means power as appropriation: take, hoard, redistribute, control, make people dependent.

**One For All** means power as entrusted inheritance: receive, cultivate, pass forward, protect others, preserve will across generations.

That makes Midoriya’s power unusually moralized. It is not “strength” in the abstract. It is strength shaped by consent, succession, memory, and resistance to domination.

This is why Izuku differs so sharply from characters like Jinwoo or Keyaru. Jinwoo’s power is autonomy and protection. Keyaru’s power is retaliation after objectification. Izuku’s power is **entrusted moral continuity**. He is not just becoming stronger; he is becoming the next bearer of a long ethical struggle.

## Bakugo and Midoriya: the ideal becomes terrifying

The final-exam setup with Midoriya and Bakugo against All Might is brilliant because it turns their shared ideal into an obstacle.

All Might is not merely their teacher here. He is the thing both boys have misunderstood.

For Midoriya, All Might is almost sacred. That reverence can become paralysis. To mature, Izuku has to stop treating All Might as an untouchable god and start responding to him as a real opponent, mentor, and human being.

For Bakugo, All Might represents victory. Bakugo’s childhood psychology becomes clearer: he was praised for power, treated as exceptional, and came to see heroism as winning. Midoriya’s attempts to help him felt insulting because they implied that the “weak” boy saw something lacking in him. That does not excuse Bakugo’s cruelty, but it makes the cruelty more psychologically specific.

The exam forces both boys to confront distorted versions of admiration:

-   Midoriya admires All Might so much he risks being overwhelmed by the ideal.

-   Bakugo admires All Might as victory incarnate and cannot tolerate retreat.

-   Both have to learn that heroism is not reducible to either worship or winning.


## Updated Midoriya thesis

Through Volume 7, I would now describe Izuku this way:

> **Midoriya Izuku is a rescue-first moral idealist whose power begins as entrusted inheritance, but whose growth depends on transforming self-sacrificial impulse into embodied discipline. He is learning that heroism means saving bodies, saving agency, resisting vengeance, and carrying inherited power without being swallowed by the symbol that gave it to him.**

The key shift from Vols. 6–7 is that Izuku is no longer merely proving he has a heroic heart. The story is now forcing him to ask whether that heart can survive contact with ideology, revenge, institutional heroism, villain recruitment, and the terrifying weight of All Might’s legacy.
