Done — I’ll treat **My Hero Academia Vols. 34–35** as incorporated into the working corpus.

Current coverage:

## The big shift: the story becomes an argument about connection versus isolation

Volumes 32–33 pulled Deku back from solitary martyrdom. Vols. 34–35 broaden that idea into the final-war structure.

All For One’s logic is isolation: isolate Shigaraki, isolate Deku, isolate Aoyama through guilt, isolate society through fear, isolate heroes by exposing their failures, isolate individuals until they become usable pieces.

The heroes’ answer is not simply “Deku gets stronger.” It is coordination:

> Aoyama lies to All For One.
> Monoma copies key quirks.
> Shinso’s brainwashing becomes strategically essential.
> Mei and the support course rebuild the battlefield.
> Class 1-A fights as a network.
> The pro heroes split threats into manageable fronts.
> Even civilians and noncombatants matter as part of the system of resistance.

This is the series rejecting lone-savior heroism at the structural level.

## Star and Stripe: All Might’s legacy goes global

Star and Stripe is not just an American cameo or power-scaling device. Her existence shows that All Might’s symbol was never only Japanese. He inspired an international heroic imagination.

That matters because she is an All Might successor in a different sense from Deku. Deku inherited **One For All**. Star inherited **the image of heroic arrival**: the overwhelming protector who comes because someone once saved her.

Her fight against Shigaraki/All For One is also important because it reframes quirk theft. New Order is not merely stolen property. It carries will, identity, and resistance. When Star weaponizes her own quirk against All For One from within, the series again insists that power is not morally neutral machinery. A quirk can be tied to personhood.

So Star’s sacrifice is a global echo of One For All’s theme:

> Power can be passed forward as will, not merely possessed as ability.

## Shigaraki/All For One becomes a crisis of selfhood

The Star fight also sharpens the strange, unstable condition of Shigaraki and All For One. The enemy is no longer simply Shigaraki using All For One’s power, or All For One wearing Shigaraki as a vessel. It is a contested identity — a fused, parasitic, unstable being.

This matters for Deku’s ethics because the question “save Shigaraki” becomes harder. What exactly is Shigaraki now? A villain? A victim? A host? A successor? A prison? A child buried under layers of hatred and possession?

Deku’s earlier insight — that he saw a crying child inside Shigaraki — remains morally central, but these volumes make the practical problem more grotesque:

> Saving Tenko may require fighting through All For One’s attempt to overwrite him.

That makes “saving the villain” less sentimental. It is not “be nice to Shigaraki.” It is more like an exorcism of domination.

## Aoyama is one of Deku’s clearest mirrors

The Aoyama reveal is one of the most important Midoriya-adjacent developments in the whole late story.

Aoyama and Deku are both originally quirkless boys who received power from someone else. But the moral structure of the gift is opposite.

That parallel is brutal. Deku’s quirklessness led to a heroic encounter. Aoyama’s quirklessness led his parents into desperation, and that desperation made him vulnerable to All For One.

This also deepens the series’ class/family/social anxiety around quirks. Being quirkless in this society is not only personally painful. For Aoyama’s family, it becomes a status panic so severe that they accept a deal with evil.

## Deku’s response to Aoyama shows his mature heroism

Deku’s response is exactly what distinguishes him from a purely punitive hero.

He does not deny Aoyama’s betrayal. He does not pretend the harm was imaginary. But he also refuses to reduce Aoyama to “traitor” as a total identity.

That is consistent with the postwar Deku we have been tracking:

> He sees the person buried under the role.
> He sees Nagant under “assassin.”
> He sees Tenko under “Shigaraki.”
> He sees Aoyama under “traitor.”

This does not make him naïve. In fact, it is a more demanding ethic than simple forgiveness. Deku’s position is closer to:

> If you still want to reach toward the light, then your past cannot be the final word on you.

That is why Aoyama matters. He becomes a test case for whether hero society can reintegrate someone compromised by All For One rather than merely discard him.

## Support course and Mei: heroism is built by unseen labor

The Mei/support course material is also thematically important. The late story could easily become only about god-tier combatants, but the support course insists that heroism is infrastructural.

Mei does not fight like Deku. But without rebuilt equipment, floating battlefield systems, emergency repairs, communications, containment, evacuation support, and technological maintenance, the heroes cannot even hold the stage on which the final battle occurs.

That broadens the moral universe:

> Heroism is not only the punch.
> It is the person who builds the brace, fixes the wall, repairs the launch system, and keeps the battlefield from collapsing.

This matters because it is another anti-lone-savior argument. Deku’s body alone cannot save the world. The world is saved, if it is saved, by a network of labor.

## Final war: the heroes weaponize cooperation

The divide-and-conquer plan in Vol. 35 is a direct answer to the villains’ overwhelming concentration of power. The heroes cannot defeat the enemy as one big chaotic mass. They have to separate threats, assign matchups, control terrain, and create artificial conditions where impossible enemies become barely fightable.

That is a very mature strategic development for the series. It means heroism is now:

-   intelligence work

-   deception

-   transport control

-   quirk synergy

-   terrain engineering

-   emotional targeting

-   civilian trust

-   moral resolve

-   and only then combat strength


This is where *MHA* becomes less like “who is strongest?” and more like “can society coordinate enough trust to resist a force built on fear?”

## Deku being pulled away by Toga is symbolically perfect

Deku is supposed to go to Shigaraki. That is the obvious protagonist path. But Toga pulls him into a different battlefield, and that matters because the final war is not only Deku’s story.

Toga is another version of the series’ central problem: someone whose quirk and desire were treated as monstrous, who became monstrous, and who now asks for recognition in a way that endangers others.

Deku cannot simply ignore her, but he also cannot be the only one who answers every wounded villain. That is why the final arc needs Ochako, Shoto, Shoji, Bakugo, and others. The villains are not merely obstacles on Deku’s way to Shigaraki. Each one is tied to a specific social failure and needs a specific moral counterforce.

That is another correction to Deku’s solo-phase mistake:

> Not every person who needs saving is Deku’s alone to save.

## Floating U.A.: society becomes a battlefield-machine

The floating U.A. battlefield is one of the clearest visual/metaphorical expressions of late *MHA*. The school itself becomes a weaponized defensive structure. The place where children trained to be heroes is converted into a mobile containment system for the worst threat in the world.

That is both inspiring and disturbing.

Inspiring, because everyone’s training, labor, and ingenuity converge.

Disturbing, because it shows how completely the students’ education has been consumed by war. U.A. is no longer just a school. It is the last fortress of hero society.

## Shoto versus Dabi: inherited power as family reckoning

The opening of the Shoto/Dabi conflict is a strong parallel to Deku/Shigaraki.

Deku faces the dark product of Nana’s broken family legacy.
Shoto faces the dark product of Endeavor’s broken family legacy.

Dabi/Toya is not simply “evil fire brother.” He is Endeavor’s ambition given a ruined body and a hatred that survived where a child did not. His existence makes Endeavor’s abuse impossible to contain as private family history. It has become a public battlefield.

Shoto’s role is therefore not just to defeat Dabi. It is to answer the question:

> Can inherited fire be used to stop the destruction caused by the way that fire was inherited?

That is very close to Deku’s One For All problem, but within a family tragedy rather than a historical quirk lineage.

## Updated Midoriya thesis

Through Volume 35, I would describe Izuku this way:

> **Midoriya Izuku is a rescue-first heir to One For All whose mature heroism now depends on accepting that he cannot be the sole answer to every wound. His task is not to replace society as a lone savior, but to fight as part of a network strong enough to save people All For One tried to isolate, use, or erase.**

The key development is that Deku’s moral instinct remains the center, but the story has stopped letting him monopolize salvation.

He can see the crying child in Shigaraki.
He can reach out to Aoyama.
He can inspire others.
But he cannot personally resolve every tragedy produced by hero society.

So the final-war question is larger than “Can Deku beat Shigaraki?”

It is:

> **Can the world that failed Tenko, Aoyama, Toga, Toya, Nagant, and others become capable of saving people before All For One reaches them first?**
