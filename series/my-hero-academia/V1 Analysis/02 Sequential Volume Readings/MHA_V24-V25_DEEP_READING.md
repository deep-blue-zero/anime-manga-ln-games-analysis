Done — I’ll treat **My Hero Academia Vols. 24–25** as incorporated into the working corpus.

Current coverage:

## The big shift: the villains now receive their own “training arc”

Volumes 24–25 are a major structural inversion. Up to this point, the students have had most of the growth framework: internships, training camps, exams, licenses, support gear, mentors, and peer rivalry. *My Villain Academia* gives the League something similar, but in a darker form.

Toga, Twice, Spinner, and Shigaraki all get development not by being taught in a school, but by being placed under lethal pressure. Their “growth” comes from injury, exhaustion, trauma recall, ideological conflict, and the need to survive.

That is important because the series is no longer letting the villains be merely external threats. They now have histories, internal solidarities, grievances, and distorted forms of self-actualization. The League becomes more frightening because it starts functioning like an anti-hero class: a group of broken people who become stronger by embracing what society wanted suppressed.

## Shigaraki becomes Midoriya’s dark inheritance mirror

Shigaraki’s origin is the most important material in these volumes.

The Tenko Shimura reveal reframes him completely. He is not simply “evil because All For One groomed him,” though All For One’s grooming remains crucial. He is also the product of a household built around suppressed heroism, inherited trauma, adult cowardice, and a child’s desperate need for someone to reach him.

The Nana Shimura connection makes this brutal. Nana gave up her family to protect them from All For One, but that sacrifice mutates across generations into Kotaro’s hatred of heroes. Her heroic choice leaves a wound that All For One later exploits.

That gives *MHA* one of its sharpest tragic ironies:

> **A heroic sacrifice meant to protect a family eventually helps create the child All For One turns into All Might’s successor’s opposite.**

Midoriya inherits Nana’s will through One For All.
Shigaraki inherits the damage left behind by Nana’s absence through family trauma.

So the two are not just hero and villain. They are two descendants of the same historical wound.

## “No one reached out” becomes the anti-rescue thesis

Tenko’s wandering after the family tragedy is one of the most damning scenes in the series. People see a disturbed, bloodied, abandoned child and assume someone else will handle it. That is exactly the kind of social failure Shigaraki later despises.

This retroactively sharpens Midoriya’s ethics. Izuku’s whole identity began with moving when others froze. He ran toward Bakugo when the adults did not. He reached Todoroki when everyone else saw only a tournament opponent. He reached Iida before vengeance consumed him. He reached Kota, Eri, and even in a limited sense Gentle.

Shigaraki is the nightmare case of the child nobody reached.

That makes him the anti-Midoriya not because he “hates saving,” but because his life is what happens when rescue never arrives and a predatory adult arrives instead.

> **Midoriya is the child who was seen by All Might. Shigaraki is the child who was seen by All For One.**

That may be the cleanest contrast in the series so far.

## Decay becomes anti-care

Shigaraki’s quirk is not just a combat ability. Symbolically, Decay is the opposite of the rescue ethic.

Midoriya’s power is touch as rescue: the hand reaching out, the body moving forward, the inherited strength used to protect.

Shigaraki’s power is touch as destruction: the hand reaching out means disintegration, contamination, loss of form, and the collapse of everything held together.

That is why the hands motif is so potent. In *MHA*, a hand can save, pass on power, restrain, comfort, abuse, or destroy. Shigaraki’s tragedy is that touch itself becomes catastrophic.

## Twice is the emotional heart of the League

Twice’s material is probably the strongest non-Shigaraki part of *My Villain Academia*. His trauma is identity collapse: he does not know whether he is the original, and his power literalizes that fracture. He is funny and chaotic, but his underlying terror is existential.

When he finally overcomes the psychological block on cloning himself, it is not because he becomes “sane” in a conventional sense. It is because his attachment to the League gives him enough certainty to act.

His breakthrough says:

> I may not know what I am, but I know who my people are.

That makes the League’s bond feel real. They are criminals and killers, but they are also one another’s refuge. This is part of what makes them dangerous: the story gives them the same emotional fuel that hero groups get, but aims it toward destruction.

## Toga and the politics of “being myself”

Toga’s conflict with Curious and the Meta Liberation Army is also important. The MLA wants to convert individual quirk-expression into ideology and propaganda. Toga resists that framing because her desire is not theoretical. She does not want to be a symbol in someone else’s political narrative. She wants to live according to her own warped feeling of love.

That makes her both more personal and more disturbing than the MLA. Her “self-expression” is not harmless authenticity; it is predatory. But the story does make clear that suppressing her nature without understanding it helped create the monster she became.

This complicates the theme of quirks as identity:

> A quirk may be part of the self, but the self is not automatically innocent.

The MLA’s liberation ideology tries to skip that problem.

## The Meta Liberation Army: quirk freedom as mass politics

Re-Destro and the Meta Liberation Army introduce a serious ideological challenge to hero society. They argue that quirks are not just tools to be licensed and regulated; they are expressions of personal identity that society suppresses.

That critique has force. *MHA* has repeatedly shown that quirk society judges people by power type, appearance, usefulness, and marketability. Shinso is suspected because his quirk “looks villainous.” Toga is pathologized without being understood. Heteromorphic discrimination exists around the edges. Quirk regulation is necessary, but it also produces resentment.

But the MLA is not a healthy liberation movement. It is corporate, militarized, cultic, and perfectly willing to sacrifice people for ideology. Re-Destro talks about liberation, but he is also measuring, branding, publishing, commanding, and mobilizing. His “freedom” is still hierarchical.

That makes Shigaraki’s victory darkly fitting. The MLA wants liberation as doctrine. Shigaraki wants destruction without apology. He defeats them because his emptiness is more absolute than their ideology.

## Shigaraki’s leadership is terrifying because it is anti-meaning

By the end of the Deika battle, Shigaraki is not a careful political theorist. He is not Stain. He is not Re-Destro. He is not Overhaul.

He becomes terrifying because he can absorb political infrastructure without being captured by its meaning. He does not need the MLA’s ideology to be true. He needs its army, money, technology, and social reach. The result is much worse than either side alone: the League’s trauma-driven destructiveness fused with an organized liberation movement.

That is why the Paranormal Liberation Front feels like a genuine escalation. The villains now have emotional cohesion, ideology, manpower, resources, and Shigaraki as a symbolic center.

## Midoriya’s absence is itself meaningful

Midoriya is largely absent from the main villain material, but that absence matters. While he is trying to understand One For All’s past users, Shigaraki is recovering his own buried past. The structure is parallel:

-   Midoriya discovers inherited memory through vestiges.

-   Shigaraki recovers repressed memory through trauma.

-   Midoriya’s history pushes him toward responsibility.

-   Shigaraki’s history releases him into destruction.


Both are becoming more fully themselves by encountering the past. But one is integrating inherited will; the other is annihilating the restraints that kept his trauma partially buried.

## Endeavor Agency: Midoriya’s next technical stage

Volume 25 then shifts into Endeavor’s agency, and this is a very important training setup.

Endeavor is not a moral ideal for Midoriya, but he is an excellent technical teacher. He understands output, heat management, simultaneous processing, bodily habit, and the difference between knowing something intellectually and making it automatic under pressure.

That is exactly what Midoriya needs now.

After Blackwhip, Izuku’s problem is no longer merely “control super strength.” He has to learn layered control:

> Full Cowl.
> Air Force.
> Blackwhip.
> situational analysis.
> rescue judgment.
> movement.
> emotional regulation.
> all at once.

Endeavor’s lesson about doing one thing at a time until it becomes automatic is therefore perfect. It reframes Midoriya’s growth as professional skill acquisition rather than shonen intensity.

## Bakugo, Todoroki, and Midoriya under Endeavor

The Endeavor Agency setup also makes the trio sharper.

**Bakugo** is still pursuing victory, but now his growth is about speed, compression, and making rescue compatible with aggression. He wants to beat everyone to the scene.

**Todoroki** is trying to learn from Endeavor without being swallowed by him. His problem is not only technique; it is whether he can take useful instruction from the father who harmed his family.

**Midoriya** is trying to transform multiplicity into discipline. He has too much inherited possibility and not enough practiced automaticity.

Endeavor becomes the strange common teacher for all three because he embodies both enormous technical mastery and moral failure. The students can learn from him without absolving him. That distinction matters.

## Updated Midoriya thesis

Through Volume 25, I would describe Izuku this way:

> **Midoriya Izuku is a rescue-first heir to One For All whose central challenge is now integration: integrating inherited power, inherited history, multiple quirks, professional technique, and the knowledge that his villain counterpart is also an heir to a broken legacy.**

The key change is that his burden is no longer only internal.

Before:

> Can he master One For All?

Now:

> Can he master One For All fast enough to answer a world where Shigaraki has mastered Decay, inherited All For One’s destructive legacy, and gained an army?

This also sets up the deepest ethical challenge ahead:

> If Shigaraki is the child nobody saved, what does “saving” mean when that child has become the person destroying everyone else?

That question is now sitting under Midoriya’s entire future arc.
