Done — I’ll treat **My Hero Academia Vols. 14–15** as incorporated into the working corpus.

Current coverage:

## The big shift: Midoriya’s heroism meets professional tragedy

Volumes 14–15 deepen the exact tension that has been building since the beginning:

> **Midoriya’s instinct is to save the person in front of him immediately, but professional heroism sometimes requires patience, secrecy, planning, and restraint.**

That becomes painful in the Eri encounter.

Earlier, Izuku’s rescue instinct was usually vindicated. He runs toward Bakugo and All Might chooses him. He saves Uraraka and earns rescue points. He reaches Todoroki emotionally and helps him reclaim his fire. He protects Kota and proves heroism to a child who hates heroes.

But Eri is different. Izuku sees a child in danger. His body and conscience want to act. Yet Mirio and Nighteye’s professional judgment says: do not blow the investigation, do not provoke Overhaul, do not ruin the chance to save her properly.

That scene is devastating because both sides are understandable. A reckless rescue could fail and endanger Eri further. But waiting also means handing her back to abuse.

So Midoriya’s ethic gains a tragic complication:

> **Sometimes “saving someone now” and “saving someone correctly” are not the same thing.**

That is a more mature moral problem than anything in the entrance exam or Sports Festival.

## Deku vs. Kacchan Part 2: rescue and victory finally become a pair

The Bakugo confrontation is one of the most important psychological chapters so far.

Bakugo’s breakdown is not just anger. It is guilt. He believes All Might’s end is connected to his own kidnapping. That reframes him sharply. He is still arrogant, cruel, and emotionally primitive in many ways, but he is not emotionally shallow. His obsession with victory is carrying shame, fear, and a desperate need to prove that the ideal he worshipped did not collapse because of him.

For Midoriya, this fight matters because Bakugo becomes more than a childhood tormentor. He becomes the person who embodies the part of All Might that Izuku lacks.

Up to this point, Izuku has understood All Might mainly through **rescue**: the smile, the hand extended, the person who saves everyone. Bakugo understood All Might through **victory**: the hero who always wins, never loses, never yields.

The fight clarifies that both boys have incomplete inheritances.

-   Izuku has “save.”

-   Bakugo has “win.”

-   All Might was great because he fused both.


This is crucial for Midoriya’s philosophy of power. His early view was basically “power exists to save people.” That remains true, but it is now incomplete. A hero who cannot win may fail to save. A hero who only wins may fail to reassure, protect, or care.

So the updated lesson is:

> **Power exists to save, but saving often requires victory; victory only becomes heroic when it serves saving.**

That is the clearest synthesis the story has given so far.

## Nighteye and Mirio make Izuku’s insecurity rational

Sir Nighteye’s presence is important because he gives institutional form to Izuku’s private doubt.

Until now, Izuku’s insecurity was rooted in his quirkless past and physical inadequacy. But Nighteye introduces a more painful question:

> **Was All Might wrong to choose him?**

Mirio makes that question dangerous because he is not a strawman. He is not secretly corrupt, arrogant, or emotionally unworthy. He is genuinely heroic, charming, disciplined, and already far more professionally complete than Izuku. His quirk is difficult and punishing, but he has mastered it through absurd levels of practice. In many ways, Mirio represents what Izuku is trying to become: a person who has made an impossible power fully livable inside his body.

So Izuku’s legitimacy crisis becomes sharper:

> It is not enough that he was chosen. He has to become someone whose choice can be justified.

That does not mean Nighteye is simply right. All Might chose Izuku because of heroic essence, not résumé optimization. But the story is honest enough to admit that heroic essence alone does not end the argument.

## Overhaul: the anti-Midoriya theory of power

Overhaul is a very different villain from Stain, Shigaraki, or All For One.

Stain is fanatic purity.
Shigaraki is cultivated resentment and destruction.
All For One is acquisitive domination and inheritance-corruption.
Overhaul is clinical instrumentalization.

He treats people as parts, infections, materials, and research inputs. His relationship to Eri is the most horrifying inversion of Midoriya’s rescue ethic so far. To Midoriya, a vulnerable child is the clearest possible reason to use power. To Overhaul, a vulnerable child is a resource.

That makes Eri central. She is not merely “a hostage.” She is the living proof of what power becomes when stripped of care.

Midoriya’s whole moral universe says:

> The more vulnerable someone is, the greater your obligation to protect them.

Overhaul’s world says:

> The more vulnerable someone is, the easier they are to use.

That is one of the cleanest ethical oppositions in the series so far.

## Eri makes Midoriya’s rescue instinct hurt

The alley encounter is painful because Izuku knows. He does not have full information, but he knows enough emotionally. Eri is terrified. She clings to him. Her body is signaling danger even when the adult situation demands restraint.

This gives Midoriya a new kind of wound: not the wound of being too weak, but the wound of having let someone go.

That is different from Muscular/Kota. With Kota, the ethical path was simple even if the fight was brutal: stop the monster, save the child. With Eri, the ethical path is contaminated by uncertainty, law enforcement, strategy, and risk management.

So his rescue-first ethics is becoming less childlike. He is learning that the world can create situations where every option feels like a betrayal.

## Kirishima is a useful contrast to Midoriya

Kirishima’s material also matters because it shows another model of heroic self-authorship.

Midoriya’s heroism often looks innate: his body moves before he thinks. Kirishima’s heroism is more consciously constructed. He is ashamed of freezing. He admires Crimson Riot. He wants to become someone who does not hesitate. His “manliness” language can sound simple, but psychologically it is about building courage over fear.

That makes him a good counterpoint to Izuku.

Izuku’s problem is not usually fear. His problem is self-erasure: he will spend himself too readily.

Kirishima’s problem is the opposite: he fears that when the decisive moment comes, he may not move.

Both are trying to become heroes, but from opposite weaknesses:

-   **Midoriya** must learn survival, restraint, and sustainable force.

-   **Kirishima** must learn commitment, presence, and not freezing.


That broadens the series’ view of heroism. Not everyone starts with the same moral reflex. Some people have to build the reflex they admire.

## Updated Midoriya thesis

Through Volume 15, I would now describe Izuku this way:

> **Midoriya Izuku is a rescue-first heir to One For All whose growth is shifting from moral instinct to moral judgment. He no longer merely has to prove that he wants to save people; he has to learn when to act, when to wait, when to delegate, when to win, and how to bear the guilt of imperfect rescue.**

That is the biggest change from these volumes.

Earlier Izuku’s ethic could be summarized as:

> See danger → move → save.

Now it is becoming:

> See suffering → understand the situation → preserve the chance of rescue → act with enough force, timing, and professionalism that saving does not become self-indulgent recklessness.

That is a harder, more adult version of heroism.

The irony is that his purest instinct is still correct. Eri really does need saving. But the world is now complex enough that correct instinct is not enough. Midoriya must become the kind of hero who can save the person in front of him **and** survive the systems, secrets, institutions, and villains surrounding that person.
