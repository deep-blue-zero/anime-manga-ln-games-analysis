Done — I’ll treat **My Hero Academia Vols. 36–37** as incorporated into the working corpus.

Current coverage:

## The big shift: the final war becomes a test of every incomplete rescue

Volumes 36–37 are where the final war stops being merely “Deku versus Shigaraki” and becomes a distributed reckoning. Each battlefield is tied to a different failure of hero society:

**Shoto vs. Dabi** is the Endeavor family’s private abuse becoming public fire.
**Shoji/Koda vs. Spinner’s mob** is heteromorph discrimination becoming political violence.
**Endeavor/Hawks vs. All For One** is the old generation confronting the manipulator who built the current catastrophe.
**Bakugo’s collapse** is the victory-obsessed rival finally reaching the limit of heroic embodiment.
**Deku’s return** is the rescue-first protagonist arriving too late to prevent pain, but not too late to continue the fight.

This is important because the story is no longer letting Deku be the single answer to every wound. The final war is structured around specific characters answering specific social and emotional failures.

## Shoto versus Dabi: inherited fire as moral answer

Shoto’s battle with Dabi is one of the cleanest examples of *MHA*’s recurring theme:

> **Inherited power can either reproduce harm or become the means of stopping it.**

Dabi is Endeavor’s ambition turned necrotic. His body, hatred, and fire are all shaped by the original Todoroki family sin: a father trying to manufacture a successor rather than raise children.

Shoto’s answer is not “I am stronger than you because I inherited better.” It is more morally precise:

> I inherited this family’s fire too, but I will not use it the way Endeavor did.

His Phosphor technique matters because it synthesizes both halves of his quirk. Earlier, Todoroki’s arc was about reclaiming his fire from Endeavor. Here, that reclamation becomes social responsibility. He uses the power born from family abuse to contain the brother produced by that abuse.

That is not a full emotional resolution, but it is a heroic answer: Shoto does not deny the family wound, and he does not let Dabi burn the world with it.

## Endeavor versus All For One: the old hero faces his own ugliness

Endeavor’s fight against All For One is interesting because it is not just a power contest. Endeavor is forced to fight while spiritually disassembled.

All For One’s main weapon is not only quirks. It is psychological violation. He understands shame, legacy, paternal failure, and the desire to collapse under guilt. He tries to reduce Endeavor to his worst truth: the man whose ambition created Dabi.

Endeavor’s counter is not absolution. He does not become pure. He keeps fighting while accepting that his sins are real. That is why this material works better than a simple redemption scene.

> Endeavor does not defeat guilt. He fights while carrying it.

That is a more mature version of atonement than “I suffered, therefore I am forgiven.” The story continues to distinguish between responsibility and absolution.

## Jiro and Tokoyami: “extras” wound All For One

Jiro’s role against All For One is easy to underrate, but thematically it is excellent.

All For One looks at weaker heroes as extras, side characters, insects, bodies with quirks to use or discard. That is his entire worldview. He can only understand people hierarchically: major players, useful pawns, stolen abilities, disposable bodies.

So when Jiro and Tokoyami help damage him, the point is not that they are secretly stronger than him. It is that All For One’s worldview is wrong.

> The people he dismisses as insignificant can still wound history.

This fits the whole final-war structure. All For One wants a world organized around singular domination. The heroes answer with distributed resistance. No one person, not even Deku, is the whole story.

## All For One’s rewind: victory as self-consumption

All For One using rewind is one of the most fitting late-stage villain developments. He gains terrifying momentum, but the cost is that his body is literally moving backward toward disappearance.

Symbolically, this is perfect.

All For One has always been a parasite of history. He steals quirks, bodies, institutions, names, and legacies. But now his attempt to reclaim dominance is also consuming the time he has left. His power is immense, but temporally unstable.

That makes him an inversion of One For All.

One For All passes will forward.
All For One rewinds himself backward while trying to possess the future.

That contrast is probably one of the best late-series symbolic structures.

## Bakugo’s collapse: victory reaches its limit

Bakugo’s apparent death is the emotional center of Volume 36’s U.A. battlefield material.

Bakugo has spent the series learning that victory alone is incomplete. By this point, he has learned teamwork, rescue, tactical humility, and the need to support Deku rather than merely surpass him. But against Shigaraki/All For One, he encounters a limit that cannot be overcome by sheer will or aggression.

The tragedy is that he is finally becoming the kind of hero who can integrate victory and saving — and then his body is crushed by the scale of the final enemy.

That is why Edgeshot’s intervention matters. Bakugo, the boy who once equated strength with solitary superiority, is kept alive by another hero literally entering the interior of his body to repair him.

It is a grotesque but powerful image of dependence:

> Bakugo can only continue because another hero gives himself to preserve him.

That is almost the opposite of early Bakugo’s worldview.

## Deku’s return: rage must be integrated, not denied

When Deku returns and sees Bakugo’s condition, his reaction is one of the best tests of his mature heroism.

His rage is understandable. It would be false if he did not feel it. But the key point is that he cannot let rage become the driver of One For All. We have already seen what happens when Blackwhip erupts through emotion. Now the stakes are much higher.

Mirio’s intervention matters because it keeps Deku connected to the actual saving goal. Deku must not become a weaponized grief response. He has to remain the moral author of One For All even while furious.

That is a major difference between Deku and Shigaraki:

-   Shigaraki’s trauma releases destruction.

-   Deku’s trauma has to be metabolized into controlled protection.


This does not mean Deku is emotionless. It means his emotions must remain answerable to saving.

## Gearshift: One For All reaches its most dangerous refinement

Gearshift is a fascinating addition because it changes Deku’s combat style from “more power” into something stranger: acceleration, timing, physics distortion, and layered quirk synergy.

By this point, Deku is no longer merely punching with inherited strength. He is stacking One For All, Fa Jin, Blackwhip, Float, Danger Sense, Smokescreen, and Gearshift into an integrated system. This is the technical endpoint of the long journey from “borrowed cannon” to “authored fighting style.”

But Gearshift also has a cost and time limit. That is crucial. Deku’s late-stage power is not simple godhood. It is extreme output under severe constraints.

So the final fight’s tension is not only:

> Can Deku hit hard enough?

It is:

> Can Deku use an inherited multi-generational power system precisely enough, quickly enough, and morally enough before his body and timing fail?

That is a much more interesting version of shonen escalation.

## Shoji and Koda: heroism against social abandonment

The heteromorph front is one of the clearest examples of the series trying to broaden heroism beyond Deku’s personal arc.

Spinner’s mob is not built from nothing. The anger of heteromorphs has a real social basis: prejudice, exclusion, fear, rural discrimination, and being treated as monstrous because of appearance. Spinner becomes a symbol because the world created people who were ready to see themselves in him.

But the mob’s grievance does not justify atrocity. That is the balance the story tries to hold:

> Social pain can explain why people become radicalized, but it does not make attacking a hospital righteous.

Shoji’s role is important because he answers from inside the wound. He does not deny discrimination. He bears it on his own body. But he refuses to let pain become indiscriminate vengeance.

That makes Shoji’s heroism very different from Deku’s. Deku often reaches toward another’s pain from outside. Shoji speaks from shared pain and says: this cannot be the answer.

## Spinner and Kurogiri: attachment as villain infrastructure

Spinner’s march toward Kurogiri is tragic because his motives are tangled. He is being manipulated by All For One’s side, but his loyalty to Shigaraki is real. Like Twice, he shows that villain bonds are not fake just because they are destructive.

Kurogiri/Shirakumo deepens the same theme. He is a person turned into infrastructure, memory turned into service, friendship turned into programmed loyalty. When Kurogiri begins to re-emerge, the question is not simply “which side gets the warp quirk?” It is:

> Can a person reduced to a tool still contain enough self to choose?

That question connects directly to Shigaraki, Aoyama, Nagant, and even Hawks. Late *MHA* is full of people turned into instruments by larger systems.

## Updated Midoriya thesis

Through Volume 37, I would describe Izuku this way:

> **Midoriya Izuku is a rescue-first heir to One For All who has finally become a complete multi-quirk combatant, but whose real test is whether he can use that completed power without becoming a rage-driven weapon or a solitary savior.**

The technical arc is approaching completion. Deku can now combine inherited quirks into something genuinely his own.

But the ethical arc is still open.

He has to defeat Shigaraki/All For One.
He has to protect Bakugo and the others.
He has to keep seeing Tenko inside Shigaraki.
He has to trust that other heroes can answer other wounds.
He has to carry rage without letting rage define the power.

The most important shift from these volumes is that *MHA* has fully committed to its anti-All-For-One answer:

> **Domination concentrates everything into one will. Heroism distributes responsibility across many people who choose to stand together.**

Deku is central, but he is not the whole answer. That is the point.
