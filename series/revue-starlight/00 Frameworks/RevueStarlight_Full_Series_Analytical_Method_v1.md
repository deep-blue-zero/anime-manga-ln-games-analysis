# 『少女☆歌劇 レヴュースタァライト』
## Full-Series Analytical Method
### Japanese-original audiovisual synthesis method for the TV anime, OVAs, and sequel film

## 0. Status and purpose

This document governs the **next complete-series synthesis** of the animated *Revue Starlight* corpus after the sequential close reading of:

- the twelve-episode television series;
- the three short OVAs;
- and the sequel film, including its ending credits and post-credit audition.

It does **not** replace the existing close-reading protocols.

Instead, the methods operate at three different scales:

1. **`RevueStarlightAnalyticalMethod_MusicalDramaturgy_v2.md`** governs episode-level audiovisual close reading and local Revue analysis.
2. **`RevueStarlightMovieAnalyticalMethod_v1.md`** governs feature-film movement analysis, long-range cinematic rhyme, and film-level Revue architecture.
3. **This document** governs the retrospective **full-series synthesis**, where evidence from the complete corpus can finally be compared across media, episodes, relationships, songs, Revues, and recurring theatrical forms.

The synthesis should not merely concatenate earlier episode analyses. It must ask what becomes visible **only after the complete corpus has been read**.

The governing principle is:

> **Read Revue Starlight simultaneously as character drama, musical theatre, animation, performance theory, relationship narrative, and a work about the ethics of turning life into roles—without allowing any one layer to erase the others.**

A second governing principle is:

> **A Revue is not simply symbolic combat. It is the form in which a relationship becomes performable.**

A third governing principle is:

> **What a character says, how she says it, what role she is performing, where she stands, what the image does, what the music does, and what the audience is asked to see may be different parts of the same argument.**

A fourth principle is:

> **The complete corpus must distinguish repetition from development. A line, image, melody, staging pattern, or relationship may return without meaning the same thing.**

The full-series synthesis should therefore be capable of supporting:

- literary and thematic analysis;
- character and relationship comparison;
- Japanese-language and vocal-performance analysis;
- musicological/dramaturgical comparison;
- visual and cinematic analysis;
- performance and theater studies;
- analysis of spectatorship and audience ethics;
- cross-series comparison with other works in the project;
- and later adaptation/franchise analysis if additional media are added.

---

# I. What the full-series pass must do differently

The sequential episode and movement analyses preserved uncertainty correctly.

The full-series pass may now compare later evidence with earlier scenes, but it must still preserve developmental sequence.

Use the formulation:

> **Earlier reading → later pressure → revised full-series understanding.**

Do not write as though the final meaning of a motif or relationship was fully present and consciously available in Episode 1.

The full-series pass should perform four operations:

## 1. Consolidation

Gather evidence that earlier analyses treated separately.

Example:

> Karen's pronoun movement, Position Zero history, Hikari's fear of becoming a fan, and the post-credit audition can now be read as one developmental sequence.

## 2. Stress-testing

Ask what evidence complicates the strongest thesis.

Example:

> If the series values relational self-authorship, where does intense possessiveness remain unresolved or even aesthetically celebrated?

## 3. Differentiation

Prevent similar-looking arcs from being flattened together.

Example:

> Mahiru's dependency, Nana's preservationism, Claudine's exclusivity, and Karen's Hikari-centered vocation all involve intense attachment but operate through different ethical structures.

## 4. Retrospective revision

Identify cases where the film does not negate a television resolution but reveals its next contradiction.

Use:

> **TV resolution → film pressure → persistence / complication / regression / extension / transcendence.**

# II. Corpus boundary and source hierarchy

## 1. Tier 1A — Direct audiovisual evidence

The governing source is the actual animated work:

- complete Japanese audio;
- full-resolution retained frames;
- chronological visual sequence;
- editing;
- choreography;
- acting;
- singing;
- score;
- silence;
- sound effects;
- staging;
- camera movement;
- movement through space.

When dialogue timing, musical entry, breath, interruption, action synchronization, or performance intensity matters, **the audio and moving audiovisual sequence are authoritative**.

A screenshot can establish a composition.

It cannot by itself establish the full timing or motion that produced the composition.

## 2. Tier 1B — Japanese textual evidence

Use:

- Japanese ASS/SRT subtitle layers;
- corrected dialogue extracts;
- Japanese song lyrics where available;
- formal title cards;
- onscreen Japanese text.

These establish:

- wording;
- tense;
- pronouns;
- address;
- register;
- lexical recurrence;
- song allocation;
- titles;
- written signs and labels.

Text is particularly important for claims such as:

- `私` versus `私達`;
- `眩しい` versus `眩しかった`;
- `再演` versus `再生産`;
- `役` versus personhood;
- `舞台` as literal stage, vocation, relationship, or life-practice;
- `スタァ` / `星` / `キラめき`;
- `次の舞台`;
- `ポジション・ゼロ`.

## 3. Tier 1C — Canonical epilogue material

Credits and post-credit scenes are narrative evidence when they contain:

- new dialogue;
- career information;
- travel;
- messages;
- institutional destinations;
- new performances;
- new auditions;
- relational continuation.

Do **not** discard the movie credits as ornamental material.

They are part of the sequel's answer to:

> **What do these characters do after the completed stage?**

## 4. Tier 2 — Navigation and survey material

Use:

- contact sheets;
- scene indices;
- screenshot manifests;
- subtitle indices;
- shot-change metadata.

These are retrieval aids.

They should not substitute for direct inspection of the scenes used to support major claims.

## 5. Tier 3 — Analytical derivatives

Optional technical measurements may include:

- approximate BPM;
- beat/pulse structure;
- RMS/loudness contour;
- onset density;
- spectral centroid/brightness;
- broad tonal-center/chroma estimates;
- waveform/spectrogram views;
- relative vocal-pitch contours where sufficiently isolated.

These are corroborative.

A measurement cannot overrule directly audible musical structure.

## 6. OVA caveat

The available OVA subtitle layer is English while the audio is Japanese.

Therefore:

- visual analysis is fully available;
- music and sound analysis are fully available;
- performed-vocal-state analysis is fully available;
- broad semantic interpretation is available;
- exact Japanese wording/register should only be asserted where recoverable confidently from the Japanese audio.

Do not silently treat English subtitle wording as Japanese linguistic evidence.

## 7. Prior analyses

The completed episode, OVA, and film analyses are valuable as:

- a retrieval index;
- a map of prior hypotheses;
- a record of interpretive development;
- a list of important timestamps and scenes.

They are **not substitutes for primary audiovisual verification** when a claim becomes load-bearing in the full-series synthesis.

# III. Epistemic discipline

The full-series corpus should use explicit evidence-state labels when necessary.

## AF — Audiovisual fact

Directly visible or audible:

- action;
- blocking;
- image;
- music entry;
- silence;
- costume state;
- movement;
- song allocation;
- formal title card.

## JT — Japanese textual fact

Directly established by Japanese dialogue, lyric, onscreen writing, or formal terminology.

## PF — Performance fact

A directly audible/visible feature of delivery:

- interruption;
- crying coloration;
- unusual register;
- pause;
- shout;
- laughter;
- vocal overlap;
- tempo change in speech.

## MF — Musical fact

Directly audible musical structure:

- percussion drops out;
- two voices enter unison;
- a melody returns;
- instrumentation becomes denser;
- the score stops;
- a pulse accelerates.

## FR — Formal recurrence

A demonstrable recurrence or transformation:

- repeated composition;
- repeated dialogue;
- repeated musical material;
- repeated stage geometry;
- repeated object;
- repeated action pattern.

The recurrence itself is factual. Its meaning remains interpretive.

## SI — Strong inference

Supported by multiple independent evidence streams.

Examples:

- lyric + staging + musical change;
- dialogue + visual recurrence + later direct restatement;
- voice performance + repeated relationship behavior.

## IT — Interpretive thesis

A higher-order literary, theatrical, ethical, or philosophical explanation organizing evidence across multiple scenes.

## SP — Speculation

Plausible but underdetermined.

Examples:

- exact fixed symbolic meaning for every tomato;
- one instrument permanently “belonging” to one character;
- an ambiguous reflection definitely representing one metaphysical entity;
- a romantic label the text does not explicitly establish.

### Mandatory distinctions

Do not collapse:

- character belief into series endorsement;
- mechanical victory into emotional or ethical victory;
- formal repetition into replay;
- admiration into consent;
- intensity into reciprocity;
- shared stage into shared identity;
- audience desire into performer welfare;
- explanation into moral justification;
- visual resemblance into identical meaning.

# IV. Working hypotheses to test rather than assume

The prior close readings produced several strong series-level hypotheses.

They should organize retrieval, but they must be tested against counterevidence.

## Hypothesis A — Revue as performable relationship

> **The Revue is the form in which a relationship becomes performable.**

Test:

- Are there major Revues whose primary object is not relational?
- When does a Revue expose a character's self-relation rather than a dyad?
- How does the movie blur the distinction between ordinary life and Revue?

## Hypothesis B — Self-authorship without isolation

The corpus repeatedly values characters becoming grammatical and theatrical subjects:

> `私`

without requiring them to abandon:

> `私達`.

Test:

- When does “we” become dependency?
- When does “I” become defensive isolation?
- Which relationships allow singular subjects to continue generating a plural future?

## Hypothesis C — Repetition versus re-production

A major series distinction appears to be:

> **再演** — reproducing a known precious form

versus:

> **再生産** — generating another living form from inherited material.

Test:

- How consistently does the series maintain this distinction?
- Are there benign forms of repetition?
- When does “again” mean replay, practice, memory, return, reunion, or genuinely new performance?

## Hypothesis D — The stage requires audience, but audience is ethically dangerous

The TV finale explicitly makes performer + viewer constitutive of theater.

The film later names the audience:

> greedy.

Test:

- When does spectatorship become recognition?
- When does it become possession?
- How do Giraffe, Nana, Claudine, Karen, Hikari, and the external viewer occupy different spectator positions?

## Hypothesis E — A completed role must be allowed to end

The film strongly associates growth with theatrical death.

Test:

- Does every completed role need death?
- What differentiates destructive emptiness, defensive emptiness, and generative emptiness?
- How do closure and continuation coexist?

## Hypothesis F — `Starlight` changes level across the corpus

Track at least:

1. the inherited play 『スタァライト』;
2. Karen/Hikari's `私達のスタァライト`;
3. “Starlight” as a broader artistic practice of turning relation/life into stage.

Test where these layers:

- separate;
- overlap;
- contaminate one another;
- or become deliberately indistinguishable.

# V. Corpus chronology and retrospective integration

The synthesis should preserve three major chronological layers.

## 1. TV series

Read as the primary formation text.

The television series establishes:

- Stage Girl identity;
- the audition;
- the Giraffe;
- Position Zero;
- `キラめき`;
- the major pair relationships;
- Nana's replay;
- Hikari's lost/reproduced radiance;
- the rewritten *Starlight*;
- the nine-girl story.

## 2. OVAs

Treat as supplementary character-color and relational texture.

Ask for each OVA:

- What existing interpretation does it strengthen?
- What does it weaken?
- What does it complicate?
- Does it reveal ordinary-life voice or social rhythm unavailable in high-stakes episodes?

Do not force the OVAs to carry major thematic architecture they do not contain.

## 3. Film

Treat as a sequel that pressure-tests completed TV resolutions.

The film is not evidence that the television arcs were false.

Use:

> **TV resolution → new life condition → hidden contradiction → new performance.**

## 4. Credits/post-credits

Treat as the final stage of the film's developmental argument.

The credits are especially important for determining:

- which stated career plans persist;
- which change;
- how separation actually functions;
- whether relationships survive geographic difference;
- what “first stage” means after the film's obsession with endings;
- how metaphysical Revue language becomes ordinary artistic labor.

Do not reduce them to labels such as:

- friends;
- rivals;
- childhood friends;
- romantic/yuri-coded;
- partners.

Track **what the relationship does**.

For every major bond, analyze:

1. origin;
2. asymmetry;
3. desire;
4. fear;
5. dependency;
6. rivalry;
7. admiration;
8. spectatorship;
9. ability to contradict;
10. ability to change roles;
11. possessiveness;
12. reciprocity;
13. ordinary-life form;
14. Revue form;
15. musical form;
16. TV resolution;
17. film pressure;
18. post-film future.

### Priority relationship clusters

- Karen / Hikari;
- Junna / Nana;
- Maya / Claudine;
- Mahiru / Karen;
- Mahiru / Hikari;
- Futaba / Kaoruko;
- ensemble / individual;
- Stage Girls / Giraffe;
- Stage Girls / audience.

### Relationship ethics question

For each intense attachment ask:

> **Does this relationship help the other person produce another self, or does it demand another performance of the self already loved?**

This question is particularly important for distinguishing:

- Nana's preservationism;
- Claudine's rivalry;
- Mahiru's early dependency;
- Karen's Hikari-centered vocation;
- Hikari's protective withdrawal.

For every major Revue song, record:

- opening musical state;
- principal musical transformation;
- speech/song boundary;
- final musical state;
- mechanical outcome;

Ask whether a character's sound-world:

- remains stable;

## 3. “Music knows first” ledger

Across the complete corpus classify major examples:

- music anticipates language;

## 4. Reprise / replay / development

Distinguish:

- exact repetition;
- reprise;
- development;
- recontextualization;
- transfer.

This is especially important because the work itself thematizes:

> `再演` versus `再生産`.

## 5. Final-mix discipline

Do not manufacture exact orchestration or harmony where the stereo mix does not support it.

Prefer:

> “low sustained string-like texture”

to false instrumental certainty.

Prefer:

> “harmonic stability increases”

to unjustified Roman-numeral analysis.

# XIII. Spectatorship and audience ethics

The full-series synthesis should distinguish several kinds of spectatorship.

## 1. Institutional spectator

The Giraffe as organizer/viewer.

## 2. Intimate spectator

Examples:

- Nana watching Junna;
- Claudine watching Maya;
- Karen watching Hikari;
- Futaba as Kaoruko's fan.

## 3. Performer-spectator reciprocity

When a viewer becomes capable of:

> performing back.

This is crucial to:

- Futaba;
- Hikari;
- Karen;
- Maya/Claudine.

## 4. External viewer

The anime audience.

Ask cautiously:

> When does the work implicate the viewer's desire to keep watching these girls perform?

The movie's “greedy audience” language makes this a strong thematic question, but do not turn every Giraffe line into a direct fourth-wall statement.

### Ethical question

> **Does watching recognize another subject—or freeze her into the performance one wants repeated?**

# XV. Ethics of care, desire, possession, and self-authorship

The full-series synthesis should make ethical claims carefully.

A useful governing question is:

> **Who gets to decide what another person's role, future, suffering, sacrifice, or beauty should mean?**

Analyze recurring problems:

- Hikari protecting Karen through unilateral disappearance;
- Mahiru needing to remain needed;
- Nana preserving people as roles/memories;
- Futaba remaining Kaoruko's fan/caretaker;
- Kaoruko relying on inherited relational hierarchy;
- Maya treating Claudine as rival-role;
- Karen treating Hikari as stage;
- Claudine's possessive but generative desire;
- Giraffe/audience appetite.

Distinguish:

### Intensity

How much the person matters.

### Reciprocity

Whether both subjects can answer back.

### Agency

Whether the other can refuse or change.

### Possession

Whether love claims authority over the other's form.

### Generativity

Whether the relationship produces another self or demands repetition of an earlier one.

The series does not demand weak attachment.

It repeatedly depicts intense attachment as artistically powerful.

The ethical distinction is:

> **Does the attachment permit the beloved to remain a changing subject?**

# XVIII. Negative-evidence and contradiction audit

Every major synthesis document should include:

## Evidence supporting the thesis

At least several independent examples.

## Evidence complicating the thesis

Scenes that:

- resist;
- partially contradict;
- reveal ethical cost;
- or show a different character solving the problem differently.

## Alternative reading

At least one plausible competing interpretation for the most important thesis.

## Why the chosen reading remains stronger

Explain the evidentiary balance.

This is particularly important for:

- romance/yuri interpretation;
- metaphysical claims about the Revue system;
- symbolic objects;
- the Giraffe;
- audience-surrogate claims;
- whether film arcs are “healthy” resolutions;
- whether rivalry is inherently liberating.

# XX. Production workflow

The full-series synthesis should be produced in this order:

1. verify the complete corpus and final source map;
2. build the episode/movement/Revue evidence ledger;
3. draft the series architecture document;
4. draft the three primary relationship documents:
   - Karen/Hikari;
   - Junna/Nana;
   - Maya/Claudine;
5. draft the secondary relationship / ensemble documents;
6. draft Revue-system and *Starlight* intertext documents;
7. draft music/voice and visual/cinematic documents;
8. draft ethics/self-authorship and next-stage documents;
9. draft comparative matrices/open questions;
10. write the README only after conclusions stabilize;
11. finish the Japanese lyric/dialogue/motif index;
12. perform a duplication, evidence, and contradiction audit.

The order is deliberate:

> **evidence first → chronology → relationships → theatrical system → formal audiovisual systems → ethics and transition → comparative export.**

# XXI. Anti-patterns

Do not:

- retell the plot at length in every document;
- reduce Revues to dream symbolism;
- treat every surreal image as literal metaphysics;
- treat every color/object as a fixed code;
- turn technical audio measurements into emotional proof;
- interpret the film as thirteen extra TV episodes;
- treat film pressure as proof the TV ending failed;
- treat the OVAs as major thematic texts merely because they are canonical;
- call every intense female relationship romance without evidentiary discipline;
- deny romantic/yuri coding merely because explicit confession is absent;
- equate rivalry with health by default;
- equate care with benevolence by default;
- equate completion with failure;
- assume separation means relationship loss;
- assume togetherness means relational health;
- let “the stage is life” become a vague universal metaphor detached from specific scenes;
- use later evidence to erase what earlier characters actually knew.

# XXII. Final synthesis standard

The completed multi-document corpus should be able to answer, with source-grounded specificity:

1. What does *Revue Starlight* ultimately believe a Stage Girl is?
2. What is `キラめき` by the end of the animated corpus?
3. What is a Revue?
4. What is Position Zero after the film?
5. What is the Giraffe?
6. What makes a relationship generative rather than preservational?
7. Why is Nana's `再演` different from the later forms of `また` and `再生産`?
8. What happens to the TV series' “we” in the movie?
9. Why does the film need graduation and theatrical death?
10. How do audience and performer create—and endanger—one another?
11. What does the internal play *Starlight* become across the corpus?
12. Why does music sometimes understand the relationship before the characters do?
13. How do ordinary life and Revue gradually become one theatrical continuum?
14. What do the credits and post-credit audition prove?
15. What, finally, does it mean that:

> **私たちはもう舞台の上**

“We are already on the stage”?

The final full-series thesis should be written **after** these questions have been answered, not before.
