# 『少女☆歌劇 レヴュースタァライト』
## Analytical Method and Episode Reading Protocol
### Japanese-original sequential analysis of the TV anime and sequel film

## Purpose

This protocol defines the method for the sequential deep reading of the *Revue Starlight* anime.

The objective is not merely to reconstruct plot or identify symbolism. *Revue Starlight* is unusually dependent on the interaction of:

- spoken Japanese;
- vocal performance;
- song;
- musical arrangement;
- choreography;
- theatrical staging;
- editing;
- composition;
- symbolic props;
- stage machinery;
- lighting;
- spatial relationships;
- and recurring visual forms.

Accordingly, the analysis should treat an episode as a **performed audiovisual text**, rather than as a screenplay illustrated by animation.

The governing principle is:

> **What a character says, how she says it, where she stands while saying it, what surrounds her, and what the music does to her words are potentially different parts of the same argument.**

The Revue sequences receive particularly intensive treatment. They should be analyzed as miniature dramatic works embedded inside the episode rather than summarized as stylized battles.

---

# I. Source hierarchy

The episode bundles provide unusually strong source coverage.

The validated TV corpus contains twelve distinct episode archives, with hundreds of retained JPEG frames per episode, dozens of contact sheets, corrected Japanese subtitle material, complete episode audio, and the associated manifests and indices. The bundle validation reports that the archives pass integrity testing, that the required manifests and indices are present, that subtitle cue counts reconcile, and that the complete audio artifact is embedded in each archive.

For interpretation, evidence should therefore be prioritized as follows.

### Tier 1 — Direct audiovisual evidence

- complete Japanese episode audio;
- individual full-resolution retained frames;
- chronological visual sequence;
- actual interaction between image, sound, dialogue, and music.

### Tier 2 — Japanese textual evidence

- corrected Japanese SRT;
- analysis ASS;
- dialogue manifests or indices contained in the bundle.

These establish wording, grammar, register, and retrieval points.

The subtitle timings have been aligned against Japanese audio and cross-checked against BD-authored subtitle timing, with different correction models used where necessary. They are therefore strong navigational evidence, but **audio remains authoritative for exact vocal timing**.

### Tier 3 — Survey material

- contact sheets;
- scene indices;
- screenshot manifests.

These are primarily navigational. A contact sheet tells us **where to look**. It should not substitute for examining the important frames themselves.

---

# II. Sequential-reading discipline

Each episode should initially be interpreted only through:

1. the current episode;
2. preceding episodes already analyzed;
3. firmly established series information necessary for basic identification.

Later revelations should not silently be imported backward.

If a later episode changes the meaning of an earlier one, the earlier interpretation should be explicitly revised:

> Earlier reading → new evidence → revised reading.

This distinction is particularly important for *Revue Starlight*, where repetition and recontextualization appear likely to be central formal devices.

Three evidentiary levels should remain separate:

**Explicit:** directly established by dialogue or action.

**Strong inference:** supported by converging visual, linguistic, musical, or structural evidence.

**Speculative interpretation:** plausible symbolic reading that remains underdetermined.

Symbolism should never be treated as a decoding exercise in which every object has exactly one fixed meaning.

---

# III. Pass 1 — Episode reconstruction

The first pass establishes what the episode actually does.

Read the Japanese subtitle track chronologically while consulting the visual sequence and complete audio.

Construct an episode map identifying:

- major scenes;
- changes in location;
- relationship confrontations;
- rehearsals and performances;
- Revue entry and exit points;
- flashbacks or temporal dislocations;
- musical transitions;
- significant silent passages;
- ending or post-credit material.

The resulting plot description should emphasize **causality**, not merely chronology.

Instead of:

> Karen talks to Hikari. They go somewhere. A Revue occurs.

Prefer:

> Karen's attempt to restore or redefine a relationship produces a confrontation whose emotional contradiction is subsequently externalized through the Revue.

The episode analysis should answer:

> **What changes because this episode happened?**

---

# IV. Pass 2 — Japanese dialogue and character voice

Japanese dialogue should be analyzed as characterization rather than merely translated for content.

Track, where meaningful:

- first-person pronouns;
- address terms and name forms;
- honorifics;
- sentence-final particles;
- politeness;
- masculine/feminine or stylized character language;
- contractions;
- hesitation;
- repetition;
- self-correction;
- rhetorical questions;
- commands;
- declaratives;
- ellipsis;
- theatrical diction;
- contrast between ordinary and performed speech.

Special attention should be paid to **register switching**.

A character may sound differently:

- with a roommate;
- with a rival;
- with a childhood friend;
- before an audience;
- during rehearsal;
- during confrontation;
- inside a Revue;
- while singing.

The analysis should distinguish:

> **lexical voice** — what kind of Japanese the character habitually uses

from

> **performed vocal state** — what the actor does with that language in this particular emotional condition.

---

# V. Pass 3 — Vocal delivery and voice acting

This should be a dedicated analytical layer in every episode, not an occasional observation.

For important scenes, listen directly to the relevant audio segment rather than inferring delivery from subtitles.

Track each principal character along several dimensions.

### Vocal identity

Describe the relatively stable character sound:

- approximate pitch region;
- brightness/darkness;
- softness/hardness;
- breathiness;
- nasal or resonant qualities;
- vocal weight;
- youthful versus mature impression;
- precision versus looseness of articulation;
- ordinary conversational tempo.

These descriptions should remain comparative rather than pretending to be laboratory measurements.

### Performed state

For emotionally significant lines, examine:

- pitch rising or falling relative to baseline;
- volume;
- tempo;
- breath support;
- constriction;
- hesitation;
- attack on consonants;
- lengthening of vowels;
- dropped or swallowed endings;
- laughter;
- sighs;
- gasps;
- crying coloration;
- tremor;
- silence before or after the line.

Particular attention should go to moments when **semantic content and delivery disagree**.

A character may say:

> “I'm fine.”

while the voice communicates:

- exhaustion;
- panic;
- resentment;
- embarrassment;
- suppressed grief.

Those contradictions are often more revealing than the sentence itself.

### Interactional voice

Voice acting should also be relational.

Track:

- interruption;
- overlap;
- who waits for whom;
- who speaks into silence;
- who becomes louder around a particular person;
- who becomes softer;
- whether one character matches another's tempo;
- whether emotional synchronization occurs;
- when conversational rhythm collapses.

### Performed-vocal-state ledger

Beginning early in the series, maintain a cumulative ledger for the principal cast:

**baseline voice → defensive voice → intimate voice → competitive voice → theatrical/Revue voice → singing voice → major deviations.**

This should allow later analysis to identify genuine changes rather than relying on general impressions such as “she sounds more confident.”

---

# VI. Pass 4 — Visual close reading

Contact sheets are the survey layer.

They should be used to identify:

- visual repetitions;
- dominant color changes;
- unusual compositions;
- major entrances;
- staging transitions;
- silent sequences;
- Revue landmarks.

After that survey, important scenes must be examined through **individual retained frames**.

## Mandatory targeted-frame inspection

For an ordinary major dramatic scene, inspect enough individual frames to establish:

- beginning composition;
- major shot/reverse-shot changes;
- significant gaze or gesture;
- important spatial repositioning;
- visual climax;
- closing composition.

For a major Revue, frame inspection should be substantially denser.

At minimum, examine individual frames around:

- Revue threshold/entrance;
- opening positions;
- first sung phrase;
- first physical exchange;
- scenic transformation;
- important lyrical turn;
- weapon contact;
- changes in lighting;
- changes in elevation;
- close-ups immediately preceding decisive actions;
- climax;
- button/costume resolution;
- final tableau.

Additional frames should be inspected wherever the contact sheets suggest that rapid editing may conceal meaningful information.

The purpose is not to count frames mechanically. It is to avoid missing meaning because a small contact-sheet image compresses:

- facial expression;
- eye direction;
- hand positioning;
- background objects;
- written text;
- costume detail;
- props;
- stage geometry.

---

# VII. Visual grammar ledger

Across episodes, track recurring visual elements without prematurely assigning them fixed meanings.

Examples include:

- stars;
- towers;
- stairs;
- elevators;
- trains and rails;
- circles;
- cages;
- curtains;
- ropes;
- spotlights;
- mirrors;
- water;
- fire;
- falling;
- height;
- machinery;
- empty seats;
- audience positions;
- weapons;
- clothing fasteners/buttons;
- the giraffe;
- Position Zero;
- stage marks and boundaries.

For every recurring element, ask:

> Where has this appeared before?

> With whom?

> In what emotional context?

> Has its function remained stable?

Repeated imagery should become meaningful through **pattern accumulation**, not one-scene symbolic declaration.

---

# VIII. The Revue protocol

Every major Revue should receive its own subsection and be treated almost like a short theatrical production.

## 1. Preconditions

Before analyzing the performance itself, establish:

- who enters;
- what unresolved relationship or desire produced it;
- what each participant appears to want;
- what each participant thinks the conflict is about;
- what the episode has already shown that they may not understand.

The Revue is then read as an answer—or attempted answer—to that contradiction.

---

## 2. Stage topology

Reconstruct the stage as physical argument.

Track:

- center versus periphery;
- elevation;
- distance;
- barriers;
- movement corridors;
- circular versus linear motion;
- confinement versus openness;
- symmetry/asymmetry;
- who controls the space;
- who forces the other to move;
- who occupies the highest point;
- who reaches or loses center.

Stage geography should be interpreted relationally.

A high platform, for example, should not automatically mean “superiority.” Its meaning depends on who occupies it, whether it isolates them, whether it can be reached, and what subsequently happens to it.

---

## 3. Weapons and bodily performance

Weapons should be considered extensions of character and theatrical role.

Examine:

- weapon form;
- range;
- offensive versus defensive use;
- stance;
- speed;
- evasiveness;
- directness;
- how much physical distance a fighting style creates;
- whether combat resembles dance, duel, chase, siege, or execution.

The question is not simply:

> Who is stronger?

It is:

> **What kind of relationship is this choreography making visible?**

---

## 4. Scenic transformation

Revue stages frequently appear capable of changing in ways ordinary physical space cannot.

Every transformation should therefore be treated as dramaturgical evidence.

Ask:

- What emotional claim immediately preceded it?
- Which character seems to “own” the resulting stage?
- What memories or metaphors does it materialize?
- Does the stage support a character's self-understanding or expose its weakness?
- Does the opponent learn how to navigate, destroy, appropriate, or escape it?

---

# IX. Music and song analysis

Revue songs should not be treated as background music with lyrics.

They are part of the dialogue.

Each important song should be read on four simultaneous levels.

### 1. Lyrics

Examine:

- pronouns;
- imperatives;
- rhetorical questions;
- images;
- repeated words;
- parallel phrases;
- contrasts;
- references to promise, stage, light, star, possession, separation, victory, etc.

Determine whether the lyric functions as:

- confession;
- accusation;
- self-mythology;
- challenge;
- defense;
- plea;
- prophecy;
- shared narration.

### 2. Vocal allocation

Track **who sings what**.

This matters enormously.

Ask:

- who initiates;
- who answers;
- who receives the melody;
- whether characters sing simultaneously;
- whether lines overlap;
- whether one character completes another's phrase;
- whether harmony indicates agreement while lyrics indicate conflict;
- whether one voice temporarily disappears;
- who receives the final musical statement.

### 3. Musical shape

Where aurally supportable, examine:

- tempo and rhythmic character;
- restrained versus explosive passages;
- instrumentation;
- register;
- melodic ascent/descent;
- sudden breaks;
- dynamic escalation;
- recurring motifs;
- changes associated with particular characters.

Claims about harmony, key, meter, or technical compositional structure should only be made where the audio supports them confidently. If necessary, selective quantitative audio analysis can supplement listening.

### 4. Song/action synchronization

Examine exactly what happens while particular lyrics are sung.

A lyric may change meaning because:

- the singer attacks while singing it;
- the opponent turns away;
- scenery collapses;
- the camera cuts to an object;
- someone loses elevation;
- a costume detail changes.

The Revue should therefore be treated as:

> **lyrics + vocal acting + score + choreography + image**

rather than five separate tracks.

---

# X. Speech inside the Revue

Special attention should be paid to transitions between:

**singing → speaking → singing**

because spoken lines inserted into musical theatre often possess unusual force.

For each such moment, examine:

- why song stops;
- whether accompaniment stops;
- whether vocal register changes;
- whether the character sounds more or less theatrical;
- whether the statement is addressed to the opponent, herself, an imagined audience, or the system governing the Revue;
- what immediately changes after the declaration.

This is one area where complete audio is particularly valuable.

The distinction between:

> a sung claim

and

> a naked spoken claim inside music

may itself be dramaturgically meaningful.

---

# XI. Revue outcome

Do not reduce the outcome to “A defeats B.”

Record separately:

### Mechanical result
Who wins according to the Revue's rules?

### Emotional result
Who actually changes?

### Epistemic result
Who learns something?

### Relational result
What becomes possible or impossible between them afterward?

### Symbolic result
What happens to the stage, costume, weapon, position, or recurring motif?

A participant may mechanically lose while accomplishing the more important emotional transformation.

---

# XII. Pass 5 — Character and relationship development

After audiovisual analysis, return to the characters.

For each materially involved character, update:

- current desire;
- current fear;
- self-conception;
- defensive behavior;
- performance identity;
- important relationships;
- contradictions;
- unresolved questions.

Avoid personality labels that flatten process.

Instead of:

> Mahiru is jealous.

Prefer:

> Mahiru's attachment currently becomes legible through a particular form of jealousy, and the episode shows what she believes that jealousy is protecting.

Relationships should likewise be tracked dynamically:

> closeness → asymmetry → dependence → rivalry → rupture → recognition

rather than merely labeled “friends,” “rivals,” or “romantically coded.”

---

# XIII. Theatricality and meta-performance

A separate layer should track the relationship among:

- the girls as students;
- the girls as actresses;
- the characters they play in *Starlight*;
- the roles they occupy during Revues;
- the performers the audience sees;
- the audience or judging structures inside the fiction.

The recurring play should be treated as an **intertext within the anime**.

Whenever its:

- roles;
- dialogue;
- imagery;
- blocking;
- promises;
- deaths;
- separations;
- reunions

echo the girls' lives, the episode should ask whether the students are:

> performing the play,

> being shaped by the play,

or

> rewriting the play through their lives.

---

# XIV. Sound beyond dialogue and song

The soundtrack should also be examined for meaningful non-vocal sound:

- footsteps;
- train sounds;
- mechanical movement;
- bells;
- curtains;
- weapon impacts;
- breathing;
- silence;
- applause;
- audience ambience;
- school noise.

Silence deserves special scrutiny.

If the music drops away before a line or image, that absence may carry as much weight as the score.

---

# XV. Episode-level synthesis

Each completed episode analysis should use approximately this structure:

### Episode thesis
What is this episode fundamentally doing?

### Dramatic architecture
The major movements and causal structure.

### Character development
What changes in the relevant girls.

### Japanese language and speech
Important register, wording, verbal motifs, or conversational asymmetries.

### Voice acting and performed vocal states
Specific delivery observations plus updates to the vocal ledger.

### Visual direction
Composition, framing, color, movement, motif, and important individually inspected frames.

### Revue deep dive
When present:
- stakes;
- stage;
- song;
- vocal allocation;
- choreography;
- visual metaphor;
- frame-level evidence;
- outcome.

### Music and sound
How score, song, silence, and sound design participate in meaning.

### Relationships
What has changed between relevant characters.

### Motif ledger
New motifs, repetitions, or transformations.

### Explicit / inferred / speculative findings
A short confidence distinction for the episode's most consequential claims.

### Cumulative revision
What this episode confirms, complicates, or overturns from previous episodes.

### Questions carried forward
Things the work has deliberately left unresolved.

---

# XVI. Cumulative series ledgers

Across the twelve episodes, maintain several evolving records.

**Character ledger**  
desire, fear, self-conception, performance philosophy.

**Relationship ledger**  
how every major pair changes.

**Voice ledger**  
stable vocal identity plus context-specific performed states.

**Revue ledger**  
participants, song, stage grammar, conflict, outcome, lasting consequence.

**Motif ledger**  
repeated visual and sonic objects without prematurely fixing their meanings.

**Position/stage ledger**  
center, elevation, audience, theatrical hierarchy.

**Starlight-play ledger**  
how the internal theatrical text is being reinterpreted.

These become the evidence base for the final series synthesis.

---

# XVII. Frame-inspection rule

The most important methodological change from a standard bundle analysis is:

> **Contact sheets are never sufficient evidence for a major Revue or major emotional confrontation.**

They are reconnaissance.

Once an important passage is identified, the individual retained frames should be opened and inspected directly.

This is especially mandatory where meaning depends on:

- facial microexpression;
- gaze;
- blocking;
- background objects;
- stage markings;
- small costume changes;
- props;
- weapon placement;
- visual parallels;
- rapid cuts;
- lighting transitions.

For particularly dense Revue stretches, inspection should proceed almost shot-by-shot where the material warrants it.

---

# XVIII. Final interpretive principle

The analysis should resist reducing the Revues to:

> “This fantasy battle symbolizes their feelings.”

That is true but insufficient.

The stronger approach is:

> **The Revue is the form in which a relationship becomes performable.**

A character cannot merely state her jealousy, dependency, ambition, fear of abandonment, desire for recognition, or need to stand beside someone.

She must:

- sing it;
- stage it;
- embody it;
- fight through it;
- construct scenery around it;
- expose herself to an opponent;
- and risk losing the position from which she understood herself.

That is why the Revues deserve unusually dense treatment.

The central question should repeatedly be:

> **What can this character express on this impossible stage that she cannot express anywhere else?**
