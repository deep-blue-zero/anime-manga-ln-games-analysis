# 0. Purpose and relationship to the core method

This document is a **film-specific addendum**, not a replacement for the existing *Revue Starlight* analytical method.

The following remain authoritative from:

> `RevueStarlightAnalyticalMethod_MusicalDramaturgy_v2`

- Japanese-original source hierarchy;
- audiovisual primacy;
- explicit / strong inference / speculative evidence tiers;
- lexical voice versus performed vocal state;
- visual close reading;
- Visual Grammar Ledger;
- Revue-as-performed-relationship principle;
- lyric and vocal-allocation analysis;
- Musical-Dramaturgy Ledger;
- music/action/staging concordance;
- motif, Position Zero, relationship, character, voice, Revue, and *Starlight* ledgers;
- anti-overinterpretation rules;
- quantitative audio analysis as corroborative rather than primary evidence.

This addendum modifies the **scale, order, and synthesis logic** required for a feature film.

The television method primarily asks:

> **What changes because this episode happened?**

The film method must additionally ask:

> **What function does this movement perform inside the architecture of the whole film?**

and:

> **How does the film remember, transform, or contradict material introduced much earlier in its runtime—or in the television series?**

The governing principle remains:

> **The Revue is the form in which a relationship becomes performable.**

For the film, add:

> **The film itself may behave like one extended Revue whose individual movements, reprises, transitions, and pair-confrontations form parts of a larger theatrical argument.**

---

# I. Why a feature-film method is necessary

The film differs analytically from a television episode in several important ways.

A television episode often creates a relatively local dramatic unit:

> contradiction → escalation → performance / Revue → episode-level revision.

A feature film can:

- plant an image in its opening and answer it an hour later;
- introduce musical material long before its dramatic meaning becomes clear;
- separate the beginning and resolution of a relationship across multiple movements;
- create visual or musical rhymes across large temporal distances;
- arrange several Revues so that each modifies the meaning of the next;
- transform earlier TV-series material through cinematic scale;
- use transitions themselves as part of the argument;
- make the entire film operate as a theatrical composition rather than a chain of independent episodes.

Therefore the principal analytical unit changes from:

> **episode → scenes → Revue**

to:

> **film → movements → trajectories → Revue units → cross-Revue synthesis → whole-film architecture.**

# II. Source hierarchy

Use the same source hierarchy as the core method.

## Tier 1 — Direct audiovisual evidence

- complete Japanese movie audio;
- individual full-resolution retained frames;
- chronological visual sequence;
- direct interaction among dialogue, song, score, sound design, choreography, editing, lighting, and stage machinery.

Audio remains authoritative for:

- vocal timing;
- musical entry;
- attack;
- silence;
- dynamic change;
- speech/song transition.

## Tier 2 — Japanese textual evidence

Use:

- Japanese subtitle layer;
- dialogue indices;
- lyric text where reliably available.

These establish:

- wording;
- grammar;
- register;
- address forms;
- lexical motifs;
- lyric content.

When subtitle speaker labels are production/seiyuu-oriented rather than character-oriented, resolve speaker identity through:

> **Japanese audio voice identity ↔ visible character/action ↔ subtitle label.**

Never let a metadata label override clearly audible or visible character identity.

## Tier 3 — Navigation material

Use:

- contact sheets;
- scene indices;
- shot manifests;
- visual timestamps.

These are reconnaissance tools only.

## Tier 4 — Analytical derivatives

Optional:

- RMS/loudness contours;
- approximate BPM/pulse;
- spectral centroid/brightness;
- onset density;
- chroma/tonal-center estimation;
- waveform/spectrogram inspection.

These remain corroborative.

# III. Two-pass film workflow

The film must be handled differently from the TV-series sequential workflow.

## Pass A — Whole-film reconnaissance

Before writing the first movement analysis, survey the **entire film once**.

The purpose is **formal mapping, not interpretive closure**.

Identify:

- approximate major movements;
- all formal Revue thresholds;
- song boundaries;
- ordinary-reality / theatrical-reality transitions;
- recurring locations;
- repeated images;
- major temporal ellipses;
- obvious musical reprises;
- reused TV-series musical material;
- repeated dialogue;
- repeated compositions or camera setups;
- post-credit material;
- large-scale character ordering;
- where unresolved material from one movement appears to pass into another.

Do not finalize character or symbolic interpretations during this pass.

The reconnaissance pass exists so an early scene is not incorrectly treated as locally complete when the film deliberately answers it much later.

---

## Pass B — Sequential close reading

After reconnaissance, return to the beginning.

Analyze the movie movement by movement, preserving internal dramatic sequence.

Do **not** use later-film information casually inside an earlier movement's local reading.

Instead use two layers:

### Local knowledge

What is knowable at this point in the film?

### Retrospective formal knowledge

What whole-film structural relation can be observed because the entire film has already been mapped?

Clearly distinguish these.

Recommended wording:

> **Locally, this scene establishes X. Retrospectively, the film later creates a formal rhyme with Y.**

This preserves dramatic development without sacrificing feature-film structural analysis.

Do not divide the film mechanically into equal time blocks.

- dominant character relationship;
- dramatic question;
- theatrical register;
- musical state;
- location system;
- visual grammar;
- future orientation;
- Revue ownership;
- temporal mode;
- performance mode.

Each movement should receive a brief architecture entry:

## Movement ledger template

**Movement number / working title**

- approximate timestamp range;
- dominant characters;
- central relationship;
- dramatic question;
- ordinary or theatrical register;
- principal visual vocabulary;
- principal musical vocabulary;
- key TV-series inheritance;
- unresolved pressure passed forward;
- major transition into next movement.

Movement names should be descriptive rather than prematurely symbolic.

# VI. Post-TV character-development rule

The television-series resolutions are **starting conditions**, not completed permanent states.

Do not treat the movie as though it must either:

> preserve the TV resolution perfectly

or:

> regress the character.

Instead use:

> **TV resolution → new film pressure → persistence / complication / regression / extension / transcendence.**

Examples of baseline TV outcomes to carry forward:

- Karen developed an independent Stage Girl “I” capable of choosing “we”;
- Hikari recovered desire and accepted shared risk;
- Nana accepted finite time and future change;
- Mahiru learned to love without requiring Karen's dependency;
- Futaba and Kaoruko reframed hierarchy into co-rivalry, while ordinary asymmetries remained;
- Junna's ambition became more self-authored and less finality-driven;
- Maya and Claudine's rivalry became explicit mutual literacy and constitutive attachment;
- the ensemble expanded “we” beyond dyads;
- the 100th *Starlight* became a collectively authored new nine-girl story.

The film analysis should ask:

> **What new contradiction is hidden inside each apparently successful TV resolution?**

# IX. Sequence-level cinematic analysis

For the movie, add **sequence cinematography** to frame analysis.

Do not only ask:

> What does this frame mean?

Also ask:

- how long does the shot last?
- when does the film cut?
- does cutting accelerate or slow?
- is the camera static or moving?
- does the camera approach, retreat, circle, track, rotate, or detach?
- do two distant scenes use graphic matches?
- does one body position repeat another?
- does a location recur from a changed angle?
- does an earlier composition return later with different characters?
- does the film use match cuts to make ordinary and theatrical space continuous?
- does visual scale isolate or connect?
- does the aspect composition place characters as equals, rivals, spectators, or objects?

For important sequences, build:

> **shot rhythm → camera motion → performance state → dramatic function.**

# XI. Long-range Musical Memory Ledger

The film requires a stronger musical-memory protocol than the TV-series episode method.

Track material across the entire runtime:

- melodic motifs;
- rhythmic figures;
- ostinati;
- instrumental colors;
- vocal gestures;
- harmonic fields;
- song quotations;
- TV-series song quotations;
- transformed reprises;
- altered tempo;
- reharmonization;
- orchestration changes;
- motif reassignment;
- melody appearing first instrumentally and later vocally;
- material belonging first to one character and later shared by another.

For every recurrence distinguish:

## Repetition

Material returns substantially unchanged.

## Reprise

Recognizable material returns in a later dramatic position.

## Development

Material returns but is expanded, fragmented, reharmonized, accelerated, slowed, or structurally transformed.

## Recontextualization

Material remains recognizable while its dramatic meaning changes because the character or situation has changed.

## Transfer

Material previously associated with one performer/relationship becomes associated with another.

This distinction is especially important after the TV series established:

> **再演 ≠ 再生産.**

The film's own score may perform that philosophy.

# XV. Transition analysis

Transitions receive their own interpretive attention.

Track boundaries such as:

> ordinary reality → theatrical reality  
> present → memory  
> rehearsal → performance  
> dialogue → song  
> song → speech  
> mundane location → impossible stage  
> one character's Revue → another's  
> one pair's emotional problem → another's  
> Revue → aftermath.

For each major transition ask:

- what changes first: sound, image, dialogue, or movement?
- is there a hard cut or gradual bleed?
- does music anticipate theatricalization?
- does ordinary space already contain Revue grammar?
- is the transition psychologically motivated or deliberately discontinuous?
- does the film eventually stop maintaining a clean ordinary/Revue boundary?

A major feature-film question is:

> **Does the entire movie progressively theatricalize ordinary reality?**

Do not answer before the evidence accumulates.

# XXXIV. Metaphysics discipline

The movie may increase visual abstraction.

Therefore preserve a strict distinction between:

## Diegetic Revue fact

Explicitly established within the story's stage mechanics.

## Performed theatrical reality

A visual/musical event that is materially real inside the performance but not necessarily ordinary-world physics.

## Subjective/poetic visualization

An audiovisual representation of emotion or relation whose literal ontology is uncertain.

Do not flatten all three into:

> “this literally happened”

or:

> “it is only metaphor.”

*Revue Starlight* often operates precisely between those categories.

---

# XXXV. Anti-overinterpretation rules for the film

Do not:

- assign one fixed meaning to every repeated color;
- assume every cut is symbolic;
- equate every visual recurrence with narrative causality;
- infer a complete leitmotif system from one or two musical similarities;
- treat cinematic extravagance as proof of metaphysical importance;
- assume the most spectacular Revue is necessarily the film's thematic center;
- read every post-TV difficulty as character regression;
- force all pair relationships into romantic, platonic, or rivalry categories if the film deliberately exceeds them;
- collapse performer, role, and character when the film may be staging ambiguity;
- assume chronological reality where theatrical continuity is more plausible;
- claim exact harmony/instrumentation beyond what the final mix supports.

Prefer:

> **converging audiovisual evidence.**

> Who can imagine a future clearly, and who cannot?

> Does future movement threaten an existing relationship?

### Relationship

> Can “we” survive different paths?

> What happens when one person reaches the next stage sooner?

> Does intimacy require shared institutional space?

### Theater

> What still counts as a Revue after the TV finale?

> Where does ordinary life end and performance begin?

> Does that boundary survive the film?

### Music

> What musical material remembers the television series?

> What returns unchanged?

> What must be transformed before it can return?

### Starlight

> Is the rewritten nine-girl *Starlight* an ending, a beginning, or another inherited script?

> Can the girls outgrow even the story they themselves rewrote?

### Audience

> Who wants these girls to remain on the same stage?

> Does the desire to keep watching conflict with the performers' need to move forward?

### Film form

> Is the movie merely containing multiple Revues—

or is the entire film itself functioning as one enormous Revue of transition?

Keep the last question deliberately open until the full film has been read.

# XL. Final interpretive principles

The film should not be treated as:

> thirteen more episodes compressed into two hours.

Nor should its Revues be treated as:

> isolated music videos with symbolic battles.

The appropriate unit is:

> **a long-form theatrical composition in which ordinary life, memory, future anxiety, Revue performance, music, cinematic montage, and inherited TV material progressively transform one another.**

The core method still asks:

> **What can this character express on this impossible stage that she cannot express anywhere else?**

The movie method adds:

> **Why does the film need this character to express it at this exact point in the larger composition?**

and:

> **What does this Revue make possible for the Revue that follows?**

and finally:

> **What can a feature film do with “next stage” that an episodic television series could not?**

The analytical objective is therefore not merely to explain each spectacular sequence.

It is to understand:

> **how the entire movie becomes a stage on which the television series' completed relationships are forced to confront the fact that completion itself cannot be permanent.**

A stage ends.

A person changes.

A relationship moves.

A song returns differently.

A Revue can be replayed—

or re-produced.

The film analysis should determine which operation the movie itself performs.
