# 『少女☆歌劇 レヴュースタァライト』
## Analytical Method and Episode Reading Protocol — Revised Musical-Dramaturgy Edition
### Japanese-original sequential analysis of the TV anime, OVAs, and sequel film

## Revision purpose

This document revises the existing *Revue Starlight* analytical protocol by preserving its core principles while substantially expanding the treatment of music.

The original method correctly treats the anime as a **performed audiovisual text** in which spoken Japanese, vocal performance, song, choreography, staging, editing, lighting, symbolic props, machinery, and spatial relationships form a single dramatic system.

The principal addition here is a dedicated **Musical-Dramaturgy Ledger**.

The revised method assumes that, especially during a Revue, music can do more than accompany the emotional state already expressed by dialogue or image. Composition may:

- anticipate a realization before the character can say it;
- encode conflicting emotional positions simultaneously;
- give different characters distinct sound-worlds;
- change the perceived meaning of a lyric;
- destabilize a character's stated certainty;
- create or deny resolution;
- synchronize with changes in choreography, lighting, weapon use, and stage topology;
- transform when a relationship itself changes;
- preserve a musical memory across episodes;
- or establish continuity and contrast between different Revues.

The governing principle remains:

> **What a character says, how she says it, where she stands while saying it, what surrounds her, and what the music does to her words are potentially different parts of the same argument.**

For the Revue specifically, add a second governing question:

> **What does the music know, express, or transform before the character can say it directly?**

---

# I. Source hierarchy

The analysis should prioritize evidence in the following order.

## Tier 1 — Direct audiovisual evidence

- complete Japanese episode/film audio;
- individual full-resolution retained frames;
- chronological visual sequence;
- actual interaction between image, sound, dialogue, song, and music.

For exact vocal timing, musical entry, attack, silence, dynamic change, and synchronization with action, the **audio is authoritative**.

## Tier 2 — Japanese textual evidence

- corrected Japanese subtitles;
- analysis ASS/SRT tracks;
- dialogue manifests or indices;
- Japanese lyric text where supplied or reliably recoverable from the audiovisual source.

These establish:

- wording;
- grammar;
- register;
- address forms;
- lexical motifs;
- lyric content;
- retrieval points.

Text timing remains navigational. Audio controls the final temporal judgment.

## Tier 3 — Survey and navigation material

- contact sheets;
- scene indices;
- screenshot manifests;
- shot-change metadata.

These identify where to inspect. They should never substitute for direct inspection of important frames or audio passages.

## Tier 4 — Analytical derivatives

Optional technical measurements may be created from the final stereo mix:

- approximate BPM / beat grid;
- onset density;
- RMS or loudness contour;
- spectral centroid / brightness;
- spectral flux;
- approximate chroma / tonal-center estimates;
- relative vocal pitch contours where clean enough;
- waveform and spectrogram inspection.

These are **corroborative**, not primary evidence.

A measurement should never override what the actual performance plainly communicates.

---

# II. Corpus-specific source caveats

## Television episodes

Use Japanese audio and Japanese textual layers as the standard analytical baseline.

## OVAs

The available OVA subtitle layer is English while the complete audio is Japanese.

Therefore:

- visual analysis remains fully available;
- musical analysis remains fully available;
- performed-vocal-state analysis remains fully available;
- broad semantic analysis of dialogue is available;
- exact Japanese lexical/register claims should only be made when the wording can be confidently recovered from the audio.

Do not silently treat the English subtitle wording as Japanese linguistic evidence.

## Sequel film

Use the Japanese audio and Japanese textual layer as the primary basis.

Because the film is much longer and more musically/theatrically dense than a television episode, analyze it as **one continuous film divided internally into dramatic movements and Revue units**.

---

# III. Sequential-reading discipline

Each episode should initially be interpreted only through:

1. the current episode;
2. preceding episodes already analyzed;
3. firmly established series information necessary for identification.

Later revelations should not silently be imported backward.

When later evidence changes an earlier interpretation, record:

> **Earlier reading → new evidence → revised reading.**

This is especially important for:

- repeated dialogue;
- repeated music;
- altered musical motifs;
- replayed staging;
- character memories;
- *Starlight* quotations;
- Revue recurrence.

---

# IV. Evidentiary confidence

Keep three interpretive levels distinct.

## Explicit

Directly established by:

- dialogue;
- lyric;
- visible action;
- stage mechanics;
- audible musical structure;
- formal title/card;
- stated rule.

## Strong inference

Supported by converging evidence across at least two or more channels, such as:

- lyric + harmony;
- vocal delivery + framing;
- music change + scenic transformation;
- choreography + repeated motif;
- instrumentation + established character pattern.

## Speculative interpretation

Plausible but underdetermined.

This includes claims such as:

- a particular chord "means" one character;
- a single instrument always represents one emotion;
- every color or object has one fixed symbolic code;
- an ambiguous harmonic event is definitely a specific named chord without adequate source support.

Symbolism and music should never be treated as codebooks with one-to-one meanings.

---

# V. Pass 0 — Integrity and analytical readiness

Before close reading:

- verify archive integrity;
- identify available audio/text/visual layers;
- confirm episode or film identity;
- reconcile dialogue-index counts where needed;
- identify subtitle language;
- identify whether the music is available only as final mix or as a cleaner source;
- note whether song lyrics are fully represented in the subtitle layer.

For a major Revue, mark its approximate start/end timestamps before detailed analysis.

---

# VI. Pass 1 — Episode reconstruction

Establish what the episode actually does.

Read the Japanese text chronologically while consulting:

- complete audio;
- individual frames;
- scene/shot indices.

Construct a causal map identifying:

- major scenes;
- location changes;
- relationship confrontations;
- rehearsals;
- performances;
- Revue entry and exit points;
- flashbacks;
- temporal dislocations;
- musical transitions;
- significant silent passages;
- ending/post-credit scenes.

The episode description should emphasize causality.

Instead of:

> Karen talks to Hikari. A Revue occurs.

Prefer:

> Karen's attempt to redefine the relationship creates a contradiction that later becomes performable through the Revue.

Ask:

> **What changes because this episode happened?**

---

# VII. Pass 2 — Japanese dialogue and character voice

Analyze Japanese dialogue as characterization, not merely content.

Track where meaningful:

- first-person pronouns;
- address forms;
- honorifics;
- sentence-final particles;
- politeness;
- stylized gendered or theatrical language;
- contractions;
- hesitation;
- repetition;
- self-correction;
- rhetorical questions;
- commands;
- declaratives;
- ellipsis;
- quotation;
- theatrical diction;
- shifts between everyday and Revue speech.

Always distinguish:

> **lexical voice** — what kind of Japanese the character habitually uses

from:

> **performed vocal state** — what the actor does with that language in this emotional condition.

---

# VIII. Pass 3 — Voice acting and performed vocal state

For important scenes, listen directly to the audio.

## Baseline vocal identity

Track relatively stable qualities:

- approximate pitch region;
- brightness/darkness;
- softness/hardness;
- breathiness;
- resonance;
- vocal weight;
- youthful/mature impression;
- articulation precision;
- ordinary speaking tempo.

These should remain comparative rather than falsely laboratory-precise.

## Performed state

For emotionally significant lines, examine:

- relative pitch movement;
- volume;
- tempo;
- breath support;
- constriction;
- consonant attack;
- vowel extension;
- swallowed endings;
- tremor;
- laughter;
- sighs;
- gasps;
- crying coloration;
- pauses;
- silence before/after speech.

## Semantic/performance contradiction

Pay special attention when delivery contradicts content.

Example:

> "I'm fine."

may be performed as:

- exhausted;
- panicked;
- resentful;
- embarrassed;
- grief-stricken.

## Interactional voice

Track:

- interruption;
- overlap;
- who waits;
- who fills silence;
- who grows louder/softer around whom;
- tempo matching;
- conversational synchronization;
- collapse of conversational rhythm.

## Performed-vocal-state ledger

Maintain:

> **baseline → defensive → intimate → competitive → theatrical/Revue → singing → major deviations**

for principal characters.

---

# IX. Pass 4 — Visual close reading

Contact sheets are reconnaissance only.

Use them to locate:

- visual repetitions;
- color changes;
- unusual compositions;
- major entrances;
- silent sequences;
- Revue landmarks.

Then inspect individual retained frames directly.

## Mandatory targeted-frame inspection for ordinary major scenes

Inspect enough frames to establish:

- opening composition;
- major shot/reverse-shot changes;
- important gaze or gesture;
- spatial repositioning;
- visual climax;
- closing composition.

## Mandatory targeted-frame inspection for major Revues

Inspect individual frames around at least:

- Revue threshold;
- opening positions;
- first sung phrase;
- first physical exchange;
- first major musical change;
- first scenic transformation;
- important lyrical turn;
- weapon contact;
- major dynamic/orchestral change;
- lighting change;
- elevation change;
- spoken interruption;
- climax;
- costume/button resolution;
- Position Zero;
- final tableau.

For especially dense Revues, inspect nearly shot-by-shot where warranted.

Examples:

- stars;
- towers;
- stairs;
- elevators;
- trains/rails;
- circles;
- cages;
- curtains;
- ropes;
- spotlights;
- mirrors;
- water;
- fire;
- falling;
- height;
- machinery;
- empty seats;
- audience positions;
- weapons;
- costume fasteners/buttons;
- the giraffe;
- Position Zero;
- stage marks;
- flowers;

- roads/pathways;
- scripts;

For each recurrence, ask:

> Where has this appeared before?

> With whom?

> In what emotional and musical context?

> Has its function remained stable?

# XI. The Revue Protocol

Every major Revue should be treated as a miniature work of musical theatre.

The Revue subsection should answer not simply:

> Who fought and who won?

but:

> **What relationship became performable here, and how did music, language, body, and stage argue about it?**

---

## 1. Preconditions

Before analyzing the performance, establish:

- participants;
- unresolved relationship/desire;
- each participant's current goal;
- each participant's misunderstanding or blind spot;
- what the episode has already shown that they do not know;
- what emotional contradiction requires theatrical externalization.

---

## 2. Stage topology

Reconstruct stage geography as relational argument.

Track:

- center/periphery;
- elevation;
- distance;
- barriers;
- movement corridors;
- circular/linear movement;
- confinement/openness;
- symmetry/asymmetry;
- who controls space;
- who forces movement;
- who holds high ground;
- who reaches/loses center.

Do not assume:

> height = superiority.

Ask how elevation functions in this specific relationship.

---

## 3. Weapons and bodily performance

Treat weapons as theatrical extensions of character.

Examine:

- form;
- size;
- range;
- offensive/defensive use;
- stance;
- speed;
- evasiveness;
- directness;
- reach;
- whether combat resembles dance, chase, duel, siege, game, execution, or ritual.

Ask:

> **What kind of relationship is this choreography making visible?**

---

## 4. Scenic transformation

Every major Revue transformation should be treated as dramaturgical evidence.

Ask:

- What claim preceded it?
- Which character appears to own the new stage?
- What memory or emotional model does it materialize?
- Does the stage confirm or undermine the character's claim?
- Does the opponent learn to navigate, destroy, appropriate, or escape it?
- Does the music transform before, during, or after the scenery?

This last question is mandatory in the revised method.

---

# XII. Lyrics as dramatic language

Revue lyrics are part of the dialogue.

Analyze:

- pronouns;
- address forms;
- imperatives;
- rhetorical questions;
- repeated words;
- parallel phrases;
- images;
- contradictions;
- tense;
- modality;
- promise;
- light/star vocabulary;
- possession;
- separation;
- victory;
- defeat;
- sacrifice;
- fate;
- stage;
- audience;
- reaching;
- repetition.

Determine whether a lyric functions as:

- confession;
- accusation;
- self-mythology;
- challenge;
- defense;
- plea;
- prophecy;
- shared narration;
- counterargument.

Avoid extensive quotation; use short Japanese anchors and paraphrase the rest.

---

# XIII. Vocal allocation inside song

Track **who sings what**.

Ask:

- who initiates;
- who answers;
- who receives the melody;
- who takes over an earlier phrase;
- whether voices overlap;
- whether they sing in unison;
- whether they harmonize;
- whether one character disappears;
- whether one voice becomes background;
- whether one voice completes another's line;
- whether musical agreement contradicts lyrical disagreement;
- who receives the final statement.

Vocal allocation is a relational structure.

---

# XIV. Dedicated Musical-Dramaturgy Ledger

Every major Revue must now receive a **Musical-Dramaturgy Ledger**.

The ledger treats composition as a dramatic evidence stream equal in importance to lyric, choreography, and image.

## A. Opening musical state

Establish:

- approximate tempo or pulse;
- perceived meter/rhythmic organization where clear;
- tonal/modal color;
- opening instrumentation;
- register;
- density;
- dynamic level;
- emotional stability/instability;
- whose subjectivity appears to dominate the sound-world.

Ask:

> What emotional world does the composition establish before the conflict develops?

---

## B. Tempo, pulse, and meter

Track:

- approximate BPM;
- acceleration/deceleration;
- half-time/double-time feel;
- stable versus unstable pulse;
- rhythmic interruption;
- ostinato;
- syncopation;
- march/dance/waltz/chase-like motion where musically defensible.

Do not assign emotional meaning mechanically.

Instead ask:

> What does this rhythmic behavior allow the bodies and relationship to do?

---

## C. Tonal and harmonic field

Where aurally supportable, examine:

- approximate tonal center;
- major/minor/modal coloration;
- chromatic tension;
- harmonic stability;
- pedal tones;
- repeated progressions;
- modulation or tonal shift;
- cadence;
- cadence avoidance;
- unresolved suspension;
- dissonance/consonance.

Claims should be proportionate to confidence.

Use:

> "the harmony becomes less stable"

when the exact chord spelling is uncertain.

Do not manufacture exact Roman-numeral analysis from an ambiguous final mix.

---

## D. Melodic shape

Track:

- ascending/descending contour;
- repeated-note figures;
- leaps;
- narrow versus expansive range;
- melodic return;
- inversion;
- sequence;
- suspended phrases;
- unresolved endings.

Ask:

> Does the melody enact aspiration, enclosure, hesitation, collapse, insistence, or release?

Treat that as interpretation supported by contour, not as a universal dictionary.

---

## E. Instrumentation and timbre

Identify audible instrumental families and textures where possible:

- strings;
- piano/keyboard;
- percussion;
- drum kit;
- brass;
- woodwind-like colors;
- bells/chimes;
- electric/electronic textures;
- distortion;
- low drones;
- high sustained textures;
- orchestral tutti;
- sparse chamber-like texture.

Ask:

> Which sounds belong to which emotional phase?

> Does one participant seem associated with a particular texture?

> Does that association persist or change?

Never infer an unavailable orchestration detail merely because a timbre resembles an instrument.

---

## F. Register

Track whether the musical energy is concentrated:

- low;
- mid;
- high;
- or spread across a broad register.

Notice:

- bass expansion;
- high-string/brass intensification;
- rising vocal tessitura;
- sudden emptying of low or high frequencies.

Register can alter perceived scale, openness, heaviness, or exposure.

---

## G. Density and dynamics

Track:

- sparse versus dense arrangement;
- gradual layering;
- sudden tutti;
- dropouts;
- crescendos;
- diminuendos;
- compression into a single voice/instrument;
- explosive re-entry.

Ask:

> Does emotional pressure increase through loudness, through density, through register, or through silence?

These are not the same thing.

---

## H. Motifs and recurrence

Track recurring:

- melodic cells;
- rhythmic figures;
- harmonic loops;
- instrumental colors;
- vocal gestures;
- sound effects integrated into music.

For each motif, ask:

> Where did it appear earlier?

> Does it return unchanged?

> Is it reharmonized, slowed, fragmented, expanded, or reassigned to another character?

A transformed motif may be more important than a repeated one.

---

## I. Character sound-worlds

For each Revue participant, ask whether the composition gives her a distinct musical identity.

Track:

- instrumentation;
- register;
- rhythmic figure;
- melody;
- harmonic field;
- vocal placement.

Then ask:

> Do the sound-worlds remain separate?

> Do they collide?

> Does one swallow the other?

> Do they begin sharing musical material?

This is one of the most important new analytical questions.

---

## J. Vocal-harmonic relationship

When two or more characters sing together, examine:

- unison;
- octave doubling;
- parallel movement;
- contrapuntal independence;
- consonant harmony;
- dissonant harmony;
- call-and-response;
- overlapping phrases.

Ask:

> Are they musically together while emotionally opposed?

> Are they lyrically aligned while harmonically unstable?

The relationship between voices may contradict the literal lyric.

---

## K. Speech–song boundary

Mark every significant:

> **singing → speaking → singing**

transition.

For each transition, examine:

- whether accompaniment stops;
- whether harmony freezes;
- whether pulse continues;
- whether sound narrows;
- whether the vocal register becomes more conversational;
- whether the character stops performing "for the stage" and speaks directly.

A naked spoken line inside music may function as an epistemic break.

---

## L. Music/action synchronization

Timestamp major moments where musical events coincide with:

- attack;
- parry;
- fall;
- leap;
- weapon transformation;
- costume cut;
- movement toward center;
- Position Zero;
- hand extension;
- gaze shift;
- stage destruction;
- entrance/exit.

Do not merely state:

> "the hit lands on the beat."

Ask:

> **What claim does that synchronization make?**

---

## M. Music/scenery synchronization

Track whether:

- a harmonic shift precedes the scenic transformation;
- percussion announces it;
- instrumentation changes after it;
- vocal ownership shifts as the stage changes;
- lighting follows musical attack;
- the stage remains visually stable while the music destabilizes.

The order matters.

If the score changes **before** the character understands what is happening, note it.

---

## N. Silence and subtraction

Silence is part of the Musical-Dramaturgy Ledger.

Track:

- complete silence;
- near-silence;
- removal of percussion;
- removal of harmony;
- exposed voice;
- exposed breath;
- lingering mechanical sound after music stops.

Ask:

> What becomes sayable only after the score withdraws?

---

## O. Climactic transformation

Every major Revue should identify the exact point at which the **musical argument changes**.

Examples of possible markers:

- key/tonal shift;
- new tempo feel;
- new instrumentation;
- new melody;
- return of an old motif;
- sudden vocal unison;
- removal of one character's sound-world;
- expansion from sparse to orchestral texture;
- lyrical perspective shift;
- "second act" or equivalent formal division.

Ask:

> **What changed in the relationship at the same moment?**

This should be one of the central claims of the Revue analysis.

---

## P. Ending and cadence

Examine how the Revue ends musically.

Does it:

- resolve firmly;
- stop abruptly;
- suspend;
- fade;
- return to an earlier theme;
- leave a harmonic question open;
- end on spoken dialogue rather than song;
- continue after the mechanical victory?

The musical ending may differ from the mechanical ending.

---

# XV. Timestamped Music / Action / Staging Concordance

For particularly important Revues, create a timestamped concordance.

Recommended format:

| Time | Lyric / speech | Vocal owner | Musical event | Choreography / visual event | Relational-dramatic function | Confidence |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 00:00 | Opening phrase | A | Sparse low-register texture | A elevated, B below | Establishes asymmetry | Explicit/Strong |
| 00:42 | Spoken interruption | B | Harmony drops out | B closes distance | Refuses prior frame | Strong |
| 01:35 | Shared phrase | A+B | New harmonic layer / broader register | Stage transforms | Relationship changes form | Strong |

The concordance should not become a mechanical transcription of every second.

Use it at **dramaturgical hinges**.

For the most important Revues, however, the concordance may become dense enough to function almost like a musical-theatre score map.

---

# XVI. The "music knows first" test

At every major Revue turning point, ask:

> **Does the score begin changing before the character verbally understands the change?**

Possible patterns:

### Music anticipates language

The composition has already shifted before the character makes the realization.

### Music contradicts language

The character claims certainty while harmony/rhythm remains unstable.

### Music confirms language

A verbal breakthrough and compositional resolution occur together.

### Music lags behind language

The character declares a change, but the score remains trapped in the earlier emotional structure until action proves it.

These patterns should be tracked cumulatively.

---

# XVII. Optional quantitative audio analysis

Use computation selectively when it materially sharpens the reading.

## Useful measurements

### Approximate tempo

Use beat tracking only to establish:

- stable approximate BPM;
- change in pulse;
- half/double-time relationship.

### RMS / loudness contour

Useful for:

- relative dynamic expansion;
- collapse before a spoken line;
- climax comparison.

### Onset density

Useful for:

- rhythmic activity;
- escalation;
- sparse versus attack-heavy passages.

### Spectral centroid / brightness

Useful for comparing:

- darker/heavier;
- brighter/higher-energy orchestral states.

Treat as a descriptor of the mix, not a direct emotion meter.

### Chroma / tonal-center estimation

Useful for:

- identifying broad tonal shifts;
- corroborating modulation or harmonic contrast.

Use cautiously under dense vocals/percussion.

### Vocal F0 / pitch contour

Use only where:

- the voice is sufficiently isolated;
- the comparison is relative;
- music does not contaminate the estimate excessively.

## Do not overuse

Quantitative analysis is not valuable merely because it is possible.

The purpose is to test or sharpen a musically meaningful hypothesis.

---

# XVIII. Final-mix limitations

The available soundtrack is generally a **finished stereo mix**, not a multitrack session.

Therefore:

- exact instrumental isolation may be impossible;
- overlapping vocals may contaminate measurements;
- reverberation may blur attacks;
- effects may resemble instruments;
- loudness is mix-dependent;
- tonal estimation may be confused by dense orchestration.

Optional source separation may be used as a listening aid, but machine-separated stems are **not authoritative evidence**.

Do not claim:

> "this exact cello line represents X"

unless the instrument and line can actually be heard confidently.

Prefer:

> "a low sustained string-like texture"

when identification is uncertain.

---

# XIX. Musical evidence confidence tiers

For musical claims, use a three-level confidence model.

## Musical Tier A — Directly audible structure

Examples:

- tempo clearly accelerates;
- percussion drops out;
- vocal unison begins;
- instrumentation becomes denser;
- a recurring melody returns;
- music cuts to silence.

These can support **explicit** musical claims.

## Musical Tier B — Analytical corroboration

Examples:

- approximate BPM;
- broad tonal-center shift;
- measured increase in spectral brightness;
- relative dynamic contour.

These support or refine direct listening.

## Musical Tier C — Dramatic interpretation

Examples:

- the rising melody enacts aspiration;
- the unresolved cadence reflects an unresolved relationship;
- a character's motif is being appropriated by another.

These require contextual support and should be labeled strong inference or speculative depending on convergence.

---

# XX. Song/action synchronization

For every important lyric, ask what occurs simultaneously.

A lyric may change meaning because:

- the singer attacks;
- the opponent turns away;
- the camera cuts elsewhere;
- the stage transforms;
- a weapon breaks;
- someone loses height;
- the costume condition is severed;
- a hand is offered/refused;
- Position Zero is reached.

The Revue must therefore be treated as:

> **lyrics + vocal acting + composition + choreography + image**

rather than five independent tracks.

---

# XXI. Speech inside the Revue

Spoken lines embedded in song require special scrutiny.

For each:

- why does song stop?
- does accompaniment continue?
- does harmony suspend?
- does the character sound more ordinary or more theatrical?
- who is addressed?
- what changes immediately afterward?

A spoken claim may be the point where performance stops being metaphor and becomes direct relationship.

---

# XXII. Revue outcome

Never reduce the Revue to:

> A defeats B.

Record five outcomes separately.

## Mechanical result

Who wins according to the Revue's rules?

## Emotional result

Who changes emotionally?

## Epistemic result

Who learns something?

## Relational result

What becomes newly possible or impossible between them?

## Symbolic/theatrical result

What happens to:

- stage;
- costume;
- weapon;
- lighting;
- motif;
- Position Zero;
- musical form?

Add a sixth category in this revised methodology:

## Musical result

What happens to the musical argument by the end?

Examples:

- one character's motif survives;
- two sound-worlds merge;
- the music refuses resolution despite mechanical victory;
- the climax introduces a new musical state that persists afterward.

---

# XXIII. Pass 5 — Character and relationship development

After audiovisual analysis, update each materially involved character:

- current desire;
- current fear;
- self-conception;
- defensive behavior;
- performance identity;
- artistic philosophy;
- important relationships;
- contradictions;
- unresolved questions.

Avoid flattening labels.

Instead of:

> Mahiru is jealous.

Prefer:

> Mahiru's attachment becomes legible through a form of jealousy that protects a self-worth system built around being needed.

Relationships should be tracked dynamically:

> closeness → asymmetry → dependency → rivalry → rupture → recognition → revised intimacy

where supported.

---

# XXIV. Theatricality and meta-performance

Track simultaneously:

- girls as students;
- girls as actresses;
- roles in *Starlight*;
- roles in the Revue;
- performers visible to us;
- audiences/judges inside the fiction;
- the Giraffe as spectator;
- music as part of the theatrical institution.

Treat *Starlight* as an intertext.

Whenever its:

- dialogue;
- blocking;
- roles;
- promises;
- separations;
- deaths;
- reunions;
- musical ideas

echo the girls' lives, ask whether they are:

> **performing the play,**

> **being shaped by the play,**

or:

> **rewriting the play through their lives.**

---

# XXV. Sound beyond song

Track meaningful non-musical and para-musical sound:

- footsteps;
- breathing;
- trains;
- machinery;
- bells;
- curtains;
- weapon impacts;
- applause;
- audience ambience;
- school noise;
- environmental hum;
- silence.

Ask whether sound design:

- continues musical rhythm;
- interrupts it;
- replaces it;
- exposes bodily reality after theatrical excess.

---

# XXVI. Episode-level output structure

A completed episode analysis should use approximately this structure.

## Episode thesis

What is the episode fundamentally doing?

## Dramatic architecture

Major causal movements.

## Character development

What changes?

## Japanese language and speech

Important register, wording, verbal motifs, conversational asymmetries.

## Voice acting and performed vocal states

Specific delivery observations and ledger updates.

## Visual direction

Composition, framing, color, movement, motifs, individually inspected frames.

## Revue deep dive

When present:

- preconditions;
- stage topology;
- weapons/body;
- scenic transformations;
- lyrics;
- vocal allocation;
- Musical-Dramaturgy Ledger;
- music/action/staging concordance for major Revues;
- speech/song transitions;
- choreography;

# XXVIII. Cross-Revue musical comparison

After several Revues have been analyzed, compare them directly.

Questions include:

- Does the same character receive similar instrumentation across Revues?
- Does a musical figure change after character development?
- Do certain relationships produce characteristic rhythmic or harmonic structures?
- Does a later Revue quote or transform an earlier one?
- Does Position Zero receive a recurring musical treatment?
- Does a character's sound-world become more open, more dense, more rhythmically stable, or more integrated with another's?
- Does a motif originally owned by one girl become shared?
- Are repeated *Starlight* lines set differently as their meaning changes?

The goal is not to prove a rigid leitmotif system.

It is to identify musical memory where the work actually provides it.

# XXIX. Film-specific musical protocol

For the sequel film, do not attempt one undifferentiated two-hour music analysis.

Divide the film into:

- dramatic movements;
- major Revue units;
- transitional musical passages;
- reprise/quotation structures.

For each major Revue:

1. build the local Musical-Dramaturgy Ledger;
2. identify links to the TV-series Revue corpus;
3. identify reused/transformed motifs;
4. inspect whether the film expands, overturns, or completes earlier musical arguments;
5. treat long-form musical recurrence as narrative memory.

The movie should eventually receive a **film-level musical architecture** showing how individual Revue units relate across the full runtime.

# XXX. Frame-inspection rule

Contact sheets are never sufficient evidence for:

- a major Revue;
- a major emotional confrontation;
- a major musical/visual synchronization claim.

They are reconnaissance.

When musical interpretation depends on a visual event, inspect the individual retained frames surrounding the relevant timestamp.

For dense sequences, proceed almost shot-by-shot.

---

# XXXI. Anti-overinterpretation rules

Do not:

- assign every instrument a permanent character meaning;
- treat major as happy and minor as sad;
- treat dissonance as morally bad;
- infer exact harmony where the final mix is ambiguous;
- interpret every beat-synchronized strike as symbolic;
- claim that quantitative audio features directly measure emotion;
- assume the loudest passage is the emotional climax;
- confuse production complexity with narrative importance;
- use source-separated artifacts as primary evidence.

Prefer converging evidence.

A strong claim often has the form:

> **The lyric changes from X to Y; the harmony destabilizes; the vocal owner changes; the stage loses its earlier geometry; and the choreography forces the characters into direct proximity. Together these strongly support a relational transformation.**

---

# XXXII. Final interpretive principles

The analysis should resist reducing the Revues to:

> "This fantasy battle symbolizes their feelings."

That is true but insufficient.

The stronger approach remains:

> **The Revue is the form in which a relationship becomes performable.**

And the revised musical corollary is:

> **The composition is the emotional architecture inside that performance.**

A character cannot merely state:

- jealousy;
- dependency;
- ambition;
- grief;
- fear of abandonment;
- desire for recognition;
- need to stand beside someone.

She may have to:

- sing it;
- harmonize against it;
- fall out of rhythm with another person;
- enter a new tonal space;
- lose musical support;
- speak into silence;
- let a motif be transformed;
- fight through the stage it creates;
- and risk losing the position from which she understood herself.

The central questions should therefore repeatedly be:

> **What can this character express on this impossible stage that she cannot express anywhere else?**

> **What does the music express before she can say it?**

> **When the relationship changes, does the composition change with it—or was the composition already telling us that change was possible?**
