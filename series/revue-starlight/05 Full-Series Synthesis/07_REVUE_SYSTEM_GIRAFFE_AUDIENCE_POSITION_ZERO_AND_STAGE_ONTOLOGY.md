# 『少女☆歌劇 レヴュースタァライト』
## Revue System, Giraffe, Audience, Position Zero, and Stage Ontology
### Who watches, who judges, who burns, and what counts as a stage

**Primary analytical home:** The hidden audition, Revue rules and exceptions, `キラめき` as extractable and re-producible theatrical capacity, the Giraffe's changing functions, performer/audience reciprocity, Position Zero's complete development, movie `wi(l)d-screen baroque`, intimate spectatorship, and the movement from the underground stage to ordinary audition.

**Spoiler scope:** Complete television series, all three OVAs, sequel film, credits, and post-credit audition.

---

# I. Executive thesis

A Revue in *Revue Starlight* is simultaneously:

- a diegetic competition;
- a theatrical performance;
- a relationship made spatial, musical, and bodily;
- a machine that turns desire into visible form;
- and an event whose meaning cannot be exhausted by who mechanically wins.

The corpus begins by making those layers appear almost identical.

The Giraffe announces an audition.

Stage Girls fight.

A costume fastening is cut.

One performer reaches Position Zero.

A ranking changes.

The visual and musical force of the event encourages the viewer to read the whole Revue through its winner.

The complete animated corpus gradually dismantles that equivalence.

Junna loses mechanically and gains a future beyond one chance.

Kaoruko defeats Futaba without restoring Futaba to permanent fanhood.

Hikari reaches Position Zero only after the decisive re-production has already occurred through Karen, memory, music, and renewed possibility.

Karen and Hikari win a pair Revue whose institution still intends to select one final person.

Hikari wins the audition and produces the most relationally destructive outcome of the television series by disappearing without consent.

Karen and Hikari later share Position Zero.

Claudine then refuses to let Position Zero end a movie Revue because the self-authored wager has not been fulfilled.

By the final film Revue, Position Zero remains theatrically potent but no longer possesses sovereign authority over what the performance means or what the performers become next.

The system's development can therefore be described as:

> **mechanical center → contested certification → shared threshold → insufficient ending condition → theatrical punctuation.**

The Giraffe undergoes a parallel transformation.

He first appears as if he were a supernatural referee standing outside the performers' desires.

The television series gradually reveals that he is not outside the spectacle.

He is:

- organizer;
- adjudicator;
- spectator;
- consumer of `キラめき`;
- lover of the stage;
- and eventually self-declared fuel.

TV12 gives his clearest stage ontology:

> `舞台とは 演じる者と見る者が 揃って成り立つもの`

A stage exists when performer and viewer are both present.

And:

> `演者が立ち 観客が望むかぎり続く`

As long as the performer stands and the audience desires it, the stage continues.

The movie adds the ethical cost of that formulation.

The audience is:

> `わがままで欲張り`

selfish and greedy.

It wants another performance after the ending.

It wants the girls to keep producing themselves.

The Giraffe recognizes his own assigned role:

> `舞台に火を灯すための 燃料！`

Fuel that lights the stage.

This does not redeem the audience as benevolent.

It implicates spectatorship in the same economy of combustion that once treated the Stage Girls' radiance as fuel.

The full corpus consequently refuses two simple positions:

### The audience is not sovereign

Its desire does not determine performer welfare, ethical legitimacy, or the correct ending.

### The audience is not optional

A performance without witness does not become the kind of social/theatrical event the work calls a stage.

The central ethical problem is therefore not whether to be seen.

It is:

> **What kind of gaze allows the performer to remain a changing subject rather than a preferred image that the viewer demands again?**

The work explores this question at several scales.

The Giraffe represents institutionalized appetite.

Nana becomes an intimate spectator whose memory freezes Junna in past tense.

Futaba's fanhood becomes insufficient once she needs to perform back at Kaoruko.

Hikari fears becoming Karen's fan because Karen's radiance threatens to turn Hikari into only a viewer.

Claudine becomes the more generative intimate spectator: she wants to expose another Maya, but she accepts reciprocal exposure and demands a performance whose result remains unknown.

Mahiru turns the Mr. White audience from invitation to pressure to shelter.

Karen finally discovers the audience's proximity, heat, and fear in the movie's last movement.

The external viewer is never simply equated with the Giraffe, but the film makes our appetite impossible to treat as neutral.

We wanted what the Giraffe wanted:

> a continuation after the ending.

The film gives it to us, then denies us the right to preserve the girls forever in the roles through which we first loved them.

The mature stage ontology of the complete animated corpus is therefore:

> **A stage is a temporary, witnessed, relational event in which performers risk making changing selves visible. It can be institutionally organized without being fully governed by the institution; it can produce a winner without making the winner its only truth; it requires an audience without granting the audience ownership; and it must be capable of ending so that performers can enter another stage as people the previous performance did not completely contain.**

# II. Methodological distinction: system, performance, and metaphor

The Revue must not be reduced either to literal supernatural sport or to “only metaphor.”

The corpus consistently operates across at least three ontological layers.

## 1. Diegetic Revue fact

The television audition has stable enough mechanics to produce factual claims.

The Giraffe:

- invites participants;
- announces matches;
- maintains rankings;
- identifies winners;
- links defeated radiance to the Top Star prize;
- recognizes formal conditions such as Position Zero;
- and interacts with Nana's replay and Hikari's refusal of fuel.

Costume fastening loss often functions as a mechanical defeat condition.

The girls remember the Revues.

Their outcomes affect later choices.

The system is not imaginary in the ordinary sense.

## 2. Performed theatrical reality

The stage also makes emotional and relational conditions materially real without requiring every image to obey ordinary-world physics.

Examples include:

- Mahiru's domestic fantasy becoming a dangerous performance space;
- Nana's remembered year becoming replay architecture;
- Hikari's penitential guilt becoming an endless build-collapse world;
- Futaba/Kaoruko's divergence becoming vehicles and highway;
- Junna/Nana's dispute over language becoming giant words, photographs, katana, and pen;
- Maya/Claudine's rivalry becoming devil, wager, mirrors, and soul;
- Karen's completed role becoming theatrical death.

These events are real **as theater** even when their ordinary material status is deliberately uncertain.

## 3. Interpretive or poetic meaning

The viewer may infer broader meanings from recurring forms:

- Position Zero as institutional center;
- Tower as summit and inherited tragedy;
- tomato as ripeness and unstable living potential;
- fire as expenditure and re-production;
- train as time, route, and chosen movement.

Those meanings remain interpretations rather than rules mechanically declared by the system.

### Why the distinction matters

If every visual is treated as literal metaphysics, the analysis becomes pseudo-technical mythology.

If every Revue is treated as only psychological symbolism, the work's insistence that performance changes what becomes possible for the characters is lost.

The more accurate formulation is:

> **The Revue is a diegetically consequential form of performed theatrical reality whose images and music remain open to interpretation.**

Its ontology is theatrical rather than merely physical or merely subjective.

# III. The hidden audition as institution

The early television series presents the audition through ritual mystery.

A normal arts school sits above:

- elevators;
- rails;
- platforms;
- gears;
- lighting rigs;
- costume mechanisms;
- the Giraffe's stage.

This spatial arrangement is already an institutional argument.

The ordinary school teaches:

- singing;
- dance;
- acting;
- craft;
- cooperation;
- professional discipline.

The hidden system converts those same capacities into:

- ranking;
- scarcity;
- spectacle;
- fuel;
- singular succession.

The underground stage is not the opposite of Seisho.

It is a distorted intensification of values already present in elite artistic training:

- visibility matters;
- casting matters;
- ranking matters;
- opportunity is unequal;
- another performer's excellence can become threat;
- the audience wants a center.

The work does not therefore portray the audition as an alien evil that corrupts an otherwise egalitarian school.

The audition externalizes pressures already latent in the institution.

## Invitation and participation

The exact limits of invitation, coercion, and withdrawal remain partly opaque.

The girls participate knowingly enough to pursue ranking, yet they do not initially understand:

- the full extraction mechanism;
- Hikari's prior London experience;
- Nana's ability to exploit recurrence;
- the actual meaninglessness of the tiara;
- or how the Giraffe's desire relates to the stage.

This asymmetry of knowledge is ethically important.

The system offers opportunity while withholding the full cost.

Hikari knows more than Karen, and this knowledge helps produce her recurrent belief that she is entitled to decide for Karen.

The Giraffe's procedural neutrality is therefore incomplete.

A referee who understands the stakes far better than the performers is not simply neutral because he applies rules consistently.

## Ranking

Ranking gives artistic desire a visible order.

It is psychologically powerful because it converts ambiguity into legibility.

Junna can know where she stands.

Maya can occupy first place.

Karen's unexpected entry can become a measurable disruption.

Nana can compare repetitions.

Yet the corpus repeatedly demonstrates that ranking cannot measure:

- whether defeat enlarges the performer's future;
- whether a relationship becomes reciprocal;
- whether a song reaches its needed truth;
- whether a role should end;
- whether the audience has become possessive;
- whether a winner's decision is ethical.

Ranking is therefore real and incomplete.

## Victory conditions

The early system teaches the viewer to recognize several recurrent signs:

- weapon strike;
- cut costume fastening;
- fallen cloak/button;
- declared winner;
- Position Zero;
- ranking movement.

The series then uses its own repetition to loosen those associations.

A fastening can be damaged without terminating the TV12 Revue.

Position Zero can certify an already-completed transformation.

A pair can win locally while the larger system still selects one.

A winner can produce the worst relational result.

A movie Revue can end without any meaningful conventional verdict.

The six-outcome method is therefore not analytical ornament.

It is necessary because the work itself separates:

1. mechanical outcome;
2. emotional outcome;
3. epistemic outcome;
4. relational outcome;
5. symbolic/theatrical outcome;
6. musical outcome.

The system recognizes primarily the first.

The story often treats the other five as more important.

# IV. `キラめき`, extraction, and the fuel economy

The audition's most disturbing mechanism is its treatment of `キラめき` as fuel.

The word remains conceptually plural across the corpus.

It can indicate:

- artistic vitality;
- desire;
- affective access to the stage;
- visible radiance;
- capability;
- the energy a performance generates;
- something the system can extract;
- something a relationship can re-produce;
- something another person's gaze may be captured by.

The system treats that plurality reductively.

It converts radiance into resource.

## Hikari's London evidence

Hikari's prior audition establishes that extraction can produce a recognizably diminished performer.

She loses:

- stage desire;
- expressive access;
- a sense of self as performer.

The television series presents this as more than disappointment.

The extraction has diegetic force.

But Hikari's later re-production demonstrates that the system's resource model is not the final ontology of radiance.

If `キラめき` were only a fixed quantity transferred from one person to another, re-production through changed possibility would be unintelligible.

## Nana and infinite fuel

Nana discovers that intense Stage Girl radiance makes the stage respond.

Her replay exploits the system's relation among:

- audience desire;
- performer radiance;
- stage machinery;
- recurrence.

She turns the system's spectacular responsiveness into a preservation device.

This is another reason Nana cannot be reduced to a victim of the audition.

She becomes an author within it.

## TV08: re-production breaks the battery model

Hikari's recovery is not restoration of a stored original quantity.

Karen introduces an emotionally credible future.

The score changes.

Hikari's relation to the stage changes.

Then:

> `きらめきの再生産`

is named.

Radiance appears as something performers can generate through changed relation and renewed theatrical orientation.

## TV11: possession without access

Karen's seven-month collapse adds a second complication.

Her radiance was not mechanically extracted.

Yet she cannot feel the stage.

The distinction between possession and access proves that `キラめき` is not merely stored energy.

It is bound to:

- desired future;
- meaningful role;
- relation;
- the ability to imagine oneself standing again.

## TV12: crown without intrinsic meaning

The Giraffe states that the star tiara has no meaning in itself.

Its purpose is to lure another sinner toward the Stage of Destiny.

This demystifies the prize without demystifying artistic transformation.

The girls' labor, skill, pain, relationships, and development remain real even if the object organizing the competition is arbitrary.

The distinction has broad institutional force:

> **A prize can be contingent while the effort and self-production organized around it remain profound.**

## Consent and chosen expenditure

TV12 also transforms the verb `奪う`.

Earlier the audition takes without meaningful consent.

Karen later says:

> `奪っていいよ 私の全部！`

Hikari answers:

> `私の全て 奪ってみせて`

The work does not conclude that loss is harmless.

It distinguishes:

- extraction;
- voluntary artistic expenditure;
- reciprocal risk.

Performance always costs something.

The ethical question is:

> who chooses the expenditure, who benefits, and whether the performer remains a subject inside it.

Document 11 develops the normative implications; here the system-level point is that the Giraffe's fuel economy is not the only way a Stage Girl can burn.

# V. The Giraffe's functional development

The Giraffe is deliberately difficult to stabilize as one allegorical object.

He is not simply:

- God;
- producer;
- audience;
- capitalism;
- anime viewer;
- theater itself;
- or neutral referee.

He occupies several functions whose tension is part of the work.

## Phase 1 — announcer and adjudicator

Early television presents him through procedural authority.

He:

- announces;
- explains;
- ranks;
- reacts to radiance;
- validates winners.

His repeated:

> `分かります`

creates a tone of calm, absurd certainty.

He appears to understand what neither characters nor viewers yet understand.

The vocal performance is important.

His measured delivery makes the surreal system sound administratively normal.

This is more unsettling than overt villainy would be.

## Phase 2 — responsive apparatus

The Giraffe repeatedly says that the stage responds to Stage Girl radiance.

He therefore functions less like an omnipotent author than an operator of a system that depends upon performer input.

The girls generate something he cannot manufacture alone.

This becomes especially clear with Nana.

Her replay is interesting precisely because it exploits the stage's responsiveness in a way even the system treats as exceptional.

## Phase 3 — spectator with appetite

TV12 makes his identity explicit:

> `舞台を愛する観客にして`

an audience member who loves the stage,

and:

> `運命の舞台の主催者`

organizer of the Stage of Destiny.

The two roles cannot be cleanly separated.

He is not a viewer innocently receiving a stage made elsewhere.

He creates conditions designed to generate what he wants to see.

This is why his spectatorship is ethically dangerous.

The system is not neutral infrastructure.

It is infrastructure shaped by appetite.

## Phase 4 — lover of unpredictability

The Giraffe does not ultimately defend the inherited rules when Karen exceeds them.

He becomes delighted by:

- chemical reaction;
- unpredictability;
- continuation after the ending;
- re-production of the Stage of Destiny.

This prevents a simple reading of him as conservative law.

He is closer to an absolutist aesthete.

He wants the event that most intensely exceeds expectation.

This openness can enable liberation.

It is also indifferent to the suffering required to produce the event.

## Phase 5 — frantic audience

The film prologue makes him run.

His anxiety is not that Karen or Hikari may be harmed.

It is:

> Did I miss it?

The controlled `分かります` Giraffe becomes a spectator terrified that the performance has already ended.

This comic panic exposes appetite more honestly than the television ritual did.

## Phase 6 — public-space presence

The Giraffe stands in a metropolitan intersection during `wi(l)d-screen baroque`.

He no longer belongs only to the underground Seisho system.

The city itself has become audience/stage space.

This relocation implies that the logic he names is not confined to one hidden audition.

Graduation, careers, travel, and ordinary adulthood are already theatrical conditions.

## Phase 7 — fuel

The film gives him the most radical reversal:

> `私はあなたたちの糧 あなたたちの燃料`

and later:

> `舞台に火を灯すための 燃料！`

The spectator who consumed radiance now calls himself material for the performers.

This does not erase the earlier extraction.

It reveals a reciprocal circuit:

- performers give audience something to desire;
- audience desire funds, sustains, and pressures performance;
- the new performance consumes both performer and spectator attention;
- the result creates another appetite.

The film does not describe this circuit as clean or fair.

It describes it as theater.

# VI. Audience taxonomy: not every gaze does the same thing

The complete corpus requires a more precise vocabulary than “audience.”

Watching can:

- recognize;
- rank;
- consume;
- preserve;
- encourage;
- judge;
- frighten;
- expose;
- or become another performance.

The ethical quality of spectatorship depends less upon distance than upon what future the gaze permits.

## 1. Institutional audience — Giraffe

The Giraffe's gaze is systemically powerful.

He can:

- create the conditions of the audition;
- legitimate results;
- convert radiance into fuel;
- continue the stage while performers stand and viewers desire.

His appetite is generalized.

He wants:

> Stage Girl radiance as event.

He can appreciate the person-specific chemical reaction without becoming personally answerable to every cost.

## 2. Public/theatrical audience

The ordinary audience appears through:

- theater seats;
- Seisho festival performance;
- Karen's late movie recognition of proximity and heat;
- post-credit audition judges and staff.

This audience makes the performer visible within a social world.

It is not necessarily intimate.

Its judgment may be professional, aesthetic, institutional, or commercial.

The final audition matters because it removes metaphysical exceptionalism.

Karen must now perform before people who do not share the childhood promise.

This is a mature audience relation.

## 3. Ensemble audience

The 99th class watches one another from within shared labor.

This differs from the Giraffe's gaze because the observers are also performers, rivals, classmates, and co-producers.

They understand:

- rehearsal;
- fear;
- failure;
- ordinary fatigue;
- backstage preparation.

Their gaze can still wound.

Maya's excellence pressures Junna.

Claudine's rank makes others compare.

But the ensemble can also produce:

- correction;
- distributed care;
- an alternate relation when one dyad collapses;
- the promise `舞台で待ってる`.

The ensemble is therefore an audience capable of becoming support and co-performance.

## 4. Fanhood — Futaba and Hikari

Futaba's TV06 line is decisive:

> `私だって 香子のファンのままじゃ嫌なんだよ`

Fanhood is not treated as fake love.

Futaba's admiration sustained:

- care;
- training;
- proximity;
- Kaoruko's development.

The problem is role permanence.

If Futaba remains only audience, Kaoruko remains the performer whose growth defines the relationship.

Futaba needs to perform back.

Hikari's movie confession produces the inverse case.

She fears:

> `ファンになってしまうのが`

Karen's radiance threatens to capture her gaze.

Hikari does not fear admiration because admiration is shameful.

She fears becoming unable to answer as performer.

The corpus's mature alternative to fanhood is not emotional detachment.

It is reciprocal theatrical agency.

## 5. Archival audience — Nana

Nana is the most knowledgeable observer.

She remembers across replay.

She photographs.

She anticipates.

Her gaze becomes dangerous when memory turns into authorized casting.

The movie's:

> `君は眩しかった`

places Junna's radiance in past tense.

Nana's archive knows the performance it loved.

It initially cannot see the performer whose present contradicts the archive.

This is spectatorship as preservation.

The final correction:

> `眩しい`

is not simply praise.

It is the gaze becoming present-tense again.

## 6. Intimate rival audience — Claudine and Maya

Claudine's gaze is invasive.

She wants to strip Maya bare.

She uses exclusivist language:

> `私だけが あんたを剥き出しにする`

This is not neutral or non-possessive.

Its generative difference from Nana lies in direction and reciprocity.

Claudine does not ask Maya to repeat the perfect performance already known.

She demands another Maya.

Maya can demand another Claudine in return.

Both become performer and audience simultaneously.

The gaze is therefore answerable.

## 7. Coercive-confessional audience — Mahiru

Mahiru constructs a massive audience around Hikari.

Mr. White transforms from:

- invitation;
- to cheering crowd;
- to judge;
- to grotesque pressure;
- to shelter.

Mahiru's Revue demonstrates that audience pressure can force truth into performance.

It also refuses to romanticize the method as gentle therapy.

The audience is frightening.

The ethical correction comes after Hikari speaks.

Mahiru stops demanding more spectacle.

She identifies with fear and releases Hikari toward another stage.

A humane audience knows when the performance has given what it can.

## 8. Karen's audience awakening

Karen's final-movie discovery is bodily:

- seats are close;
- lights are hot;
- someone is looking;
- the stage is frightening.

Until then, Hikari had occupied too much of Karen's theatrical field.

The audience awakening enlarges the stage from dyad into social practice.

It prepares Karen for the post-credit audition, where strangers' judgment is no longer metaphysical threat but ordinary professional condition.

## 9. External viewer

The work never says:

> the Giraffe literally equals the anime viewer.

That equation would be too simple.

But TV12 and the movie deliberately align the viewer with his appetite.

We watched the girls.

We wanted the continuation.

The film names the audience selfish and greedy, then supplies another spectacular production.

The implication is not moral condemnation for watching.

It is a demand for a better form of watching:

> **Do not confuse loving the performance with the right to keep the performer inside it forever.**

# VII. Position Zero: complete developmental ledger

Position Zero is one of the corpus's clearest examples of a repeated form whose meaning changes.

Its visual consistency makes the development legible.

Its interpretation must remain chronological.

## TV01 — ordinary coordinate becomes ritual center

The phrase first identifies:

> `舞台のセンター ポジション ゼロ`

A stage coordinate.

The hidden audition rapidly invests the mark with:

- contest;
- visibility;
- victory;
- the ability to declare a self before the audience.

Karen's first occupation is disruptive.

She has not yet earned Maya's kind of mastery.

The act matters because she turns observer into performer.

## TV02 — Karen certifies another temporal model

Karen's Position Zero follows victory over Junna.

The more important development is not the mark itself.

It is the proposition that defeat does not end Junna's Stage Girl future.

Position Zero certifies a local result while Karen's words loosen the result's existential authority.

## TV03 — Maya makes the mark sovereign

Maya's:

> `ポジション ゼロ This is 天堂真矢！`

is the strongest early fusion of:

- center;
- identity;
- rank;
- capability.

The performance justifies the claim.

That is why later revisions matter.

The series does not begin with an obviously empty symbol.

It begins with a mark convincingly occupied by someone who can make it mean supremacy.

## TV07 — Nana uses center to preserve recurrence

Nana's Position Zero is not only victory.

It is access to a stage responsive enough to sustain replay.

The mark becomes part of an architecture through which a performer can turn centrality into authored time.

This reveals a danger:

> center can become not merely visible superiority, but jurisdiction over everyone else's future.

## TV08 — Hikari's transformation precedes the mark

Hikari's `ポジション・ゼロ` arrives after the decisive re-production.

The causal order matters.

Karen creates possibility.

Music reorganizes.

Hikari re-enters the relation to stage.

Then Position Zero confirms the result.

The mark is becoming certification rather than origin.

## TV10 — duet exposes institutional mismatch

The pair Revue makes two-person excellence visible.

Position Zero remains tied to winner grammar even though the performance has already demonstrated value not reducible to one person.

The system can stage the duet.

It cannot fully legitimate what the duet proves.

## TV12 — shared Position Zero

Karen and Hikari say:

> `(二人) ポジション・ゼロ`

Their joined hands occupy the mark.

This is not only proximity to the center.

The relationship itself becomes the center's occupant.

The mark has not changed shape.

The performers have changed what it can mean.

This is one of the television finale's most radical formal acts.

It proves that a singular coordinate need not imply a singular subject.

Yet the film later shows that even this achievement cannot become permanent resting place.

## MOV-PRO — Position Zero as fragment from the future

The film prologue gives Position Zero before explaining the path that leads to it.

The mark becomes memory, prophecy, and cinematic fragment.

The ending already inhabits the beginning.

This weakens any reading of Position Zero as a simple linear prize at the end of a contest.

## MOV-M07 — Claudine refuses its authority

Maya attempts the familiar conclusion.

Position Zero appears ready to certify the end.

Claudine says the Revue is not complete.

Their self-authored contract demands a radiance nobody has seen.

Maya's appeal to:

> `舞台の理`

reveals that Position Zero belongs to inherited stage law.

Claudine's refusal establishes:

> **participants can define a dramatic success condition that supersedes the formal coordinate.**

This is the mark's most explicit loss of sovereignty.

## MOV-M08 — final punctuation

Karen and Hikari return to Position Zero amid the final clash.

But the Revue's real outcome is:

- Karen's role-death;
- Hikari's confession;
- re-production;
- singular self-claims;
- rivalry;
- generative emptiness;
- next-stage search.

Position Zero cannot summarize that development.

It punctuates it.

## Full-series conclusion

Position Zero develops through five functions:

1. **coordinate** — a place onstage;
2. **prize** — visible winner/summit;
3. **certification** — formal recognition of a change produced elsewhere;
4. **shared threshold** — singular geometry inhabited relationally;
5. **punctuation** — one dramatic mark inside an ongoing life beyond the Revue.

The corpus does not destroy Position Zero.

It puts it back in its proper place:

> **inside theater, not above personhood.**

# VIII. Revue thresholds: how a stage begins

The work's changing thresholds reveal the development of stage ontology.

## Early television — clear descent

The early pattern is comparatively legible:

> ordinary school → summons → elevator/descent → machinery → transformation → title/announcement → combat.

The threshold protects a distinction between:

- normal life;
- heightened truth.

The girls may conceal a conflict in school, then perform it underground.

## TV04 — Revue without formal title

Karen/Hikari's reconciliation uses:

- travel;
- voice;
- spatial distance;
- recurring *Starlight* language;
- Tokyo Tower;
- renewed promise.

The relationship becomes performable without the hidden audition.

This is the first major sign that the form is larger than the system.

## TV11 — distributed stage

The ensemble relay links different physical locations through:

- shared song;
- repeated identity;
- tickets;
- `舞台で待ってる`.

The stage becomes a network rather than one arena.

## OVAs — ordinary production already contains the ethics

No supernatural Revue is needed for:

- costume fitting;
- role interpretation;
- stage fright;
- distributed backstage care;
- ensemble huddle.

The OVAs demonstrate that the Revue's philosophical questions belong to ordinary craft.

## Movie — threshold collapse

The film progressively eliminates the need for a distinct portal.

### Career interview

A Stage Girl introduction without costume or weapon.

### Train

Ordinary transit becomes rails, lights, music, and `wi(l)d-screen baroque`.

### City intersection

The Giraffe stands within public traffic.

### Road / highway

Futaba and Kaoruko's institutional divergence becomes moving vehicles.

### Sports stadium / backstage maze

Mahiru's bodily history and Hikari's concealed fear become one continuous performance.

### Photographs / giant words

Junna and Nana's relationship argument becomes archive and textual architecture.

### Animal shogi → rivalry Revue

Maya/Claudine's ordinary game already contains the same rule as the spectacular wager:

> Claudine wins; Maya asks for next.

### Final audition

Ordinary professional procedure becomes the final stage.

By the end, the question is no longer:

> When did the Revue begin?

It is:

> **What part of a performer's life is not already shaped by roles, audiences, rehearsals, choices, and the possibility of being seen?**

---

# IX. What makes a Revue a Revue?

The corpus does not supply a single checklist, but the accumulated evidence supports several recurring conditions.

A major Revue usually contains most, not necessarily all, of the following.

## 1. A relationship or self-relation that ordinary speech cannot yet carry

Examples:

- Junna's one-chance ambition;
- Mahiru's usefulness-bound love;
- Nana's preservation;
- Hikari's fear;
- Maya's empty perfection;
- Karen's completed promise.

## 2. A heightened theatrical form

This may include:

- song;
- title card;
- costume;
- weapon;
- impossible space;
- stylized role language;
- public audience;
- choreography.

## 3. A body placed at risk

The risk may be:

- defeat;
- humiliation;
- exhaustion;
- costume loss;
- exposure;
- symbolic death;
- surrender of a role.

## 4. An audience

The audience may be:

- Giraffe;
- classmates;
- one intimate rival;
- mascot crowd;
- ordinary spectators;
- implied external viewer.

## 5. A changing success condition

The early audition supplies success mechanically.

Later Revues redefine success relationally:

- say the true line;
- permit separate roads;
- recognize the present person;
- expose the self under the role;
- survive the completed promise.

## 6. A transformation not reducible to victory

The performer leaves the Revue with:

- a new word;
- a new role;
- a new relation;
- a new capacity to act;
- a changed understanding of the past or future.

### Working definition

> **A Revue is a witnessed theatrical event in which a performer risks a current self or relationship form so that something ordinary discourse cannot yet contain can become embodied, audible, and answerable.**

This definition is broader than the Giraffe's audition and narrower than saying all life is metaphorically theater.

It identifies the corpus's recurrent dramatic operation.

# X. `wi(l)d-screen baroque`: the movie's systemic overture

The Revue of Massacre is especially important because it announces that the film will no longer use the TV system primarily to rank.

Kaoruko initially mistakes it for the audition's return.

Nana denies:

> `これは オーディションにあらず`

The distinction matters.

A ranking tournament would allow the girls to repeat a familiar relation to desire:

- seek top;
- defeat others;
- gain certification.

The movie's problem is different.

The girls have already completed those roles.

They need theatrical death.

Nana sings:

> `役終われば 花と散れ`

The Revue does not ask:

> Who is best?

It asks:

> What must end before any of you can produce a next stage?

## Group Revue without harmonious unity

`wi(l)d-screen baroque` is ensemble performance, but not consensus.

The girls react differently:

- Kaoruko wants the old audition;
- Nana denies its return;
- Maya recognizes that stage and audience desire already place her onstage;
- Claudine tries to restore ordinary conversational rules;
- the group is intoxicated and disoriented.

The shared song does not mean shared interpretation.

This is an important music/ontology principle developed further in Document 09:

> **Voices can inhabit one musical time without agreeing about what the performance means.**

## Public stage

The Giraffe in the intersection relocates the system.

The audition's logic is no longer hidden beneath Seisho.

Graduation, professional choice, transit, and city life are already stages of visibility and selection.

## No winner

The Revue ends on an empty rail and Nana's:

> `私たち……もう……死んでるよ`

No Position Zero.

No crown.

No ranking.

The absence confirms the Revue's function:

> **not selection, but diagnosis of completed identities.**

---

# XI. The final ordinary audition as system contrast

The post-credit audition is the corpus's most important institutional counterimage.

It still contains:

- evaluation;
- competition;
- forms;
- candidate numbers;
- songs;
- dance;
- judges;
- the possibility of rejection.

The series has not escaped selection.

What has changed is the relation between the performer and the system.

## No metaphysical promise of justice

No Giraffe says the result will reveal destiny.

No crown claims cosmic authority.

No Position Zero guarantees personhood.

Karen may pass or fail.

The film does not tell us.

This contingency is maturation.

## Karen's identity is portable

Karen introduces herself:

> `１番 愛城華恋`

and:

> `みんなを スタァライトしちゃいます！`

She carries the accumulated Stage Girl history into an institution that did not create it and cannot fully define it.

The ordinary audition can judge:

- this role;
- this performance;
- this opportunity.

It cannot become the total authority over whether Karen remains a performer.

This is the mature institutional relation the early hidden audition lacked.

## The system is no longer the stage's metaphysical source

The stage exists because performers, collaborators, and viewers make it.

An institution can:

- host;
- train;
- cast;
- reject;
- fund;
- frame.

It does not own the final meaning of the performer.

# XIII. Rule, exception, and system exploitation

The audition system is never presented as a perfectly closed machine.

Its rules matter.

Characters suffer because of them.

But nearly every major development occurs through somebody finding that the rule is:

- conditional;
- exploitable;
- incomplete;
- or unable to describe the value the performance has already produced.

## Karen's unauthorized entry

Karen's first participation is not the system functioning smoothly.

It is an intrusion.

The moment matters because it establishes that the Revue apparatus can admit:

> a desire it did not initially organize.

Karen does not yet possess the skill to replace the institution's logic.

But her presence proves that the system is not metaphysically identical to theater itself.

The stage can contain:

> more than the organizer intended.

## Nana's replay

Nana exploits the winner's privilege to reproduce time.

Her use of the system reveals both its power and its indifference.

The apparatus does not ask whether the winner's wish preserves the other performers as subjects.

It grants enough authority for Nana to transform:

> shared performance

into:

> privately authored recurrence.

This is not a malfunction external to the system.

It exposes what the system already rewards:

> concentrated authority over the meaning of everyone else's radiance.

## Hikari's refusal of fuel

Hikari wins and then rejects the radiance she could consume.

The act demonstrates that:

- the winner can refuse the prize;
- mechanical success does not determine ethical use;
- and the system cannot force Hikari to interpret victory as entitlement.

But refusal alone does not solve the relationship problem.

Hikari still unilaterally decides:

> who must disappear;
> what Karen should be spared;
> and how the cost will be allocated.

The system can be exploited against extraction while its sovereign grammar survives inside the winner's behavior.

## Karen's Encore

TV12 goes further.

Karen does not merely refuse the intended prize.

She changes:

> what the stage is doing.

The Final Chapter becomes Encore because the performer/audience circuit finds the continuation more theatrically compelling than the original closure.

The Giraffe's appetite, which helped sustain exploitation, becomes one of the conditions through which the rule is exceeded.

This is an important ambiguity.

The system is neither:

- an omnipotent metaphysical law;
- nor an irrelevant illusion that disappears when the girls believe harder.

It is:

> **a powerful theatrical institution whose rules can be transformed when performance produces a form the rules did not know how to value.**

## Movie pair Revues

The film completes the shift.

Each pair Revue establishes a local success condition:

- Futaba must own a separate road;
- Hikari must perform fear truthfully;
- Junna must reject Nana's casting;
- Maya and Claudine must expose the particular people beneath their roles;
- Karen must survive the completion of “Hikari is my stage.”

None of these conditions can be measured by:

> one ranking table.

The Revues still use:

- weapons;
- costume;
- audience;
- spectacle;
- Position Zero language.

But the old system no longer supplies the decisive criterion.

The performers do.

# XIV. Metaphysical discipline: three levels of theatrical reality

The corpus is strongest when its ontology remains layered.

A full-series synthesis should resist two opposite reductions.

## Reduction 1 — everything literally happens under one coherent supernatural physics

This approach overstates what the work explains.

The series gives real diegetic consequences:

- Hikari loses radiance;
- Nana repeats time;
- the girls remember some events and not others;
- winners receive privileges;
- Karen enters and changes the stage;
- bodies and costumes respond materially.

But it does not provide a complete technical cosmology capable of resolving every cinematic transformation.

The movie especially refuses stable ordinary-world physics.

City, train, memory, stage, performance, and internal role pass into one another without requiring a manual of causal mechanics.

## Reduction 2 — nothing “really” happens; the Revues are only metaphor

This loses just as much.

The characters:

- act upon Revue knowledge;
- remember outcomes;
- change decisions;
- bear costs;
- carry new relationship states into ordinary life.

The Revue is not decorative visualization added for the viewer.

It is part of the story's causal reality.

## A three-level model

### 1. Diegetic rule

Explicitly established operations:

- audition invitation;
- ranking;
- Position Zero;
- costume defeat;
- winner privilege;
- radiance extraction;
- Nana's replay;
- Giraffe's organizing role.

### 2. Performed theatrical reality

Events materially true within the Revue even when ordinary physics is suspended:

- a road becoming relationship topology;
- giant photographs surrounding Junna;
- animal shogi expanding into metaphysical wager;
- a mascot audience becoming both terror and shelter;
- Karen's role-death and re-production.

These are not “merely imaginary.”

They are theatrical facts whose truth lies in what the performance makes happen between people.

### 3. Poetic/cinematic visualization

Images whose exact ontology remains deliberately underdetermined:

- every tomato recurrence;
- some reflections and multiplied bodies;
- certain transitions through fire, landscape, or scale;
- the exact physical relation between the city and Revue stage.

The image can be interpretively load-bearing without receiving one literal explanation.

## Why the distinction matters

The three levels allow us to say simultaneously:

- the audition is a real exploitative system;
- a Revue can exceed that system;
- the film's impossible images are causally meaningful;
- and not every image needs to be converted into a rule of supernatural physics.

This is the ontology most consistent with the work's own theater theory.

The stage makes something real by:

> making it performable and consequential.

---

# XV. The external audience and the right to departure

The film's “greedy audience” language creates a final system-level problem.

The audience wants:

- another Revue;
- another song;
- another disclosure;
- another version of the girls.

The sequel exists because that desire was answered.

But the film does not allow the audience to keep the 99th class in one beloved arrangement.

The credits disperse them.

The performers:

- choose institutions;
- change plans;
- travel;
- send messages;
- meet again under new conditions.

This is an ethical limit placed upon spectatorship.

> **The audience may ask for another performance. It does not acquire the right to decide which role the performer must repeat.**

The post-credit audition is particularly important.

Karen enters another evaluative system.

The film does not promise that every audience or institution will recognize her fairly.

Nor does it abolish selection.

Instead, it relocates agency:

- Karen chooses to audition;
- she brings a portable Stage Girl identity;
- the result remains open;
- the next role is not guaranteed by destiny.

The mature stage ontology is therefore neither:

> performer sovereignty without audience

nor:

> audience sovereignty over performer.

It is an unstable reciprocal structure in which:

- the performer needs to be seen;
- the viewer's desire helps sustain the stage;
- the viewer can misrecognize or possess;
- the performer can answer, refuse, change role, or leave;
- and the stage remains alive only while none of these functions becomes permanently absolute.

# XVI. Strongest supporting evidence

The following evidence most strongly supports this document's thesis.

## 1. TV12's explicit stage ontology

> performer + viewer;
> stage continues while performer stands and audience desires.

This directly establishes spectatorship as constitutive.

## 2. The crown's lack of intrinsic meaning

The institutional prize is lure rather than metaphysical truth.

This separates system symbol from performer transformation.

## 3. Hikari's TV11 mechanical victory

Winning does not produce the ethically or relationally best outcome.

Mechanical and relational results diverge decisively.

## 4. Shared Position Zero

A singular coordinate becomes relationally inhabitable.

## 5. Claudine's refusal of Position Zero

The inherited stage rule cannot end a Revue whose self-authored relational condition remains unmet.

## 6. Greedy audience / Giraffe fuel

The film makes spectatorship an appetite and a material condition rather than neutral reception.

## 7. Post-credit ordinary audition

The corpus ends inside evaluation while denying evaluation total authority over Karen's personhood.

---

# XVII. Strongest complicating evidence

## 1. The Giraffe enables liberation as well as exploitation

His appetite allows Karen's unpredictable Encore to continue.

The system can host the event that exceeds its initial rules.

Therefore he cannot be reduced to villainous control.

## 2. Position Zero remains emotionally powerful

Its loss of sovereignty does not make it meaningless.

The mark still carries accumulated history, center, risk, and self-declaration.

## 3. Audience pressure can produce truth

Mahiru's coercive audience is ethically uncomfortable yet dramatically successful.

A simple anti-audience ethic would fail to explain the Revue.

## 4. The ordinary audition still ranks and excludes

The final scene does not escape institutional scarcity.

It changes how the performer relates to it.

## 5. Some Revues remain strongly dyadic or self-directed

The working definition of Revue as relational performance must include self-relation without forcing every event into a romance/friendship template.

---

# XVIII. Plausible alternative reading

A plausible alternative is:

> **The Giraffe and Position Zero remain fundamentally sovereign throughout; the apparent rule-breaking is simply the system's planned flexibility, and the Revues continue serving a larger metaphysical selection process the characters do not understand.**

This reading has support.

The Giraffe repeatedly adapts rather than disappears.

He recognizes exceptional outcomes.

The stage appears capable of incorporating transgression.

Karen's Encore delights him rather than truly defeating him.

Position Zero continues appearing even after being shared or contested.

## Why the present reading remains stronger

The corpus repeatedly emphasizes self-authored success conditions that the old mechanical signs do not determine:

- Mahiru's medal is irrelevant to Hikari's confession;
- Junna's role rejection matters more than formal win;
- Claudine explicitly says Position Zero cannot end the wager;
- Karen's final future lies beyond the completed Revue and ordinary audition outcome.

The system may be flexible enough to host these transformations.

That does not make it their sovereign author.

The strongest formulation is therefore:

> **The Revue system is generative infrastructure with extractive and spectacular biases. It can be exceeded from within because the performers supply the desires, relationships, and unforeseen forms that give the stage life.**

---

# XIX. Final synthesis

The hidden audition teaches the girls and viewers to think that theater has a center.

One winner.

One star.

One mark.

One audience looking upward.

The complete corpus does not simply invert that model into a cooperative world without rank, spectators, or conflict.

It does something more difficult.

It preserves:

- performance;
- evaluation;
- rivalry;
- audience;
- center;
- risk;
- artistic cost.

Then it strips those forms of their claim to define the whole person.

The Giraffe is necessary because the stage requires desire to see.

He is dangerous because desire to see can become desire to keep.

Position Zero is meaningful because a stage needs form and center.

It is dangerous when the mark becomes jurisdiction over who counts as a star.

`キラめき` can be extracted because institutions really can consume artistic vitality.

It can be re-produced because vitality is not finally a commodity.

A Revue can have a winner because performances have outcomes.

The winner does not therefore own the only truth of the event.

The full-system conclusion is:

> **The stage belongs neither entirely to the organizer, nor the winner, nor the audience, nor the inherited script. It exists in the unstable circuit among performers, collaborators, spectators, rules, bodies, and desires. Its ethical possibility depends upon no part of that circuit claiming permanent authority over the performer's next role.**

That is why the final stage is an ordinary audition.

The institution can evaluate Karen.

The audience can watch her.

Karen can want the role.

She can lose.

She can win.

None of those outcomes can return her to the early belief that one mark, one promise, or one gaze possesses the right to tell her whether another stage exists.

# Appendix C — Revue criteria for comparative use

A scene is most usefully analyzed as a Revue when it contains several of the following:

1. a relationship or self-conception ordinary speech cannot yet resolve;
2. heightened theatrical form;
3. bodily or identity risk;
4. an audience or witnessing structure;
5. a success condition that changes during performance;
6. an outcome larger than mechanical victory;
7. music, staging, or choreography that produces knowledge rather than merely illustrating it.

This definition allows:

- TV04 to possess Revue grammar without a formal Revue title;
- OVA role/care scenes to remain ordinary theater rather than being overclassified;
- movie animal shogi to function as miniature relationship theater without becoming literally identical to the spectacular wager;
- the post-credit audition to be recognized as a stage without claiming it is another supernatural Revue.
