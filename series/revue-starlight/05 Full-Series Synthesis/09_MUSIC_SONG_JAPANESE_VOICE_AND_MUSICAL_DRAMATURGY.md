# 『少女☆歌劇 レヴュースタァライト』
## Music, Song, Japanese Voice, and Musical Dramaturgy
### What the music knows before the characters can say it

**Primary analytical home:** The complete animated corpus's Japanese speech, performed vocal states, Revue songs, song allocation, counterpoint, silence, score, reprise, musical memory, technical final-mix corroboration, and the question of when music anticipates, contradicts, confirms, or waits for language.

**Spoiler scope:** Complete TV series, OVAs, sequel film, credits, and post-credit audition.

---

# I. Executive thesis

Music in *Revue Starlight* does not merely intensify an emotional truth already known by the characters.

It often participates in creating the truth.

A relationship can become musically reciprocal before either girl admits reciprocity.

A fatal ending can begin sounding unstable before Karen names another possibility.

A character can remain inside one song while singing an interpretation incompatible with her partner's.

A spectacular orchestration can contradict the spoken command to stop.

A score can withdraw because language has finally become adequate.

The complete animated corpus therefore uses four principal music-language relationships.

## 1. Music anticipates language

The composition begins reorganizing before the character can state what has changed.

Canonical examples:

- Hikari's Episode 8 re-production;
- TV12 Karen's continuation after the tragic ending;
- movie Futaba/Kaoruko, where the song knows the road is dividing while Futaba still insists the promise was not a lie;
- Junna/Nana, where Nana's music has already become elegy before Junna identifies the wound as past tense;
- final-movie re-production, where the musical world produces another Karen before Karen fully names rivalry.

## 2. Music contradicts language

The spoken line asks for one thing while the composition refuses it.

Examples:

- Nana begs Junna to lower the curtain while the Revue approaches maximum force;
- Maya reaches Position Zero, but the score does not behave as if the Revue has ended;
- a gold-medal sports premise continues toward confession rather than victory.

## 3. Music confirms language

The performer speaks first and the stage responds.

Examples:

- Mahiru says she decided to live onstage; the music then re-expands;
- Junna rejects other people's words; her self-authored language receives immediate sonic body;
- Karen says she does not want to lose to Hikari; the final duet gathers toward its terminal clash.

## 4. Music waits for language

The score cannot solve the problem before the performer speaks.

Examples:

- Hikari's movie confession `怖かったの`;
- Karen's movie role-death;
- the quiet after Maya/Claudine's spectacular exposure, when “tomorrow” must become ordinary speech;
- Karen's post-Revue `空っぽ`.

These are not four rigid compositional categories.

A single Revue may move through all four.

Their importance is methodological:

> **The analysis cannot assume that dialogue is the sole carrier of knowledge and music the carrier of intensity.**

Sometimes dialogue explains.

Sometimes music understands.

Sometimes staging pressures language into existence.

Sometimes silence protects the line from spectacle.

The voice acting is equally central.

The series repeatedly distinguishes:

- lexical voice — the words, pronouns, register, dialect, quotation, and role language a character uses;
- performed vocal state — how the voice carries control, fear, play, strain, tears, competition, or self-revelation.

Hikari's compressed ordinary style can expand into an OVA theater lecture, deadpan teasing, `バッ華恋`, frightened confession, and maximal star-solo without ceasing to sound like Hikari.

Maya's resonant ceremonial control can become overt jealousy, panic, “cute” indignation, and direct beauty-recognition without revealing her earlier voice as fake.

Junna's literary precision can become the rough:

> `殺してみせろよ 大場なな！`

without abandoning the intellectual self that made the line possible.

Mahiru's softness can become frightening directorial pressure because the character has learned to command a stage rather than because a hidden “real Mahiru” replaces the gentle one.

This is the corpus's general acting philosophy in sonic form:

> **Authenticity is not one unchanging natural voice beneath performance. It is the ability to make different performed states adequate to a changing truth.**

The Revue songs provide the large-scale architecture for that process.

Each song is not merely assigned to the characters who happen to be fighting.

Vocal allocation encodes relationship.

Who begins?

Who interrupts?

Who receives a solo?

Who overlaps?

Who sings against another line?

Who finally shares unison?

Who stops singing so somebody can speak?

The full corpus moves from songs in which conflict is largely organized through alternating claims toward increasingly complex forms:

- duet as contest;
- duet as mutual literacy;
- ensemble relay;
- vocal counterpoint that preserves disagreement;
- one character's solo becoming the other character's reason to re-enter performance;
- ending theme distributing future-tense statements across nine separated lives.

The final musical development is therefore not simply “bigger songs.”

It is:

> **greater capacity for several subjectivities to inhabit the same musical world without being forced into identical meaning.**

# II. Source and final-mix discipline

The primary evidence for this document is the supplied audiovisual corpus:

- complete Japanese episode audio;
- complete Japanese movie audio;
- Japanese dialogue/lyric layers for TV and film;
- retained frames and chronological sequence;
- prior movement-level Musical-Dramaturgy Ledgers.

The OVA subtitle caveat remains:

- Japanese audio is authoritative for performance;
- the supplied English subtitles support broad semantic navigation;
- exact Japanese lexical claims are used only where aurally reliable.

## Official track-title metadata

Official release metadata was used only to verify formal track titles and credited vocal pairings.

Interpretive claims remain grounded in the supplied animated audio, not in promotional descriptions or external commentary.

## Final-mix caveat

The analytical audio is the finished stereo mix.

Therefore:

- vocals, score, effects, machinery, and environmental sound are combined;
- exact instrumental identification may be uncertain;
- exact harmony/key claims should remain conservative;
- RMS, spectral centroid, frequency-band share, and tempo estimates are corroborative rather than authoritative.

A loud explosion can raise final-mix energy without making the music emotionally triumphant.

A quiet spoken line may sit over low-frequency ambience not captured by the word “silence.”

Technical numbers are therefore used to support directly audible contrasts, not replace close listening.

# III. Japanese lexical voice versus performed vocal state

The series' characterization depends upon the interaction of two layers.

## Lexical voice

This includes:

- pronouns;
- dialect;
- sentence endings;
- honorifics;
- role language;
- quotation;
- formality;
- directness;
- repetition;
- names versus categories.

Examples:

- Karen's `私` / `私達` / `二人` movement;
- Kaoruko's Kyoto speech;
- Junna's quotation habit;
- Claudine's movie shift from devil-role `俺様 / 俺` back to `私`;
- Nana's `私の純那ちゃん`;
- Hikari's rough `あんた` in heightened Revue mode;
- Maya's elevated ceremonial diction;
- the past-tense wound `眩しかった`.

## Performed vocal state

This includes:

- breath;
- pitch range;
- attack;
- pace;
- volume;
- hesitation;
- crying coloration;
- laughter;
- vocal roughness;
- interruption;
- overlap;
- sudden simplification.

A character can retain lexical style while the performed state changes radically.

Maya can still speak with confidence while the confidence is now comic and exposed.

Hikari can use short clauses in both defensive and intimate states.

Junna can sound literary when composed and use unusually rough language when self-authorship finally exceeds quotation.

### Analytical rule

Never infer:

> polished wording = calm;

or:

> simple wording = lack of thought.

In the most important scenes, the simplification of language often marks the point at which a performed explanation can no longer contain the actual feeling.

Examples:

- Hikari: `怖かったの`;
- Futaba: repeated `ずるい`;
- Nana: `もうやめてよ`;
- Karen: `何もない`;
- Hikari: `死なないで`.

# IV. Baseline voice profiles — the nine Stage Girls

These are functional synthesis profiles rather than claims of immutable vocal essence.

Each character's corpus arc depends upon the baseline being capable of changing.

## 1. Aijō Karen — open declaration and affective overflow

### Baseline

Karen's ordinary voice is:

- bright;
- forward;
- emotionally legible;
- quick to call names;
- willing to ask direct questions;
- prone to exuberant self-introduction.

Her lexical voice often treats theatrical language as ordinary speech.

`スタァライト` becomes a verb because Karen speaks it as something one can do to people.

### Revue state

Karen's voice gains:

- projection;
- formal self-introduction;
- declarative certainty;
- rising insistence.

Yet her central strength is not polished abstraction.

She repeatedly breaks fatalistic language with ordinary directness:

- `ノンノン`;
- another stage;
- Flora got up;
- I do not want to lose to Hikari.

### Film development

The movie adds states uncommon in early Karen:

- private fear;
- narrow, almost emptied delivery;
- `何もない`;
- bodily awareness of audience;
- calm post-performance emptiness.

The final audition voice returns to brightness, but it is no longer the untested brightness of TV01.

It carries death, completion, and re-production.

## 2. Kagura Hikari — compression, precision, and delayed expansion

### Baseline

Hikari's ordinary speech is economical.

She often sounds:

- lower;
- controlled;
- precise;
- emotionally compressed;
- careful with distance.

The style can be mistaken for social incapacity.

OVA01 corrects that interpretation.

Given theater knowledge and a concrete reason to help Futaba, Hikari can become unusually verbose.

She can also tease with deadpan timing.

### Defensive state

Under threat, the compression becomes control.

Hikari explains, instructs, forbids, and withdraws.

The voice can make a unilateral decision sound like settled necessity.

### Break state

The most revealing Hikari lines often break the controlled surface:

- `バカ… バカバカ バッ華恋！`;
- `会いたく… なっちゃうじゃない`;
- `怖かったの`;
- `死なないで`.

The language becomes shorter because the role/explanation has failed.

### Star state

The movie finally gives Hikari maximal projection:

> `神楽ひかり 私がスタァだ`

This is not another character replacing quiet Hikari.

It is Hikari choosing spectacle rather than treating visibility as loss of control.

## 3. Tendō Maya — ceremonial resonance and controlled amplitude

### Baseline

Maya speaks with:

- authority;
- measured pace;
- clear projection;
- formal vocabulary;
- aphoristic compression;
- little audible uncertainty.

Her voice often makes an interpretation sound like stage law.

### Competitive state

The control sharpens rather than disappears.

Maya does not need to shout to dominate early Revues.

Her vocal authority matches bodily mastery.

### OVA exposure

OVA02 demonstrates that the composure is discipline, not absence of jealousy.

The admission of frustration widens the voice into a more ordinary teenage register.

### Movie exposure

Claudine forces:

- anger;
- personal naming;
- embarrassment;
- comic self-assertion:

> `私 いつだってかわいい！`

Maya's voice does not become less truthful when it becomes less ceremonial.

The corpus enlarges what “Tendō Maya” can sound like.

## 4. Saijō Claudine — direct challenge and theatrical appropriation

### Baseline

Claudine's speech is:

- confident;
- quick;
- confrontational;
- performatively elegant;
- capable of moving between Japanese and French-inflected theatrical identity.

She often addresses Maya as somebody already expected to answer.

### Rival state

Her voice gains:

- speed;
- bite;
- exasperation;
- exposed need for recognition.

### Movie role/self marker

The devil role uses:

> `俺様 / 俺`.

The return to:

> `私`

marks the point where archetype gives way to Claudine herself.

This is a rare case where pronoun change makes role-boundary audible.

### Intimate audience state

Claudine's most revealing lines combine aggression and intimacy:

- only I can strip you bare;
- you are full of greed and jealousy;
- keep looking;
- another match.

Her voice is not softened into conventional tenderness.

Recognition remains competitive.

## 5. Hoshimi Junna — precision, quotation, and authored rupture

### Baseline

Junna speaks with:

- careful articulation;
- intellectual precision;
- formal phrasing;
- explanatory structure;
- frequent quotation.

The quotation habit is not merely pretension.

It gives her a vocabulary for ambition larger than immediate insecurity.

### Early Revue state

The controlled voice becomes urgent as one-chance ontology tightens around her.

### Movie crisis

Nana's `眩しかった` destabilizes the lexical system Junna trusts.

Junna attempts more quotations.

Then:

> `他人の言葉じゃ だめ！`

Her voice becomes rougher and more self-authored.

The line:

> `殺してみせろよ 大場なな！`

is powerful partly because it is such a marked departure from her baseline register.

The performance reveals not a hidden anti-intellectual Junna but a literary Junna finally writing without quotation marks.

## 6. Daiba Nana — warmth, play, control, and the voice of remembered authority

### Baseline

Nana's ordinary voice is:

- warm;
- rounded;
- playful;
- caretaking;
- capable of comic maternal rhythm.

The warmth helps make her preservationism emotionally credible.

### Replay authority

When Nana speaks as author of recurrence, the same confidence becomes unnervingly settled.

She can describe total control in a voice still associated with care.

### Panic and possession

The movie strips the control away:

- `もうやめてよ`;
- `幕を降ろして`;
- `私の純那ちゃん`;
- `お前は何者だ?!`

The shift from warm observer to frightened possessor is one of the corpus's strongest performed-voice transformations.

### Final present tense

`眩しい` is quiet.

The correction does not require restored grandeur.

Nana sees rather than announces.

## 7. Tsuyuzaki Mahiru — warmth, accommodation, and frightening directorial force

### Baseline

Mahiru's ordinary speech tends toward:

- softness;
- care;
- social accommodation;
- visible embarrassment;
- attention to others' needs.

### Jealous Revue

The voice becomes insistently intimate and controlling.

The contrast with baseline care makes the possessiveness more disturbing.

### Movie actor/director state

Mahiru begins with a clear oath and professional projection.

She then becomes one of the film's most frightening interrogators:

> `もっとちゃんと演じてよ`

The character does not reveal that gentleness was fake.

She uses acquired stage authority to force a line Hikari cannot otherwise perform.

After confession, the voice immediately becomes receptive.

This transition demonstrates control of theatrical pressure rather than uncontrolled aggression.

## 8. Isurugi Futaba — rough directness and delayed resentment

### Baseline

Futaba's speech is:

- blunt;
- colloquial;
- physically grounded;
- less ornamental than Kaoruko's;
- often organized around practical action.

Her voice fits the caretaker role because she tends to solve rather than theorize.

### Revue state

The roughness becomes self-assertion:

> `香子のファンのままじゃ嫌`.

### Movie breakdown

Futaba's respectable explanation for New National collapses into:

- repeated `なんで`;
- repeated `ずるい`;
- a list of bodily care labor.

The vocal simplification makes resentment audible as something not yet domesticated by moral explanation.

## 9. Hanayagi Kaoruko — Kyoto languor, inherited elegance, and cutting command

### Baseline

Kaoruko's lexical voice is marked by Kyoto speech and an ability to sound:

- languid;
- teasing;
- entitled;
- traditional;
- socially devastating without losing composure.

Her speech can make demand sound like established order.

### Competitive state

The languor sharpens.

Kaoruko can become cuttingly direct:

> `本音 晒せや`.

She has an unusual ability to detect when Futaba's respectable explanation is still organized around Kaoruko.

The voice that once benefited from Futaba's subordination becomes the voice demanding a more selfish Futaba.

# V. Giraffe voice: administrative calm becomes appetite

The Giraffe's vocal identity is one of the corpus's most effective sonic jokes and one of its clearest philosophical tools.

## Television baseline

The repeated:

> `分かります`

is delivered with measured certainty.

The impossible is made bureaucratically normal.

The voice implies:

- superior knowledge;
- procedural stability;
- emotional distance.

This makes the extraction system more disturbing than overt villainous speech would.

## TV12 self-disclosure

When he calls himself audience and organizer, the same voice reveals that procedural calm was never neutral.

The system has taste.

The organizer wants to see.

## Film prologue rupture

The Giraffe becomes frantic:

- Did it end?
- Did I miss it?

The deviation is funny because the controlled baseline has been so strong.

It also reveals appetite more honestly than the TV ritual did.

## Fuel declaration

The final Giraffe state is emphatic and theatrical.

He calls himself:

> `燃料！`

The spectator becomes part of the performance's sonic body.

# VI. Ordinary speech → Revue register

A Revue rarely begins only when music starts.

The transition is often audible in speech.

## Formal self-introduction

The Stage Girl introduction typically includes:

- class/year;
- name;
- compact performance identity;
- heightened projection.

It transforms biography into theatrical claim.

## Role language

Movie Revues often begin with consciously fictional registers:

- ninkyō/gambling speech for Futaba/Kaoruko;
- ceremonial athletic oath for Mahiru;
- hunt/execution language for Nana;
- devil and wager for Claudine/Maya.

The role supplies enough distance for the actual conflict to begin.

Then the Revue strips the role away.

## Names as endpoint

Several movie Revues collapse from categories into names:

- devil / stage person → Maya / Claudine;
- hunter / prey → Junna / Nana;
- former attachment rivals → Mahiru / Hikari.

Direct naming often marks the point where archetype can no longer contain the relationship.

## Speech interruption

The most important lines frequently interrupt song rather than fitting neatly inside it.

Examples:

- Hikari's `会いたく…なっちゃうじゃない`;
- Mahiru's `どうして演技しないの？`;
- Junna's `どうして 過去形なのよ？`;
- Claudine's refusal of Position Zero;
- Karen's `何もない`;
- Hikari's fanhood confession.

The song creates pressure.

Speech changes the success condition.

# VII. Song allocation as relationship grammar

A Revue song is not simply a character song placed beneath combat.

It organizes:

- who may speak;
- who must answer;
- whose musical material dominates;
- when two interpretations coexist;
- when one singer disappears;
- and whether the relation ends in unison, counterpoint, interruption, or silence.

The distribution of vocal material is therefore part of the drama.

## Solo does not always mean isolation

A singer may receive a solo because:

- she currently owns the stage;
- the other person has become audience;
- the relationship has not yet become reciprocal;
- or one self must be asserted before duet becomes possible.

Hikari's final film solo is the clearest case.

`神楽ひかり 私がスタァだ` does not declare the final Revue a one-person ontology.

It creates a sufficiently strong first-person Hikari for Karen to answer:

> `私も ひかりに負けたくない`.

The solo generates the duet's mature premise.

## Duet does not always mean agreement

Two voices may occupy the same song while giving opposite interpretations of:

- the promise;
- the road;
- ambition;
- memory;
- rivalry.

Maya and Claudine can sing in one musical structure while fighting over:

> whether the actor is an empty vessel or a greedy human person.

Junna and Nana can sing simultaneously while one calls persistence:

> `我が道`

and the other hears:

> `煙る景色`.

Musical co-presence proves relationship.

It does not prove harmony.

## Unison is not fusion

When Karen and Hikari sing together, the analytical question is not merely:

> Are the notes the same?

It is:

> **What first-person identities had to become viable before the unison could mean reciprocity rather than erasure?**

TV10's shared `アタシ 再生産` is meaningful because both girls retain formal self-introductions and distinct dramatic histories.

TV12's shared Position Zero is meaningful because each voice has already named the other as star.

The plural musical form is built from singular ownership.

## The ensemble song distributes identity

TV11's `舞台少女心得 幕間` carries a Stage Girl identity across several performers and ordinary spaces.

No single singer owns the definition.

The relay matters because Karen's relation to theater has collapsed around one absent person.

The ensemble does not answer:

> Hikari never mattered.

It answers:

> **Hikari is not the only voice through which Karen's Stage Girl self can be remembered.**

The song's architecture is therefore social evidence.

## TV01 — participant structure can change

The first Revue song teaches the audience that song ownership is not closed merely because the performance has begun.

Karen's intrusion changes:

- the number of performers;
- the direction of attention;
- the meaning of the contest;
- and who can occupy Position Zero.

This is the musical seed of the series' later claim that a script can be revised by the person not originally assigned a role.

## TV02 — ambition receives dignity before correction

`The Star Knows` does not make Junna's desire sound ridiculous so Karen can defeat it morally.

The song gives ambition:

- momentum;
- precision;
- dramatic seriousness.

Karen's anti-finality therefore has to answer a real desire rather than a straw version of it.

This is a recurring compositional virtue of the series:

> **music often dignifies the position the narrative will later complicate.**

## TV03 — Maya's authority is audible

`誇りと驕り` makes the gap between Karen and Maya bodily.

Maya's controlled projection, command of phrase, and alignment with stage machinery support her thesis that the summit requires more than sincerity.

The music does not merely accompany Maya's dominance.

It helps make the dominance experientially persuasive.

## TV05 — comedy does not neutralize possession

`恋の魔球` uses a playful, exaggerated frame.

That tone allows Mahiru's desire to appear charming and frightening within the same performance.

The song's comic idiom should not be treated as evidence that the conflict is unserious.

Comedy is the role through which Mahiru's need becomes performable before its coercive logic is exposed.

## TV06 — inherited style becomes argument

`花咲か唄` draws strength from the relationship's inherited cultural and bodily history.

Futaba and Kaoruko do not become generic rivals.

Their competition remains shaped by:

- Kaoruko's traditional performance identity;
- Futaba's practical proximity;
- and years of asymmetrical care.

The song allows change without aesthetic deracination.

## TV08 — the canonical re-production hinge

`RE:CREATE` contains the clearest early case where musical change precedes explicit conceptual explanation.

The score's field reorganizes as Karen's remembered possibility reaches Hikari.

Then:

> `きらめきの再生産`

is spoken.

The relationship has already changed the music before the rule is named.

## TV09 — bond becomes future rather than enclosure

`星々の絆` is not a simple celebration of everyone being connected.

The song must argue over what bond means.

Nana's bond initially preserves:

> a known cast and known stage.

Karen and Junna push bond toward:

> a relationship capable of producing a role the old script did not contain.

The music gives shared history continuity without requiring shared authorship to remain centralized in Nana.

## TV10 — pair-performance exceeds institutional result

`-Star Divine- フィナーレ` is crucial because the song makes two dyads artistically legible before the audition produces a singular winner.

The music proves:

> the value exists.

The institution's later allocation does not erase what was heard.

This distinction between:

- aesthetic fact;
- institutional recognition

becomes central to the full-series account of Position Zero.

## TV11 — identity as relay

`舞台少女心得 幕間` transfers the Stage Girl definition through an ensemble.

The song does not merely remind Karen of encouraging slogans.

Its allocation demonstrates:

> no one relationship owns the identity.

The absence of Hikari remains painful.

The vocal relay prevents that absence from becoming total ontology.

## TV12 — duet after role-break

`スタァライト` arrives only after:

- Hikari's assigned role breaks;
- Star-Sin is named;
- guilt is collectivized;
- the Final Chapter becomes Encore;
- and Hikari chooses to want another performance.

The duet is therefore not a magical reconciliation pasted over conflict.

It is the sound of two performers becoming able to answer one another after the old script has failed.

## `wi(l)d-screen baroque` — overture rather than contest

The group Revue song is not musically organized around harmonious ensemble consensus.

Nana's repeated question:

> next?

prevents the career plans from functioning as sufficient answers.

The song's mass and aggression make role-death bodily before any pair has discovered what the new role will be.

## `わがままハイウェイ` — selfishness as propulsion

The title's `わがまま` is not simply moral criticism.

It names the first-person insistence Futaba has historically subordinated to care.

The highway pulse gives selfish desire forward motion.

The song reaches its strongest body around:

- Futaba's resentment;
- the declaration that they cannot go together;
- the acceptance of separate travel.

The quiet aftermath becomes possible only after divergence is performed at full intensity.

## `MEDAL SUZDAL PANIC◎〇●` — spectacle exhausts itself before confession

The song begins inside Mahiru's enormous sports/entertainment grammar.

The false objective is gold.

The real objective is:

> make Hikari perform a true line.

At:

The spectacle was loud because the truth had not yet been spoken.

It stages the point at which literature must become:

> a voice capable of writing rather than only citing.

## `美しき人 或いは其れは` — counterpoint as mutual exposure

The Maya/Claudine Revue begins through devil and divine-vessel roles.

- simultaneous incompatible propositions;
- appropriation of one another's self-introductions;
- exchange of audience and performer positions;
- and a climax that occurs after Position Zero fails to end the stage.

- star-picking formula;
- train;
- Position Zero;
- Karen/Hikari history;

The musical recurrence is the vehicle through which the old role dies.

## `私たちはもう舞台の上` — thesis after climax

Its allocated lines connect each character's next state to:

- travel;
- changed scenery;
- closing a familiar script;
- beginning;
- and a future fitted like costume.

The song's importance lies partly in its placement over canonical credit images.

# XI. Silence and subtraction

Silence in *Revue Starlight* is rarely simple absence.

It can:

- expose a line;
- mark failure of a role;
- suspend the system's judgment;
- create room for consent;
- or confirm that a performance has genuinely ended.

## TV12 Revue title

The industrial transformation is loud and rhythmically forceful.

Then the title:

> `終章`
>
> `星罪のレヴュー`

arrives in dramatic subtraction.

Machinery makes the Stage Girl body.

Silence names:

> guilt.

The ethical question cannot remain buried beneath spectacle.

## Hikari's atonement

The penitential Stage of Destiny becomes low-centered and contracted.

The sonic impoverishment matters.

Hikari calls it eternal theater.

The music makes it sound like:

> less theater.

No co-performer.

No backstage.

No variation.

No future.

## `全部ちょうだい`

Karen's intimate demand does not receive immediate maximum orchestral force.

The music clears space.

This preserves Hikari's answer as necessary.

Karen cannot make the relation reciprocal merely by declaring that it is.

Hikari must respond.

## MOV-M04 `怖かった`

The confession receives one of the most extreme local reductions in the film.

The mix's low, sparse state makes breath and speech newly exposed.

Mahiru's stage has spent minutes demanding a real line.

When it arrives, the stage stops competing with it.

## MOV-M05 `眩しかった`

Nana's past-tense admiration moves the Revue into an elegiac state.

The reduced music does not make the line gentle.

It makes the burial audible.

Junna notices that she is being mourned while still present.

## MOV-M07 coda

The largest duet/revelation is followed by near-ordinary quiet.

Claudine has won the wager.

Maya says:

> tomorrow.

The absence of bombastic closure is the point.

Their “eternity” becomes credible because it can survive without eternal climax.

## MOV-M08 death and aftermath

Karen's death is quieter than re-production.

The role has ceased producing sound.

After the final clash, music withdraws again.

This second quiet is different.

The first is destructive absence:

> no next line.

The second is generative completion:

> the line has been fully performed.

The distinction is central to the film's theory of emptiness.

## General rule

Subtraction should not automatically be read as:

- sadness;
- defeat;
- intimacy;
- truth.

Its function depends upon what the score removes and what becomes audible in the space.

The strongest analytical question is:

> **What could not be heard until the music stopped doing something else?**

# XII. Speech → song → speech

The Revue's relation to speech and song is not one-directional.

Characters do not simply:

> stop talking and begin singing.

The work uses several boundary types.

## 1. Speech ignites song

A formal introduction, challenge, or oath establishes:

- participants;
- relationship;
- theatrical register.

Then the music enlarges the proposition.

Examples:

- Mahiru's athletic oath;
- Claudine's devil wager;
- Junna's hunt declaration;
- Karen/Hikari's re-production formula.

## 2. Song contains unresolved speech

The singers occupy one structure, but spoken interruption reveals the problem the song cannot yet solve.

Examples:

- `どうして演技しないの？`
- `どうして 過去形なのよ？`
- Claudine's refusal of Position Zero;
- Karen's `何もない`.

The interruption is not a departure from the Revue.

It changes the Revue's success condition.

## 3. Speech strips role away

Hikari's:

> `会いたく…なっちゃうじゃない`

cannot be comfortably contained inside Claire's scripted register.

The direct personal line breaks continuity.

The stage stutters because role and person no longer coincide.

## 4. Song resumes after a new truth

After confession or self-authorship, music returns in changed form.

Mahiru/Hikari:

> shared fear → chosen life onstage → duet.

Junna/Nana:

> borrowed quotation fails → Junna's own words → expanded musical body.

Karen/Hikari:

> death → Hikari confession → re-production song.

The resumed song is not the old song simply continuing.

Its dramatic subject has changed.

## 5. Speech completes song

Several Revues end through ordinary or nearly ordinary lines:

- `またね`;
- “tomorrow”;
- “you were really scary”;
- “go find the next role.”

The relationship no longer needs the maximum musical apparatus.

This is one reason the movie's Revues feel complete rather than merely stopped.

# XIII. Unison, harmony, and counterpoint

## Unison as achieved relation

Karen/Hikari unison is most meaningful after the work has established:

- separate self-introductions;
- separate fears;
- separate capacities;
- ability to contradict.

Unison without those conditions would risk sonic fusion.

With them, it becomes:

> **chosen simultaneity.**

## Harmony as coexisting difference

Harmony does not require the voices to perform identical lines.

A harmonized or coordinated duet can make:

- two emotional positions;
- two timbres;
- two trajectories

audible as one relationship.

The series repeatedly values this over a single voice speaking for both.

Mahiru's counterfeit duet in TV05 is the negative control.

The dyad sounds complete because one person scripts it.

The later true duets remain:

> answerable.

## Counterpoint as argument

Movie Junna/Nana and Maya/Claudine use counterpoint to stage interpretive conflict.

The singers share:

- pulse;
- harmonic time;
- theatrical space.

They do not share:

- meaning.

This is an ideal form for rivalry.

Counterpoint says:

> **we are together enough to disagree precisely.**

## Ensemble relay rather than mass unison

TV11's ensemble structure is important because it does not simply make the eight girls one undifferentiated chorus.

Individual voices enter sequentially.

Identity is distributed without being homogenized.

The ensemble is not valuable because difference disappears.

It is valuable because:

> different voices can carry one shared category without one voice monopolizing it.

# XIV. Sound-world ownership and transfer

The corpus frequently begins with a musical or sonic form strongly associated with one performer and then lets another person enter, appropriate, or transform it.

## Nana: recurrence as ownership

Nana's replay makes repetition feel controlled.

Familiar form becomes evidence of Nana's authorship.

When Hikari and Karen produce re-production, the sound-world stops behaving as Nana's sealed possession.

A recurring form can be entered by:

> somebody the old arrangement did not authorize.

## Karen/Hikari: re-production becomes shared vocabulary

The industrial self-reproduction formula is initially a striking Karen marker.

It becomes:

- Hikari's renewed capacity;
- a pair declaration;
- a movie-wide grammar of self-transformation.

Ownership broadens from:

> one spectacular transformation

into:

> a reusable theatrical process.

## Maya/Claudine: self-introductions become weapons

The movie lets each woman take the other's earlier verbal-musical identity and distort it.

This is not simple quotation.

It is:

> musical-character appropriation.

Because each knows the other's style, each can reveal the shadow inside it.

The sound-world becomes evidence of intimate literacy.

## Junna: quotation becomes authored cadence

Junna's early voice is supported by established texts.

The movie makes the inherited cadence fail.

Her own language still sounds literary.

That continuity matters.

Junna does not become a different person by ceasing to quote.

The sound-world she inherited has become:

> internalized enough to generate new speech.

## Credits: individual futures inside ensemble song

`私たちはもう舞台の上` distributes future-oriented lines according to character trajectories.

The ensemble song does not erase individual destinations.

It makes dispersion audible as:

> one continuing corpus.

# XV. Recurring musical material: repetition, reprise, development, transfer

The series' own terminology requires unusually careful classification.

## Exact or near repetition

A familiar musical statement may return with little audible alteration.

This alone does not determine whether the dramatic operation is replay.

Context still matters.

## Reprise

Recognizable material returns after an interval.

The return invites comparison.

The analytical task is to ask:

- who owns it now?
- what occurred between appearances?
- what is newly audible because the audience remembers?

## Development

Material is altered through:

- expansion;
- fragmentation;
- new orchestration;
- different vocal allocation;
- changed harmonic or rhythmic placement;
- altered dramatic function.

This is the most direct musical analogue to re-production.

## Recontextualization

The musical surface may remain relatively stable while the dramatic meaning changes.

A Position Zero formula after the movie's critique of Position Zero cannot mean exactly what it did in TV03.

The listener's archive changes the hearing.

## Transfer

Material associated with one character or relation becomes available to another.

The transfer can signal:

- identification;
- appropriation;
- rivalry;
- ensemble belonging;
- or the dissolution of private ownership.

## Diagnostic question

The best way to distinguish replay from musical development is:

> **Does the return make the later performance predictable—or does it expose how much the performers and listeners have changed?**

Nana's replay aims for predictability.

The final movie reprises produce:

> difference made legible through recognition.

# XX. Strongest supporting evidence

## 1. Music repeatedly precedes conceptual language

TV08 and TV12 provide the clearest cases.

The score makes another future available before the administrator or performer names it fully.

## 2. Mechanical and musical climaxes diverge

Position Zero repeatedly arrives after or before the actual dramatic transformation.

MOV-M07 makes the divergence explicit.

## 3. Speech interruption changes Revue success conditions

`どうして演技しないの？`, `どうして 過去形なのよ？`, Position Zero refusal, and `何もない` all redirect songs rather than merely decorating them.

## 4. Vocal register tracks role versus self

Claudine's `俺様 / 俺` to `私`, Junna's quotation voice to rough self-authored challenge, Hikari's compressed speech to star-solo, and Maya's ceremonial persona to `私 いつだってかわいい` are strong examples.

## 5. Song allocation makes relationship architecture audible

TV11 ensemble relay, TV10 pair field, movie counterpoint, and the ending theme's distributed future lines all carry information that dialogue summary would lose.

## 6. Subtraction consistently carries distinct dramatic functions

Guilt, confession, consent, eulogy, completion, and ordinary continuation are differentiated through what the score removes.

# XXI. Strongest complicating evidence

## 1. Not every musical change is an independent argument

Some changes follow:

- action;
- editing;
- genre convention;
- simple dramatic escalation.

The analysis must not personify the score as an infallible narrator.

## 2. The final mix is dense

Dialogue, effects, environmental sound, and music overlap.

A spectral or loudness measurement may describe the full audiovisual mix rather than composition alone.

## 3. Revue songs also have franchise functions

They are:

- character songs;
- album tracks;
- performance pieces;
- marketable franchise music.

The literary reading should not pretend these industrial functions do not exist.

Nor should commercial function be treated as evidence that musical dramaturgy is absent.

## 4. Voice interpretation can become subjective

Terms such as:

- warm;
- regal;
- compressed;
- frightening

must be tied to observable performance features and scene comparison rather than intuition alone.

## 5. OVA Japanese wording is less secure

Because the available OVA subtitle layer is English, exact lexical claims there must remain conservative.

The vocal/performance observations remain stronger than fine-grained grammar claims.

## 6. Some songs remain intentionally spectacular rather than analytically transparent

The work sometimes wants excess, pleasure, and sensory overload.

Not every flourish needs a separate symbolic translation.

---

# XXII. Plausible alternative reading

A serious alternative interpretation is:

> The score largely amplifies emotional developments already established by dialogue and image. “Music knows first” risks exaggerating normal cinematic foreshadowing into an autonomous layer of meaning.

This reading explains several facts:

- composers and directors routinely prepare transitions before dialogue states the result;
- loudness and instrumentation naturally follow action;
- audience perception integrates image and sound rather than isolating musical intention;
- exact causality between relationship and musical change cannot be proven from the final mix.

## Why the present reading remains stronger

The full-series evidence supports a more active musical role because:

1. musical changes repeatedly occur **before** the decisive conceptual line;
2. formal closure is sometimes musically refused even when stage mechanics announce it;
3. the score withdraws in patterned ways that distinguish confession, consent, death, and completion;
4. vocal allocation itself creates relationship structures not reducible to the lexical content;
5. reprise and altered return participate directly in the corpus's `再演 / 再生産` argument;
6. the film's climaxes are placed around relational exposure rather than always around visible victory.

The safest formulation is not:

> music secretly knows the objectively correct answer.

It is:

> **music changes what the scene can emotionally and theatrically do before dialogue always knows how to describe the change.**

---

# XXIII. Final synthesis

The complete animated corpus begins with a familiar musical-theater promise:

> hidden feeling can become song.

It ends with something more difficult:

> song can make a self and relationship possible that did not exist before the performance.

Karen's voice begins by overflowing institutional grammar.

Hikari's voice begins by compressing feeling until crisis breaks the line.

Maya's voice makes perfection audible.

Claudine's makes challenge and appropriation audible.

Junna's gives inherited language precision before producing an authored rupture.

Nana's turns warmth into authority and finally back into present-tense recognition.

Mahiru's transforms accommodation into terrifying directorial force.

Futaba's roughness acquires the right to express resentment.

Kaoruko's languor and dialect become instruments of both inherited hierarchy and cutting insight.

The Giraffe's calm `分かります` eventually breaks into appetite and fuel.

These are not ornamental seiyuu distinctions.

They are character architecture.

The songs then place those voices into relations that prose cannot reproduce exactly.

A duet can:

- argue;
- overlap;
- harmonize;
- interrupt;
- refuse closure;
- and return differently.

An ensemble relay can carry identity without making every singer the same.

A solo can produce the singular self a later duet needs.

A silence can expose the line spectacle could not force.

A reprise can make the audience hear both continuity and difference at once.

The series' strongest musical insight is therefore not merely:

> emotion is expressed through song.

It is:

> **musical form is one of the ways relationships think.**

Nana's replay attempts to make recurrence secure.

Hikari's re-production lets a changed musical future emerge.

Karen and Hikari's shared phrases become viable only after each can own a first-person starhood.

Junna's quotations become power only after they can be transformed into her own cadence.

Maya and Claudine's counterpoint keeps disagreement inside shared time until personhood breaks through role.

The final credits song then performs the corpus's most mature feat.

It lets the climactic stage end.

It does not abandon music.

It redistributes song across:

- trains;
- schools;
- companies;
- messages;
- auditions;
- beginnings.

The stage continues without the climax continuing.

That is the musical meaning of the complete animated work's central distinction:

> **Replay asks the performer to sing the beloved song again as the person we remember.**

> **Re-production lets the returning song reveal the person neither performer nor audience could have heard before.**

The table reinforces a general conclusion:

> **Japanese lexical detail identifies the conceptual hinge; performed voice reveals whether the character can actually inhabit it.**

The same sentence could function differently if delivered:

- ceremonially;
- defensively;
- jokingly;
- breathlessly;
- with broken timing;
- or inside musical silence.

A full-series account of voice therefore requires both:

> what the language says

and:

> what the body of the voice is able to do with it.

This distinction remains analytically decisive.
