# 露崎まひる / 石動双葉 / 花柳香子
## Care, Dependence, and Different Roads
### Three models of loving someone without remaining necessary to them

# I. Executive thesis

Mahiru Tsuyuzaki, Futaba Isurugi, and Kaoruko Hanayagi occupy different parts of the cast, but their arcs belong in one analytical home because each asks a variation of the same question:

> **What happens when care, usefulness, admiration, and long familiarity become roles that no longer fit the people inside the relationship?**

Mahiru loves Karen partly through being needed.

Futaba loves Kaoruko partly through serving and carrying her.

Kaoruko loves Futaba partly through the certainty that Futaba will remain closest, watching and supporting the star Kaoruko expects herself to be.

None of these relations is false.

All become dangerous when the historical form of care is mistaken for the only legitimate future form of love.

Mahiru's television crisis begins when Hikari's return makes Karen less dependent upon her.

Her deepest question is not simply:

> Does Karen like Hikari more?

It is:

> `私はもういらないの？`

Am I no longer needed?

Mahiru has made usefulness carry too much of her self-worth. The counterfeit duet and doll imagery reveal the danger: a relationship can look like two-person intimacy while one person controls both roles.

Mahiru's resolution is not detachment from Karen. She recovers:

> `ちゃんとキラめいてる 私`

and reconnects care to a wider vocation:

> making precious people smile.

The OVAs and movie prove that this change holds. Mahiru can care directly for Hikari, become Hikari's theatrical rival, force Hikari's honest line, receive the confession, and then send Hikari onward toward Karen. She no longer needs exclusive access to prove she matters.

Futaba and Kaoruko begin with an equally intimate asymmetry.

Kaoruko is the inherited prodigy, dancer, and future head of a tradition.

Futaba is the fan, caretaker, driver, errand-runner, physical support, and person who stands closest.

Futaba's television declaration:

> `私だって 香子のファンのままじゃ嫌なんだよ`

is not a rejection of admiration.

It is a refusal to let admiration exhaust her subjecthood.

Episode 6 re-produces their bond as co-rivalry:

> `うちら二人で 世界一`

The movie then reveals the next contradiction.

If Futaba genuinely possesses her own road, it may lead to New National while Kaoruko goes to Kyoto.

Futaba initially explains the choice through respectable relational language:

- she must improve;
- stand on her own feet;
- become worthy of Kaoruko;
- change for both of them.

Kaoruko rejects the moralized explanation:

> `本音 晒せや`

Show me what you really want.

Futaba finally erupts with the cost of years of care and says:

> `今度はあたしが わがまま言う番`

Now it is her turn to be selfish.

The road Revue's decisive line is:

> `もう一緒には行けない`

They cannot continue traveling to the same destination.

The song's answer is not severance:

> `離れはしないよ`

Their mature bond distinguishes:

- same road;
- from continuing relation.

Taken together, Mahiru and Futaba/Kaoruko produce the corpus's fullest account of care:

> **Care becomes mature not when people stop needing, helping, or longing for one another, but when the cared-for person's growth no longer threatens the caregiver's right to exist—and when the caregiver can want a future that does not require the loved person to remain dependent.**

This document therefore argues for three connected developments:

1. Mahiru moves from **usefulness as identity** to **vocation as chosen care**.
2. Futaba moves from **fan/caretaker** to **performer whose selfish desire has equal standing**.
3. Kaoruko moves from **prodigy guaranteed an audience** to **rival who must tolerate being the one left waiting**.

The relationships survive because the roles change.

# PART A — MAHIRU

# II. Mahiru's baseline: warmth organized through usefulness

Mahiru's ordinary presence is warm, attentive, practical, and comparatively socially legible.

She notices:

- food;
- fatigue;
- schedule;
- comfort;
- small emotional changes.

Her care is genuine.

The problem is not that she is secretly manipulative in every act of support.

The problem is that care has become one of the principal structures through which she knows she matters.

Karen's dependence gives Mahiru:

- role;
- intimacy;
- predictability;
- proof of value.

This is why Hikari's return is existentially disruptive.

Hikari does not only compete for Karen's affection.

Hikari changes Karen.

A changing Karen may no longer need the same Mahiru.

The threat is therefore role-death before Mahiru possesses another self-definition.

---

# III. Mahiru before Episode 5: the accomplished self she does not fully see

Mahiru has a substantial pre-Seisho history:

- baton competition;
- bodily discipline;
- family affection;
- the aspiration to become a warm star who makes precious people smile.

The series repeatedly implies that Mahiru's problem is not absence of achievement or artistic identity.

It is perceptual reorganization.

Karen's importance becomes so great that Mahiru's earlier capacities are experienced as secondary.

This resembles the wider audience/performer problem in miniature.

Mahiru watches Karen's radiance and begins to understand herself primarily as:

> the person supporting that radiance.

Episode 5 must therefore recover not a brand-new Mahiru but an obscured one.

---

# IV. Hikari's return and the fear of dispensability

Hikari's presence makes several changes visible:

- Karen has another formative intimacy;
- Karen can become more independent;
- Karen's future may not be organized around Mahiru's support;
- Mahiru's specialness is no longer guaranteed by proximity.

The deepest line is:

> `私はもういらないの？`

This is not vanity.

It is a self-worth system collapsing.

If Mahiru's value lies in being the person Karen needs, Karen's development feels like evidence that Mahiru has become unnecessary.

The episode's emotional rigor lies in refusing to answer:

> no, Karen will always need you in exactly the same way.

That answer would preserve Mahiru by limiting Karen.

The relationship must survive through role change instead.

---

# V. The counterfeit duet: two bodies, one author

Mahiru's fantasy of restored intimacy is one of the series' clearest formal critiques of togetherness without reciprocity.

The scene looks like a duet.

Structurally, Mahiru controls both parts.

This reveals a central distinction:

> **A two-person form is not necessarily a two-subject relationship.**

The fantasy's comfort comes from eliminating Karen's independent answer.

Mahiru can preserve:

- closeness;
- predictable affection;
- old roles;
- the version of herself Karen needs.

The price is the living Karen.

The dolls develop the same idea.

A doll can be:

- held;
- placed;
- dressed;
- made to preserve an image.

It cannot contradict.

The Episode 5 Revue therefore turns Mahiru's care into a question of authorship:

> Does Mahiru want Karen to be happy, or does she need to remain the person who defines what Karen's happiness looks like?

The answer is not pure. Both desires coexist.

---

# VI. Recovery of `私`

Mahiru's resolution depends upon seeing:

> `ちゃんとキラめいてる 私`

She is already shining.

The line does not ask her to become less relational.

It changes the source from which relation operates.

Mahiru can now care because:

- care expresses a self;
- not because care creates the only available self.

Her broader formulation:

> `大切な人達を幸せにしたいと思えば 何度でもキラめける`

moves from one person to plural precious people.

This does not mean Karen becomes ordinary or interchangeable.

It means Mahiru's capacity to love is no longer dependent upon exclusive necessity.

The warm-star aspiration returns as vocation rather than compensation.

---

# VII. Mahiru after Episode 5: growth without personality replacement

Mahiru does not become emotionally detached, cool, or radically less caring.

That would be a weaker arc.

She remains:

- attentive;
- domestic;
- relational;
- sensitive to exclusion;
- capable of intense feeling.

What changes is the function of care.

She can increasingly:

- circulate it;
- receive care herself;
- recognize other relationships without treating them as theft;
- use her own history rather than only Karen's needs as a source of action.

The ensemble is crucial here.

Mahiru gains a social world in which she is:

- actor;
- friend;
- classmate;
- source of practical knowledge;
- performer with a professional future.

Karen no longer has to carry the whole proof that Mahiru matters.

---

# VIII. OVA03: care circulates

The OVA gives one of the clearest confirmations of Mahiru's development.

Hikari is nervous before performance.

Mahiru recognizes the bodily state and warms Hikari's hands using a technique from her own baton-competition history.

This scene matters in several ways.

## Mahiru cares for Hikari directly

Hikari is no longer primarily:

> the person who displaced Mahiru in Karen's life.

She is a fellow performer whose fear Mahiru understands.

## Mahiru's past becomes usable

The care does not come from Karen.

It comes from:

- Mahiru's body;
- family history;
- competition experience;
- remembered support.

Mahiru becomes a conduit of inherited care.

## The care is distributed

Mahiru is one among several people helping Hikari.

She does not need to be sole rescuer.

This is the ordinary-world answer to the counterfeit duet.

A real ensemble contains:

- many actions;
- many caregivers;
- no single author of the cared-for person's state.

---

# IX. Movie career clarity: the warm star becomes a professional project

Mahiru's stated future is one of the film's clearest.

She aims for New National and says she wants to become a star who makes everyone smile.

The continuity with childhood aspiration is direct.

The difference is institutional and embodied.

The desire is no longer:

> a comforting image discovered beneath jealousy.

It is:

- application;
- risk;
- professional competition;
- chosen life.

Mahiru becomes one of the characters most prepared for graduation not because she is fearless but because she has a sentence capable of orienting fear:

> `舞台で生きていく`

I will live onstage.

This is a model Karen lacks until the final movement.

---

# X. The Mahiru / Hikari movie Revue: former triangle becomes direct peerhood

Hikari enters looking for Karen.

Mahiru insists:

> the person on this stage now is Hikari.

This is the first sign that the relationship has become direct.

Mahiru does not want Hikari merely as:

- Karen's partner;
- Karen's problem;
- the rival who once displaced her.

She wants to perform with Hikari.

The sports-stadium architecture draws upon Mahiru's own embodied history.

The stage belongs to Mahiru as:

- former competitor;
- actor;
- athlete-performer;
- person with a professional oath.

The Revue's title `競演` contains both competition and co-performance.

Mahiru can now define Hikari as rival without needing Karen as mediator.

---

# XI. “Why aren't you acting?”: authenticity through performance

Mahiru's decisive accusation is:

> `どうして演技しないの？ 舞台の上なのに`

Hikari is obviously moving, singing, and responding.

Mahiru means that Hikari is withholding the person whose truth the performance should make visible.

Then:

> `もっとちゃんと演じてよ`
>
> `もっと感情込めてよ`
>
> `もっと本物の台詞を`

This is a sophisticated theory of acting.

Mahiru does not say:

> stop performing and become authentic.

She says:

> **perform well enough that the line contains the real stake.**

The stage goes backstage because Hikari's polished explanation must be taken behind itself.

The sports spectacle becomes horror/entertainment because Mahiru weaponizes audience pressure.

Her method is coercive and frightening.

The Revue does not sanitize it into ordinary therapy.

Its function is theatrical:

> make the unsayable line performable.

---

# XII. Hikari's confession and Mahiru's response

Hikari finally says:

- she ran;
- she was afraid;
- she is sorry.

Mahiru's response is not triumph:

> `一緒だね 私と`

We're the same.

Mahiru remains afraid too.

Hikari asks how Mahiru can stand onstage.

Mahiru answers:

> `決めたから`
>
> `舞台で生きていくって`

This is the mature Mahiru in one exchange.

Fear does not disappear.

A chosen orientation contains it.

The music returns after the decision because Mahiru's first-person vocation gives the stage another body.

The relation becomes:

- actor and actor;
- rival and rival;
- two frightened people who stand anyway.

---

# XIII. Mahiru lets the stage end

Mahiru eventually tells Hikari to go to Karen.

This is the definitive reversal of Episode 5.

The old Mahiru wanted the Karen/Hikari stage to stop because it threatened Mahiru's role.

The movie Mahiru recognizes that Hikari still has an unfinished line with Karen.

She sends Hikari onward **after** receiving the stage she wanted with Hikari herself.

This qualification matters.

Mahiru is not reduced to the nice side character who helps the central pair.

She first insists upon:

- being seen;
- being answered;
- receiving Hikari's truthful performance;
- having their direct relationship recognized.

Only then does she release the stage.

Generosity is healthy here because it does not require self-erasure.

Hikari's final praise:

> `本物の舞台女優だった！`

returns Mahiru's demand for the real Hikari as recognition of the real actress Mahiru.

---

# XIV. Mahiru's full development

The complete movement is:

> **need to be needed → jealousy and control → recovered self-radiance → distributed care → professional vocation → direct peerhood with Hikari → ability to let a desired stage end.**

Mahiru does not stop loving through care.

She stops making the loved person's dependence the proof that care has value.

This distinction will become the comparative standard for Futaba and Kaoruko.

---

# PART B — FUTABA / KAORUKO

# XV. Childhood promise and inherited asymmetry

Futaba and Kaoruko begin with a relationship whose emotional truth and structural inequality coexist.

Kaoruko is:

- prodigy;
- heir to a dance tradition;
- the person expected to bloom visibly;
- accustomed to being carried toward performance.

Futaba is:

- admirer;
- practical support;
- driver;
- errand-runner;
- protector;
- audience;
- person who makes Kaoruko's daily life function.

The arrangement is not simply imposed by Kaoruko.

Futaba actively loves through it.

That matters because later resentment should not retroactively make the care false.

The problem is that the arrangement distributes subjecthood unevenly.

Kaoruko gets to:

> want.

Futaba gets to:

> enable the wanting.

The childhood promise protects closeness while fixing roles.

Futaba remains nearest because she remains the person who watches, carries, and serves.

---

# XVI. Kaoruko's prodigy identity and the guaranteed audience

Kaoruko's self-conception depends upon more than talent.

It includes an inherited social world in which:

- family tradition legitimates her;
- Futaba confirms her centrality;
- performance and attention appear naturally connected.

Futaba is therefore not just a friend.

She is the first audience through whom Kaoruko's star identity feels socially real.

This is why Futaba's growth is so threatening.

If the audience becomes performer, Kaoruko loses:

- guaranteed proximity;
- guaranteed admiration;
- guaranteed role division.

The relationship must become more uncertain.

Kaoruko's laziness, imperiousness, and dependency should not be treated only as comic personality.

They are also evidence that the old arrangement has made support feel infrastructural rather than chosen.

Futaba's presence is taken for granted because it has always been there.

The movie later forces Kaoruko to confront what it means when infrastructure chooses another road.

---

# XVII. Futaba's fanhood: love and self-limitation

Futaba's line:

> `香子のファンのままじゃ嫌なんだよ`

is one of the television series' most important audience-to-performer transitions.

She does not say:

> I was never your fan.

Nor:

> admiration was degrading.

She says:

> remaining **only** a fan is no longer enough.

Fanhood has given her:

- direction;
- intimacy;
- knowledge;
- motivation;
- joy.

It also limits the relation if it becomes permanent ontology.

The fan stands before the performer as:

- witness;
- supporter;
- receiver.

Futaba wants to become someone Kaoruko must also watch.

The change is therefore not from love to competition.

It is from:

> one-way recognition

into:

> reciprocal performance.

This structure becomes a direct precedent for Hikari's movie fear of becoming Karen's fan.

---

# XVIII. Episode 6 Revue: equality through changed roles

Futaba's development with Claudine makes Kaoruko feel abandoned and displaced.

Kaoruko initially blames an external rival for changing Futaba.

The displacement is revealing.

If Claudine stole Futaba, then Kaoruko does not have to accept that Futaba chose growth.

The Revue forces the conflict into the relationship.

Futaba says:

- standing behind is no longer enough;
- she wants to see radiance from the nearest place as a performer;
- she too has found a road.

Kaoruko wins mechanically.

Futaba's new subjecthood survives.

This distinction is essential.

The episode refuses:

> winner = ethically correct person;
> loser = return to old role.

Their final:

> `うちら二人で 世界一`

re-produces the promise.

“Together” no longer means:

> Kaoruko shines and Futaba watches.

It means:

> two people aim upward from positions that can answer one another.

The movie tests whether this new form can survive separate geography.

---

# XIX. OVA evidence: equality is a practice, not a completed event

OVA03 shows Kaoruko still dissatisfied with how Futaba treats her.

She wants more recognition as rival rather than a return to automatic caretaking.

This is important because Episode 6 could otherwise be misread as a permanent relational fix.

The series' stronger position is:

> **a Revue can establish a new standard without instantly reorganizing every habit built over years.**

Futaba still knows how to:

- help;
- carry;
- anticipate Kaoruko's needs.

Kaoruko still knows how to:

- demand;
- rely;
- interpret care as proof of closeness.

Equality must be rehearsed in ordinary life.

The Revue changes what they can now object to.

It does not erase the embodied past.

---

# XX. Movie destinations: Episode 6 becomes logistical reality

Kaoruko chooses Kyoto and the family tradition.

Futaba chooses New National.

Both decisions are coherent extensions of their television development.

## Kaoruko

Kyoto need not mean regression.

She explicitly reframes it as beginning rather than simply returning.

The inherited tradition can become Kaoruko's active stage.

## Futaba

New National is difficult enough to expose genuine self-authorship.

She cannot rely on:

- guaranteed success;
- Kaoruko's presence;
- the old caretaker role;
- the comfort of shared institution.

The relationship's TV promise now faces its adult consequence:

> **If both are truly performers, they may choose stages the other does not choose.**

---

# XXI. The film Revue begins by externalizing the threat again

Kaoruko initially directs hostility toward Claudine through the candy-box metaphor.

Futaba becomes something precious Kaoruko owned and another woman contaminated.

This repeats the Episode 6 displacement.

The movie then makes it impossible to sustain.

The real threat is not Claudine.

It is:

> Futaba's autonomous desire.

Futaba arrives through spectacular vehicle imagery. The future drives into Kaoruko's inherited theatrical world.

The contrast is exact:

- Kaoruko: tradition, house, ritual, inherited stage;
- Futaba: road, vehicle, motion, chosen destination.

The Revue moves from static roles into mobility.

---

# XXII. `別れの一本道`: their own road reaches divergence

Episode 6 Futaba said she found her own straight road.

The film cashes out the implication:

> an independently found road may stop being the same road.

Both girls name:

> `別れの一本道`

The phrase does not automatically mean emotional severance.

It names the place where the historical itinerary divides.

The Revue's later highway transforms relational difference into:

- lanes;
- speed;
- passing;
- distance;
- risk of collision and disappearance.

This is why a simple conversation cannot carry the conflict.

The bodies must experience:

> two trajectories that can no longer be synchronized by habit.

---

# XXIII. Futaba's mature explanation is still incomplete

Futaba says she must:

- stand on her own feet;
- become better;
- reach higher;
- become worthy of Kaoruko;
- change for herself and Kaoruko.

The explanation contains truth.

Kaoruko rejects the phrase:

> “for your sake.”

Then asks why Futaba decided New National alone.

This is ethically sharp.

Futaba uses relational benefit to justify unilateral decision.

The structure rhymes with Hikari's protective control:

> I know this separation will ultimately be good for both of us, so I can decide it without you.

The stakes differ.

The autonomy problem is similar.

Kaoruko's demand is:

> Do not make me the moral excuse for something you want.

This is a surprisingly mature intervention from the character who benefits most from Futaba's service.

---

# XXIV. `本音 晒せや`: Kaoruko forces selfish first-person desire

Futaba compares herself with Junna, Mahiru, Maya, and Claudine.

She gives:

- realistic assessment;
- correct developmental language;
- respectable ambition.

Kaoruko wants:

> `本音`

The real desire beneath the correct answer.

This is not anti-rationalism.

Futaba's reasoning remains valid.

It is incomplete because it converts desire into obligation.

If Futaba truly possesses an independent road, she must be able to say:

> I want it.

without first proving:

> it will make me a better person for Kaoruko.

Selfishness becomes ethically necessary because Futaba's historical role has been over-socialized around service.

The person who always provides needs a legitimate first-person want.

---

# XXV. `ずるい`: the cost of care becomes speakable

Futaba eventually abandons professional explanation.

She lists years of practical care:

- snacks;
- bedtime;
- rides;
- daily assistance.

Then:

> `ずるい！`

Unfair.

This is one of the film's most important additions to the relationship.

The television series established that caregiving limited Futaba's development.

The film allows caregiving to produce resentment.

This does not make the care insincere.

It makes it human.

Love can be:

- chosen;
- joyful;
- identity-forming;
- and costly.

A mature relationship must survive the cared-for person hearing:

> the care took something from me.

Kaoruko needed this less admirable Futaba because the altruistic Futaba could still leave while psychologically remaining in service.

The angry Futaba finally wants something that is not made respectable through Kaoruko.

---

# XXVI. `もう一緒には行けない`: different road, continuing bond

Futaba says:

> `もう一緒には行けない`

The verb is travel.

She does not say:

- I no longer love you;
- our history was false;
- I never want to see you;
- you mean nothing now.

She says:

> our journeys can no longer remain the same journey.

The song later answers:

> `離れはしないよ`

This is the film's cleanest distinction between:

- itinerary;
- relationship.

The Revue does not solve the problem by making one girl abandon her plan.

It creates a bond capable of spanning two destinations.

The lack of a conventional Position Zero result is appropriate.

They do not need one center.

They need:

> two valid futures and a relation that survives the interval.

---

# XXVII. `今度はあたしが わがまま言う番`

Futaba says it is now her turn to be selfish.

This is the Revue's true redistribution of power.

For years:

- Kaoruko wanted;
- Futaba provided.

Now Futaba claims a turn.

Kaoruko completes the thought:

> her turn to be made to wait?

This is relational knowledge adapting in real time.

Kaoruko does not fully like the future.

She understands it.

The person who was always carried may have to become the person who remains while the carrier leaves.

Waiting changes meaning.

It is no longer proof of abandonment.

It can become evidence that the other person possesses a journey worth returning from.

---

# XXVIII. Motorcycle and key: the caretaker role becomes an object

The motorcycle has long embodied:

- Futaba's mobility;
- errands;
- physical carrying;
- Kaoruko's dependence;
- the practical form of their intimacy.

The film's closing return to the key/bike is therefore not random logistics.

It suggests that the old relationship function can be:

- remembered;
- transferred;
- held;
- used differently.

Futaba does not need to destroy the vehicle to become independent.

She needs to stop being reducible to:

> the person who carries Kaoruko.

The object remains.

The role becomes revisable.

This is another application of the corpus's archive principle:

> preserve material without letting material dictate personhood.

---

# XXIX. Credits: Kyoto, New National, and continuing ordinary intimacy

The credits confirm separate futures.

Futaba enters New National with Maya and Mahiru.

Kaoruko returns to Kyoto.

The imagery and message fragments show continued relation across the split.

This is the empirical proof of the Revue's thesis.

The pair does not need:

- same school;
- same company;
- same city;
- same daily routine

to remain meaningful.

The bond survives through:

- remembered practice;
- communication;
- anticipation;
- future encounter;
- and the fact that each person's development remains legible to the other.

The relationship has become portable without becoming abstract.

# PART C — COMPARATIVE CARE

# XXX. Three care structures

## Mahiru — care as proof of worth

Mahiru's danger is:

> if I am no longer needed, I may no longer matter.

Her growth requires a self whose care expresses value rather than creates all value.

## Futaba — care as inherited role and labor

Futaba's danger is:

> I may love so long through service that I no longer know which desires are mine.

Her growth requires legitimate selfishness and a separate road.

## Kaoruko — care as guaranteed infrastructure

Kaoruko's danger is:

> I may confuse another person's lifelong support with a permanent entitlement to that person's direction.

Her growth requires being able to wait and receive a Futaba she did not supervise.

---

# XXXI. Care that permits growth versus care that requires dependency

| Dimension | Dependency-preserving care | Growth-permitting care |
|---|---|---|
| Source of caregiver worth | Being necessary | Chosen vocation / mutual value |
| View of cared-for change | Threat to relationship | New condition relationship must learn |
| Decision structure | Caregiver defines what is best | Other person can answer and refuse |
| Future | Preserve known roles | Permit different roads and new roles |
| Emotional honesty | Resentment hidden under goodness | Cost and selfish desire can be spoken |
| Audience relation | Watch/support from fixed position | Performer can become someone watched in return |
| Separation | Abandonment | Possible interval within continuing bond |

The corpus does not suggest all dependence is bad.

People genuinely:

- help;
- carry;
- warm hands;
- feed;
- plan;
- wait.

The ethical distinction is whether support becomes jurisdiction over:

- role;
- route;
- identity;
- future.

---

# XXXII. Japanese voice and performed-state synthesis

## Mahiru

Mahiru's baseline voice is warm and socially attentive. Episode 5 expands it into breathless jealousy, pleading, and controlling fantasy. The film then gives her an unusually forceful theatrical register.

Her frightening movie performance is important because it proves warmth is not weakness. Mahiru can use:

- projection;
- repetition;
- command;
- audience address;
- genre transformation

to force another performer into truth.

After Hikari confesses, Mahiru's voice softens immediately. The change demonstrates that the aggression was organized around a dramatic demand rather than desire to keep Hikari helpless.

`決めたから` is especially strong because it is simple. Mahiru's vocation no longer needs elaborate justification.

## Futaba

Futaba's ordinary register carries directness, practicality, and grounded emotional force. Her film Revue moves from stylized self-introduction into rational career speech, then collapses into repeated `なんで` and `ずるい`.

The simplification is the truth event. Years of socially acceptable care finally become personal grievance.

Her closing declaration of selfishness integrates the rough emotional register into agency.

## Kaoruko

Kaoruko's Kyoto speech and stylized elegance help her control the relation theatrically. She can be cutting while retaining form.

Her most important film move is lexical refusal:

- dismiss the respectable explanation;
- reject comparison with others;
- demand `本音`.

She operates as a director of Futaba's confession.

After the climax, Kaoruko completes Futaba's sentence. The girl who began by misdescribing Futaba as possession becomes the person capable of anticipating Futaba's new independent desire.

# XXXIII. Relationship and yuri coding

## Mahiru / Karen

The television relation is strongly yuri-compatible through jealousy, exclusive desire, domestic closeness, and fear of replacement. The work ultimately refuses to make romantic exclusivity the condition of Mahiru's worth.

Mahiru's development does not invalidate the intimacy. It prevents intimacy from requiring Karen to remain dependent.

## Mahiru / Hikari

The movie creates a direct, theatrically intimate relation:

- Mahiru wants a stage with Hikari;
- Hikari is frightened and changed by Mahiru;
- they share fear;
- Hikari recognizes Mahiru as a real actress.

The coding is less conventionally romantic than Karen/Hikari, but the direct desire to perform together and the bodily/emotional intensity are significant.

## Futaba / Kaoruko

Their relationship is extensively yuri-coded through:

- lifelong exclusivity;
- domestic caretaking;
- jealousy;
- possessive metaphors;
- shared promise;
- bodily transport;
- separation anxiety;
- the emotional framing of different roads.

The explicit language remains childhood friendship, care, and rivalry.

The most disciplined conclusion is:

> **The pair is strongly yuri-coded, with the film treating geographic separation almost like a relationship breakup while refusing to make shared destination the proof of love.**

# XXXV. Contradiction audit

## Strongest supporting evidence

1. Mahiru's `私はもういらないの？` and counterfeit duet.
2. Recovery of `ちゃんとキラめいてる 私`.
3. OVA03 distributed care and Mahiru's direct care for Hikari.
4. Mahiru's movie `舞台で生きていく` and willingness to send Hikari onward.
5. Futaba's `ファンのままじゃ嫌`.
6. Film demand `本音 晒せや`.
7. Futaba's `ずるい` and claim to selfishness.
8. `もう一緒には行けない` paired with `離れはしないよ`.
9. Credits showing different institutions and continuing contact.

## Strongest complicating evidence

1. Mahiru's movie method is coercive and uses public pressure to force confession.
2. Futaba still frames much ambition through Kaoruko even after claiming selfishness.
3. Kaoruko's possessiveness is softened rather than eliminated.
4. The relationships continue to romanticize unequal service and exclusivity.
5. Geographic separation does not prove the pair can sustain equality over a longer adult future.

## Plausible alternative reading

A plausible alternative treats Mahiru's Episode 5 arc as primarily self-confidence, and Futaba/Kaoruko as primarily a childhood-friend rivalry where the caretaking asymmetry is comedic rather than ethical architecture.

## Why the present reading remains stronger

The repeated language of:

- need;
- fanhood;
- assigned support roles;
- unilateral decision;
- selfish desire;
- waiting;
- different roads

makes care and dependency structurally central.

Comedy softens the presentation but does not remove the role distribution the characters explicitly fight over.

# XXXVII. Final synthesis

Mahiru, Futaba, and Kaoruko reveal why care is one of *Revue Starlight*'s most dangerous and valuable relationship languages.

Care feeds.

Carries.

Warms hands.

Drives motorcycles.

Plans support.

Makes the stage possible.

Care can also become a role so complete that the loved person's growth feels like betrayal.

Mahiru fears that if Karen no longer needs her, Mahiru no longer matters.

Futaba fears that if she remains only the person who serves Kaoruko, Futaba may never discover what she wants.

Kaoruko fears that if Futaba discovers what she wants, Kaoruko may lose the audience and infrastructure that made specialness feel secure.

The television series begins the revisions:

- Mahiru recovers a shining `私`;
- Futaba refuses permanent fanhood;
- Kaoruko recognizes co-rivalry.

The OVAs prove that change must be practiced in ordinary life.

The movie makes the consequences material:

- Mahiru chooses a professional life and can let Hikari leave her desired stage;
- Futaba chooses New National;
- Kaoruko chooses Kyoto;
- the relationship survives without one road.

The mature form of care is therefore not:

> I will never need you.

Nor:

> I will always remain necessary to you.

It is:

> **I can help you, be changed by you, resent what care has cost me, ask for something selfish, and still allow your next role to exceed the place my care once occupied in your life.**

That is how care becomes compatible with Stage Girl subjecthood.

# Appendix A — Mahiru and Futaba as audience-to-performer transformations

Mahiru and Futaba appear very different.

Mahiru's attachment is organized through domestic usefulness and emotional centrality.

Futaba's is organized through practical service, fanhood, and physical accompaniment.

Yet both begin near the audience side of another girl's stage.

## Mahiru

Mahiru's gaze is fixed on Karen's need. She becomes the person who supports, comforts, and remains close.

Her Episode 5 crisis occurs when the watched performer no longer needs the same audience/caregiver.

Mahiru's mature movement is not simply from audience to performer; she was always a performer. It is from:

> **performer whose relational worth depends upon the other girl's need**

into:

> **performer whose own stage identity makes care elective and shareable.**

The movie confirms this by letting her become Hikari's demanding audience and co-performer. She can watch, affect, and be recognized without disappearing into support.

## Futaba

Futaba's fanhood is more explicit. She stands nearest to Kaoruko's radiance and makes the life around that radiance possible.

Her television line rejects permanent spectatorhood.

Her movie route completes the movement by giving her an institution Kaoruko does not choose.

The mature Futaba wants Kaoruko to watch:

- Futaba's road;
- Futaba's risk;
- Futaba's future performance.

Both arcs therefore revise the audience/performer boundary.

A loving audience need not stop watching.

She must remain capable of:

> **performing something the beloved did not already request.**

---

# Appendix B — Kaoruko as director of Futaba's first-person desire

Kaoruko's film role is ethically paradoxical.

She is the person most threatened by Futaba's independence.

She is also the person who refuses to accept an independence still narrated through service.

When Futaba says the decision is for herself and Kaoruko, Kaoruko hears the residue of the old role. Futaba remains the caretaker who makes even departure morally useful to the cared-for person.

`本音 晒せや` therefore performs a director's function.

Kaoruko strips away:

- comparison with Junna;
- comparison with Maya and Claudine;
- correct career logic;
- admirable self-improvement;
- the claim that Kaoruko benefits.

What remains is:

> I gave you years of care.
>
> I resent that you received so much of me.
>
> I want my turn.

Kaoruko's demand does not erase her possessiveness. It reveals that she would rather face a selfish Futaba than keep an altruistic attendant who leaves while pretending not to want the leaving.

This is one of the film's most interesting accounts of intimacy:

> **the person who fears your independent desire may still know the sound of your false selflessness better than anyone else.**

Hikari confesses fear; Mahiru admits continuing fear. Neither is cured.

## Epistemic

Hikari learns that the protective explanation for leaving Karen was incomplete. Mahiru demonstrates that courage means chosen performance while afraid.

## Relational

Former attachment rivals become direct artistic rivals and audiences for one another.

## Symbolic/theatrical

Sports arena, backstage, horror entertainment, and Mr. White transform competition into confession and shelter.

## Musical

Spectacle reaches saturation before Hikari's truth; the score then withdraws. It returns when Mahiru names her decision to live onstage.

# Appendix E — Comparative risk map

| Care form | Virtue | Characteristic danger | Mature correction |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mahiru's emotional/domestic care | attentiveness, warmth, social stability | worth depends on being needed | chosen vocation and distributed care |
| Futaba's practical care | loyalty, embodied support, reliability | service consumes independent desire | legitimate selfishness and separate route |
| Kaoruko's reception of care | trust, lifelong intimacy, artistic confidence | support becomes entitlement | tolerate waiting and unknown Futaba |
| Hikari's protective care | accurate risk perception, sacrifice | unilateral control | truthful fear, invitation, waiting |
| Ensemble care | distributed competence | social pressure and diffusion of responsibility | preserve individual answer and refusal |

The comparison shows why the series does not simply praise care as moral virtue.

Care is a power relation whenever one person can define:

- the need;
- the solution;
- the future role;
- or the meaning of sacrifice.

Its ethical form depends upon whether the other person can answer back.

# Appendix F — Mahiru and Kaoruko: two responses to threatened specialness

Mahiru and Kaoruko are useful contrasts because both experience another person's growth as a threat to a privileged place.

Mahiru initially turns the threat inward:

> If Karen no longer needs me, perhaps I have no value.

Kaoruko initially turns it outward:

> Someone else changed or stole Futaba; the new Futaba violates the promised arrangement.

The different responses produce different Revue tasks.

Mahiru must recover a self capable of caring without exclusive necessity.

Kaoruko must recognize that Futaba's independent desire is not evidence that the relationship was false.

Yet each woman also contributes something the other lacks.

Mahiru's warmth makes vulnerability socially habitable. Kaoruko's harshness refuses the false comfort of morally polished explanation.

The mature care ethic needs both capacities:

- enough tenderness that fear and resentment can be admitted;
- enough confrontation that care does not hide the first-person desire it has displaced.

This is why the film does not resolve every relationship through gentle conversation. Sometimes the person who loves you must comfort the frightened self. Sometimes she must demand that the agreeable self stop speaking for the person underneath.

# Appendix G — Care as embodied labor

The three characters also make care materially visible.

Mahiru warms hands, notices bodily fear, and translates memory into technique.

Futaba buys food, manages sleep, drives, carries, and organizes movement.

Kaoruko receives this labor so habitually that its scale becomes visible only when Futaba lists it in anger.

The work therefore refuses to leave care as a vague emotional virtue.

Care consumes:

- time;
- attention;
- bodily effort;
- planning;
- opportunity.

That materiality explains why resentment is not a moral failure by itself. A caregiver can love what she does and still need the cost recognized.

The ethical task is not to eliminate dependence but to make labor:

- visible;
- answerable;
- non-compulsory;
- compatible with the caregiver's own future.

Futaba's separate road is convincing because the film has shown how much of her body has historically been spent making Kaoruko's road easy.

# Appendix A — Six-outcome audit of the Mahiru / Hikari Revue

## Mechanical outcome

The sporting premise advertises a single gold medal, but the Revue abandons that metric. No stable winner, Position Zero claim, or fastening loss explains its success. Mahiru succeeds because Hikari finally performs the line the stage was built to demand.

## Emotional outcome

Hikari admits that she ran because she was afraid. Mahiru admits that fear also belongs to her and has not disappeared. Fear ceases to function as evidence that one should leave the stage.

## Epistemic outcome

Hikari's protective explanation is revealed as incomplete. Mahiru establishes that authenticity is not the absence of acting: a performance becomes truthful when its form is adequate to the emotion it carries.

## Relational outcome

Mahiru and Hikari cease being primarily two people connected through Karen. They become direct artistic peers capable of frightening, recognizing, and releasing one another.

## Symbolic/theatrical outcome

Sports arena, backstage corridor, horror spectacle, mascot audience, and giant Mr. White move from competition through judgment into shelter. The audience that pressures the performer eventually becomes capable of catching her.

## Musical outcome

The loudest spectacle does not contain the deepest truth. The mix contracts radically around Hikari's confession, then expands after Mahiru states that she has chosen to live onstage. Subtraction makes the real line audible; renewed musical body confirms the decision to continue.

# Appendix B — Six-outcome audit of the Futaba / Kaoruko film Revue

## Mechanical outcome

The Revue does not produce an old-audition victor. The highway stage crashes, Position Zero is irrelevant, and the decisive result is the successful articulation of differentiated futures.

## Emotional outcome

Futaba's respectable explanation breaks into accumulated resentment: food, rides, sleep, years of practical care, and the sense that Kaoruko has monopolized her. Kaoruko's anger is exposed as fear that equality may mean being left behind.

## Epistemic outcome

Futaba learns that an independent choice cannot remain fully honest while justified mainly as something done “for Kaoruko.” Kaoruko learns that the unknown Futaba is not a counterfeit version of the childhood companion.

## Relational outcome

`もう一緒には行けない` is separated from relational annihilation. The pair cannot continue down one itinerary, but the song's `離れはしないよ` preserves constitutive attachment without requiring co-location.

## Symbolic/theatrical outcome

Gambling house, inherited dance world, decorated vehicles, highway, fall, hand, motorcycle, and key convert the old caretaker arrangement into movable history. The vehicle remains meaningful after “carrying Kaoruko” ceases to define Futaba's whole role.

## Musical outcome

The composition contracts when Kaoruko rejects Futaba's morally polished explanation, rebuilds when she demands `本音`, and reaches its greatest sustained force around Futaba's selfish desire and declaration of separate travel. The score treats first-person wanting—not dutiful justification—as the Revue's propulsion.

# Appendix C — Comparative significance: care without jurisdiction

These arcs are useful for comparison because they reject two easy moral summaries.

The first is:

> dependency is bad, so mature people should stop needing one another.

The second is:

> care is good, so the person who cares most should be trusted to know what the relationship requires.

*Revue Starlight* accepts neither.

Mahiru, Futaba, and Kaoruko continue to need, support, frustrate, and organize one another's lives. Their growth does not produce emotional detachment. What changes is the authority attached to care.

Mahiru can no longer infer:

> because I cared for Karen, Karen's independence deprives me of my rightful role.

Futaba can no longer infer:

> because caring for Kaoruko was genuine, continuing to provide that care must remain my principal future.

Kaoruko can no longer infer:

> because Futaba's care helped make me, I possess a claim over where Futaba must go next.

The mature alternative is not weak attachment. It is attachment capable of surviving another person's first-person sentence.

That sentence may be:

- I want to live onstage;
- I want New National;
- I cannot go with you;
- I want my turn to be selfish;
- I wanted this stage with you, and now I can let you leave it.

The three characters therefore provide a compact comparative test for caregiving relationships in other fiction:

> **Does care make the recipient more capable of choosing, and can the caregiver remain a person when the recipient chooses something that reduces the caregiver's old necessity?**

Where the answer is no, care risks becoming jurisdiction.

Where the answer is yes, practical dependence can coexist with personhood.

# Appendix D — Unresolved tensions

The film does not certify these relationships as permanently solved.

Mahiru's professional self remains young, frightened, and capable of again attaching worth to how strongly her performance affects particular people. Her terrifying success with Hikari is generative because it ends; repeated reliance on coercive confession would be ethically different.

Futaba's separate road does not erase eighteen years of habitual responsiveness. A new institution may give her independent roles, but Kaoruko's needs will remain unusually difficult for her to refuse.

Kaoruko's willingness to be the one left waiting remains verbally resistant and emotionally possessive. She recognizes Futaba's right to move without becoming serene about it. That imperfection is believable, but it means the pair's mature equality is a practice rather than a settled state.

The credits establish continuing contact, not the permanent future of the relationships.

That open-endedness is consistent with the corpus's larger claim:

> **A relationship remains alive not because one Revue solves it forever, but because later versions of the people remain able to contradict the roles that once organized their care.**

# Appendix H — Professionalization changes the meaning of care

Graduation matters because school permits care and artistic development to remain unusually entangled.

Friends can:

- share dorm life;
- rehearse together;
- notice moods;
- provide transportation;
- enter the same productions;
- repair conflict through repeated proximity.

Professional life makes these forms less automatic.

Mahiru, Futaba, and Kaoruko therefore face a second-order question:

> Which forms of care remain possible when the institution no longer guarantees daily access?

## Mahiru

Her warm-star vocation must become legible to new colleagues and audiences who do not know her Karen history. This tests whether care has truly become artistic practice rather than private relational function.

## Futaba

New National removes the practical routines through which she historically expressed love. She must discover who she is when nobody needs her to buy the snack, drive the bike, or manage Kaoruko's body through the day.

## Kaoruko

Kyoto gives her a stage rich in inheritance but removes the guaranteed Futaba infrastructure. She must make tradition and daily life function without translating every difficulty into a demand that Futaba return.

Distance therefore tests whether the Revue solutions are portable.

A school relationship can feel egalitarian because the participants keep occupying the same social field. Adult equality becomes more demanding:

- different schedules;
- unequal success;
- new peers;
- reduced access;
- choices made without immediate consultation.

The credits offer evidence of continuation, not proof that every future test is solved.

This open-endedness is a strength. The film does not make one cathartic Revue magically substitute for years of ordinary renegotiation.

The mature care ethic is a practice:

> keep noticing without surveilling;
> keep helping without pre-assigning need;
> keep communicating without demanding identical routes;
> keep allowing the other person's professional world to become larger than the relationship.
