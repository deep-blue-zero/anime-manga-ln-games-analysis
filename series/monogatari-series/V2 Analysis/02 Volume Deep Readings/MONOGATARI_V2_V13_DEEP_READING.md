# 〈物語〉シリーズ V2 — Volume 13 Deep Reading
## 『恋物語』
### 第恋話「ひたぎエンド」

> **Publication-local core:** 『恋物語』 does not vindicate lying as a superior form of truth. It stages a failed deception and then asks what becomes possible when a professional liar stops treating a person as a credulous target. Kaiki spends a month constructing the perfect lie for Nadeko and discovers that she is not deceived because she never granted him enough relational reality to be deceived. The successful intervention begins only after he calls her simply `千石`, exposes the manga she hid more fiercely than her love for Araragi, and argues that one failed dream does not acquire jurisdiction over every other possible future. Nadeko leaves godhood not because Kaiki proves that her love was fake, nor because he guarantees happiness, but because he makes another unfinished human project visible enough for her to choose it.
>
> **Retrospective V2 core:** V13 is the Second Season's strongest demonstration that **truth and lies are relational acts rather than stable substances**. Kaiki can lie factually while acting responsibly, tell truths cruelly, misread motives, conceal evidence, and nevertheless become the person who recognizes a part of Nadeko no one else knew existed. Hitagi can manipulate Kaiki while refusing to forgive him. Nadeko can genuinely love Araragi while using the impossibility of that love as a totalizing life-story. Gaen can command Kaiki to stop in a way Kaiki suspects may actually fund and provoke continuation. Ougi can supply accurate information in order to precipitate violence. The volume therefore rejects an ethics in which `truth = good` and `lie = bad`; what matters is what a statement permits, forecloses, assigns, or returns to another person's agency.

## 1.3 Paratext and afterword

The afterword explicitly identifies the book as `恋物語　第恋話　ひたぎエンド` and calls attention to the narrator-substitution experiment: people and stories look radically different depending on who tells them. ## 1.4 Source-confidence rule

Kaiki's words remain primary-source Japanese prose but not omniscient fact. V13 therefore uses a **claim-specific reliability protocol**:

1. direct action witnessed and materially corroborated;
2. statements constrained by another speaker's response;
3. Kaiki's factual reports with no visible incentive to distort;
4. Kaiki's motive explanations and personality judgments;
5. openly admitted inventions, jokes, provocations, and self-mythology.

The hierarchy is not absolute. A professional liar can tell the truth; an earnest character can be mistaken. The point is to resist both naïve trust and global dismissal.

# 2. Publication-local volume thesis

The most obvious version of V13 is: **Kaiki deceives Nadeko and saves Hitagi and Araragi.**

The actual novel spends its climax dismantling that description.

Kaiki does indeed accept Hitagi's commission to `千石撫子を騙す`. He interviews Nadeko's parents, meets the new snake god, spends a month cultivating familiarity, brings offerings, plays `綾取り`, drinks with her, constructs a false wish of his own, and prepares a clean final lie: Araragi, Hitagi, and Shinobu died in a traffic accident. `[§019:P026–033] [§032:P001–025] [§035:P043–066]`

And the lie fails immediately.

Nadeko smiles and says:

`貝木さんも『私』を騙すんだね`

Kaiki's technical work was not sloppy. His mistake was relational. He had assumed that because Nadeko rarely suspects deception, she would trust him. But trust and suspicion are not opposites here. Nadeko has built a world so closed that Kaiki was never admitted far enough inside it for his words to carry the weight required for betrayal. He realizes:

- she did not trust him;
- she did not exactly doubt him either;
- she simply did not fully treat him as someone whose assertions could reorganize her interior world. `[§036:P001–014]`

That makes the successful intervention structurally different from the planned fraud.

Kaiki first considers abandoning the job. His self-description says that allowing five or more people to die is something he could normally compartmentalize and later forget by making money elsewhere. But he reaches the point where even he says:

`俺はもう自分を騙せなかった`

Then he does something the previous month of performance never quite did. He calls her:

`千石`

not “god,” not “snake god,” not target, not cute child. He calls her as one middle-school girl. `[§036:P194–215]`

He then reveals the closet.

Inside are piles of notebooks containing manga Nadeko has created and hidden from everyone: parents, Araragi, Tsukihi, Hanekawa, classmates. The material is embarrassing, derivative, romantic, sometimes erotic, and—to Kaiki's eye—not worthless. `[§037:P053–095]`

That discovery changes the intervention's axis.

Kaiki does **not** prove that Nadeko's Araragi-love was unreal. He explicitly says that dream was a dream too. It failed. His argument is that a failed dream has no right to drag every other desire into the grave with it:

`その夢は破れた。`

`だからと言って、他の夢まで巻き添えにする必要がどこにある？` `[§037:P135–137]`

V13's publication-local answer is therefore not “a lie saved Nadeko.”

It is:

> **A planned lie fails; a different future becomes persuasive because it returns multiplicity to a girl who had made one impossible romance total.**

The novel still allows Kaiki to call that `騙す`, and Nadeko finally says `騙されてあげる`. But the phrase has changed meaning. She is no longer the passive object of a scam. She knowingly accepts a proposal whose future cannot be guaranteed.

That is closer to **chosen uncertainty** than deception.

# 3. Retrospective V2 thesis

V13 is the culmination of several Second Season corrections.

V08 showed that Hanekawa's self could not survive if every incompatible feeling had to be cut away.

V09 showed that later catastrophe does not retroactively rewrite the moral character of an earlier rescue.

V10 showed that mixed motive does not cancel genuine help.

V11 showed that Nadeko's shyness, touch aversion, passivity, resentment, desire, and self-deception all coexist without one becoming the hidden “true” self.

V12 showed that law and moral justice are not identical and that change does not equal fraudulence.

V13 adds:

> **A person becomes especially vulnerable when one relationship, one dream, one role, or one “irreplaceable thing” is allowed to monopolize the meaning of an entire life.**

Kaiki's philosophy of money is morally ugly in many applications, but philosophically useful here. He says he likes money because it substitutes for almost anything while itself remaining replaceable. What he hates is the claim that one thing is absolutely irreplaceable:

`『これ』がなきゃ生きていけない`

`『あれ』だけが生きる理由だ`

`『それ』こそは自分の生まれてきた目的だ` `[§037:P138–142]`

Applied to human beings, this doctrine can become callous. It can deny grief, loyalty, singular attachment, or responsibilities that are not fungible. V13 does not certify Kaiki's worldview globally.

But against Nadeko's structure it has diagnostic force.

If Araragi is the single irreplaceable object, then rejection annihilates future meaning. If godhood is the single resolution, then returning to humanity looks like total defeat. If being cute is the only socially legible identity, then anger or artistic ambition threatens belonging.

Kaiki reopens substitution and plurality:

- another dream can follow a failed dream;
- another activity can become serious;
- another version of Nadeko can exist;
- she may never become happy;
- becoming what she wants can matter even without guaranteed happiness. `[§038:P112–117]`

This is not consumerist interchangeability as a full ethics of personhood. It is **anti-totalization**.

V13 therefore refines the V1 claim that Kaiki “finds Nadeko's real dream.” The manga is not proven to be a metaphysically deeper essence than her love. Kaiki himself overstates when he calls it `本当の夢`. Nadeko denies ever consciously wanting to become a manga artist. What the closet proves more securely is that she possesses a private creative practice sufficiently important, shameful, sustained, and self-protected that no one else knew it existed.

The mature V2 formulation is:

> **Kaiki does not discover Nadeko's one true self. He discovers evidence that her life was always larger than the story she had made total.**

# 4. Narrative architecture and arc map

## Movement A — false narrator contract: §§001–006

Kaiki opens by directly attacking the reader's expectation that Hitagi will narrate. He declares writing, speech, truth, and his own identity suspect. This is not merely warning; it makes **doubt itself part of the narrative performance**. The story then begins on January 1 with Hitagi contacting him.

## Movement B — commission, price, and motive: §§007–010

Hitagi asks Kaiki to neutralize Nadeko. Kaiki performs a motive audit and rejects several flattering explanations. The decisive remembered name is Kanbaru Suruga, Gaen Tooe's daughter. He concludes he can help Hitagi/Araragi and deceive Nadeko `神原駿河のためなら`. He takes **100,000 yen only as expenses** and waives the ordinary fee. `[§009:P218–257] [§010:P017–038]`

## Movement C — investigate the family and meet the god: §§011–019

Kaiki interviews Nadeko's parents, reads the material environment of her room, and meets god Nadeko. He discovers that she is lonely, bored, pleased to receive offerings, comfortable with permanent unrequited love, and unusually happy when he describes her as frightening rather than cute. He concludes she should be easy to deceive. Hitagi later cries in the restroom after he tells her he expects the plan to work, then returns and says `ありがとう。感謝するわ`. `[§017] [§019:P102–110]`

## Movement D — competing specialist governance: §§020–024

Ononoki carries Gaen's instruction: `手を引け`. The argument is systemic: Nadeko's godhood has stabilized an otherwise spiritually disordered town; a failed scam could kill many. Gaen offers Kaiki **3,000,000 yen** to stop. Kaiki accepts the money, tells Ononoki he will withdraw, and immediately continues. `[§022:P156–179] [§023:P001–004]`

## Movement E — closet, warning, and distributed investigation: §§025–031

Kaiki illegally enters Nadeko's home and opens the forbidden closet. He initially classifies its contents as irrelevant. A second `手を引け` warning appears under his hotel door. He later meets Hanekawa, receives a broader reconstruction of the town, and learns Hanekawa's perceptive description of Nadeko as someone who does not truly “look at” other people. Hanekawa also asks whether Oshino has a niece; Kaiki says, to the best of his knowledge, Oshino has no siblings and is `天涯孤独`. `[§031:P030–052] [§031:P092–123]`

## Movement F — one month of ordinary contact: §032

Kaiki visits Nadeko essentially every day through January. They play string games, talk, sometimes drink, and develop routine. He spends over 300,000 yen in offerings. The routine humanizes both parties without giving him the trust he thinks he has earned. `[§032:P001–025]`

## Movement G — planned deception collapses: §§033–036

Kaiki calls Hitagi before the operation. She insists that saving her will not erase her hatred of him. She also says, paradoxically but precisely, that Kaiki has never lied **to her**. Kaiki then delivers the traffic-death fabrication. Nadeko sees through it immediately. Her power rises, but she remains eerily controlled rather than simply berserk. She reveals Ougi's involvement in steering her toward godhood. `[§033:P082–095] [§035:P043–067] [§036:P120–179]`

## Movement H — the intervention changes category: §§036–037

Kaiki gives up on the scam and invokes the closet. Nadeko physically attacks him out of shame. He reframes the notebooks as evidence of another possible future, criticizes her self-contempt toward her own work, and opposes one irreplaceable desire swallowing all others. She eventually says `騙されてあげる`; the snakes vanish and she accepts deification's reversal. `[§036:P194–215] [§037:P053–191]`

## Movement I — extraction, boundary, and apparent ending: §038

Araragi arrives. Kaiki lies to him that Gaen commissioned the job, hiding Hitagi. Kaiki extracts the god-talisman from Nadeko, returns it to Araragi, and tells Araragi to become a memory rather than continuing to center himself in Nadeko's life. `[§038:P001–120]`

## Movement J — Hitagi's end and Kaiki's non-ending: §039

Hitagi thanks Kaiki but reiterates separation. Kaiki finally identifies Hitagi as the anonymous warning-letter sender and suspects Gaen's 3,000,000 yen may likewise have been reverse psychology/funding. Then a previously scammed middle-school boy attacks him with an iron pipe, saying `扇さんの言った通りだった`. Kaiki loses consciousness. Publication-locally, survival beyond the narrated frame remains unresolved. `[§039:P001–090]`

# 5. Narrator and focalization audit

## 5.1 Kaiki announces unreliability too loudly to be read simply

The opening warning is one of the series' most aggressive narratorial contracts. Kaiki says he will lie, fabricate, conceal, and disregard fair play. `[§001]`

A simplistic reading would therefore treat every claim as unknowable.

That would make the book analytically useless and would also fall into Kaiki's stated trap: taking his self-description literally.

The more productive question is:

> **What kind of statement is this, who benefits from it, and what constrains it?**

Kaiki's observable actions—travel, payments, repeated visits, physical assault, talisman extraction—carry different evidentiary weight from his claims about why he acts or what other people “really” feel.

## 5.2 Kaiki is unusually good at seeing what others miss—and unusually eager to turn seeing into contempt

He notices:

- the absence of family photos in Nadeko's album;
- the artificial childishness of her room;
- the parents' pride in her lack of rebellion;
- her delight at being called frightening rather than cute;
- the closet's privacy function;
- the mismatch between public Nadeko and hidden creative practice.

But he repeatedly translates observation into labels like `馬鹿`, `狂っている`, spoiled, overprotected, or emotionally shallow.

Those labels are not TF. They are Kaiki's diagnostic rhetoric.

V13's strongest readings occur where we preserve the observation but contest the totalizing judgment.

## 5.3 His self-explanations are narratively productive but not final motive truth

In §009 Kaiki tests why he might accept Hitagi's request and lands on Kanbaru. That is a highly valuable motive datum because he performs the test privately and takes action immediately afterward. But it does not prove that Kanbaru is his **only** motive.

Other behavior suggests additional pressures:

- Hitagi's vulnerability affects him;
- Hanekawa's trust affects him;
- he becomes attached to the work itself;
- he is bothered by Nadeko's self-contempt;
- he resists Gaen's jurisdiction;
- he enjoys the intellectual problem;
- he likes money;
- he cannot finally abandon people he has allowed to become concrete to him.

The correct V2 formulation is **motive plurality under self-misdescription**, not “Kaiki secretly has a heart of gold.”

## 5.4 The book repeatedly catches Kaiki lying to himself

His most important admission is not that he lies to other people. It is:

`俺はもう自分を騙せなかった` `[§036:P204]`

That line reveals the book's deeper reliability structure. Kaiki can consciously fabricate facts while remaining partly opaque to himself. Conversely, he can recognize something true only after his preferred self-description becomes unsustainable.

The decisive transition is therefore less “liar tells truth” than:

> **the narrator loses the ability to maintain the lie he uses to narrate himself.**

## 5.5 Other narrators remain perspectives, not corrective gods

Hanekawa's statement that Nadeko does not really see anyone is incisive and strongly corroborated by V11/V13 behavior. It is still a character judgment. Hitagi's claim that Kaiki never lied to her is relationship testimony, not universal certification. Araragi's guilt about Nadeko is sincere and still self-centering. Gaen's strategic message comes through Ononoki. Ougi is known only through mediated traces.

V13's narrator substitution therefore confirms V2's Second Season rule:

> **A new narrator changes the evidence field; it does not end interpretation.**

# 6. Chronology map

## 6.1 January 1 — commission and entry

The volume explicitly begins **January 1**. Hitagi's graduation deadline is March 15, and the narration counts 74 days including January 1. Kaiki travels, accepts the job, and begins investigation.

## 6.2 Early January — parents, first Nadeko contact, Hitagi's breakdown

Kaiki interviews the Sengoku household, visits the shrine, judges Nadeko easy to deceive, and reports that confidence to Hitagi. Hitagi leaves the table, cries intensely in private, returns, and thanks him. The crying is not evidence of romantic feeling toward Kaiki; publication-locally it is most securely relief after months of death threat and helplessness.

## 6.3 Early January — Gaen's intervention and closet discovery

Ononoki delivers Gaen's stop order and three million yen. Kaiki lies that he will stop and immediately continues. He later enters Nadeko's room illegally and opens the closet.

## 6.4 January 4–5 range — Hanekawa's return/contact

Hanekawa briefly returns to Japan, meets Kaiki without telling Araragi or Hitagi, exchanges information, asks about Oshino's family, and departs again the next morning according to Kaiki's account. Kaiki himself notes this departure could be another deception.

## 6.5 January — repeated shrine visits

Kaiki visits essentially every day for about a month, spending approximately 300,000 yen in offerings. He and Nadeko establish a routine of `綾取り`, conversation, occasional alcohol, and mundane boredom. `[§032]`

## 6.6 Beginning of February — final operation

The text says January ends and `二月になった。予定日である.` It does not independently provide a calendar numeral beyond that transition. The safest canonical phrasing is **the beginning of February**, very likely the first planned day after January's completed routine, but not over-specified beyond the text.

Kaiki attempts the traffic-accident lie, fails, pivots to the manga argument, removes Nadeko's divine talisman after her acceptance, hands the talisman to Araragi, calls Hitagi, and leaves.

## 6.7 Same departure frame — assault

On the snowy road Kaiki is struck from behind by a middle-school boy whom he previously scammed. The boy invokes `扇さん`. Kaiki loses consciousness under a second attack. The volume does not supply a post-assault recovery scene.

Publication-local state:

> **Kaiki is gravely assaulted and narratively loses consciousness; death is not independently established.**

# 7. Causal plot reconstruction

## 7.1 Hitagi chooses an indirect intervention

The causal chain begins not with Kaiki deciding to rescue anyone, but with Hitagi deciding that the people already inside the crisis cannot safely solve it by themselves. She contacts a man who harmed her, flies to meet him, frames the problem in operational rather than cathartic terms, and asks him to deceive Nadeko before the March 15 deadline.

This matters because V13 is sometimes remembered as “Kaiki saves everyone.” Publication-locally, the intervention is already collaborative before Kaiki accepts it. Hitagi identifies the need, chooses an external actor, supplies the target and deadline, imposes a nonviolence constraint, and continues carrying the Araragi-management problem herself.

She also refuses to make the commission into retroactive forgiveness. She tells Kaiki that what he did to *her* has been filled in by Araragi, but later insists she will continue to hate and resent him. The task is therefore not a redemption ritual in which the victim authorizes the perpetrator as good. It is a pragmatic use of someone whose capabilities fit the crisis.

## 7.2 Kaiki accepts after a motive audit centered on Kanbaru

Kaiki initially treats the request as irrational and personally disadvantageous. He then tests possible motives. The decisive private thought is whether he can help Hitagi and Araragi, and deceive Nadeko, **for Kanbaru Suruga's sake**. His answer is `YESだ`. `[§009]`

That is strong evidence that Tooe/Kanbaru matters to his choice. It does not reduce all later action to one pure motive. Once the operation begins, money, professional pride, curiosity, dislike of Gaen's control, growing familiarity with Nadeko, and Kaiki's own inability to walk away from a concrete person all become relevant.

He charges no ordinary fee, taking only 100,000 yen for expenses. This is evidence against a purely profit-maximizing explanation, not proof of hidden sainthood.

## 7.3 The household investigation discovers a role before it discovers a cause

Kaiki visits the Sengoku home and finds sincerely worried parents rather than obvious monsters. The more troubling evidence is structural.

Their daughter is remembered as exceptionally cute, obedient, and lacking a rebellious phase. Her room is intensely childlike. Her photographs are strikingly solitary. Her parents know little about the interior life that produced either the June curse crisis or current godhood. They respect the closet prohibition because Nadeko told them not to open it, which is a real boundary respected—but they do not know what is behind the boundary.

Kaiki's harsh diagnosis of the parents remains narrator-mediated. The stronger source-secure conclusion is narrower:

> **The household contains affection and concern, but it also materially corroborates the social role “cute good child” that V11 showed Nadeko inhabiting.**

It would be overreach to make the parents the single cause of godhood.

## 7.4 The first shrine encounter disproves several easy Nadeko models

God Nadeko is not continuously enraged. She is bored, lonely, delighted by offerings, eager for diversion, and looking forward with unnerving calm to the promised March killings. She often fails even to retain Hitagi and Shinobu as fully differentiated people.

Kaiki's refusal to call her cute produces one of the first genuinely novel reactions he gets from her. He tells her the snake hair is frightening. Nadeko is delighted because almost everyone else only says `可愛い`.

The scene matters because Kaiki accidentally offers **description without protective idealization**.

Nadeko also refuses a hypothetical return to ordinary humanity even if Araragi's love could be guaranteed. She says she is content with permanent unrequited love:

`片思いをずっと続けられたら──それは両想いよりも幸せだと思わない？貝木さん`

This strongly corroborates V11's thesis that the impossibility of the romance is part of its psychological utility.

## 7.5 Kaiki mistakes credulity for trust

After the first contact Kaiki forms a simple theory: Nadeko is easy to deceive because she does not expect other people to lie in the ordinary way.

His own formulation is more interesting than the insult `馬鹿`. Nadeko neither trusts people normally nor actively suspects them. Her world is closed enough that other people's independent intentions do not receive much weight.

Kaiki converts that observation into a professional prediction:

> Tell Nadeko that Araragi and Hitagi died accidentally; remove the object of the March murder plan; let godhood continue without the target that made it dangerous.

The theory will fail because it assumes that low suspicion creates relational trust. It does not.

## 7.6 Hitagi accepts Kaiki's plan but keeps Araragi as her responsibility

When Kaiki discusses the need to manage Araragi, Hitagi draws a line:

`阿良々木くんを騙すのは、あなたの仕事じゃなくて、私の仕事だ`

and says that if she outsources even that, she can no longer remain his lover.

The statement is not a universal relationship ethic. It is Hitagi's account of her own obligation: asking for help with Nadeko does not permit her to outsource every difficult responsibility created by the plan.

Her private crying after Kaiki reports confidence in success should likewise be read conservatively. The strongest supported interpretation is release after sustained death threat and helplessness. Nothing requires romantic revival with Kaiki.

## 7.7 Gaen tries to stop the operation—and finances it

Ononoki delivers Gaen's command: `手を引け`.

The rationale is systemic. Nadeko's godhood has become part of a provisional equilibrium in the town; intervention could make things worse, and failure could permit Nadeko to destroy the town. Gaen offers Kaiki three million yen to withdraw and threatens to sever their senior/junior relationship if he refuses.

Kaiki says he will stop.

Then immediately narrates that he has no intention of stopping.

The three million yen therefore becomes one of V13's most characteristic material jokes: money offered to halt a fraudster's intervention becomes operating capital for continuing it.

In §039 Kaiki wonders whether Gaen knows him so well that the entire stop-order/payment may have been reverse psychology or covert funding. The text does not verify that. It remains an important UA because it would radically alter how much of Gaen's apparent opposition was genuine.

## 7.8 Kaiki discovers the closet before he understands it

Kaiki illegally enters Nadeko's room and violates the boundary her parents had respected. He opens the closet and discovers its hidden contents.

Crucially, he does **not** immediately recognize salvation or authentic selfhood there. He initially classifies the contents as irrelevant and unpleasant.

That protects V13 from an overly neat providential reading. The manga does not announce itself as a destiny-object. Its significance becomes legible only after the planned deception collapses.

## 7.9 The anonymous warning exposes Kaiki's own overinterpretation

A note reading `手を引け` appears under Kaiki's hotel door. Kaiki imagines elaborate intrusion. Hitagi immediately points out the banal mechanism: the note could simply have been pushed beneath the door.

The scene is comic but methodologically useful:

> **The professional skeptic is still capable of overfitting complexity.**

Later Kaiki infers that Hitagi herself wrote the warning precisely because she knew telling him to stop would make him continue. The internal logic is strong, but the book still gives us Kaiki's inference rather than independent confirmation.

## 7.10 Hanekawa supplies a different model of Nadeko

Hanekawa secretly returns from overseas and meets Kaiki. Her key diagnosis is not that Nadeko is shy, quiet, passive, or cute. It is:

`相手にされてない`

and then:

`あの子の世界は、徹底的に閉じている`

`あの子は誰のことも見ていません`

This is not omniscient truth. It is a character judgment from someone with strong reasons to understand self-separation and closed internal systems. V11 and Kaiki's later failure strongly corroborate it.

Hanekawa also refuses the demand that everyone must be saved simultaneously. The homicide crisis can be stopped first; Nadeko's longer recovery can remain future work.

This is an important rescue principle:

> **Triage is not abandonment.**

## 7.11 Hanekawa destabilizes Ougi's claimed genealogy

Hanekawa asks Kaiki whether Oshino Meme has siblings and specifically whether he could have a niece. Kaiki says that, to the best of his knowledge, Oshino has no brother, sister, or ordinary family from which such a niece would arise.

Because Kaiki is the narrator, this remains strong contradictory testimony rather than settled TF. But publication-locally it materially destabilizes Ougi's claimed family position.

Hanekawa pays him 500 yen for keeping the inquiry secret, neatly reproducing Kaiki's preferred transactional grammar even inside a cooperative information exchange.

## 7.12 January routine creates familiarity, not trust

Kaiki visits Nadeko essentially every day through January. He spends roughly 300,000 yen in offerings. They play `綾取り`; eventually they play it together. He brings toys. They talk. Sometimes she drinks sake. He admits that he enjoys seeing her cheerful.

This month matters because it blocks an easy explanation of the final failure. Kaiki does not fail because he rushed the target. He invests substantial time and creates real familiarity.

But familiarity is not the same as relational admission.

Nadeko can enjoy Kaiki while still not allowing his words enough authority for the decisive lie.

## 7.13 The masterpiece deception fails immediately

At the beginning of February Kaiki delivers the carefully prepared lie:

Araragi and Hitagi died in a traffic accident.

Nadeko's answer is immediate:

`貝木さんも『私』を騙すんだね`

The planned intervention has failed.

Kaiki recognizes that the underlying model was wrong. Nadeko did not trust him. More precisely, she did not grant him enough independent reality inside her closed world for his story to overwrite hers.

Her reaction is also not simple berserk rage. The landscape fills with snakes while she remains eerily calm. Kaiki explicitly notes that calling it `暴走` is inaccurate.

## 7.14 Nadeko's god-self supplies a totalizing rescue story

Nadeko argues that because she cannot escape being someone she hates, she must become a godlike person capable of loving any version of herself:

`大嫌いな自分でも愛せるような──どんな自分でも愛せるような、神様みたいな人になるしか、ないじゃない`

The statement contains genuine self-analysis. It also converts accidental godhood into a retrospective destiny-story.

Kaiki attacks that conversion. Nadeko did not choose godhood as a long project of self-transformation; she swallowed a talisman during crisis and then built meaning around the result.

The V2 point is not that her account is simply false. It is that **explanatory usefulness does not equal causal origin**.

## 7.15 Ougi moves from shadow to causal trace

During the failed operation Nadeko says there is definitely an element of `扇さんのせい` in how she reached the talisman/door/key sequence.

This is the strongest publication-local bridge yet between Ougi and Nadeko's escalation. It does not by itself establish Ougi's ontology or complete purpose.

V13 then adds a second trace in the epilogue when Kaiki's attacker says `扇さん` told him Kaiki had returned.

The repeated pattern is now difficult to dismiss:

> **Ougi supplies information or prompts that help hidden grievance reach a point of action.**

Intent and ontology remain open.

## 7.16 Kaiki fails to abandon Nadeko because he can no longer maintain his own self-story

Kaiki considers simply pretending to lose consciousness and letting the entire situation proceed without him.

Then:

`俺はもう自分を騙せなかった`

This is the hinge of the volume.

His professional identity as fraudster remains intact. What fails is the self-description that permits total detachment.

Immediately afterward his mode of address changes. He calls the god simply:

`千石`

not as target, goddess, monster, or abstract victim, but as a middle-school girl whose future is being consumed by one relationship-story.

## 7.17 The manga supplies plurality, not one hidden true self

Kaiki reveals that he has seen the hidden manga notebooks. Nadeko's shame becomes physical violence. He responds with a mixture of cruelty, insight, exaggeration, and encouragement.

His strongest useful statement is:

`創作は恥ずかしいものだし、それに夢も恥ずかしいものだ`

His weakest is the claim that because she told no one, manga must be her `本当の夢`.

The evidence supports a narrower conclusion. Manga is:

- sustained private labor;
- intensely shame-laden;
- unknown even to her parents;
- independent of Araragi;
- aesthetically imperfect but visibly skilled;
- proof that Nadeko possesses desires and practices her romantic self-story does not exhaust.

So the manga matters not because it reveals one secret “real Nadeko.” It matters because it **breaks the monopoly** of the Araragi narrative.

## 7.18 Kaiki's money philosophy becomes anti-totalization

Kaiki explains why he likes money:

money matters enormously, but it is replaceable.

He distrusts the rhetoric of `かけがえのない`—the single irreplaceable thing without which life cannot continue.

This is not a universal ethics of fungibility. Love, people, and works are not therefore equivalent to currency.

In this scene, however, the doctrine is precisely useful because Nadeko has collapsed her future into one supposedly irreplaceable object: `暦お兄ちゃん`.

Kaiki asks whether Araragi is literally all that matters: school, magazines, movies, parents, drawing, other pleasures, future work.

Nadeko answers no.

That `違うよ` is the opening through which the intervention can work.

## 7.19 The future is restored through revisability

Kaiki says:

`その夢は破れた`

about the Araragi dream, then asks why every other possible dream should die with it.

He tells Nadeko:

`お前のことはお前しか、大切にできないんだぜ`

`そしてお前の夢も、お前にしか叶えられない`

When Nadeko asks whether it is acceptable to abandon one path and try another, Kaiki answers:

`いいんだよ、人間なんだから`

`人間は...いくらでもやり直せる`

This is the mature intervention. It does not promise success. It restores **revisability**.

Nadeko explicitly says she never previously thought “I want to be a manga artist.” Kaiki accepts that she may fail or may never become one. The point is not destiny.

The point is that another future can be attempted.

## 7.20 `騙されてあげる`: deception becomes knowingly entered uncertainty

Kaiki concludes:

`騙されたと思って、チャレンジしてみな`

Nadeko replies:

`わかった。騙されてあげる`

This is the conceptual climax of *Koimonogatari*.

The successful “deception” differs completely from the failed traffic-accident lie.

The first deception asks Nadeko to accept a false fact without agency.

The second asks Nadeko to **knowingly risk a future that may not pay off**.

She agrees.

Thus V13's deepest rehabilitation of deception is not “lies are good.” It is:

> **A provisional fiction can become ethically productive when the person entering it knows it may fail and retains authorship over whether to try.**

## 7.21 The fake oddity works because Nadeko accepts it

When Araragi arrives, Kaiki explains the physical resolution through `蛞蝓豆腐`, explicitly calling it a fabricated, fake oddity.

But he adds the crucial condition:

`千石撫子自身に、ぬらりと生きる蛞蝓を受け入れる気持ちがなければ、こうも括抗はしなかった`

The fake mechanism works because Nadeko has acquired the internal disposition to accept it.

Kaiki then reaches into her mouth/body and removes Gaen's talisman. Her hair, clothes, and body return to the pre-god state.

The resolution is therefore neither:

- Kaiki's lie alone;
- Nadeko saving herself alone;
- Araragi's rescue;
- nor pure exorcistic technique.

It is collaborative causation:

> **Kaiki supplies diagnosis, language, and a fabricated occult frame; Nadeko supplies acceptance; the talisman is physically removed; Araragi carries the returned girl home.**

## 7.22 Araragi is told to become memory

Kaiki's most controversial post-resolution instruction is:

`さっさと思い出になってやれ`

Araragi answers that responsibility requires continued involvement because Nadeko's crisis was partly caused by him.

Kaiki rejects this. His situated argument is that Araragi's continued centrality will keep Nadeko's identity organized around him.

He contrasts two relationships:

- Hitagi became stronger in relation with Araragi;
- Nadeko becomes more trapped by him.

This is not a universal claim that Araragi is harmful. It is a relational diagnosis:

> **The same person's care can be developmental in one relationship and obstructive in another.**

## 7.23 Happiness is decentered from the purpose of life

Araragi asks whether Nadeko will be happy without him.

Kaiki answers:

`別に幸せになることが、人間の生きる目的じゃあない`

`幸せになれなくとも、なりたいもんになれりゃいい`

and finally:

`生きてりゃそのうち、いいことあるんじゃねえのかよ？`

Kaiki calls his own answer casual. Its very casualness matters. He does not replace the impossible romance with another guaranteed salvation story.

He offers continuation without guarantee.

## 7.24 The ending refuses redemptive closure

Hitagi thanks Kaiki:

`ありがとうございます。助かりました`

Then they end contact.

She does not forgive him.

Kaiki infers that Hitagi wrote the warning letter precisely because she knew he would react perversely to `手を引け`. He also suspects Gaen's three million yen may have functioned similarly. Neither inference becomes independent fact.

Then the story imposes a material remainder.

A former scam victim attacks Kaiki with an iron pipe. The boy says Ougi told him Kaiki had returned. Kaiki perceives a snake around him and wonders whether old curse causality has returned.

The volume closes with Kaiki losing consciousness.

That ending blocks the easy “fraudster redeemed through one good deed” structure:

> **Past harm remains capable of striking the helper after the successful intervention.**

# 8. Oddity dossiers

## 8.1 God Nadeko / current snake deity

**Status:** real supernatural god-state, not merely narration or fantasy.

**Material cause:** ingestion of Gaen's divine talisman in V11.

**Psychological/narrative layer:** Nadeko constructs meanings around godhood—self-love, permanent unrequited love, freedom from ordinary adolescence—that are real as self-story without necessarily being the causal origin of the god-state.

**V13 condition:** stable enough that Gaen regards the town as provisionally balanced, but catastrophic if destabilized unsuccessfully.

**Key distinction from V11:** the first Kuchinawa voice in V11 was self-generated. By V13 the divine body is materially real.

## 8.2 Gaen's talisman

The talisman remains the central material carrier of Nadeko's godhood. Kaiki physically extracts it after Nadeko's acceptance shifts. Her body immediately reverts to its pre-deification form.

This is strong evidence that the god-state is not only psychological narrative.

## 8.3 `蛞蝓豆腐`

Kaiki explicitly describes the slug-tofu oddity as:

`でっち上げられた、偽物の怪異`

Yet it functions.

This is one of the strongest fake/real cases since *Nisemonogatari* because the book gives us both halves at once:

- the oddity's genealogy is fabricated;
- its intervention has real efficacy.

But efficacy is conditional:

> Nadeko has to possess `受け入れる気持ち`.

Thus the fake does not dominate her from outside. The false frame becomes operational because her internal state has changed enough to receive it.

## 8.4 The snake around Kaiki's attacker

Kaiki perceives a large snake coiling around the middle-school boy who attacks him. He suspects possible relation to the earlier curse network around Nadeko and the boys who sought revenge through snake curses.

The source does not establish the exact ontology.

**Classification:** UA.

Do not turn the assault into supernatural “karma” merely because Kaiki experiences snake imagery around it.

## 8.5 Ougi's role

Ougi does not appear as a conventional monster or specialist in the present action. Instead, V13 gives two strong mediated traces:

1. Nadeko says Ougi materially contributed to her reaching the talisman.
2. Kaiki's attacker says Ougi told him Kaiki had returned.

The safest current formulation is:

> **Ougi is an information/intervention node who appears to help latent grievance or contradiction reach action.**

Whether this is a supernatural function, deliberate moral project, personal animus, or something else remains unresolved.

# 9. Character-pressure updates

## 9.1 Kaiki Deishuu — the fraudster whose most important failed deception is self-deception

V13 does not reveal Kaiki as secretly good.

It reveals a more difficult person:

- he lies deliberately;
- cheats Gaen;
- invades Nadeko's home;
- violates her closet privacy;
- humiliates her;
- uses money instrumentally;
- withholds information;
- fabricates occult mechanisms;
- remains capable of cruelty and victim-blaming;
- and is attacked by someone he previously harmed.

At the same time:

- he refuses to let one failed romance consume a child's entire future;
- he sees something in her that her protectors did not;
- he continues despite little financial incentive;
- he accepts bodily risk;
- he gives away the shrine money for art materials;
- and when the elegant professional lie fails, he improvises from genuine attention.

The central change is not moral purification. It is:

> **Kaiki's practiced detachment becomes less credible to Kaiki himself once Nadeko ceases to be an abstract target.**

## 9.2 Sengoku Nadeko — vulnerability, evasion, desire, godhood, and surplus selfhood

V13 strengthens the V11 refusal to divide Nadeko into innocent fake self versus angry real self.

She is simultaneously:

- socially avoidant;
- sincerely vulnerable;
- self-protective;
- possessive;
- capable of deception;
- capable of violence;
- bored;
- lonely;
- theatrically divine;
- genuinely attached to Araragi;
- largely uninterested in Araragi as a full person;
- creative;
- ashamed of her creativity;
- and capable of accepting an uncertain future.

The crucial new evidence is the manga corpus.

It does **not** reveal “the true Nadeko.”

It demonstrates that the romantic/god story never contained the whole person.

## 9.3 Senjougahara Hitagi — strategic vulnerability without absolution

Hitagi's agency is unusually strong throughout V13.

She:

- identifies her limits;
- chooses an outside specialist;
- negotiates terms;
- tolerates dependence on someone she hates;
- protects Araragi from a plan he might disrupt;
- carries the emotional burden of managing him herself;
- cries when credible hope appears;
- expresses gratitude when the job succeeds;
- and refuses to reinterpret the past as forgiven.

Her most important moral achievement may be the ability to say both:

> **You helped me.**

and:

> **I still hate what you did.**

without requiring either statement to cancel the other.

## 9.4 Araragi Koyomi — responsibility can become continued centrality

Araragi arrives too late to author the solution.

His instinct after the crisis is nevertheless familiar: because he contributed to Nadeko's suffering, he believes responsibility requires continued involvement.

V13 forces a new distinction:

> **Sometimes the responsible act is not further intervention but relinquishing the position from which another person's life keeps being organized around you.**

This should not be generalized mechanically. Kaiki's injunction to avoid Nadeko forever is a situated judgment, not universal law.

## 9.5 Hanekawa Tsubasa — triage without ownership

Hanekawa is globally mobile now, but she remains capable of targeted intervention. She returns, supplies Kaiki with information, asks the Ougi/Oshino question, and accepts that Nadeko's homicide crisis and Nadeko's complete human flourishing need not be solved in the same act.

This is a mature extension of V08:

> **Help need not claim total solution.**

## 9.6 Gaen Izuko — systemic stabilization can conflict with person-level rescue

Gaen's objection is intelligible. The town has a provisional spiritual equilibrium, and Kaiki's failed intervention could destroy it.

But her frame is system-level.

Hitagi and Kaiki are concerned with specific threatened people.

V13 therefore sharpens a recurring specialist conflict:

> **The intervention that best stabilizes a system may not be the intervention that best preserves a particular person's authorship.**

## 9.7 Ononoki Yotsugi — messenger and partial participant

Ononoki appears primarily as Gaen's messenger. Her information is bounded by what Gaen asked her to transmit. This keeps V13 from turning her into an omniscient mouthpiece.

Her continued use of `貝木お兄ちゃん` also gives her speech an oddly relational register even while she is delivering institutional pressure.

## 9.8 Oshino Ougi — increasingly visible through effects on other people's stories

Ougi's direct ontology remains hidden, but the pattern of effects grows stronger.

Ougi's information intersects with:

- Nadeko's transformation;
- Kaiki's return;
- a prior victim's revenge.

V13 therefore moves Ougi from peripheral curiosity toward a major unresolved causal actor.

# 10. Relationship-state updates

## 10.1 Hitagi ↔ Kaiki — gratitude without forgiveness

This relationship should not be simplified into either:

- “Hitagi still loves Kaiki,” or
- “Hitagi feels nothing toward Kaiki.”

V13 supports a more disciplined state:

- long history;
- real prior harm;
- possible historical romantic ambiguity;
- present strategic trust in competence;
- willingness to become vulnerable in front of him;
- gratitude for successful help;
- continuing hatred and refusal of absolution;
- desire for final separation.

The book's title `ひたぎエンド` partly describes this closure without proving that the relational past was emotionally empty.

## 10.2 Kaiki ↔ Nadeko — target → routine companion → failed deceiver → disruptive recognizer

The relationship develops through several states:

1. professional target;
2. god accepting offerings;
3. daily play/talk routine;
4. object of a planned falsehood;
5. person whose lack of trust defeats the plan;
6. middle-school girl addressed as `千石`;
7. creator whose shame becomes visible;
8. person who knowingly agrees to be “deceived” into trying another future.

This is not friendship in a conventional sense. It is nevertheless enough relationship to destabilize Kaiki's original detached method.

## 10.3 Nadeko ↔ Araragi — from beloved person toward monopolizing role

V13 further weakens the interpretation that Nadeko knows Araragi deeply.

Her tiny list of people to kill around him, her poor differentiation of people he values, and Hanekawa's critique all indicate that `暦お兄ちゃん` functions heavily as an internal role.

The affection itself can still be real.

The problem is:

> **real feeling can attach to an insufficiently known person.**

Kaiki's proposed solution is therefore not “stop feeling.” It is to stop allowing that role to monopolize the future.

## 10.4 Hitagi ↔ Araragi — secrecy as burdened care

Hitagi conceals the Kaiki plan because she expects Araragi to interfere.

This is strategically effective and paternalistic at once.

Her insistence that deceiving/managing Araragi remains *her* job shows that she experiences the secrecy not as cost-free manipulation but as part of her own relational obligation.

The ethical verdict remains mixed.

## 10.5 Hanekawa ↔ Kaiki — functional trust across incompatible moral styles

Their conversation works because neither requires the other to become morally similar.

Hanekawa gives information because Kaiki is presently the actor most likely to use it effectively. Kaiki answers her Ougi/Oshino question and preserves secrecy for a nominal fee.

This is cooperation without intimacy or ideological convergence.

## 10.6 Gaen ↔ Kaiki — hierarchy contested by perversity

Gaen attempts to govern Kaiki through professional seniority, money, threat, and information.

Kaiki's perversity makes ordinary command unreliable: `手を引け` can function as motivation rather than restraint.

Whether Gaen exploits that knowingly remains open.

## 10.7 Ougi ↔ Kaiki — indirect adversarial relation

By the ending, Ougi has apparently supplied another person with the information required to attack Kaiki.

Kaiki and Ougi therefore become causally linked without having needed a conventional direct confrontation.

# 11. Rescue/intervention audit

## 11.1 Who initiates rescue?

Hitagi.

She recognizes that neither she nor Araragi can safely solve the crisis by remaining its only agents.

## 11.2 Who performs the central intervention?

Kaiki.

But “Kaiki saves Nadeko” is incomplete.

The final causal structure includes:

- Hitagi's commission and risk;
- Kaiki's investigation;
- Nadeko's month of contact with him;
- Hanekawa's diagnostic information;
- Kaiki's discovery of the manga;
- the failure of his original lie;
- Nadeko's eventual willingness to risk another future;
- the fake slug-oddity frame;
- physical talisman extraction;
- Araragi's arrival and transport home.

This is distributed rescue.

## 11.3 Why does the first intervention fail?

Because it tries to change Nadeko's world **without granting Nadeko meaningful authorship over the change**.

The traffic-accident lie removes Araragi from the story by fiat.

Nadeko does not accept it.

## 11.4 Why does the second intervention work?

Because the second proposal does not require Nadeko to believe a false factual report.

It asks her whether she is willing to treat one failed dream as non-total and attempt something else despite uncertainty.

She explicitly agrees.

This makes `騙されてあげる` substantially more agency-respecting than the professional con Kaiki originally planned.

## 11.5 Does Kaiki pass the non-possession test?

Partially.

He does not ask Nadeko to become attached to him. He gives her money and leaves. He tells Araragi not to turn rescue into renewed centrality.

But his method also includes serious autonomy violations:

- illegal home entry;
- opening the private closet;
- humiliating exposure;
- physical manipulation of her body;
- withholding information;
- and a highly paternalistic adult/child lecture.

The intervention is effective.

It is not ethically clean.

## 11.6 What does Araragi's “responsibility” get wrong?

His instinct assumes that because he was part of the causal problem, continued presence must be part of the repair.

Kaiki proposes the opposite:

> **Responsibility can sometimes require removing oneself from the role another person cannot stop organizing herself around.**

This is one of the strongest non-possession corrections to Araragi so far.

## 11.7 V13's rescue principle

> **The goal of rescue is not to make the rescued person grateful, happy, attached to the rescuer, or permanently compliant. It is to restore enough plurality that one catastrophic story no longer controls every available future.**

# 12. Specialist knowledge and metaphysical claims

## 12.1 Kaiki's competence remains real despite fraudulence

V05 already established that being a fake specialist does not make every technique fake in consequence.

V13 strengthens this.

Kaiki can:

- investigate supernatural cases;
- recognize talisman mechanics;
- use fabricated oddity stories functionally;
- negotiate with a deity;
- understand specialist networks;
- and complete a dangerous extraction.

Yet his professional confidence is also fallible. His main deception fails because his psychology of Nadeko is wrong.

Competence remains claim-specific and domain-specific.

## 12.2 Gaen's town-level model

Gaen's intervention implies:

- the town has a broader spiritual ecology;
- Nadeko's godhood participates in a provisional equilibrium;
- specialist action is partly governance, not merely case treatment;
- a person can become institutionally useful in a state that is personally disastrous.

The last point matters most.

> **Systemic stability is not proof of person-level justice.**

## 12.3 The intended shrine-god plan remains only partly visible

Kaiki interprets Gaen's comments as implying that Shinobu was originally intended for the god role now occupied by Nadeko. This fits the larger specialist architecture but should remain tied to the actual chain of testimony rather than promoted beyond it.

## 12.4 The fake `蛞蝓豆腐` and recipient acceptance

The fabricated oddity is a major metaphysical data point.

Its functional efficacy appears to depend on:

1. a symbolically compatible frame (`三すくみ` snake/slug logic);
2. Kaiki's deliberate construction;
3. Nadeko's changed willingness to accept that frame;
4. the already-existing divine talisman that can be extracted once the balance shifts.

This does not prove that any sufficiently believed fiction becomes supernatural fact. The local conditions are too specific.

## 12.5 Ougi's causal information role

Ougi is implicated through information rather than direct occult display.

This means V13 cannot yet settle whether Ougi should be classified primarily as:

- specialist;
- oddity;
- correction mechanism;
- manipulator;
- social actor;
- or some combination.

## 12.6 Specialist ethics after V13

The emerging comparison is now sharper:

- **Oshino:** balance, negotiated burden, boundaries, partial solutions.
- **Kaiki:** deception, transaction, diagnostic provocation, anti-irreplaceability.
- **Kagenui:** ontological classification followed by extermination logic.
- **Gaen:** system stabilization, information asymmetry, strategic governance.

These are not merely four skill sets.

They are **four different theories of what knowledge authorizes the knower to do**.

# 13. Japanese voice and address

## 13.1 Kaiki's `俺` and the rhetoric of transactional adulthood

Kaiki's narration is built around `俺`, declarative judgments, money vocabulary, professional self-description, and direct address to the reader. He frequently reduces emotional questions into prices, debts, contracts, fees, replacement value, and transaction.

That register is not merely comic cynicism. It is one of the ways he resists the absolutist emotional vocabularies surrounding Araragi's circle.

Where other characters say:

- precious;
- irreplaceable;
- true love;
- responsibility;
- salvation;
- destiny;

Kaiki asks:

- what does it cost?
- who pays?
- what replaces it?
- what result is actually required?

The limit is obvious: not everything important is fungible. The analytical value lies in the pressure the vocabulary puts on totalizing sentiment.

## 13.2 Hitagi's precision and retained distance

Hitagi continues to use `私` and to name Araragi as `阿良々木くん`. Toward Kaiki she maintains a comparatively bare, adversarial address structure. Her speech shifts between:

- controlled negotiation;
- old hostility;
- tactical questioning;
- emotional fracture;
- and formal gratitude.

The final `ありがとうございます。助かりました` is especially significant because politeness does not imply reconciliation. It gives successful help its proper name without rewriting the relationship.

## 13.3 Nadeko's movement among `撫子`, `私`, and quoted selfhood

V11 already made self-reference part of Nadeko's agency problem. V13 gives an especially revealing line when Kaiki's traffic-death lie fails:

`貝木さんも『私』を騙すんだね`

The quotation marks around `私` deserve preservation.

They do not prove a fully stable new identity. They show that the narratorially available Nadeko can mark “I/me” as something under examination, not merely fall automatically into the childlike third-person `撫子` register.

God Nadeko still uses `撫子` elsewhere. V2 should therefore resist turning pronoun selection into a simple maturity ladder.

Better:

> **Nadeko's self-reference becomes one of the places where competing self-stories are audible.**

## 13.4 `千石` as Kaiki's decisive address shift

During most of the operation Kaiki can treat her as `千石撫子`, god, target, idiot, or case.

At the hinge where he can no longer abandon her, he addresses her simply:

`千石`

The surname-only call is not warm in a conventional affectionate register. That is precisely why it works.

It marks a shift from categorical narration to direct person-address.

The intervention begins succeeding when Kaiki stops speaking *about* Nadeko as a type and starts speaking *to* Sengoku as a particular girl.

## 13.5 Hanekawa's polite distance

Hanekawa addresses Kaiki as `貝木さん`, maintains controlled politeness, and negotiates information without pretending moral closeness.

Her speech illustrates a recurring series principle:

> **Formal distance can coexist with genuine cooperation.**

## 13.6 Ononoki's `貝木お兄ちゃん`

Ononoki's `僕` and `貝木お兄ちゃん` create deliberate friction between her doll-like messenger role and familial-sounding address.

V13 does not make that address into actual kinship. It is part of Ononoki's performed relational register and the odd intimacy of the specialist network.

# 14. Names, wordplay, rubies, and translation-sensitive passages

## 14.1 『恋物語』 and 「ひたぎエンド」

`恋物語` is not merely “romance story.” `恋` can name desire, longing, infatuation, one-sided attachment, and the condition of being romantically oriented toward someone without guaranteeing mutual union.

That makes it an unusually apt title for a volume where:

- Nadeko prefers permanent `片思い`;
- Hitagi and Araragi's relationship is being protected indirectly;
- Kaiki alludes to his own unresolved attachment to a dead woman;
- Kaiki and Hitagi negotiate a past that may contain romantic ambiguity without returning to romance;
- and the successful intervention asks Nadeko to let one love cease being total.

`ひたぎエンド` carries several overlapping functions:

- a named arc ending;
- Hitagi's threatened “end” being prevented;
- the apparent end of Hitagi and Kaiki's renewed contact;
- route/ending vocabulary familiar from games and romance fiction;
- and Kaiki's explicit `ジ・エンド` in the final call.

The title sounds final while the book repeatedly demonstrates that people continue after an “end.”

## 14.2 `騙す` / `騙されてあげる`

This is the volume's key lexical pair.

`騙す` normally places agency with the deceiver.

Nadeko's:

`騙されてあげる`

reverses the grammar. The benefactive `〜てあげる` makes being deceived something she will **do for / permit in relation to** Kaiki.

She knows the proposed future may be wrong.

The phrase therefore transforms deception from unilateral epistemic domination into knowingly accepted experiment.

No English rendering should flatten that agency reversal.

## 14.3 `本当の夢`

Kaiki calls the manga Nadeko's `本当の夢`.

This is rhetorically powerful and evidentially excessive.

The volume itself gives Nadeko the counterevidence: she says she had not actually thought “I want to become a manga artist.”

Therefore `本当の夢` should be quoted as **Kaiki's intervention language**, not adopted as neutral narrator truth.

## 14.4 `かけがえのない` and `代わり`

Kaiki's money philosophy turns on replacement.

Money is valuable partly because it can stand in for other money. He distrusts the romantic elevation of the irreplaceable.

Translation should preserve the tension between:

- `かけがえのない` — irreplaceable, uniquely precious;
- and `代わり` — substitute/replacement/standing-in.

The moral claim is not “people are replaceable.” The scene attacks the dangerous proposition that **one object of desire must be the sole condition of continued life**.

## 14.5 `今している恋が常に初恋`

Kaiki invokes a formulation associated with Hitagi:

> the love one is in now is always first love.

The statement matters because it refuses two opposite errors:

- later love does not prove earlier feeling was fake;
- earlier feeling does not permanently own the future.

It is a theory of **serial authenticity**.

## 14.6 `思い出になってやれ`

Kaiki's instruction to Araragi is difficult to translate without losing the benefactive aggression of `〜てやれ`.

He is not merely saying “become a memory.”

He is saying, roughly:

> **do her the favor of becoming a memory.**

The phrasing turns absence into an active contribution rather than passive abandonment.

## 14.7 `助かりました`

Hitagi's final gratitude uses the same broader `助かる` field that has structured the series' rescue language since V01.

She does not say Kaiki authored her salvation in an absolute sense. She says his action helped / saved / made the situation workable.

This is another reason V2 should preserve bounded rescue rather than the binary “people only save themselves” doctrine.

## 14.8 `手を引け`

The repeated imperative means withdraw, back off, stop participating.

It becomes a linguistic mechanism through which different people attempt to control Kaiki:

- Gaen uses it directly;
- the anonymous letter repeats it;
- Kaiki's perverse psychology converts it into motivation.

The phrase's meaning therefore depends not only on semantic content but on **who knows how Kaiki responds to being told to stop**.

## 14.9 `親交` / `信仰`

V13 repeatedly places ordinary social relation beside shrine/god vocabulary. The closeness of `親交` (social intimacy/association) and `信仰` (faith) is part of the book's comic-religious texture even where not formalized as a single explicit pun.

Nadeko receives offerings and worship, but Kaiki's intervention increasingly depends on the failure of worship and the emergence of ordinary interpersonal contact.

## 14.10 `百度参り`, `賽銭`, and commercialized devotion

Kaiki's daily shrine visits turn religious practice into transactional routine. Offerings become both literal shrine money and the eventual capital for art supplies.

The transformation is materially elegant:

> **money spent maintaining a god is redirected toward financing a human future.**

## 14.11 “manga god” language

Nadeko's:

`漫画を描いて、神様って呼ばれた人もいたよね`

creates one of V13's cleanest semantic bridges.

She can cease being a literal god and still imagine becoming “godlike” through craft—a human honorific produced by work rather than ontological escape.

Do not overread this as a guaranteed career prophecy. It is a witty redirection of the same grandiosity into an ordinary practice.

# 15. Body, appetite, sexuality, comedy, and materiality

## 15.1 Money is bodily and material throughout the intervention

V13 repeatedly refuses to let abstract rescue float free of material support.

The operation has:

- travel costs;
- hotel costs;
- 100,000 yen expenses;
- Gaen's three million yen;
- roughly 300,000 yen in shrine offerings;
- money finally handed to Nadeko for art materials.

The flow of cash maps changing purpose.

What begins as professional operating money and devotional money ends as practical support for pencils, paper, tools, and future work.

## 15.2 Shrine offerings become relationship ritual

Kaiki's 10,000-yen offerings are partly strategic, partly transactional, partly comic, and eventually part of the daily rhythm through which Nadeko anticipates his visits.

The money never becomes pure affection. It becomes one of the media through which familiarity forms.

## 15.3 Alcohol and god-body comedy

Kaiki sometimes gives Nadeko alcohol during the January routine. The scene plays on her apparent middle-school body, divine status, and Kaiki's disregard for ordinary decorum.

The comedy should not be used to erase the age/power asymmetry. Nadeko remains a fourteen-year-old person even while occupying a god-state.

## 15.4 `綾取り` and snake-form grotesquerie

String figures become the surprisingly ordinary center of the month. The image is especially apt because Nadeko herself is surrounded by snake imagery: tangled line, loops, forms, and mutually held strings become harmless play where snake-form imagery elsewhere represents curses and overwhelming power.

The progression from solitary to two-person `綾取り` is a small material sign that interaction has become reciprocal enough to sustain shared play—even though, importantly, not reciprocal enough to support Kaiki's decisive lie.

## 15.5 Nadeko's violence against Kaiki

When Kaiki exposes the manga, Nadeko hits him from shame and eventually kicks because her fists hurt.

The scene is comic, but it also marks how physically unbearable exposure of the creative self feels to her.

She could calmly threaten mass killing as a god; being seen as an embarrassing amateur creator provokes immediate bodily panic.

That asymmetry is psychologically revealing.

## 15.6 The talisman extraction is effective and invasive

Kaiki inserts his hand into Nadeko's mouth/body and extracts the talisman.

The procedure has technical justification inside the supernatural mechanics and follows her changed willingness to accept the proposed future.

It is still physically invasive.

V2 should not allow successful outcome to erase bodily ethics.

## 15.7 The body returns before the social problem is solved

After extraction Nadeko's:

- hair;
- clothing;
- apparent body-state

return to the pre-god configuration.

But returning the body does not instantly solve:

- family dynamics;
- school absence;
- shame;
- romance fixation;
- responsibility for violence;
- or future identity.

Once again:

> **oddity resolution is not human-problem resolution.**

## 15.8 Kaiki's final body absorbs historical consequence

The iron-pipe assault prevents Kaiki's body from exiting the story untouched by his past.

The scene should not be moralized into cosmic justice without evidence. It is enough that a person he harmed has remained in the world long enough for harm to return socially and physically.

# 16. Family, home, school, institutions, and social context

## 16.1 The Sengoku home: room without recognized interiority

V07 gave Hanekawa the inverse problem: a human girl living in a house without a room that materially belonged to her.

Nadeko has a room.

But the room itself is heavily organized around the cute-child role her family recognizes.

The comparison is useful:

> **Having private space does not guarantee being known inside it.**

Hanekawa lacked a room.

Nadeko has a room containing a self nobody knows is there.

## 16.2 Parents who care but do not understand

The Sengoku parents are not reducible to abuse. They are frightened by their missing daughter and respect a stated privacy boundary around the closet.

At the same time, pride in “no rebellious phase” and the sustained cute-child image suggest a family environment in which conflict has not become a normal language of individuation.

The safest conclusion is:

> **care and misrecognition coexist.**

## 16.3 School as missing developmental world

Nadeko's school life remains structurally important even while much of it is offstage.

V11 showed the disastrous class situation and the adult delegation of social repair onto her. V13's manga intervention implicitly restores a future in which school, adolescence, ordinary embarrassment, and development can resume rather than being bypassed through godhood.

## 16.4 Gaen as quasi-government

Gaen's concern is not merely one client's welfare. She manages the town as a supernatural system.

That makes the specialist network resemble governance:

- information control;
- allocation of roles;
- maintenance of equilibrium;
- use of money;
- use of intermediaries;
- decisions about acceptable risk.

The moral problem is familiar from institutions generally:

> **a system can prefer a stable arrangement that a particular person has good reason to reject.**

## 16.5 Informal rescue fills gaps left by ordinary institutions

Nadeko is a missing middle-school girl who has become a deity and threatens multiple homicides. Yet the decisive actors are not school counselors, police, doctors, or formal child-welfare institutions.

The narrative's occult social world handles the crisis through:

- former victims;
- lovers;
- freelance specialists;
- information brokers;
- family;
- and friends.

This does not prove ordinary institutions are absent from the world. It does show that the series' primary intervention infrastructure remains extra-institutional.

## 16.6 Money as institutional coordination

The volume's money flows also show that material resources coordinate informal governance:

- Hitagi pays expenses;
- Gaen pays for withdrawal;
- Kaiki spends offerings;
- the offering pool becomes art capital.

Money does not explain motive completely.

It makes action possible.

# 17. Themes

## 17.1 Lie and truth

V13's mature claim is not that truth is good and lies are bad, nor that lies are secretly superior.

Instead:

> **truthfulness, factuality, sincerity, utility, and ethical legitimacy are separate dimensions.**

The traffic-accident lie is skillfully constructed and fails.

The exaggerated claim that manga is Nadeko's “true dream” is factually too strong yet helps open a viable future.

The fake slug oddity has fabricated genealogy and real efficacy.

Kaiki's self-description as detached fraudster becomes less truthful than some of the explicit lies he tells other people.

## 17.2 Irreplaceability and plurality

Nadeko's crisis has become organized around the idea that one person is irreplaceable enough to determine whether every other future matters.

Kaiki's intervention restores plurality:

- love can matter without monopolizing life;
- one dream can fail without all dreams failing;
- one identity can end without personhood ending;
- one relationship can become memory without becoming meaningless.

## 17.3 Love can strengthen or damage depending on the relationship

Kaiki's:

`恋は人を強くすることもあれば、人を駄目にすることもある`

is intentionally relational.

The same Araragi is developmental for Hitagi and obstructive for Nadeko.

This rejects romance as an automatically ennobling force.

Love is not morally certified by intensity.

## 17.4 Happiness is not the sole purpose of life

Kaiki's answer to Araragi is one of the volume's sharpest anti-teleological moments.

A life does not need a guarantee of happiness before it becomes worth continuing.

This matters because Nadeko's godhood offers a kind of stable “happiness”: permanent unrequited love, exemption from ordinary failure, control over the future.

V13 prefers a riskier human life.

## 17.5 Art as surplus personhood

Nadeko's manga matters because it proves the self exceeds the dominant story.

Art becomes:

- private work;
- embarrassment;
- desire without audience;
- skill developed outside recognition;
- a possible future;
- and evidence of a person not exhausted by romance or victimhood.

## 17.6 Role versus person

`暦お兄ちゃん`, `可愛い撫子`, `蛇神`, `詐欺師`, `恋人`, `専門家`—V13 is saturated with roles.

The volume becomes ethically productive when characters cease treating those roles as exhaustive.

Kaiki is more than fraudster without ceasing to be one.

Nadeko is more than cute girl, victim, god, or rejected lover without any of those histories becoming fake.

## 17.7 Gratitude and forgiveness are independent

Hitagi's final stance is one of the clearest demonstrations in the series:

> **A person can owe gratitude for present help without owing absolution for past harm.**

This is a major corrective to redemption narratives that use one heroic act to cleanse a perpetrator's history.

## 17.8 Adulthood as permission to revise

Kaiki tells Nadeko she can try again because she is human.

This is less a doctrine of youthful infinite possibility than a doctrine of **revision**:

> humans are allowed to change projects without proving the old project was fake.

That connects V13 directly to V12's distinction between falsifying and changing.

## 17.9 Recognition outside cuteness

Kaiki's frightening-description moment and manga exposure both offer Nadeko something rare:

being seen under predicates other than `可愛い`.

Recognition hurts.

It also enlarges the person who can be recognized.

## 17.10 Past consequence survives present usefulness

The final assault is thematically important because it makes V13 refuse moral accounting as cancellation.

Kaiki's good result for Nadeko does not erase what he did to other people.

That parallels Hitagi's refusal to forgive him.

# 18. Counterreadings and disconfirming evidence

## 18.1 Counterreading: “V13 reveals Kaiki was good all along”

**Rejected.**

The volume preserves:

- fraud;
- invasive conduct;
- cruelty;
- prior victimization;
- lying;
- theft-like opportunism;
- and the assault by an old victim.

His capacity for useful care complicates moral classification; it does not reverse it.

## 18.2 Counterreading: “Kaiki saves Nadeko because lying works better than truth”

**Rejected.**

His central factual lie fails immediately.

The successful intervention depends on direct exposure, partly truthful diagnosis, exaggeration, and Nadeko's explicit decision to risk being “deceived.”

## 18.3 Counterreading: “The manga is Nadeko's one true self”

**Rejected.**

Kaiki calls it her `本当の夢`, but Nadeko herself says she had not formed the explicit ambition to become a manga artist.

The stronger claim is plurality, not replacement essence.

## 18.4 Counterreading: “Nadeko never loved Araragi at all”

**Too strong.**

V11–V13 show genuine romantic attachment.

What they undermine is the assumption that the attachment entails deep knowledge of Araragi as a full person.

## 18.5 Counterreading: “Nadeko is simply stupid and therefore easy to manipulate”

**Rejected by the plot.**

Kaiki adopts a version of this theory and loses.

The deeper problem is closed relational processing, not low intelligence alone.

## 18.6 Counterreading: “The parents caused everything”

**Rejected.**

The household role matters, but the causal network includes:

- June victimization;
- Araragi idealization;
- class dysfunction;
- Ougi's intervention;
- Nadeko's own choices;
- talisman access;
- family dynamics;
- and supernatural opportunity.

## 18.7 Counterreading: “Hitagi forgives Kaiki by asking for and thanking him for help”

**Explicitly contradicted.**

She says she will continue to hate and resent him.

## 18.8 Counterreading: “V13 rekindles a Hitagi/Kaiki romance”

**Unsupported.**

The volume preserves ambiguity around historical feeling and Kaiki's interpretation of it. Present action is structured around Hitagi's commitment to Araragi and her desire to end renewed contact with Kaiki.

## 18.9 Counterreading: “Gaen definitely wanted Kaiki to continue and secretly funded him”

**UA.**

Kaiki speculates that the stop order and three million yen may have exploited his contrarian psychology. The text does not independently verify Gaen's intention.

## 18.10 Counterreading: “Kaiki dies at the end of the novel”

**Not established publication-locally.**

He is struck twice and loses consciousness. No death confirmation occurs within V13.

## 18.11 Counterreading: “Ougi is now proven to be the supernatural villain behind everything”

**Too strong.**

V13 strongly implicates Ougi in information/prompt chains around Nadeko and Kaiki's attacker. Ontology, full purpose, and moral classification remain unresolved.

## 18.12 Counterreading: “Araragi must literally never speak to Nadeko again for her to recover”

**Too universal.**

Kaiki's instruction is a situated intervention aimed at breaking immediate romantic centrality. Later relational possibilities must be evaluated on later evidence.

The first-pass corpus contains several claims that V13 can now route more precisely.

|---|---|---|
| Kaiki's declared unreliability does not make every sentence false | **STRENGTHEN** | V13 makes claim-specific reliability a formal reading requirement. The book tells us to distrust him, then makes action impossible to understand if we discard him wholesale. |

| `暦お兄ちゃん` is partly a preserved role rather than a deeply known beloved | **STRENGTHEN** | Hanekawa's closed-world diagnosis, Nadeko's tiny kill-list knowledge, and Kaiki's questions all support insufficient knowledge of Araragi as a full person. |

| Kaiki is morally ugly yet diagnostically effective; lies can open routes truth cannot | **REVISE / COMPLICATE** | The professionally engineered factual lie fails. The successful route combines exposure, exaggeration, truthful recognition, fictional occult framing, and Nadeko's knowingly chosen uncertainty. |
| Hitagi/Kaiki show recovery without cathartic final-boss closure | **STRENGTHEN** | Hitagi can be grateful and still refuse forgiveness. Later help does not retroactively cleanse abuse. |
| “People save themselves” is necessary but insufficient | **STRENGTHEN** | Nadeko's acceptance is indispensable, but so are Hitagi's commission, Kaiki's diagnosis/intervention, the talisman extraction, Hanekawa's information, and Araragi's transport. |

| Oddities can be narrative bodies without reducing every human problem to metaphor | **STRENGTHEN** | The fabricated `蛞蝓豆腐` can function causally, yet the talisman remains a literal material mechanism and Nadeko's family/romance problems remain human. |

The largest V1 correction is therefore:

> **Kaiki does not prove that a brilliant lie can save a girl better than truth. He proves that even a professional deceiver can fail when he treats a person as an epistemic target rather than a participant—and that a knowingly entered fiction can be more ethical than an imposed lie.**

# 20. Later-material revisions and quarantined hypotheses

This section records only forward-facing questions. It does **not** import later answers into V13's publication-local authority.

## 20.1 Kaiki's survival

V13 ends with loss of consciousness after a severe assault.

Later appearances, if any, must revise this state when their source boundary is reached. Until then:

**OPEN.**

## 20.2 Nadeko's creative future

V13 establishes manga as real existing practice and a possible future project.

It does not establish:

- professional success;
- lifelong vocation;
- artistic greatness;
- or that manga remains her only important future.

Later material must test whether the creative opening becomes sustained self-authorship.

## 20.3 Ougi's identity and purpose

V13 strengthens the causal case that Ougi is not an ordinary incidental student.

It does not establish Ougi's ontology.

**OPEN.**

## 20.4 Gaen's real intention in the three-million-yen stop order

Kaiki suspects reverse psychology / funding.

No independent confirmation occurs.

**OPEN.**

## 20.5 The anonymous letter

Kaiki infers Hitagi wrote it because she understood how he would react to `手を引け`.

The inference is strong enough to preserve prominently, but it remains narrator inference.

**OPEN with high local plausibility.**

## 20.6 Oshino's claimed niece

Kaiki's statement that Oshino has no siblings is strong contradictory testimony against Ougi's claimed relation.

Later evidence must determine whether:

- Kaiki is wrong;
- Ougi is lying;
- “niece” is nonliteral;
- or another ontological explanation applies.

**OPEN.**

## 20.7 Kaiki's dead beloved / Gaen Tooe

Kaiki thinks of an ongoing unrequited attachment to a dead woman in a context strongly suggestive of Gaen Tooe.

The woman is not named in the relevant passage.

Treat identification as **SI**, not TF, until later textual confirmation.

## 20.8 Araragi and Nadeko after “become a memory”

V13 gives a strong immediate case for separation.

Later contact must be judged on its own relational state rather than condemned in advance.

The operative question is whether Nadeko can meet Araragi as a person after her future ceases to require `暦お兄ちゃん` as a governing role.

## V13 closing synthesis

V13 initially offers the perfect fantasy for a professional liar.

A socially isolated fourteen-year-old has become a god because a self-story, an occult object, and a romantic obsession converged. A master deceiver enters, studies her for a month, and plans the elegant fiction that will remove her murderous motive without needing to change her.

It does not work.

The failure is the point.

Kaiki has understood Nadeko as **deceivable** but not as **relationally closed**. He has treated her as a case whose belief-state can be manipulated rather than as a person whose future requires her own participation.

What saves the operation is not superior lying technique. It is the collapse of Kaiki's professional script.

He stops trying to win the con.

He calls her `千石`.

He exposes the embarrassing notebooks.

He tells her the Araragi dream failed.

He overreaches by calling manga her true dream, but underneath the rhetoric he offers something much more defensible: **there are other things in you**.

Nadeko tests the proposition.

Could she simply try something else?

Could a human being fail at one dream and not treat that failure as proof the rest of the future should be burned down?

Kaiki says yes.

And then, crucially, she chooses to be deceived.

That phrase makes the ending far richer than “the liar saves the girl.” Nadeko agrees to enter a story that neither of them can prove will come true. She may never become a manga artist. She may not become happy. She may regret it. Kaiki may be wrong.

But the future has become revisable again.

That is enough to let a fake oddity operate, a real talisman be removed, and a god become a middle-school girl again.

V13 therefore gives the V2 corpus a mature synthesis of several arguments developed since *Bakemonogatari*:

> **People cannot be reduced to the story that hurts them, the role that protects them, the love they cannot relinquish, the origin that produced them, or the specialist diagnosis that explains them. Rescue becomes more ethical when it restores the ability to revise rather than installing a new final story.**

Kaiki never becomes clean enough to own that lesson.

That may be why he is the right narrator for it.
