# Gaibil — directing envy while becoming involved in a patient's life

## 1. Identity and access

ガイビル is the devil impersonating Doreck, not the thin patient who acknowledges the arrangement. His public human appearance, exposed giant form and injured return to Doreck's appearance are states of Gaibil. They do not create separate people or establish that the real Doreck physically transformed (DUST-CONT U013 P202–218,306–316; U014 P303–356,519).

Gaibil is a working Roman label for the native name. He is distinct from Vanir, Maxwell, Ragcraft and the other devils Dust uses as comparisons. Dust's recognition of an Elroad-like predicament is a structural comparison, not an identity join (U012 P298–306; U013 P406–409).

## 2. Chronology and knowledge

The castle's apparent groom acts politely and consumes Feit's cookie. Later reports describe illness followed by an energetic public presence. Dust does not yet know the actor is Gaibil, and the analyst must not write those earlier beliefs as if the disclosure had already occurred (U011 P404–452; U012 P129–138).

Dietary evidence prompts a substitute hypothesis. The real Doreck acknowledges the duplicate, explains his wish and eventually discloses shared sight and hearing through one eye and ear. Gaibil then appears and speaks in a changed register. The link helps explain his timely arrival without establishing unlimited perception or knowledge of every later disguise (U013 P79–118,202–218,294–311).

He confines the investigators until the wedding, visits them, reacts to Cecily and later releases Dust and Small while retaining leverage over Rionor. The bride at the ceremony is Leen. The missing electric reaction surprises him, and Dust also initially misses the cue. Exposure therefore shows a failed expectation, not an omniscient director successfully controlling every participant (U013 P317–450; U014 P144–146,274–334).

After exposure he discovers a new source of envy, reshapes the confrontation, is defeated with combined tactics, performs a final villain role, is stopped by Rionor's electric intervention and survives. His final claim that everything followed his design must be read against those actual surprises and reversals (U014 P356–573; U016 P8–34).

## 3. Motives, values and reasons

Gaibil directly confirms a particular appetite for jealousy. Doreck's account of their first meeting is corroborated by Gaibil's own description of the unusually valuable producer. He praises the patient as a source of sustained emotional reward (U013 P264–286,309–328).

The contract gives the appetite an organized project: impersonate a more socially and professionally successful Doreck, secure the marriage, and make the real man experience the contrast. Gaibil describes future work and domestic performance with relish. Those are plans, not completed governance, marriage or intimacy. The two men's agreement does not establish Rionor's informed acceptance (P332–350).

The reward can change his means after the initial plan fails. He first laments the loss of the contract and expected meal, then notices envy directed at Dust's heroic appearance. He refuses to leave and supplies an embellished public story to stimulate more of it. This is adaptive staging with a specific reward, not random destructiveness or a proven desire to rule for its own sake (U014 P356–397).

Professional pride and particular care complicate the appetite. He acknowledges failure in the private ending and asks for a new wish because the failure concerns his standing as a devil. He also repeatedly attends to Doreck's medicine, meals and rest. Instrumental maintenance of a valued emotional source remains a rival to broader affection; the narrative does not require a single pure motive (U013 P312–314; U014 P539–542; U016 P15–33).

## 4. Affect and self-presentation

Gaibil enjoys the patient's mixed jealousy, but he also shows irritation, surprise, disappointment, renewed appetite and concern. His reaction to Axis conduct includes concrete complaints and disgust. His own dislike does not make every complaint independently verified or every proposed restriction just (U013 P410–449).

Exposure visibly disrupts him. He recovers enough to call the bride's statement a joke and ask for evidence, but the text has already shown uncertainty about the absent electric trigger. Later he becomes enthusiastic about the crowd's reward and about a possible final spectacle (U014 P281–287,361–397,524–531).

When the real Doreck approaches, his voice and manner become concerned about the patient's condition. Dust reads this as sincere, while the questions about food, medicine and rest are directly represented. In the private ending, being cared for reverses their former roles and produces a rueful response. Neither episode proves universal gentleness (P536–542; U016 P23–28).

## 5. Competence and embodied limits

His impersonation supports social access and a favorable public image. He manages disclosure to his client and some staff, captures intruders, and uses contract and hostage leverage. Yet he fails to identify the substituted bride before exposure. A performance can be effective without being perfect (U012 P502–510; U013 P294–318; U014 P274–334).

His giant form has blue scales, a long serpentine body, short limbs, four wings and a dragon-like head. The appearance does not establish that his species is a dragon. The represented low voice is a written description, not an acoustic measurement (U014 P344–353).

He resists ordinary spear strikes and Leen's magic, uses water attacks and counters Feit's fire. Feit has greater maneuverability in the actual exchange. A terrain feint brings Gaibil into holy-water containers; a practiced Dust–Feit fire-spear attack then breaks his scales. Taylor's spear provision, Keith's staff provision and Leen's obscuring magic are real contributions. His loss is not proof of a solitary Dust victory or unlimited weakness to any holy object (P438–508).

He is injured, shrinks and later wakes bandaged. No permanent death is established. His willingness to risk death and the dangerous attacks remain distinct from whether anyone was actually killed.

## 6. Conditional decisions

The [reconstruction model](../06%20Character%20Modeling%20and%20Reconstruction/KONOSUBA_GAIBIL_RECONSTRUCTION_MODEL.md) proposes two bounded rules. The first treats him as an organizer of emotionally productive performances. Under an active contract he can invest in impersonation and public competence; after failure he can exploit a newly discovered source of jealousy. The available reward, its credibility to him, contract limits and the actual danger must be bound before generating a choice. He is not required to select every risky spectacle.

The second concerns this particular client's maintenance and the professional meaning of a failed wish. Care can continue when the original public project collapses, and a new agreement can become preferable to simply abandoning the relationship. Instrumental preservation, attachment and pride remain live explanations.

Neither rule turns his no-killing statements into a universal no-harm policy. He imposes captivity, threatens a painful device and continues dangerous attacks. A generated answer must preserve the actor's stated constraints and the adverse conduct rather than retrospectively sanitizing them.

## 7. Relationships and their costs

Doreck is the client whose jealousy initially makes the relationship attractive. He is also a patient whose needs require work, an adviser in Gaibil's proposed future administration and someone who later returns care. Gaibil's public claim that he brainwashed him conflicts with the earlier private willing contract. Dust's reading and Doreck's later thanks support a protective interpretation of blame-taking, while Gaibil maintains his own performance-and-reward explanation (U014 P544–556; U016 P21–28).

Rionor is an intended bride and a person he restrains with leverage. Her prior knowledge, promises and actual actions must be tracked separately from those of the impersonating Leen. A contract to obtain a marriage is not her consent to the means or the result.

Dust is investigator, captive, apparent emotional target and later a valuable public spectacle. Gaibil's threat to use Rionor's ring prevents Dust treating the encounter as a freely chosen performance. The threat remains coercive even if Gaibil also wants an exciting story (U014 P383–426).

Cecily is a hostile religious interlocutor whose conduct disgusts him. The guards are agents with their own complaints and decisions. Do not attribute every staff action, dietary remark or sarcastic line to Gaibil personally.

## 8. Ordinary work and care

The plot supplies mundane labor beneath the elaborate scheme. Someone prepares restricted food; Gaibil checks medicine and sleep; future official work would require continuing advice and performance. These are not all his witnessed manual actions. The kitchen guard's labor and irritation belong to that guard, while Gaibil's reminders belong to Gaibil (U013 P79–112,312–314,342).

The epilogue shows a recovering devil without visible restraints or a guard in the described room. It does not prove every legal or practical restriction has disappeared. Doreck reports asking for his survival and obtaining lenience; no independent royal hearing is shown (U016 P8–20).

The proposed restart begins with health management and light exercise for Doreck. No work schedule, training completion, new price, refunded old price, enduring household arrangement or successful cure is provided. Ordinary obligations remain prospective at the ending.

## 9. Japanese written voice

The polished public groom voice is a role. At the reveal, Dust notices a sharp change; Gaibil uses rough forms, including 俺, and then adopts elevated 我 language and grand villain declarations. His register changes with performance and addressee rather than expressing a single invariant level of formality (U013 P306–326).

The giant-form declarations amplify the staged antagonist. Questions to Doreck about food and medicine soften the interaction. In the private ending he answers thanks with an expansive claim about constructing a hero, then coughs and accepts a patient's concern. The juxtaposition is part of the written characterization, not evidence that every grand claim is literally true (U014 P388–396,529–554; U016 P21–30).

Reconstruction should distinguish role voice, own irritation, theatrical self-description and patient-directed concern. No pronunciation, acoustic imitation, acting timing or tested new-context idiolect is certified.

## 10. Comedy, threat and the public account

The premise makes a false groom unusually attentive to the man he replaces. The humor does not erase the uninformed bride, imprisonment or threatened pain. His proposed domestic scenes are part of how he intends to provoke Doreck, not evidence that those scenes occurred.

The battle likewise turns heroism into an emotional production. Gaibil broadcasts Dust's identity and invents framing; the audience reacts. The hero's striking pose partly results from a stuck spear and accidental separation from Feit. The narrative can make the spectacle funny while showing a ruined church, dangerous combat and real injuries (U014 P388–397,490–519).

His final mass-kill threat accompanies an actual charge, even though Dust reads it as a bid to die in the villain role. Later mercy does not remove the risk. Doreck's statement that he had not harmed humans is a defense the analyst must qualify against the received events (P554–573; U016 P14–19).

## 11. Counterevidence and rival explanations

A simple evil-takeover model misses the specific envy appetite, care and professional concern. A harmless-entertainer model fails on restraint and hostage threats. A pure friend model ignores his instrumental description of humans and deliberate emotional extraction. An omniscient-planner model fails on the false bride, surprise, exposure, defeat and acknowledged failed wish.

His care may preserve the source of his preferred reward, express a particular attachment, fulfill a professional identity, or combine them. His blame-taking may protect Doreck and complete his desired scene at once. The source supports the actions more strongly than a unique causal decomposition.
