# Nos — knightly self-command, trusted voices and the armor left behind

## 1. An individual across two narrators

Nos, ノス, is the armored knight encountered in the Demon King's castle in MAIN17 and the displaced swordsman Dust encounters in Axel in DUST07. This is a high-confidence cross-source identity join rather than a join from spelling alone. MAIN17 names the knight, shows his transfer leaving armor behind, and gives Kazuma's stated police-station destination. DUST07 shows a suddenly present swordsman at that police station, his own name, complaint about the missing armor, demand to know where the Demon King's castle and the glib opponent went, and instruction to himself to behave like a knight. The conjunction is unusually specific. Neither narrator explicitly supplies a cross-volume identification sentence, and Dust does not witness Kazuma casting. Those limits remain part of the join. (MAIN17 C1/U011 P452/560–586; DUST07 U013 P205–239.)

Nos earns **Tier 2 treatment** through this concentrated, revealing context. It exposes an aspiration about conduct, cooperation and distress toward companions, responses to correction and humiliation, real practical inference, formidable fighting, changing resource conditions, anger and harmful public consequences. These are his choices and limits, beyond a generic obstacle to Kazuma. The evidence remains narrow in everyday life, long-term development and novel-context decision-making. No separate behavioral reconstruction is earned merely by writing a monograph, joining two sources or identifying several mechanisms. A descriptive account can be substantial while a reliable generative model remains underidentified.

## 2. Cooperation makes him vulnerable to a particular deception

Nos participates in an organized defense with Logia and the wraith Pain. When the opponents are reassigned, he accepts the task and offers that Pain can shelter behind them. This is an actual cooperative offer in combat, rather than evidence that he cares equally for every person or that the offer is performed. Flash then leaves Nos and Logia unable to see, while Pain can still perceive the situation differently. Their dependence on the available voice is situational. (MAIN17 U011 P403–424.)

Kazuma learns the names and uses Pain's voice to tell Nos that the human is in front of him. Nos strikes Logia. When the real Pain explains the mistake and the danger of further movement, Nos is distressed, asks what happened and agrees to remain still. His attack has a grave result, but it is not a freely chosen betrayal of Logia or a refusal to process the correction. Correctly separating action, information and intention preserves both the harm and the specific error. (P446–469.)

The next deception exploits the same relationship differently. Kazuma again uses Pain's voice, now insulting Nos. Nos responds with shock at the apparent judgment of a comrade; Pain protests that these words also came from the human. The source thus shows sensitivity to a trusted person's evaluation, not proof that Pain actually held the quoted contempt. A reconstruction that copies the insult into their true relationship would turn an adversary's performance into biography. (P470–474.)

His trust is fallible under extraordinary vocal mimicry and sensory deprivation. The episode does not establish general inability to distinguish every lie, low intelligence in every domain or indifference to companions. It also does not establish complete learning from the first correction: a second impersonation still reaches him. Both the local response and its limitation matter.

## 3. Distress does not erase fighting skill

After Pain is destroyed by Aqua, Nos advances. Kazuma and Aqua taunt him for seeking revenge after cutting his own companion. Nos correctly identifies Kazuma as the person who induced that strike. The taunts do not create the opening Kazuma wants. Despite anger and the earlier deception, Nos's guard and attacks remain too strong for Kazuma to challenge straightforwardly. An automatic Evasion activation saves Kazuma from a fast strike; Nos acknowledges the unexpectedly good avoidance. (MAIN17 U011 P497–518.)

This is a useful competence boundary. Being misled in one information channel does not remove weapon skill, speed, equipment or the ability to notice an opponent's performance. Kazuma's self-description of inferiority has a practical local basis in the attempted encounter; it should not become a universal comparison across every task. Conversely, Nos's technical advantage does not establish an ability to solve every magical or social problem.

Kazuma applies a game stereotype that this sort of physical specialist will be weak to magic. Lightning makes Nos react and drop his sword, then he picks it up and says it only tingled. The initial physical effect remains real. So does the failure to achieve the hoped-for decisive result. Neither universal magical immunity nor a general rule of susceptibility follows from this one spell and report. (P519–530.)

Nos then prepares a thrust timed to spell release and threatens to strike through Kazuma and Aqua together. This is a deliberate tactical threat toward both intruders, not benevolent restraint or merely a noble-looking stance. He can cooperate with his own group and impose serious danger on opponents in the same episode. Recognizing technical competence must not sanitize what it is used to do. (P532–555.)

## 4. He can infer the protagonists' mistake

Before the spell is released, Nos asks how these two reached an upper floor so quickly. He connects their location with a transfer trap, recalls what the room is used for, and infers that they fell into the absurd trap. Kazuma and Aqua deny the implication while the narrative has already established their predicament. Nos's conclusion is locally accurate. (MAIN17 U011 P547–553.)

This moment matters because it appears beside repeated labels of stupidity. The same character who accepts a false familiar voice can use local institutional knowledge and visible circumstances to diagnose the protagonists' embarrassment. His reasoning is not optimal or comprehensive, but it exists. DUST07 later gives his own statement that thinking is difficult for him; that self-account also cannot erase a demonstrated inference. The account should distinguish self-image, others' judgments and actual performance.

His information remains local. He does not know all of Kazuma's registered destinations or the narrator's plan. He reacts quickly when Kazuma casts Teleport, but is transferred before his thrust reaches. The armor stays. His unfinished confidence that Teleport will not work is not a completed successful resistance. It is an expectation contradicted by what happens. (P554–564.)

## 5. Transfer solves one person's phase and exports danger

Kazuma says he sent Nos to Axel's police station and explains why he did not choose the other registered destination. The transfer and armor left behind are directly narrated at the castle. The destination is Kazuma's statement there, and Aqua's proposed armor-enchantment explanation is expressly tentative. The scene does not independently test the precise magical mechanism. DUST07's compatible arrival strongly strengthens the cross-source identity sequence without turning that explanation into a universal rule for armor. (MAIN17 U011 P565–586; DUST07 U013 P205–239.)

DUST07 supplies what the castle narrator does not see: armed police cannot safely approach the disoriented swordsman, several already lie injured, and Nos demands to know why he is there. His complaint about the glib man and the vanished castle belongs to his own limited understanding. He remains responsible for the sword violence described; his disorientation is context, not a claim that it caused no harm. (U013 P205–223.)

Dust intervenes, tells police to evacuate the injured and takes over the fight. This means the transfer successfully removes Nos from Kazuma's immediate encounter while leaving a dangerous problem for other people. A provider's institutional title did not demonstrate adequate capacity or a completed safe handoff. The resulting comparison is an expanded-source qualification of the action's consequences; it does not rewrite the earlier MAIN17 observation or backdate Dust's later evidence into a frozen main-only forecast.

Nos's eventual confinement is still not witnessed. Dust knocks him unconscious and assumes that, outside the station, he will be put in a cell. Neither an actual booking scene, a death, a resurrection nor permanent incapacitation is shown in this bounded passage. A useful ending should not fill those absences. (DUST07 U013 P248–254.)

## 6. The knight he tries to appear to be

Displaced and angry, Nos tells himself to calm down and behave like a knight. This is a distinctive self-regulatory script, even though it does not immediately stop his dangerous conduct. The script has a source history: at the castle, Mammon tells the apparent Nos to speak more like a knight so that he will seem wiser. That turn is addressed to Kazuma in disguise. It supplies an attributed prior coaching relationship, not an actual new Nos reply. Nos's later self-instruction is compatible with internalizing such a norm, without proving exactly when, how often or why he learned it. (MAIN17 U012 P111–127; DUST07 U013 P207.)

The aspiration is therefore about visible conduct and presentation at least as much as an established moral code. Real Nos has already threatened two opponents; later he has wounded police. Colleagues' surprise at the disguised Kazuma proposing a hostage tactic cannot be converted into a proven rule that actual Nos always rejects coercion. Likewise, learned knightly phrasing is not evidence of a formal order's exhaustive duties or historical chivalric practice outside this fiction.

His first-person **俺**, imperious address, formal-looking challenge, distress toward Pain and agitated self-command produce a printed voice with changes of function. Kazuma's U012 imitation is a negative control for attribution: sounding like Nos is not being Nos, and Kazuma himself forgets the usual manner of speaking. The difference between audible likeness, social style and speaker identity is explicit within the story. No acoustic measurement, full Japanese generation grammar or native-human adequacy is claimed from these printed turns. (MAIN17 U011 P465–473/528–549; U012 P114–127; DUST07 U013 P207/233–245.)

## 7. Equipment changes the practical cost of his choices

Against Dust, Nos quickly recognizes a serious opponent, controls distance and blocks strong attacks. The narrator describes lighter rapid attacks landing, leaving accumulating wounds. Nos complains that armor would stop this level of attack. The complaint confirms that he notices the missing protection; it does not prove exactly why he allows those lighter attacks. Habitual reliance on armor, assessment of damage, urgency and agitation remain alternatives. (DUST07 U013 P223–240.)

Dust announces a fair decisive blow, then uses a carried local resource to create an opening. Nos rejects the apparent projectile as a petty trick and cuts it; the resulting distraction makes the knockout possible. The sequence is not a proof of fair dueling, inevitable stupidity or a universal defeat condition. It depends on the actual resource and missing armor. Dust's performed rescue of police and his deceptive claim about fairness coexist. (P241–254.)

Across the two confrontations, Nos repeatedly has useful capacity without complete control of information or context. He is physically formidable after anger, briefly processes a comrade's correction, correctly diagnoses the trap and adapts his stance to Dust's skill. He also accepts a false trusted voice, misjudges a spell's effect on his own body, inflicts serious public harm and rejects a misleadingly presented threat. These are different errors with different information and resource conditions, rather than repeated votes for one undifferentiated personality score.

## 8. Comedy, harm and remaining limits

The comic machinery moves from vocal impersonation to humiliating displacement and a second trick. It makes the protagonists resourceful, exposes their self-serving descriptions and leaves other people bearing costs. Literal injuries, actual threats and the loss of companions do not cease to matter because the narration is funny. A magnified indignity can support a comic interpretation while the underlying choice remains usable behavioral evidence.

The two narrator views are not two independent readers or two independent prospective trials. Their temporal and spatial handoff is part of why they are useful. The same underlying removal is seen from different ends; it must not be counted once as Kazuma's success, once as an independently confirmed harmless outcome and again as a wholly unrelated opponent's failure.

Everyday preferences, family, enduring friendships, political commitments beyond the defensive role and long-term changes after these defeats remain unobserved. Comrade cooperation and distress are better supported than a general loyalty hierarchy; knightly self-command is better supported than a comprehensive ethical code. A full generative model would need to distinguish these mechanisms from ordinary combat incentives and responses to extraordinary deception. The current evidence justifies this monograph's asymmetric interpretation without a separate prediction apparatus or an appearance quota.

Nos's present disposition is thus a substantial, source-qualified individual account: a technically skilled and dangerous fighter, susceptible to particular misinformation, capable of specific inference and correction, and concerned with a knightly presentation that he does not consistently achieve. The joined episode clarifies his agency and the protagonists' exported consequences while leaving unknown outcomes and unfamiliar contexts open. Frozen models, source reports, historical scores and original null fields retain their authority at their original boundaries.
