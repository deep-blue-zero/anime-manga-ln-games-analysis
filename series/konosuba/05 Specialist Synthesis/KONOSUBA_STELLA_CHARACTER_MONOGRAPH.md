## Experience is part of the mistake

Stella remembers more than six months of patient mapping and attention to monster ecology149. She also identifies the success on floor12 as the source of overconfidence150-151. The resulting descent is therefore a failure of previously careful adventurers, rather than evidence that they never plan. Her shame is retrospective and specific. It is not an independent historian's certification of every day's method, nor a universal diagnosis of recklessness.

The floor-times-three level guideline152-155 is knowledge she reports, not a measured population law. Their average falls just short of the stated target. She recognizes the implication of the destroyed barrier tool and lost food170-174: respite, magical recovery and even eventual return are in doubt. These practical dependencies explain why escaping one pursuer would not by itself settle the expedition. No numerical balance, exact shopping budget or remaining mana total is supplied.

## Fear does not remove judgment

The sequence gives Stella physical fear, tears and regret, but also appraisal. She knows the last Flash scroll was already used141. She lights the route147 and thinks through a possible floor boundary159-176. Gil voices the hopeful plan168, while her private assessment remains less secure174. His encouragement and her reservations can coexist; neither becomes a verified rule that the pursuer will stop at the stairs. The party does not reach those stairs in this scene.

When Gain spills treasure179, Stella's first impulse is to reproach him180. She corrects it immediately on recognizing the wound181. Everyone stops182 because he cannot escape. This is a useful contrary case to an account of selfish panic: she updates an unfair interpretation, and the group interrupts flight. It is still not proof that she always sacrifices herself for others, or that Gain refused to discard wealth. The loss of treasure follows the attack, rather than establishing its victim's greed.

## An interrupted defense and a warning to a stranger

Gil's call to ready magic186 is interrupted by footsteps from ahead188-189. No spell is actually cast by Stella. The simultaneous threat cues explain despair without certifying the approaching man's hostility. When the newcomer starts his shop announcement192, the group freezes195. Stella then warns him about the pursuer204. Her warning is an outward act of concern even while her own party needs help; its practical effect is limited because Vanir approaches instead207.

Vanir initially treats the hell-neroid as an approachable pet205/208. The bite210-211, destruction213 and reconstruction of his arm214 are witnessed through Stella. She revises his apparent human identity215 and recognizes the rescue219. That inference is more accurate than her later religious explanation. A careful study can retain both. Her encounter does not independently prove Vanir's limitless durability or establish which pet the creature was.

## Buying the means to continue

Stella intends to thank him220, but he interrupts with goods221-223. Her companions actually buy the supplies227; the stock sells out239. Barrier stones, potions and food answer the exact losses already described. Untagged requests224-226 cannot be individually distributed among Gil, Gain and the sister. No itemized price, payer assignment or personal expenditure for Stella is shown. Vanir's earlier fivefold-price proposal to Kazuma132 does not supply this party's bill.

The expected safe return225 is a buyer's hope, not a narrated completed journey. Gain's recovery is not shown. Material rescue includes the removal of the pursuer and access to supplies, but it does not end every risk. Equally, payment does not erase gratitude or the lifesaving intervention. The scene joins a commercial transaction to extraordinary relief without reducing either to an analyst's preferred moral label.

## A good reason for gratitude, a mistaken explanation

Stella calls the rescuer a god234. After his objection235, she apologizes236 and interprets the objection as religious humility238. The reader knows more about Vanir than she does. Her reasoning makes sense from the rescue and its apparent power, yet the conclusion is wrong. It neither transforms him into a deity nor proves her globally unintelligent. The ending preserves her own sincere thanks249 after he has left244; she has not learned his name247.

This disparity matters to her characterization. She can correct an initial judgment of Gain, identify a nonhuman rescuer and still misunderstand why he rejects divine comparison. The varying quality of her appraisals is stronger evidence than a single trait such as gullibility. Her final gratitude is her contribution, not simply a device for praising Vanir's salesmanship.

The sister is explicitly her younger sibling158, but no name, exact age, class or further family is given.
