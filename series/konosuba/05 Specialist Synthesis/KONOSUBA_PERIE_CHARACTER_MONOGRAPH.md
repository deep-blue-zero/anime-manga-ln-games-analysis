# Perie — personal taste, claimed responsibility and force under frustration

Perie has a preference that the surrounding cast repeatedly explains away: she likes her clothes. She also has a military assignment, a claim to advancement through strength, a mistaken account of her targets' preparation, and responsibility for escalating a live-capture mission toward killing. Her sensitivity to ridicule and the coercion directed against her belong in the same individual account as her own threats and attacks. Neither set of facts cancels the other.

## 1. Identity and evidence access

The red-haired woman who emerges from concealment in Dust 6 names herself ペリエ at DUST06 U012P336. Perie is the project's retained Roman working label. Her name is available after this introduction; descriptions of the initially unidentified woman preserve the earlier knowledge boundary rather than replace a known name throughout her account.

The narrator identifies a woman at U012P297. Wings and horns appear when she discards her jacket at U013P208, where Dust identifies her as 魔族. In Dust 7, Dust retrospectively calls her 悪魔ペリエ while comparing bat-winged forms (DUST07 U011P518). These source formulations support a demon/devil identification at their respective scopes. They do not assign every contract mechanic, lifespan, emotion preference or allegiance of Vanir, Maxwell, Gaibil, succubi or other devils to Perie.

Her demon-army employment, superior's instructions, executive-candidate standing, quoted endorsement and prior mission transfer reach the reader through her speech. Her fighting is directly narrated. Dust's assumptions about a royal pursuer, Rionor's explanations of clothing and the later species comparison have different sources. The distinctions matter when reconstructing her knowledge and responsibility.

## 2. Chronology and unequal information

The first encounter begins after Feit's detection of a concealed presence. Dust immediately interprets it through Rionor's escape from her retinue. Perie's striking dress becomes the subject of the group's discussion before her business is heard. She objects, becomes visibly affected by criticism, states her own preference, introduces herself and asks for the particular woman to accompany her (U012P289–357).

Perie has given a name while withholding affiliation. Those are different disclosures. She promises no harm conditional on compliance and challenges interference. Rionor and Small cast Paralysis together during her speech (P370–374). Dust subsequently attempts unwanted exposure while she cannot move; Rionor strikes him, identifies Perie's loss and stops him. The group applies paralysis again and leaves (P382–406). Neither the asserted harmlessness of paralysis nor Perie's later antagonist role turns incapacity into assent.

In the next guild encounter Perie has added a jacket. She renews her demand, receives another insulting attempted apology, threatens to involve bystanders and publicly condemns day drinking. Patrons surround her and force alcohol on her; she subsequently asks for more and drinks with them (U013P54–102). The beginning and later participation are separate choices and conditions.

She catches up after Dust and Rionor leave. Perie attributes her restored state to an unknown priest's Cure, then reveals that a demon-army executive ordered her to secure Bridol's princess. She had interpreted the substitution as a clever defense against the army; Dust and Rionor admit that was never their plan (P127–156). Her explanation concerns their preparation, not a narrator-certified mastermind scheme.

The live-capture plan changes after Dust's explicitly false claim of low hostage value. Perie first loses interest in the burdensome captive, then proposes killing Rionor and the witness. She fights Dust, intends to pursue Rionor afterward and is defeated through a planted spear and the geometry of her dive (P174–254). The subsequent return to the mansion and epilogue belong to other agents. Dust 7's recollection supplies no new encounter with Perie.

## 3. Motives, values and claimed justification

Her most direct ordinary preference is U012P326: she likes this kind of clothing and considers it attractive. That answer resists Dust's claim that the clothes invite any man's attention and Rionor's different hypothesis about attracting a desired admirer. Neither speaker has established her motive merely by proposing one. Later Rionor notices that the open back accommodates Perie's wings (U013P209–210). A practical feature and personal liking can coexist; the observation does not establish an exclusive design motive.

Perie wants her business heard. She repeatedly calls attention back after the others interrupt or converse among themselves. She also tries coercive bargaining: compliance is offered as the way to avoid harm, and possible bystander involvement is used against a royal target (U012P347–370; U013P63–75). Her adverse judgment of Rionor's disregard for others is not proof of a general humanitarian principle. It occurs within an attempted threat.

She explains a military purpose for a hostage: restraining Bridol's dragon knights would help the army's planned campaign (U013P160–170). This is her proposed causal account, not an achieved strategic result. She later says killing Rionor would amount to the same thing despite an instruction to bring her alive (P200). The substitution of outcomes is Perie's justification. The narrative does not certify that the superior would accept it or that both means have identical military consequences.

The account of responsibility contains a concrete contradiction. At P205 Perie blames the superior's personnel choice and describes herself as poor at thinking. At P225 she says the task originally belonged to someone else and she took it by force. Dust expressly notices the inconsistency. The earlier transfer remains her report, but it cannot be omitted while presenting the mismatch solely as an imposed assignment. Her participation includes claimed initiative as well as hierarchy.

## 4. Affect and regulators

Clothing criticism draws anger, later narrated discouragement and renewed attempts to redirect the conversation. She recovers enough to state her name and pursue the mission. This gives a sequence of reactions rather than an unrestricted trait of emotional helplessness. The jacket is an actual change in presentation; the causal explanation remains uncertain. Criticism, unwanted attention, the preceding exposure attempt and practical convenience are not isolated from one another.

Her guild rebuke follows renewed criticism of her clothes. Dust speculates that resentment contributes (U013P82), while Perie's actual speech invokes work, sobriety and family disappointment. The stated standards, their timing and Dust's interpretation should remain distinct. After attention turns against her, she becomes alarmed; forced alcohol is followed by apparent rapid intoxication, a boast about resistance to drink and an active demand for more. One narrated occasion does not establish a measured permanent tolerance or an ordinary drinking routine.

Irritation and loss of expected control accompany the decision to kill. Her own account later makes revenge against Dust an immediate aim (P219). The military target is still relevant: she intends to attack Rionor after him. It would overstate the change to say that she forgets every mission concern, just as it would understate it to describe the killing plan as straightforward obedience.

## 5. Competence and its limits

Perie's actual combat is capable. Her claws are fast, her reach exceeds Dust's sword and both hands threaten him. He struggles to defend. When endangered she withdraws upward; flight is a usable means of managing distance (U013P221–233). These acts qualify a simple account of a fighter who can only rush blindly.

The eventual dive has a different constraint. Rionor supplies a spear while Perie is committed to a rapid approach. Dust plants it and moves aside; her speed prevents the needed immediate change of direction (P239–254). The result depends on weapon supply, timing, reach and movement. It does not establish a universal hierarchy among classes, weapons or demon species.

She reports that combat strength brought her to executive-candidate standing and quotes a superior who expects only that strength from her (P225). No appointment is witnessed. Her command of magic outside this fight, exact level, training history and full repertoire remain unknown. Dust observes that she is not using magic in this engagement (P230); that is narrower than incapacity to use any magic.

Planning and social appraisal are uneven. She proposes a comprehensible hostage mechanism and chooses negotiation because she thinks the target strategically prepared. She misreads that preparation, the royal target's response to bystander leverage and the drinking audience she insults. Her self-description of poor thinking is given during a blame dispute. It is not a diagnosis or sufficient explanation of every choice.

## 6. Decisions and conditional reconstruction

The record supports a bounded sequence of revisions. A target initially treated as strategically prepared is revealed to be improvising an escape. A hostage initially treated as valuable is represented as unwanted and demanding. Perie then rejects the captive and substitutes killing for live delivery. Dust's manipulation of believed value is explicitly false, but her response to the information she accepts is still her choice.

This does not isolate a single generator. Perceived utility, personal irritation, prior humiliation, task difficulty and a possible wish to defend her standing change together. Her account of taking over the assignment suggests initiative backed by force; her account of the superior's mistake deflects responsibility. Those claims constrain an interpretation without identifying a reliable policy for every future task.

Two opposite reductions fail. “She always obeys” cannot absorb the live-capture violation and reported forced takeover. “She never thinks and always charges” cannot absorb the negotiation rationale, bystander leverage or aerial retreats. A useful reconstruction must preserve both strategic attempts and the conditions under which she abandons them. The current monograph retains that problem without promoting a trait label into a frozen rule inventory.

## 7. Relationships and particular recipients

Rionor is a military asset, a source of clothing criticism and an unexpectedly difficult interlocutor. Those roles overlap. Perie initially expects a royal target to be constrained by bystander risk. Rionor's refusal to accept that premise surprises her. The later demand for luxurious hostage conditions adds a practical burden after the value dispute; Perie's reaction is not simply refusal of an offered friendship.

Dust is first an intrusive observer and source of unwanted conduct, then an interlocutor who misidentifies her employer, manipulates the hostage premise and becomes a witness and revenge target. Responsibility can be assigned to each actor at each stage. Dust's later defensive purpose does not authorize his earlier act; Perie's earlier victimization does not excuse her announced intention to kill him and Rionor.

Yunyun's attempt to protect the apparent Leen briefly draws Perie's comment about defending a friend. That remark does not establish Perie's friendship ethics. Small and Rionor are the actual co-casters of the first paralysis. The unnamed drinking patrons act collectively in the guild, with individual voice mapping unresolved. Perie's unknown priest is known through her report. The superior and original assignee are also reported roles; no name, gender or global personal distinctness is supplied for them.

## 8. Ordinary life and personal history

Dress is an owned preference with visible social stakes. It deserves inclusion without being converted into romantic availability, proof of profession or a complete account of identity. The later jacket indicates some change in presentation while the underlying dress remains. Its cause is not directly confessed.

The guild episode gives a limited relation to work and drink. Perie condemns daytime drinking while pursuing her own assignment, is then forcibly made to drink and proceeds to request more. This supports a local conflict between an expressed standard and conduct under changed conditions. It does not supply a home routine, financial history, family composition or freely chosen first drink.

Her proud statement of candidate status and reported seizure of another person's task supply occupational history through self-report. Whether she wanted promotion, recognition, convenience or some other benefit from this particular mission is unprinted. No childhood narrative, intimate relationship, exact age, salary or stable social circle should be filled from the antagonist role.

## 9. Japanese written voice and addressee

Perie's あたい and rough conversational forms distinguish her written self-presentation. She can assert her own name grandly, complain that others ignore her, offer a conditional assurance, rebuke drinking, negotiate an explanatory misunderstanding and threaten particular people. These are different speech acts; a single angry catchphrase would discard useful variation.

Her opening attempt to project menace repeatedly loses the conversational floor. After the name at U012P336, requests at P347 and P353 try to restore the business. The later lecture at U013P81 invokes everyday propriety, while P170 explains military leverage and P200 changes the intended outcome. Register does not by itself distinguish honesty from self-excuse: the reported takeover and selection complaint require their content and chronology.

Printed disruption under paralysis and intoxicated wording belong to those bodily states, not a universal idiolect. The priest's rebuke and superior's endorsement are quotations mediated by Perie. Untagged surrounding voices are not assigned to her by turn order. These observations concern written Japanese and interaction; they provide no acoustic or native-human voice certification.

## 10. Comedy, seriousness and consequences

The episode repeatedly interrupts the expected antagonist performance. Her clothes receive a discussion instead of a fearful reception; the target fails to care about bystanders; the lecture addresses a room of drinkers; a supposed royal capture becomes a dispute over the inconvenience of keeping a hostage. Comic reversals depend on specific participants and mistaken expectations.

They retain actual costs. The first encounter includes paralysis and an unwanted-exposure attempt. The guild includes forced drinking. The final encounter includes explicit threats to kill, rapid attacks and a severe aftermath. No comic framing, absence of a completed sexual act, eventual defensive success or later sympathy makes those acts disappear.

The ending is described from Dust's perspective as he shields Rionor's view and leaves. The current packet contains no independent final examination, checked permanent death, recovered Perie or successful hostage delivery. Dust 7 recalls a previously fought devil when classifying other flying figures. That is neither another Perie appearance nor an independent outcome trial.

## 11. Counterevidence and competing accounts

| Proposed simplification | Evidence it would lose |
|---|---|
| Dress exists only to attract men or conceal official identity | Perie's stated liking; the targets' initially mistaken affiliation; a later practical wing-opening observation with no exclusive motive claim. |
| Perie is an obedient implement of a superior | Her proposed violation of live-capture instructions and reported forcible takeover of someone else's assignment. |
| She is incapable of strategic thought | Her negotiation premise, hostage rationale, bystander leverage and actual aerial withdrawals. |
| Her force is uniformly well directed | The social misreadings, abandonment of the required outcome and committed dive against the newly supplied spear. |
| Victimization establishes harmlessness, or villainy removes her boundaries | Her actual threats coexist with the coercion against her; each actor retains responsibility. |

Presentation sensitivity, ordinary frustration, military opportunism, defended status and the immediate demands of a fight are serious competing explanations. The jacket is especially poor evidence for a uniquely identified regulator because several relevant conditions changed before it appeared. The military escalation is causally informative but also confounded. A general rule that simply restates her anger would add apparent precision without discriminating these accounts.
