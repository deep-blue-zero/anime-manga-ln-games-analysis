# KONOSUBA - Japanese Humor and Voice Analytical Protocol

## 1. Purpose

This protocol governs Japanese-language analysis during the KONOSUBA reread. Its purpose is not to decorate an English character profile with isolated Japanese words. It is to identify how linguistic choices contribute to:

- individual voice;
- social positioning;
- relationship state;
- comic timing;
- escalation;
- embarrassment;
- boasting;
- pleading;
- denial;
- insults;
- self-justification;
- boke/tsukkomi dynamics;
- narrator perspective;
- behavior reconstruction.

Japanese analysis is performed from the primary novels during sequential reading. If an official English translation is later added, it may be used as a comparison surface, but it does not replace the Japanese source for claims about register, wording, or pragmatic effect.

## 2. Governing caution

Do not convert individual Japanese forms into fixed personality labels.

For example, a pronoun, sentence ending, honorific, or polite form only becomes character evidence through **distribution, context, contrast, and recurrence**.

The task is not:

> "This particle means the character is masculine/feminine/rude."

The task is:

> "Across these contexts, this speaker repeatedly chooses this register or stance relative to plausible alternatives, and the pattern changes in these specific relationships or emotional states."

## 3. Speaker-voice dimensions

Track the following when evidence is recurrent or analytically significant.

### 3.1 Self-reference

Record first-person forms and when they change.

Relevant possibilities include forms romanized as `ore`, `boku`, `watashi`, `atashi`, names used as self-reference, or omitted subjects whose interpretation depends on context.

Do not assume any form for a character before observing it in the source.

### 3.2 Addressee reference

Track:

- second-person forms;
- surname/given-name use;
- title use;
- honorifics such as `-san`, `-chan`, `-sama`, and others when they occur;
- deliberate honorific removal;
- insulting or status-marked substitutes;
- changes under anger, intimacy, mock respect, fear, or supplication.

Address changes can be more informative than vocabulary because they encode relationship and stance.

### 3.3 Register

Track:

- plain versus polite speech;
- sudden politeness;
- exaggerated politeness;
- mock politeness;
- ritual/formulaic speech;
- role-playing or stylized register;
- deference to authority;
- collapse of politeness under emotion;
- strategic politeness used to obtain something.

A register shift should be analyzed as an event when it materially changes the social meaning of a scene.

### 3.4 Sentence-final stance

Track recurring sentence-final patterns and combinations, including stance markers, assertions, questions, softening, insistence, challenge, and confirmation-seeking.

Do not build the model from isolated endings. Prefer repeated patterns associated with specific contexts or relationships.

### 3.5 Contraction, clipping, and deformation

Written dialogue may represent:

- clipped forms;
- slurring;
- whining;
- shouting;
- lengthening;
- repeated syllables;
- childish or exaggerated pronunciation;
- emotional loss of syntactic control.

These may carry humor or affect that a normalized translation would flatten.

### 3.6 Interjections and minimal responses

Short reactions often control comic rhythm.

Track recurrent types such as:

- disbelief;
- exasperation;
- interruption;
- refusal;
- correction;
- stunned silence;
- sharp attention call;
- plea;
- boastful acknowledgement.

The exact form matters less than its distribution and interactional function.

### 3.7 Lexical signatures

Accumulate recurring vocabulary for:

- complaint;
- insults;
- boasts;
- excuses;
- denial;
- pleading;
- self-praise;
- accusation;
- money/reward talk;
- fear;
- embarrassment;
- magical/technical jargon;
- status and honor.

Avoid catchphrase overfitting. A word becomes a voice marker when it participates in a broader recurrent pattern.

### 3.8 Syntax and rhetorical organization

Track patterns such as:

- long self-justifying chains;
- abrupt fragments;
- delayed revelation;
- list-like escalation;
- repetition of another speaker's wording;
- rhetorical questions;
- conditional bargaining;
- parallel phrasing;
- sudden collapse from grand register into mundane complaint.

These can encode both personality and comic timing.

## 4. Humor interaction analysis

Japanese humor in the novels should be analyzed as interaction, not just vocabulary.

For a significant verbal gag, reconstruct:

```text
setup
-> expectation
-> linguistic or pragmatic deviation
-> listener recognition/non-recognition
-> correction or reaction
-> counter-escalation
-> punchline/callback
-> relational consequence
```

Track who performs which role:

- boke/fool contribution;
- tsukkomi/corrective contribution;
- deadpan nonreaction;
- opportunistic escalation;
- deliberate misunderstanding;
- sincere misunderstanding;
- status inversion;
- mock formalization of an absurd premise.

Do not permanently assign one character "the boke" or "the tsukkomi." Role switching is an important ensemble feature.

## 5. Translation-sensitivity classification

For notable jokes or voice features, use four classes.

### L0 - Structurally portable

The humor/character effect is largely preserved regardless of language because the causal pattern is situational.

Example type: boast -> ignored warning -> failure -> blame displacement.

### L1 - Phrasing-sensitive

The basic joke survives translation, but rhythm, concision, repetition, or tone changes materially.

### L2 - Register/pragmatics dependent

Literal semantic content can be translated, but important information about hierarchy, intimacy, mock respect, social aggression, or stance is difficult to preserve without explanation.

### L3 - Japanese-form dependent

Wordplay, grammar, homophony, script choice, syntactic ambiguity, or another specifically Japanese form is central enough that translation requires reconstruction or annotation.

These categories are analytical, not quality judgments about translation.

## 6. Cultural versus linguistic dependence

When a joke feels difficult to transfer, distinguish:

### Linguistic dependence

The mechanism relies on Japanese wording, grammar, register, sound, or script.

### Cultural dependence

The words can be translated, but the joke assumes cultural knowledge such as hierarchy conventions, otaku/genre language, RPG terminology, role-language conventions, honorific expectations, or social scripts.

### Structurally portable comedy

The joke is primarily generated by character, situation, or causal reversal and would remain intelligible in another language.

Do not label a joke "Japanese humor" simply because it occurs in Japanese.

## 7. Kazuma narrator language

Kazuma's narration must be analyzed separately from his spoken dialogue.

Track possible recurrent forms of:

- self-exoneration;
- grievance construction;
- attention to attractive or embarrassing details;
- dismissive labeling;
- rhetorical self-pity;
- exaggerated internal retort;
- rationalization before action;
- hindsight;
- hostile or affectionate framing of party members;
- divergence between internal narration and what he actually says aloud.

The goal is to recover a **narrator grammar of appraisal**, not simply an inventory of funny lines.

When a claim about another character originates primarily in Kazuma's narration, mark that dependency.

## 8. Rationalization sequences as linguistic evidence

The language pass should identify recurring verbal or internal sequences that accompany bad decisions.

A useful pattern might be represented abstractly as:

```text
notice tempting stimulus
-> detect obvious objection
-> minimize objection
-> invent fairness/necessity justification
-> intensify self-persuasion
-> act
-> reframe failure or assign blame
```

The exact sequence must be discovered from evidence rather than presumed.

If a character repeatedly uses a particular rhetorical structure before a particular kind of mistake, that structure becomes high-value reconstruction evidence.

## 9. Relationship-conditioned voice

For each major dyad, ask whether the same character changes:

- politeness;
- address;
- sentence length;
- willingness to insult;
- directness;
- pleading style;
- boasting;
- teasing;
- emotional disclosure;
- use of shared callbacks;
- tolerance for interruption.

A mature voice model is relational. It should not generate the same speech toward Kazuma, a stranger, an authority figure, and a vulnerable person unless the corpus genuinely supports that uniformity.

## 10. Seriousness shifts in language

Mark linguistic evidence that the comic mode has stopped or weakened.

Possible indicators to examine include:

- reduced joking;
- loss of performative register;
- shorter/direct speech;
- changed address;
- disappearance of habitual boasts/excuses;
- unusually explicit concern;
- unusually formal or unusually plain speech;
- silence where a comic response would normally be expected.

Do not assume any specific marker in advance. The importance lies in **contrast against the character's own baseline**.

## 11. Evidence capture format

A Japanese voice/humor ledger entry should normally contain:

```text
volume
chapter/section
scene locator
speaker
addressee
relationship state
Japanese evidence anchor (short)
literal/basic semantic gloss
register/address notes
pragmatic effect
humor mechanism if relevant
L0-L3 translation sensitivity
narrator vs dialogue
recurrence link
model implication
confidence
```

Keep quotations short. The ledger is an index and analysis layer, not a reproduction of the novel text.

## 12. Building a voice grammar

At specialist-synthesis stage, each major character should receive a voice grammar describing:

- normal register range;
- register-switch triggers;
- address patterns;
- characteristic sentence organization;
- complaint style;
- boast style;
- excuse/rationalization style;
- insult style;
- plea style;
- emotional speech changes;
- pair-specific differences;
- comic rhythm habits;
- seriousness-mode changes;
- low-confidence areas.

The voice grammar should support new utterances without copying canonical sentences.

## 13. Anti-caricature rules

### Do not spam catchphrases

Frequency in fandom memory is not frequency in ordinary dialogue.

### Do not make every sentence maximally distinctive

Real character voice includes neutral connective language. Overstylization makes simulations less accurate.

### Do not infer personality from one pronoun or ending

Use context and distribution.

### Do not erase register variation

A character who speaks differently under fear, authority, intimacy, or embarrassment needs those states in the model.

### Do not translate first and analyze second

For language-dependent claims, analyze the Japanese form first, then provide an English explanation.

## 14. Interaction with the reconstruction suite

The Japanese protocol feeds the reconstruction model in two ways.

### Behavioral inference

Language reveals appraisal, motive, status sensitivity, rationalization, and relationship state.

### Surface generation

The mature voice grammar constrains how those underlying states are expressed.

The order matters:

> psychology/relationship/state -> communicative goal -> register/stance -> utterance

not:

> catchphrase -> vaguely appropriate scene.

## 15. Completion standard

The Japanese-language analysis is mature when it can explain:

- why two semantically similar lines would feel different from this character;
- how speech changes by relationship and emotional state;
- which comic effects are portable into English and which are not;
- which jokes depend on Japanese pragmatics rather than plot mechanics;
- how narrator language shapes judgments about other characters;
- how a character's bad decisions are foreshadowed or rationalized linguistically;
- how to generate new speech that sounds structurally consistent without copying the source.

The objective is not perfect sociolinguistic description. It is an evidence-grounded account of the language features that materially improve character interpretation and simulation.
