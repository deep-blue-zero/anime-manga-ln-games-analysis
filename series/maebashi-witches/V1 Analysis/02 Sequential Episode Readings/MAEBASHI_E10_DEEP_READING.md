# Episode summary

## The memory-erasure condition

Keroppe confirms that failure will return the girls to ordinary life and erase every memory connected to witch training.

He attempts to present this as considerate aftercare:

> 「挫折感も喪失感も全部忘れちゃうから」
> “You’ll forget the frustration and sense of loss too.”

> 「アフターフォローもバッチリ」
> “So the aftercare is perfect.”

The girls are horrified. Keroppe, by contrast, says that becoming emotionally invested in every generation of apprentice witches would be exhausting for him.

They have twenty-four hours to reclaim the shop.

Azu, expelled from the magical space and returned to her original body, hides beneath Yuina’s bedding and begins repeating that everything is impossible and that she wants to disappear.

## Eiko begins her own training

Inside the stolen flower shop, Eiko rejects Keroppe’s description of the five as her companions:

> 「仲間じゃない」
> “They aren’t my companions.”

She argues that she never formally began training with them. They called her an “apprentice-apprentice” on their own initiative, so she considers herself unrelated to them.

Her actual witch training begins now.

Keroppe is delighted by the dramatic takeover. He sets Eiko’s solo promotion target at approximately twenty thousand Mapo and treats her attack on the five as evidence of exceptional natural ability.

Eiko then asks why Keroppe created the shop at all.

His answer is startlingly empty. He compares it to humans:

-   growing herbs in the kitchen;

-   keeping a hamster;

-   or raising ants.


He has no grand moral mission. He cultivates apprentice witches because observing them interests him.

## Eiko’s home

The episode briefly shows Eiko at home.

Her mother enters her room without permission, rummages through the trash, finds a hidden mock-exam result, and mocks Eiko’s medical ambitions:

> 「あんな模試の結果で
> よく医者になるなんて言えたもんだね」
> “With a mock-exam result like that, I can’t believe you can still talk about becoming a doctor.”

Eiko’s wish remains undisclosed, but the scene reveals the domestic environment surrounding it:

-   privacy is not respected;

-   failure is searched for;

-   ambition is treated as presumptuous;

-   and vulnerability invites judgment rather than support.


## A sleepover at Yuina’s home

Yuina responds to the crisis by declaring an overnight gathering.

The girls cannot return to the shop, so Yuina’s bedroom becomes their replacement base. Her mother readily agrees to host them, brings food, offers bedding and toiletries, contacts their families, and asks what they want for breakfast.

She is talkative and somewhat intrusive, but hospitable rather than judgmental.

Yuina’s mother says she is relieved to see her daughter with friends. Yuina’s deceased grandfather had effectively been Yuina’s best friend, and Yuina has apparently never invited school friends home before.

After her mother leaves, Yuina admits:

> 「うち友達つくるの下手なんだよね」
> “I’m bad at making friends.”

She says people often find her irritating. Azu was unusual because she stated her objection directly. With most classmates, Yuina does not realize she has overwhelmed them until around summer vacation, when they begin avoiding her.

She therefore assumes she probably did something similarly intrusive to Eiko.

Yuina wants to apologize, but then admits the more fundamental reason she needs the shop back:

> 「みんなのこと、絶対絶対忘れたくない」
> “I absolutely, absolutely don’t want to forget everyone.”

The others are the first friends who did not withdraw from the version of Yuina other people found excessive.

## Azu lets the group see her

Azu eventually emerges from beneath the blanket in her original body.

The other girls avoid commenting. Azu becomes irritated by their excessive tact:

> 「今のアズ見て、なんかコメントないわけ？」
> “You’re looking at Azu as she is now—don’t you have anything to say?”

Kyouka says that seeing her clarifies why Azu treated Rinko as she did, although it still does not excuse the cruelty.

Yuina says:

> 「アズちゃんがなりたいアズちゃんでいてほしい」
> “I want Azu to be whichever Azu she wants to become.”

Azu then states her future plainly:

> She will recover the shop.
> She will become a full witch.
> She will become beautiful.
> She will become an Azu who can go out proudly with the others.

She has already created a detailed Tokyo itinerary:

-   Ikebukuro Sunshine City;

-   an idol stage for flower-shop performance research;

-   Takeshita Street crepes;

-   shopping in Shibuya;

-   purikura;

-   and an overnight stay with Kyouka’s relative.


The conversation expands into other trips and ordinary plans.

“Forever” is no longer merely a slogan. It becomes an itinerary.

## Searching Maebashi

The following morning, the girls travel back toward central Maebashi.

Azu covers her face and body heavily, even in the heat. She tells the others they cannot understand what it feels like to expose her face publicly.

At the same time, their conversation remains ordinary and affectionate:

-   Azu is outraged that Kyouka looks good with only sunscreen.

-   Mai’s makeup is supposedly careless.

-   Everyone proposes a makeup-study gathering.

-   Azu’s expertise becomes something she may teach rather than a weapon for ranking them.


Yuina also reveals the history of her family business. Her grandfather’s photography studio became a combined photo and manju shop after her mother began serving sweets to the older people who gathered there.

Her family home was already a small local social space before the magical flower shop existed.

The girls then divide central Maebashi into search zones and begin opening shutters one by one.

## Mai’s update

While traveling, Mai reveals that Yua has followed the Kumai account again and sent a message asking:

> 「もしかしてマイなの」
> “Could this be Mai?”

Yua should no longer consciously remember discovering Mai’s identity.

Nevertheless, something has led her toward the same recognition.

Mai also notices that her own reaction has changed. Previously, she would have:

-   leapt with joy;

-   screenshotted the message;

-   replied immediately;

-   and tried to arrange a meeting.


Now she is pleased but comparatively calm.

She says that the part of her mind occupied by Yua has become smaller because the other witches occupy more of it.

## Choco’s update

Choco reports that her family situation has improved:

-   her grandmother has more energy;

-   neighbors visit;

-   her mother has more breathing room;

-   Choco has met young people from households resembling her own;

-   and practical support has reduced her burden.


But she adds that disclosure does not help everyone. Some young people are rejected after telling others about their circumstances, while some receive sympathy without any meaningful improvement.

Choco therefore calls herself:

> 「ラッキーチョコちゃん」
> “Lucky Choco-chan.”

She understands that asking for help was necessary, but not sufficient. She also needed trustworthy people and institutions capable of answering.

## The false alarm

During the search, Yuina sees a shutter glowing and excitedly calls everyone.

She believes their desire has finally reached the shop.

It is not the entrance.

Yuina apologizes repeatedly for making everyone hopeful before checking properly. The others reassure her that time remains and that they should continue searching.

As the deadline approaches, however, Yuina refuses to end the call:

> If this is goodbye, she wants to see everyone’s faces.

The girls move toward a common meeting point.

## The final conversation

The girls begin articulating what they will do if their memories disappear.

Kyouka says she will become mayor and make the city better.

Choco promises to protect both her own happiness and her family’s happiness.

Mai promises to find things that can fill her life besides Yua, and to become at least a seventy-point—preferably one-hundred-point—version of herself.

Azu cannot formulate a grand ambition. She says she cannot endure forgetting them and becoming alone again.

Yuina answers:

> 「今日のこと、忘れても忘れないよ」
> “Even if we forget today, we won’t forget it.”

> 「忘れちゃっても
> それは忘れても消えないんだから」
> “Even if we forget it, forgetting won’t make it disappear.”

The others repeat the contradiction:

> 「忘れても忘れない」
> “We’ll forget, but we won’t forget.”

The countdown expires.

The girls gather in the shopping arcade, but the visual continuity breaks. Their intimacy appears to vanish. They stop relating as a group and drift apart like strangers.

Yuina walks alone and discovers one of the photographs.

# Core thesis

Episode 10 distinguishes two meanings of memory.

One is **explicit recollection**:

-   knowing who someone is;

-   recalling an event;

-   retaining a shared history;

-   and being able to narrate what happened.


The other is **formative persistence**:

-   altered habits;

-   new values;

-   emotional reflexes;

-   relationships changed outside oneself;

-   choices already made;

-   institutions activated;

-   objects preserved;

-   and the person one became through an experience.


Keroppe can erase the first.

The episode questions whether he can erase the second.

Its central claim is:

> **To forget that something happened is not necessarily to make it unhappen.**

---

# Keroppe’s moral framework is finally explicit

Keroppe has always behaved less like a benevolent magical guide than a producer, game master, or irresponsible employer.

Episode 10 reveals something more fundamental:

> The flower shop was never created around a human moral purpose.

His herb, hamster, and ant analogies place the witches in the category of cultivated life observed for interest.

He does not open the shop because:

-   Maebashi needs hope;

-   wishes deserve fulfillment;

-   young women need agency;

-   or suffering should be reduced.


He opens it because watching humans develop through magic is entertaining.

## Why he avoids emotional investment

Keroppe says that becoming attached to every group of apprentice witches would be unbearable.

This implies:

-   the present five are not the first;

-   failure and memory erasure have happened before;

-   and his detachment is at least partly a strategy developed through repetition.


That makes him more complicated than a sadist. He may have learned emotional nonattachment because the system repeatedly produces loss.

But his defense becomes institutional cruelty. Because he refuses investment, he does not feel obligated to:

-   disclose catastrophic rules in advance;

-   protect the girls from Eiko;

-   intervene in the takeover;

-   or treat memory erasure as harm.


## Eiko’s lower target

Keroppe appears to set Eiko a solo target of roughly twenty thousand Mapo, compared with the five girls’ original 99,999.

Whether this reflects her greater ability or simply Keroppe’s arbitrary management is not fully explained.

Either way, the promotion requirement now looks less like a universal magical law and more like a quota he can adjust.

The entire labor system becomes even less legitimate.

---

# Eiko’s legalistic denial of relationship

Eiko says:

> They are not her companions.
> She had not officially begun training.
> They called her an apprentice-apprentice without her consent.
> Therefore, she has no relationship with them.

This reasoning is technically structured but emotionally evasive.

The five:

-   encouraged her original dream;

-   performed for her twice;

-   welcomed her into their private culture;

-   taught her about the shop;

-   and offered provisional membership.


Eiko cannot truthfully claim that no relationship exists.

But she can claim no formal contract existed.

That distinction allows her to seize the shop without naming the act as betrayal.

## Eiko and emotional debt

Her position recalls Yua’s discomfort with Mai.

Yua felt that Mai’s enormous devotion imposed a relationship and debt that Yua had not consented to.

Eiko may similarly experience the witches’ rapid emotional incorporation as something performed around her rather than chosen by her:

-   Yuina names her wish.

-   The five sing for her.

-   Yuina recruits her.

-   They create an apprentice category.

-   They assume she will join.


The group’s warmth is real, but it often moves faster than the recipient’s self-definition.

This does not justify the takeover. It helps explain why Eiko insists upon the absence of relationship.

She is reclaiming authorship through a brutally unilateral act.

# Eiko’s mother and the origin of control

The editing places Eiko’s home immediately beside Yuina’s sleepover.

Both scenes involve a mother entering a daughter’s room.

The contrast is severe.

## Eiko’s mother

-   enters against her daughter’s wishes;

-   searches the trash;

-   finds concealed evidence;

-   evaluates Eiko through performance;

-   and treats aspiration as arrogance.


## Yuina’s mother

-   brings food;

-   welcomes unexpected guests;

-   arranges beds and breakfast;

-   contacts their parents;

-   and expresses relief that Yuina has friends.


Yuina’s mother can also be overbearing and talks without stopping, but her boundary crossing expands the social space.

Eiko’s mother’s intrusion contracts it.

This may help explain the girls’ opposite social defenses:

> Yuina assumes everyone can be pulled into connection until they explicitly reject her.

> Eiko assumes private desire must be protected through control, secrecy, and self-sufficiency.

---

# Yuina is not socially secure

Until this episode, Yuina could easily be read as the naturally popular extrovert who overwhelms more reserved people.

Episode 10 reveals that her warmth is not built upon effortless belonging.

She has a recurring social pattern:

1.  She becomes enthusiastic.

2.  She fails to recognize another person’s limit.

3.  The person gradually withdraws.

4.  Yuina notices only when the distance has become obvious.

5.  She concludes that she has failed again.


Her grandfather was effectively her closest friend, and the witch group may be the first peer community that has continued accepting her after recognizing her intensity.

This changes the meaning of her behavior throughout the series.

## Her intrusive friendliness is partly compensatory

Yuina does not pursue people because she is certain everyone will like her.

She pursues them because she cannot reliably perceive when they do not.

Her confidence is partly an absence of social calibration, followed later by painful self-awareness.

This explains:

-   her refusal to accept Azu’s rejection;

-   her photography without warning;

-   her rapid recruitment of Eiko;

-   her tendency to declare what another person needs;

-   and her panic when she suspects Eiko’s anger may be her fault.


## “Kindness is decided by the recipient”

Yuina says:

> 「親切って、自分じゃなくて相手が決めることだからさ」
> “Whether something is kind is decided by the other person, not by you.”

This is one of her most mature statements.

It reflects everything the flower shop has taught her. Good intention does not guarantee good care.

But the statement also risks becoming excessive self-blame. Yuina assumes Eiko’s takeover probably occurred because Yuina was irritating or intrusive, even though Eiko made an autonomous decision to endanger all five girls.

Yuina is learning accountability.

She has not yet learned the boundary between accountability and taking responsibility for another person’s harmful choice.

# Yuina’s house replaces the flower shop

Eiko takes the official magical venue.

The five immediately recreate its essential functions elsewhere.

At Yuina’s house they:

-   gather;

-   eat;

-   sleep;

-   disclose painful truths;

-   negotiate identities;

-   make plans;

-   support one another;

-   and imagine a future.


They cannot transform or generate Mapo.

They can still perform the social work through which they became the Maebashi Witches.

This is decisive.

> **The group was not created solely by the magical architecture. By Episode 10, they can carry the architecture relationally.**

Yuina’s home is particularly appropriate because her family business already combines:

-   photography;

-   food;

-   hospitality;

-   local memory;

-   and community gathering.


The magical flower shop may be new, but Yuina has inherited a local tradition of making emotionally meaningful spaces.

---

# Azu’s original body and the ethics of acknowledgment

The girls’ initial silence around Azu is respectful but awkward.

They know:

-   her history;

-   her shame;

-   and that she did not choose this reveal.


So they avoid making her body into a group event.

But Azu experiences total silence as another form of exceptional treatment.

She asks them to say something.

This identifies a subtle problem:

> Respectful noncomment can become a way of signaling that something is too uncomfortable or fragile to acknowledge normally.

## The series refuses easy reassurance

Nobody delivers a generic:

> “You were beautiful all along.”

That would undermine the complexity of Azu’s chosen transformation.

Kyouka instead addresses moral meaning:

> Understanding Azu’s history helps explain what happened with Rinko, but does not excuse it.

Yuina addresses agency:

> Azu should be the Azu she wants to become.

They do not decide which body is the true one.

They recognize Azu’s right to decide how she wishes to live in her body.

## Azu’s revised reason for witchhood

Azu once appeared to want beauty primarily as proof of worth.

Now she says she wants to become beautiful so she can:

> 「堂々とみんなと遊びに行ける」
> “Go out proudly with everyone.”

Her desired body is still aesthetically important. But it has gained a relational purpose.

She does not want to become beautiful merely so the world will approve of her.

She wants a body in which she can participate openly in friendship.

---

# The Tokyo itinerary is a promise of ordinary life

Azu’s plan is comically overdesigned, but emotionally significant.

It contains no great magical destiny:

-   watch an idol performance;

-   eat crepes;

-   shop;

-   take purikura;

-   visit landmarks;

-   sleep at a relative’s home.


These are ordinary adolescent activities.

The 99,999-point quest had made the future abstract.

Azu converts it into a day schedule.

The group’s desire for permanence becomes credible because it is attached to things they can imagine actually doing together.

---

# Ordinary Maebashi becomes the final battlefield

The girls cannot transform, sing magically, or teleport to the shop.

They have to:

-   take the train;

-   coordinate schedules;

-   inspect maps;

-   make phone calls;

-   divide search zones;

-   walk through the arcade;

-   and physically test shutters.


The final arc temporarily removes their magical grammar and asks whether their ordinary selves can preserve what magic created.

The answer is not triumphant. They fail to find the door.

But they continue behaving as a team without supernatural enforcement.

## The door paradox

The shop normally appears before someone with a wish.

The five possess one of the most desperate wishes in the series:

> They want their shop, friendships, and memories back.

Yet no entrance appears.

This may be because Eiko controls access, but the episode does not explain the mechanics.

Thematically, it exposes the system’s arbitrariness:

> Having a sincere wish does not guarantee access to the institution that claims to serve wishes.

---

# Mai and Yua prove that memory is not the whole relationship

Yua’s new message is one of Episode 10’s most important pieces of evidence.

She should not remember learning that Mai was Kumai.

Nevertheless, she asks:

> “Could Kumai be Mai?”

Something remains.

Possible explanations include:

-   subconscious familiarity with Mai’s writing;

-   an emotional pattern surviving the reset;

-   external evidence;

-   or a trace the wish did not erase.


The episode does not define the metaphysics.

What matters is that explicit memory loss did not produce a perfectly clean return to the previous state.

## Mai has already changed

Even more important is Mai’s reaction.

She does not return to the old emotional pattern.

The former Mai would have treated Yua’s recognition as the central event of her life. The present Mai feels pleased but not consumed.

The other witches now occupy more of her inner world.

If her explicit memories are erased, the distribution of emotional importance may remain altered.

That is the episode’s strongest argument for:

> forgetting without returning to who one was before.

---

# Choco’s “luck” complicates the help narrative

Episodes 7–8 might have suggested a reassuring formula:

> Tell someone you are suffering, and support will appear.

Episode 10 corrects that simplification.

Choco has met people whose disclosures led to:

-   social distancing;

-   pity without help;

-   or no meaningful change.


She calls herself lucky because her teachers, neighbors, family, and friends responded constructively.

This does not invalidate Yuina’s advice.

It places the advice inside a social reality:

> A person must risk asking for help, but institutions and communities must also be capable of answering.

Choco’s improvement is not proof that individual courage alone solves structural hardship.

# 「メジルシ」: the song as a theory of memory

The song title means a marker, landmark, or sign by which something can later be located.

Unlike most previous insert songs, **「メジルシ」 is not performed on the magical stage for a customer.**

It accompanies the five girls as they search separately through Maebashi and speak to one another by phone.

This makes it their first major song primarily about preserving themselves.

## The shutter

The opening lyrics begin:

> 「その手で押したシャッターが
> 焼き付けた記憶さえ」
> “Even the memory burned in by the shutter your hand pressed…”

The shutter has a double meaning:

-   a camera shutter;

-   the metal shutters of the shopping arcade they are opening one by one.


Photography and the city’s closed storefronts collapse into one image.

The girls are searching shutters for access to their past while Yuina’s camera has been creating shutters against forgetting.

## A photograph is not memory itself

The lyric immediately says that even something “burned in” can be lost.

This prevents a simplistic answer in which photographs automatically defeat memory erasure.

A photograph preserves visual information.

It does not automatically preserve:

-   context;

-   intimacy;

-   recognition;

-   or the feeling of the moment.


The photograph is a **marker**, not a replacement consciousness.

It can point toward something that has become inaccessible.

## Life as undeveloped film

The song imagines the edge of a film beginning to turn at birth, bearing the provisional title:

> **“jinsei” — “life.”**

The title is provisional because the meaning of a life is not fixed at the start. Its images are developed through encounters.

## Colored memory

The central refrain says:

> 「大事な思い出は
> 色がついてる」
> “Important memories have color.”

> 「心動いたとき
> 君が彩った」
> “When my heart moved, you colored it.”

Memory becomes relationally authored.

The important experience is not merely something the individual recorded. Another person colored it.

This is why losing the others would mean more than losing information. The girls would lose access to the people who gave emotional color to their lives.

## The colorless negative

The second half contrasts those memories with avoided time:

> 「背けたい　やり過ごす時間は全部
> 色なんて持たない
> 無色のネガのようさ」
> “All the time we avert our eyes from and merely endure has no color, like a colorless negative.”

The metaphor distinguishes:

-   passing through time;

-   and emotionally inhabiting time.


Before the flower shop, many of the girls were surviving without fully living:

-   Azu hid from school and her body.

-   Mai organized life around Yua.

-   Choco converted every hour into care labor.

-   Kyouka performed the future her parents authored.

-   Yuina drifted through a vaguely enjoyable but insufficient daily life.


The flower shop gave those periods color because the girls became visible to one another.

---

# The false door and the failure of optimism

Yuina mistakes an illuminated shutter for the shop entrance.

The visual sequence allows hope to grow before dismantling it.

She calls everyone prematurely, exactly as she prematurely interpreted:

-   Eiko’s first wish;

-   Rinko’s situation;

-   Choco’s motivation;

-   and Eiko’s return.


Yuina again acts before confirming.

But this time, the group does not punish her or treat her mistake as proof of unreliability.

They say:

-   she does not need to apologize;

-   time remains;

-   and they should continue.


Yuina’s old social fear is that one mistake will cause people to withdraw.

The group now demonstrates the opposite:

> Error can be corrected without ending belonging.

---

# The final phone call

The visual structure places each girl alone in the night city.

They are separated physically, illuminated by blurred points of urban color, and connected through ordinary phones rather than magic.

Their faces are wet with tears, but each speaks toward the others.

The calls become a substitute stage.

Instead of singing to a customer, they witness one another’s final declarations.

## Their promises are personal testaments

**Kyouka** promises to become mayor and create conditions in which people can live more happily.

**Choco** promises to sustain both herself and her family rather than sacrificing one for the other.

**Mai** promises to find sources of identity outside Yua and to permit herself ambition beyond a defensive seventy.

**Azu** cannot articulate a public mission. Her deepest statement is that she cannot endure becoming alone again.

**Yuina** offers the theory holding the others together:

> The event can be forgotten without becoming nonexistent.

These are not merely future plans.

They are summaries of who the girls became through the first ten episodes.

---

# Azu’s photograph completes the Episode 9 argument

Before the search, Yuina gives Azu an envelope and asks her to open it later.

Near the deadline, Azu reveals that she opened it early.

It contains a photograph.

Azu says:

> 「いい写真だった」
> “It was a good photograph.”

This is the emotional resolution of Episode 9’s opening dispute.

Azu originally wanted imperfect pictures destroyed because they violated her visual self-authorship.

Now she acknowledges a photograph as good because of the relationship it preserves.

That does not mean her consent concerns were wrong. The episode does not establish that Yuina may photograph her without restriction.

It shows that Azu’s criterion has expanded:

> A photograph can be good without presenting the most optimized Azu if it preserves an Azu who was genuinely participating in something she values.

# “Forget, but don’t forget”

The phrase is logically contradictory because the episode uses “forget” in two different senses.

## First meaning: lose conscious recall

They may no longer know:

-   the names;

-   the events;

-   the shop;

-   or why a photograph exists.


## Second meaning: cease to be affected

Yuina refuses the second meaning.

The experiences have already altered:

-   the girls;

-   their families;

-   their customers;

-   their online communities;

-   their ambitions;

-   their behavior;

-   and Maebashi itself.


The memories may be deleted as accessible stories.

Their consequences remain distributed through the world.

## Existing evidence

The episode provides several examples before making the claim:

-   Yua gravitates toward Mai and Kumai despite erased knowledge.

-   Mai’s emotional dependence on Yua has already decreased.

-   Choco’s family support network already exists.

-   Kyouka has begun pursuing student leadership.

-   Rinko has already rejected humiliating work.

-   Eiko’s medical ambition has already become active.

-   Yuina’s mother has now met the other girls.

-   physical photographs remain.


Keroppe can remove the internal record.

He cannot easily reverse every causal consequence.

---

# The ending: the photograph as an orphaned marker

When the countdown expires, the girls’ behavior changes.

They stand in the same arcade but no longer act like people sharing a crisis. Their paths separate.

Yuina ends up alone.

She finds the photograph.

The image has become an orphaned marker:

-   it proves something occurred;

-   the person holding it may no longer understand what;

-   and its emotional color is no longer directly accessible.


But the marker creates a question.

That is the song’s modest theory of recovery:

> A signpost cannot walk the path for you.
> It can make the lost path discoverable again.

The episode does not guarantee that the photograph will restore anything. It establishes that complete erasure has failed to eliminate every trace.

# The title

「今日のこと、忘れても忘れないよ」 is not simply a promise of supernaturally perfect memory.

It is a promise that today will continue through:

-   changed people;

-   changed relationships;

-   external consequences;

-   material records;

-   bodily feelings;

-   and paths that may be rediscovered.


The title would be sentimental if the episode let the girls recover the shop immediately.

Instead, the episode appears to let the memory loss happen.

That makes the claim serious:

> “We will not forget” does not mean “nothing painful will be taken.”

It means:

> “What was taken cannot be made identical to something that never existed.”

# Character updates

## Yuina

Episode 10 gives Yuina her deepest vulnerability.

She is not merely an exuberant girl collecting emotional experiences. She is someone with a history of friendships dissolving because she could not read the limits of others.

The witches matter because they remained.

Her growth is visible in her statement that kindness is determined by the recipient, but she still overassigns herself blame for Eiko’s behavior.

Her photography now becomes central to the narrative’s theory of memory rather than merely a character hobby.

## Azu

Azu is seen outside the magical body by the group and remains one of them.

She does not abandon her desire to transform. Instead, friendship changes what transformation is for.

She wants beauty as a form of public participation rather than merely proof of superiority.

Her most revealing fear is:

> 「またぼっちになるなんて」
> “Becoming alone again…”

The group, not thinness, has become the primary thing she cannot lose.

## Kyouka

Kyouka’s mayoral ambition survives the loss of the shop as something she can articulate independently.

She continues to provide moral precision, telling Azu that context does not erase responsibility.

Her future is becoming increasingly civic rather than purely magical.

## Choco

Choco understands that her successful request for help depended partly upon luck and available community.

Her arc broadens from personal disclosure into awareness that social support is unevenly distributed.

She also remains able to interrupt emotional heaviness through humor without using humor to deny the underlying truth.

## Mai

Mai provides the episode’s strongest proof of lasting internal change.

Even before memory erasure, she can observe that Yua occupies less of her mind.

Her final promise modifies the seventy-point philosophy:

> seventy is no longer a ceiling designed to prevent exposure; it is a humane minimum from which she may still desire one hundred.

## Eiko

Eiko becomes the dark mirror of the show’s self-authorship ethic.

She has identified a wish and acted decisively.

But she treats other people’s relational claims as technically invalid and uses superior power to replace their shared authorship with her own.

Her home scene suggests why she may associate dependence with judgment and control, but her true wish remains unrevealed.

## Keroppe

Keroppe is no longer credibly interpretable as a conventional moral mentor.

He is a cultivator and observer.

He can facilitate growth, but growth is not necessarily his ethical objective.

The girls have developed a moral practice largely in spite of the institution he created.

# Cumulative reading after ten episodes

The series has passed through three broad movements.

## Episodes 1–3: interpreting desire

The witches learn that a visible problem is not automatically the real wish.

## Episodes 4–8: boundaries inside care

They learn that support can become:

-   projection;

-   appropriation;

-   self-erasure;

-   coercion;

-   judgment;

-   or unwanted rescue.


## Episodes 9–10: whether relationships exceed their institution

The magical shop is taken away.

The five continue to:

-   care;

-   disclose;

-   plan;

-   repair;

-   and affirm one another.


The larger thesis can now be refined:

> *Maebashi Witches* is about building relationships whose effects exceed the systems that produced them. The flower shop can create encounters, quantify labor, supply transformations, and erase memories—but it cannot completely control what those encounters set into motion.\*

Episode 10 is therefore not simply a cliffhanger about recovering lost memories.

It is the show’s strongest argument that:

> **A shared life is larger than anyone’s possession of its complete record.**

# Clip 1: Keroppe, Eiko, and the institution behind the flower shop

## Keroppe treats catastrophe as entertainment

Keroppe’s delivery is even more disturbing in motion than the transcript suggests.

When he explains that the girls will forget:

-   the shop;

-   their training;

-   their frustration;

-   their grief;

-   and one another,


he does not sound maliciously delighted. He sounds administratively cheerful.

That distinction matters. He frames memory erasure as a feature of a well-designed system:

> The failed apprentices will not suffer because they will no longer remember what they lost.

His moral reasoning is built around **experienced distress**, not continuity of personhood. If the later girl cannot report pain, he sees no serious harm in destroying the earlier girl’s relationships and memories.

The witches understand instinctively that this is grotesque. Keroppe does not.

## Keroppe’s detachment is partly cultivated

When the girls ask whether he will miss them, Keroppe explains that he cannot become emotionally invested in every generation of apprentices.

The scene therefore implies a history:

-   there have been previous groups;

-   some have failed;

-   memories have been erased;

-   and Keroppe has survived repeated cycles that the humans cannot consciously retain.


His casualness may be a defense against serial attachment. But the defense has hardened into institutional ethics:

> Because attachment would inconvenience the manager, the managed people are treated as replaceable.

This makes the shop resemble an exploitative entertainment or employment system more than a sacred magical institution. Keroppe enjoys watching girls develop, but does not accept reciprocal obligations toward the people whose development entertains him.

## Azu’s collapse is bodily, not merely verbal

Expelled from the shop, Azu returns to the body she has hidden from the group and immediately disappears beneath Yuina’s bedding.

Her repeated “impossible” language combines several losses at once:

-   the shop;

-   her chosen appearance;

-   her future witchhood;

-   her friendships;

-   and the possibility of going outside with them as the Azu she wants to be.


The blanket is both concealment and temporary substitute for the transformed body. She has been stripped not only of power but of the visual form through which she can socially participate.

This prepares the later bedroom disclosure: she does not emerge because she has suddenly accepted her original body. She emerges because hiding would consume the last available hours with the others.

# Eiko’s 「仲間じゃない」

Eiko tells Keroppe:

> 「仲間じゃない」
> “They aren’t my companions.”

Her reasoning is precise:

-   she had not officially joined;

-   the girls invented the “apprentice-apprentice” designation;

-   and her proper training only begins after the takeover.


This is clearly defensive legalism.

She avoids describing what happened in relational terms. She does not say:

-   “They meant nothing to me.”

-   “I hate them.”

-   “They mistreated me.”

-   “I wanted to destroy their memories.”


She says the relationship was procedurally unofficial.

That allows her to avoid the word **betrayal**.

## Why formal status matters to Eiko

The previous episode showed the witches absorbing Eiko into their emotional world very quickly:

-   Yuina identifies her anxiety as a wish;

-   they perform for her;

-   Yuina invites her to join;

-   a provisional role is invented;

-   everyone begins imagining six witches.


Kyouka asks what Eiko wants, but the group’s social momentum remains powerful.

For Yuina, rapid inclusion is affection.

For Eiko, whose mother invades her room, searches her trash, and treats her ambitions as available for judgment, unsolicited inclusion may feel like another situation in which others define her status before she has finished defining herself.

Her response is monstrously disproportionate. But the emotional logic is becoming clearer:

> She secures authorship by denying that anyone else acquired a claim upon her.

## Eiko does not appear triumphant

Her posture with Keroppe is comparatively restrained. She does not perform villainous glee over the five girls’ impending loss.

She appears intent on establishing:

-   the formal beginning of her training;

-   her independence from them;

-   and the legitimacy of her chosen wish.


This makes her more dangerous than if she were acting from obvious rage. She has built a moral framework in which what she did does not count as betrayal because she rejects the premise that a mutual relationship existed.

# Keroppe’s hobby explanation

When Eiko asks why he operates the shop, Keroppe compares it to:

-   keeping herbs;

-   raising a hamster;

-   or observing ants.


The humor does not soften the revelation. It clarifies it.

He understands human emotional development aesthetically:

-   girls encounter difficulties;

-   wishes produce conflict;

-   personalities change;

-   teams form and dissolve;

-   and the results are interesting to observe.


The flower shop’s humane ethic was not supplied by its creator.

The five girls produced it themselves through repeated failure.

That is a major cumulative distinction:

Eiko possesses superior magical aptitude and aligns herself more readily with the formal system. The five possess a weaker but ethically richer practice developed in resistance to it.

# Clip 2: Yuina’s home, friendship history, and Azu’s disclosure

## Yuina’s mother is overfamiliar, but creates room

Yuina’s mother enters with considerable energy:

-   food;

-   bedding;

-   toiletries;

-   questions;

-   family communication;

-   and breakfast plans.


She is mildly embarrassing and does not maintain much interpersonal distance. But unlike Eiko’s mother, her intrusion creates capacity for other people.

The contrast between the two mothers is not:

> one respects boundaries and one does not.

Neither is especially restrained.

The difference is what boundary crossing is used for:

-   Eiko’s mother crosses boundaries to inspect, judge, and expose failure.

-   Yuina’s mother crosses boundaries to feed, accommodate, and connect.


This distinction helps explain the daughters.

Eiko learns that exposure leads to evaluation.

Yuina learns that opening the door generally leads to inclusion.

Both carry those assumptions into their relationships.

# Yuina’s history of failed friendship

Yuina’s confession is one of the most important character revelations in the series.

She is not the effortlessly social center that the opening episodes allowed us to assume. She says that she is bad at making friends and describes a recurring pattern:

1.  She becomes intensely enthusiastic.

2.  The other person initially participates.

3.  Yuina fails to notice discomfort.

4.  The relationship gradually cools.

5.  By summer, the other person is avoiding her.

6.  Yuina realizes only after withdrawal has already occurred.


Her exuberance is therefore not confidence built upon repeated success.

It coexists with a history of social rejection she cannot reliably predict.

## Azu mattered because she rejected Yuina directly

Yuina says Azu was unusual because she openly told her:

> that she was annoying;
> that she was intrusive;
> and that she did not want friendship.

That direct hostility was easier for Yuina to navigate than polite gradual avoidance.

Azu gave her usable information.

This reframes their early dynamic. Yuina’s persistence still violated Azu’s stated preference at times, but Azu’s bluntness also created a relationship in which disagreement could remain visible rather than becoming silent disappearance.

The witches have not accepted Yuina because they never find her irritating. They have accepted her while telling her when she is irritating.

That may be the first sustainable peer model she has experienced.

## Her self-blame over Eiko

Yuina concludes that she probably did something similarly overwhelming to Eiko.

Her statement:

> 「親切って、自分じゃなくて相手が決めることだから」
> “Whether something was kind is decided by the recipient, not by the person doing it,”

is evidence of real ethical growth.

But her character acting also suggests the danger of overcorrection. She moves rapidly from:

> “My good intentions do not settle whether my actions were kind”

to:

> “Eiko’s destructive choice was probably my fault.”

Those are not the same proposition.

Yuina needs to learn two complementary limits:

1.  She does not possess the authority to define what another person needs.

2.  She does not possess total responsibility for what another person chooses.


She has learned the first much better than the second.

# The true reason Yuina cannot lose the memories

Yuina initially frames recovery of the shop through apology:

> she wants to find Eiko;
> understand her;
> and apologize for whatever she imposed.

Then the more fundamental truth emerges:

> 「みんなのこと、絶対絶対忘れたくない」
> “I absolutely, absolutely don’t want to forget everyone.”

This is not merely fear of losing a pleasant recent friendship.

The witches are the first group to survive contact with the version of Yuina that previous peers eventually avoided.

For Yuina, forgetting them would mean losing:

-   the relationships;

-   the evidence that she can have lasting friends;

-   and the altered understanding of herself made possible by their staying.


The shop gave her something more important than an “emotional maximum” photograph:

> proof that her intensity does not inevitably end in abandonment.

---

# Azu emerges because silence becomes worse than exposure

The girls initially avoid commenting on Azu’s original body.

Their restraint is compassionate. They know the revelation was involuntary and do not want to turn her body into a discussion topic.

But their silence becomes conspicuous. Azu finally demands:

> 「今のアズ見て、なんかコメントないわけ？」
> “You’re seeing Azu like this—don’t you have anything to say?”

This is an excellent complication of respectful care.

Sometimes refusing to comment communicates:

-   “Your body is not ours to assess.”


But it can also communicate:

-   “Your body is so difficult that we are afraid to acknowledge it.”


Azu wants neither ridicule nor generic reassurance. She wants the others to demonstrate that they can see this body without the social world collapsing.

## Kyouka does not absolve her

Kyouka says that seeing Azu now helps explain why she treated Rinko as she did.

Then she adds that the behavior was still wrong.

This repeats the ethical advance from the Choco arc:

> Context changes understanding without automatically canceling responsibility.

Azu accepts this much better than she could have earlier in the series. She does not need her wound to make her morally innocent before allowing the group to know it.

## Yuina refuses to identify one body as “true”

Yuina says:

> 「アズちゃんがなりたいアズちゃんでいてほしい」
> “I want Azu to be the Azu she wants to be.”

This avoids two inadequate responses:

-   “Your transformed body is fake; accept your true body.”

-   “You were already beautiful, so transformation was unnecessary.”


Both would overwrite Azu’s authorship.

Yuina instead validates the desire to choose a bodily future.

This does not resolve whether Azu’s self-image is healthy, whether becoming thin will heal her shame, or whether her magical ideal is sustainable. It preserves her right to participate in deciding.

# Azu’s ambition has become relational

Azu still wants to:

-   become a witch;

-   become beautiful;

-   and appear publicly in her chosen body.


But the reason now culminates in:

> going out proudly with the others.

Earlier, beauty functioned primarily as proof that she could become lovable and escape the despised body.

Now it is also the condition she imagines for participating in shared life.

That remains painful—she still believes she must change before ordinary public friendship becomes tolerable—but it shows that her desired future is no longer solitary.

The detailed Tokyo itinerary is therefore emotionally significant. She has transformed an abstract body goal into anticipated memories:

-   crepes;

-   shopping;

-   an idol performance;

-   purikura;

-   staying together overnight.


The future Azu wants is not merely a thinner image.

It is an image in motion among friends.

# Clip 5: the ordinary journey back to Maebashi

## Public exposure remains qualitatively different from private disclosure

At Yuina’s home, Azu can reveal herself to four trusted girls.

Outside, she covers nearly everything:

-   hat;

-   mask;

-   dark clothes;

-   lowered posture;

-   and attempts to minimize notice.


She explicitly says the others do not understand what showing her face publicly feels like.

This prevents the sleepover scene from becoming a false cure. Being accepted by friends does not automatically dissolve:

-   body dysphoria;

-   shame;

-   fear of strangers’ judgment;

-   or years of social avoidance.


Private relational safety is not the same as public bodily freedom.

## The makeup conversation restores ordinary peerhood

The girls quickly begin discussing:

-   sunscreen;

-   Kyouka’s naturally effective appearance;

-   Mai’s makeup habits;

-   Azu’s standards;

-   and a future makeup-study meeting.


This matters because the conversation does not remain solemnly centered on Azu’s pain.

Her expertise becomes socially useful without being turned into a trauma lesson. She can once again be:

-   judgmental;

-   knowledgeable;

-   irritated;

-   fashionable;

-   and funny.


The group does not reduce her to “the suffering girl whose body we now know.”

That is precisely the kind of ordinary treatment Choco also wanted after disclosure.

## Azu can teach rather than rank

Earlier, Azu’s aesthetic knowledge often operated vertically:

> she judged who was cute, who was not, and who had failed to optimize herself.

Here, the same knowledge starts moving horizontally:

> she can teach friends how to use makeup.

The underlying skill has not changed. Its social function has.

This mirrors the broader series ethic:

> A personal strength becomes caring not because it is softened into vagueness, but because it is placed at another person’s disposal without erasing her authorship.

# Yuina’s family business as a prehistory of the flower shop

Yuina explains how the photography studio also became a manju shop.

Her grandfather photographed people.

Her mother began serving food to the local older people who gathered there.

The home became a hybrid of:

-   visual memory;

-   sweets;

-   hospitality;

-   neighborhood conversation;

-   and intergenerational gathering.


This is effectively a mundane predecessor to the magical flower shop.

The flower shop combines:

-   emotional interpretation;

-   performance;

-   flowers;

-   food;

-   photography;

-   and local community.


Yuina did not invent her form of care from nothing. She inherits an informal family practice of creating spaces where people gather, are fed, and leave with a record of having been there.

The difference is that Yuina initially treats emotional intensity as something to capture.

Her family history suggests a more durable vocation:

> tending a place in which emotion can happen repeatedly.

# Clip 4: Mai, Choco, and evidence that change survives memory disruption

## Mai’s reaction to Yua is quieter than the event itself

Mai reveals that Yua has followed Kumai again and asked:

> 「もしかしてマイなの」
> “Could this be Mai?”

The important development is not simply that Yua has partly rediscovered the truth.

It is Mai’s response.

The earlier Mai would have:

-   treated this as destiny;

-   replied immediately;

-   converted recognition into a relationship opportunity;

-   and allowed Yua’s attention to occupy her entire mental field.


The current Mai is pleased, but observant.

She notices her own changed response.

That capacity for self-observation is one of the clearest signs of growth. She can hold happiness without surrendering her entire life to it.

## The witches have redistributed Mai’s emotional world

Mai says that the portion of her mind occupied by Yua has become smaller because the others now occupy more of it.

This does not mean she has replaced Yua with a new dependency upon the group.

It means her emotional life has diversified.

Previously:

> Yua’s happiness structured almost every meaningful decision.

Now Mai has:

-   Kumai as an independent creative identity;

-   a game-stream audience;

-   friendship with Azu;

-   the shop;

-   the other witches;

-   and personal standards she can articulate herself.


The episode thereby gives a concrete answer to its memory question:

> Even before explicit recall is threatened, relationships have already reorganized the architecture of the self.

Erasing the names would not straightforwardly restore the earlier architecture.

# Yua’s recognition as emotional residue

The show does not yet explain how Yua suspects Kumai is Mai after the magical reset.

Several possibilities remain:

-   stylistic familiarity;

-   unconscious emotional memory;

-   external clues;

-   or incomplete magical erasure.


But the narrative function is clear:

> Memory removal did not return the relationship to a perfectly untouched state.

The past remains discoverable through the shape it left behind.

Yua’s message is itself a kind of **marker**—a clue pointing toward a relationship she cannot consciously narrate.

# Choco corrects the previous arc’s optimism

Choco reports meaningful improvement:

-   neighbors visit;

-   practical support exists;

-   her grandmother has more energy;

-   and her mother has more room to breathe.


Then she immediately refuses to universalize her success.

Some people she meets have experienced:

-   distancing after disclosure;

-   sympathy without assistance;

-   and no material change.


Choco calls herself:

> 「ラッキーチョコちゃん」
> “Lucky Choco-chan.”

The bright persona remains active, but it is now capable of naming structural inequality.

## “Ask for help” is necessary but incomplete

Episodes 7–8 might be reduced to:

> Speak up and people will help.

Episode 10’s Choco gives the more mature formulation:

> Speaking creates the possibility of help.
> Whether help arrives depends upon the people, institutions, and resources available.

This is a major social refinement.

The burden cannot remain entirely on the suffering person to:

-   disclose correctly;

-   ask courageously;

-   locate services;

-   persuade others;

-   and transform her own circumstances.


A society also has to become answerable to the request.

This connects directly to Kyouka’s mayoral ambition. Choco’s experience gives Kyouka a civic problem:

> How do you make constructive help less dependent upon luck?

# The photograph envelopes

Yuina prepares photographs for the other girls before the deadline.

Photography is now functioning differently from Episode 1.

Initially, Yuina wanted a photograph for herself:

> the ultimate emotional image filling the blank center of her wall.

Now she distributes photographs outward.

The image becomes something each girl can possess separately if their shared conscious history disappears.

That is a substantial maturation of her photographic ethic:

> from capturing others into her archive
> toward giving people markers of their own shared lives.

The unresolved consent issue does not vanish, but the direction has changed. The photograph is no longer only Yuina’s trophy of feeling. It becomes an emergency object of continuity.

# Clip 3: 「メジルシ」 and the progressive collapse of recognition

This clip is the episode’s formal and emotional center.

## The song does not interrupt reality

Unlike the customer performances, **「メジルシ」** does not create a separate magical stage.

It overlays:

-   trains;

-   shopping streets;

-   shutters;

-   phone calls;

-   maps;

-   loneliness;

-   and the ordinary night city.


The song does not transport the girls out of their crisis. It gives musical form to the crisis they are already inhabiting.

That makes it closer to an internal ensemble monologue than a magical intervention.

## The arrangement begins with movement, not triumph

The early sequence has forward momentum:

-   the girls continue searching;

-   the city passes around them;

-   they remain connected through calls;

-   and each personal update offers evidence that the shop mattered.


The song initially seems to organize scattered activity into common purpose.

But as the deadline approaches, its emotional function changes. It stops sounding like a search anthem and becomes an archive being created in real time.

They are no longer confident they can recover the shop.

They begin recording, through speech and song, who they currently are.

# The false shutter

Yuina sees light beneath a shutter and calls everyone before checking.

In motion, the hopeful escalation is painful because the others respond immediately. Everyone wants to believe her.

When it proves false, Yuina’s apology is not merely for wasting time. She believes she has briefly made the others experience the recovery before taking it away.

This connects to her social fear:

> Her enthusiasm raises people’s expectations, then disappoints or overwhelms them.

But the others refuse the pattern she expects.

They do not:

-   mock her;

-   withdraw;

-   or treat the mistake as proof she should stop leading.


They reassure her and continue.

The group’s response is itself evidence of what she cannot bear to forget:

> These are people who survive her mistakes without silently disappearing.

# The final phone call becomes their last stage

When the search becomes hopeless, Yuina insists that they keep the video call active because she wants to see their faces.

The girls are physically separated across Maebashi. Their phones become:

-   stage;

-   audience;

-   mirror;

-   and witness.


Each girl offers a condensed statement of the self produced through the series.

### Kyouka

She retains the mayoral ambition.

This suggests that the dream is no longer merely a magical-shop reaction. It has become narratively integrated into her self-concept.

### Choco

She promises to protect her happiness alongside her family’s happiness.

Her development survives in the grammar of the promise: self and family are coordinated rather than mutually exclusive.

### Mai

She promises to fill her life with things other than Yua and revises her seventy-point rule upward.

She retains both self-compassion and renewed ambition.

### Azu

Azu cannot convert the moment into a socially admirable mission.

She says, in essence:

> I do not want to become alone again.

This is perhaps the least polished and most direct declaration.

Her true fear is no longer simply inhabiting the wrong body.

It is losing the people beside whom she discovered a future.

### Yuina

Yuina supplies the contradiction:

> 「忘れても忘れない」
> “Even if we forget, we won’t forget.”

She does not claim magic will fail.

She claims the event’s existence exceeds their ability to recall it.

# The vocals and phone dialogue coexist

The sequence’s strongest formal decision is that the song does not replace conversation.

The girls’ spoken voices continue entering the musical structure.

This prevents the insert from turning distress into polished spectacle. They remain:

-   breathless;

-   interrupted;

-   crying;

-   trying to reassure one another;

-   and speaking in their ordinary registers.


The song provides coherence without eliminating mess.

That is a concise version of the show’s ideal performance ethic:

> Give emotion form without making the form deny the emotion.

---

# The title phrase changes meaning through repetition

At first:

> “Even if we forget, we won’t forget”

sounds like desperate reassurance.

As the countdown advances, the girls repeat it to one another. It becomes a collective interpretive commitment:

> Whatever happens to our accessible memories, we refuse to define the resulting loss as proof that the relationship never existed.

They cannot control the magical event.

They attempt to control what the event means.

---

# The erasure is depicted as relational disconnection

The countdown reaches zero while the girls are converging in the arcade.

The sequence does not simply cut from:

> friends
> to amnesiac strangers.

Instead, spatial continuity remains while relational continuity breaks.

They occupy the same physical environment, but:

-   eye lines stop connecting;

-   bodies cease orienting toward one another;

-   group movement fragments;

-   and each person proceeds along an individual path.


The city remains.

The girls remain.

The social pattern joining them disappears.

That visual distinction is important:

> Memory erasure does not annihilate bodies or geography. It annihilates the relational map through which those bodies and places had meaning.

## There is no dramatic farewell recognition

The erasure denies them a clean final group embrace or perfectly synchronized goodbye.

They tried to reach one another.

They arrive too late to inhabit the meeting knowingly.

This makes the loss much harsher than a conventional emotional farewell. The story withholds the closure their final declarations seemed to prepare.

---

# Yuina and the orphaned photograph

Yuina ends alone with a photograph that appears to lack an immediately accessible context.

Her body does not react as though the object is completely meaningless. The scene preserves ambiguity:

-   Does she feel familiarity?

-   Does the image create unease or curiosity?

-   Has explicit memory vanished while affect remains?

-   Will the photograph function as a path back?


The episode does not answer.

The photograph is neither a magical cure nor useless paper.

It is precisely what the song title promises:

> a **メジルシ**—a marker.

It says:

> Something is here.
> Someone stood here.
> You once belonged inside this image.
> The route is lost, but not entirely unmarked.

# Why the song’s photography metaphor works

The lyrics invoke:

-   a shutter;

-   images burned into memory;

-   film;

-   negatives;

-   color;

-   and signs left behind.


The episode then distinguishes several stages:

### Event

The girls actually live something together.

### Memory

They retain an internal, narratable record.

### Photograph

A selected external visual trace remains.

### Marker

The trace may no longer explain itself, but it can direct attention toward absence.

### Rediscovery

Someone may follow the marker and reconstruct a relationship—not necessarily identically, but meaningfully.

The photograph does not contain the friendship.

It contains evidence that friendship once gave the world this shape.

# Major revision after the clips

The Episode 10 bundle suggested that the final claim was:

> experiences continue through consequences even after memory loss.

The clips support that, but they add a more relational formulation:

> **A relationship can disappear as conscious recognition while remaining distributed through posture, desire, habit, objects, other people, and the routes each person is already walking.**

The episode spends substantial time showing that distribution before the countdown:

-   Mai no longer gives Yua total psychological control.

-   Yua rediscovers a trace of Mai.

-   Choco’s family support remains active.

-   Kyouka retains civic ambition.

-   Azu has created a future itinerary.

-   Yuina’s mother has encountered the group.

-   photographs have been distributed.

-   customers’ lives have changed.

-   the girls know Maebashi differently.


Keroppe can delete the private index connecting each consequence to “witch training.”

He cannot trivially recall every consequence from the world.

# Revised character readings

## Yuina

Yuina’s defining wound is now clearer:

> She repeatedly enters relationships without perceiving the moment the other person wants distance, then discovers abandonment after it has already taken place.

Her photography, declarations of friendship, and insistence upon emotional peaks can all be read partly as attempts to secure relationships before they quietly disappear.

Her growth lies in learning:

-   consent;

-   patience;

-   privacy;

-   and that care is defined by the person receiving it.


Her remaining danger is totalized self-blame.

## Azu

The group’s acceptance does not cause Azu to abandon transformation.

Instead, it changes the imagined purpose of transformation from solitary self-validation into shared public life.

Her candid admission that she cannot bear being alone again suggests that friendship has become more foundational than aesthetic victory, even though bodily shame remains severe.

## Mai

Mai is the clearest evidence that personal change has become structurally embedded.

Her reaction to Yua has changed before any final magical test. She has become capable of receiving Yua’s attention without reorganizing her entire self around it.

## Choco

Choco advances from personal help-seeking into social analysis.

She understands that disclosure is not an individually sufficient solution and that successful care depends upon response capacity.

This makes her experience relevant to Kyouka’s political future.

## Eiko

Eiko treats formal nonmembership as proof that no relational betrayal occurred.

Her position is the inverse of the episode’s thesis.

Eiko says:

> If the relationship was not officially constituted, it did not bind me.

The witches say:

> Even if the relationship is no longer consciously remembered, it still occurred and still binds the shape of our lives.

Eiko privileges formal status over lived relation.

The five privilege lived relation over formal and even mnemonic continuity.

## Keroppe

Keroppe’s institution can regulate:

-   access;

-   points;

-   transformation;

-   employment status;

-   and explicit memory.


It cannot fully regulate meaning.

That is the limit of his power exposed by Episode 10.

# Final synthesis

The Episode 10 clips confirm that this is not simply the low point before the girls inevitably recover their memories.

The episode stages a serious philosophical test:

> If everything consciously remembered about a friendship vanished, what exactly would remain?

Its answer is not a mystical claim that true friends can never forget.

The answer is more material and more interesting:

-   changed habits remain;

-   decisions already made remain;

-   other people’s memories remain;

-   institutions contacted remain;

-   relationships outside the group remain altered;

-   objects remain;

-   photographs remain;

-   desires developed through friendship may remain;

-   and the person who has forgotten is not identical to the person who existed before the friendship.


The episode’s strongest proposition is therefore:

> **Forgetting can remove access to a shared past, but it cannot turn the resulting present into the present that would have existed without that past.**

The memories are lost.

The lives shaped by them are still here.
