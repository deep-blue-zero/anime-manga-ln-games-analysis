### “Is that seventy percent of one hundred percent?”

# Episode summary

## Yua overhears what the beauty-contest staff really think

The episode opens during a remote beauty-contest production meeting. Staff members believe the schedule is running late and casually discuss the finalists during what they think is a break.

They call this year’s lineup mediocre and suggest that beauty contests themselves no longer attract much interest. What the event really needs, they say, is an influencer such as Kumai-chan.

One person asks what contestants outside first place even expect to accomplish.

Their microphone is still active.

Yua hears everything.

She looks at her fourth-place ranking and initially tries to apply her usual language:

> 「4位よき…」
> “Fourth place is good…”

Then corrects herself:

> 「くない。微妙すぎ」
> “No, it isn’t. It’s far too mediocre.”

The staff members have effectively taught her that participation without victory has no value.

## Mai gives Yua the Kumai account

Yua goes to Mai’s home. Mai hands her the login information and password for the Kumai account.

Yua asks whether this is really acceptable and briefly apologizes for falling into her habitual **よき** persona. Mai assures her that it is fine.

Mai almost explains what the account originally meant, then stops:

> 「今日から優愛姉ぇが熊井ってことで」
> “From today onward, Yua-nee is Kumai.”

Mai does not merely lend Yua her audience. She formally transfers the identity.

As Yua leaves, Mai tells her:

> 「私、優愛姉ぇをずっと応援してるからね」
> “I’ll always support you, Yua-nee.”

## The other witches discover the transfer

At the shop, Choco and Yuina are initially dazzled by the revelation that Mai was Kumai. Their enthusiasm quickly gives way to the unresolved issue of the points she stole.

Azu points out that the register contains only one remaining Mapo.

Mai apologizes again. Kyouka observes that after being confronted in Azu’s manner, apologizing is effectively the only response Mai is permitted to give. The group still does not understand her motive.

They then notice that Yua, rather than Mai, is appearing on the Kumai account.

Mai admits that she gave it to her because Yua asked.

Azu suspects blackmail or manipulation. Mai insists that it was her decision and says:

> 「そもそも熊井は優愛姉ぇのために始めたようなものだから」
> “Kumai was practically something I started for Yua-nee in the first place.”

The others immediately ask what that means, but Mai again withholds the full story.

## Granting a wish outside the shop earns nothing

The girls expect Mapo because Mai apparently fulfilled Yua’s wish.

Keroppe explains that they receive points only for conducting magical work through the flower shop’s stage. Helping someone elsewhere does not count.

He compares their expectation to working at McDonald’s and then asking Starbucks to pay the wage.

This reveals that Mapo are not a metaphysical measure of whether good has been accomplished. They are compensation issued by a specific magical labor system.

Mai may have given Yua precisely what she asked for, but the platform does not recognize the act.

## Zero points means dismissal

Kyouka asks what happens if their balance reaches zero.

Keroppe casually reveals another major term of their employment:

> At zero Mapo, their training is classified as a failure and terminated.

They will cease being witch apprentices and return to being ordinary girls.

For Azu this is catastrophic. Losing access to witchhood means losing the possibility of permanently maintaining her chosen body outside the shop. Choco also immediately rejects the prospect of losing magic.

Keroppe activates an austerity mode. The flower shop loses its flowers, furniture, light, and much of its color. Every fixture and supply has been sustained through magical expenditure.

Even the sanctuary in which they help people has operating costs.

The remaining one point is therefore not just a score. It is the final unit holding the shop—and their group identity—together.

## Yua temporarily prospers as Kumai

A flashback shows Mai and Yua as children happily playing games at Mai’s house. Yua is confident, affectionate, and competitive, while Mai studies strategies in hopes of defeating her.

In the present, Yua posts as Kumai and begins teasing a connection between Kumai and her own beauty-contest identity. Her ranking improves, and she assumes first place is approaching.

Meanwhile, two weeks pass without another customer entering the flower shop. The girls distribute flyers daily, but the register repeatedly threatens to fall to zero.

The contrast is severe:

-   Mai’s borrowed social capital produces immediate visible results for Yua.

-   The witches’ ordinary outreach work produces almost nothing.

-   The magical workplace approaches collapse while the appropriated influencer identity appears successful.


## Mai considers sacrificing herself to restore the points

While distributing flyers, Mai tells the others that she intends to ask Keroppe whether the Mapo can somehow be restored. She is prepared to do “anything” if the cost can be placed upon her alone.

Yuina immediately rejects that formulation:

> Mai herself cannot be what gets “used up” to balance the account.

Kyouka then asks whether Yua was the reason Mai spent the points.

Mai admits that she used magic to submit extra beauty-contest votes. The contest allowed one vote per person each day, so she used Mapo to cast more.

The theft was therefore not merely related to maintaining Kumai. Mai consumed a communal resource to cheat on Yua’s behalf.

## Yua created the 70-point philosophy

Mai finally explains the relationship.

As a child, she had difficulty fitting into school. She tried to do everything perfectly, became exhausted, and began struggling to attend.

Yua came to see her every day and told her:

> 「100点取ろうとするから疲れちゃうんだって」
> “You’re exhausted because you’re trying to score one hundred.”

> 「70点くらいでいいんだって」
> “Around seventy is enough.”

Mai asks whether seventy can really be enough.

Yua answers:

> 「それだけ取りゃ十分すぎ」
> “If you can get that much, it’s more than enough.”

This is the origin of both Mai’s philosophy and Kumai’s brand.

Yua gave Mai permission not to be perfect. Mai says that everything she has become followed from that intervention:

-   she relaxed;

-   daily life became enjoyable;

-   she studied fashion because Yua had begun liking it;

-   she created a social-media presence because Yua was trying to gain followers;

-   and she became a witch because Keroppe said it might allow her to become Yua’s closest friend.


Mai then admits something more disturbing:

> Even after endangering the entire group, she believes she would use their Mapo again if Yua asked.

## Yua’s impersonation is exposed

The girls happen to see Yua nearby.

Her attempt to become Kumai has failed. Kumai’s existing followers recognize that the account no longer sounds or behaves like the person they followed. They accuse the new Kumai of being fake.

Yua’s ranking falls, she receives hostile messages, and her university life becomes embarrassing. She declares that she will quit the beauty contest and may be unable to return to university.

She blames Mai:

> 「全部マイのせいだからね」
> “This is all your fault.”

Mai nevertheless asks her to enter the shop. She promises to fix everything through magic.

Keroppe warns that Yua cannot be forced inside. The door will open only if she herself has a wish she wants fulfilled.

Mai tells Yua that she knows Yua does not want things to remain this way.

The door opens.

## Yua rejects Mai’s interpretation of their friendship

Inside the darkened, nearly bankrupt shop, Yua denies that she and Mai were ever as close as Mai believes.

She says she visited because Mai’s home contained:

-   many games;

-   cosmetics belonging to Mai’s older sister;

-   and interesting accessories.


From Yua’s perspective, they were children who happened to play together.

Mai has built her identity around a relationship Yua experienced much more casually.

Yua then explains why Mai’s sacrifices disturb her:

> 「ずっとあんたのおかげでよきになったって
> 私に思い続けさせたいの？」
> “Do you want to make me keep thinking forever that everything good about me came from you?”

She interprets the account transfer and magical offer as a form of indebtedness. Mai’s generosity makes Yua feel that she is being assigned the role of the person whom Mai saved, elevated, and eternally worships.

## The group debates whether support can become complicity

Azu points out Yua’s contradiction: if she wants nothing from Mai, why did she willingly enter a wish-granting shop?

Yuina then tries to reason through the problem aloud.

She believes people should ideally accomplish meaningful things through their own effort. She also understands that Mai desperately wanted to support someone important to her. But supporting Mai’s desire meant indirectly supporting cheating and identity theft.

Yuina cannot find a clean answer.

Mai rejects the entire moral framework:

> 「ズルの何がダメなの」
> “What’s wrong with cheating?”

> 「私的には、優愛姉ぇが喜んでくれれば
> 手段はなんでもいいし」
> “As far as I’m concerned, if Yua-nee is happy, any method is fine.”

Her morality has become entirely relational. Right and wrong are subordinate to Yua’s happiness.

Yua calls this devotion too heavy.

Mai asks:

> 「重くちゃダメなの？」
> “Is it wrong for it to be heavy?”

She says she has always loved Yua and that she performs these sacrifices because she wants to.

Yua answers:

> 「私にそこまでする価値ないから」
> “I’m not worth doing that much for.”

Mai refuses Yua’s evaluation:

> 「私にとっては価値ありすぎ」
> “To me, you’re worth far too much.”

> 「むしろ価値しかないから」
> “You’re nothing but value to me.”

Mai asks Yua to let her bloom her heart-flower.

# Central thesis

Episode 5 asks what happens when gratitude becomes the architecture of an entire identity.

Yua once helped Mai at a crucial moment. That kindness was real.

Mai subsequently turned it into:

-   a life philosophy;

-   a fashion identity;

-   a professional-scale social-media account;

-   a reason to study witchcraft;

-   and a lifelong obligation to make Yua happy.


The episode’s central distinction is:

> **A person can genuinely save your life without acquiring the right—or accepting the responsibility—to become your entire world.**

Mai’s devotion is sincere. It is also suffocating.

Yua’s rejection is cruelly expressed. It is also a legitimate boundary.

# The cold open: the beauty contest does not care about beauty

The staff conversation exposes the contest as a content-production system.

They do not ask:

-   who has worked hardest;

-   who possesses an interesting personality;

-   who has a meaningful reason to compete;

-   or what the finalists want.


They ask who can generate attention.

An influencer would make the event more marketable. Lower-ranked contestants are treated as people whose participation has no coherent purpose.

This is the institutional counterpart to Yua’s follower-count worldview. She did not invent the idea that visibility determines human usefulness. The surrounding media system openly teaches it.

Yua’s reaction to fourth place is telling. Fourth place is not failure in any ordinary sense. But it occupies an intolerable middle:

-   visible enough to be judged;

-   not successful enough to gain prestige;

-   too high to be anonymous;

-   too low to become the story.


It is almost exactly a “70-point” position.

Yua cannot tolerate being good but unexceptional because the people producing the contest have just called such contestants pointless.

---

# Why the Kumai transfer fails

The followers recognize that Yua is not Kumai.

This demonstrates that an account’s value is not simply stored in its follower number. Kumai consists of:

-   Mai’s judgment;

-   her writing voice;

-   her practical knowledge;

-   her selection of affordable items;

-   her understanding of audience anxieties;

-   and the trust produced through repeated consistency.


Yua receives the credentials but not the authorship.

The failed impersonation proves:

> **Social capital can be transferred technically without being transferred relationally.**

Yua had previously treated followers as a visible resource that could identify useful people. Episode 5 reveals that the number depends upon an invisible relationship between creator and audience.

The account cannot simply be worn like an outfit.

---

# Mai did not create Kumai accidentally

Episode 4 left open whether Mai’s account was merely a defensive hiding place. Episode 5 shows that it began as an attempt to remain connected to Yua.

Yua moved from games toward fashion and social media. Mai followed.

This gives Kumai a painful double meaning.

It is genuinely Mai’s creation:

-   the expertise is hers;

-   the labor is hers;

-   the audience trusts her;

-   and the account’s philosophy reflects her experience.


But the original motivational center is Yua.

Mai constructed an autonomous creative identity while believing she was merely building a bridge toward someone else.

When she gives the account away, she treats her own authorship as disposable because she still understands the product as belonging, in some spiritual sense, to the person who inspired it.

That is why the transfer is not simple generosity. It is self-erasure.

---

# The difference between being inspired by someone and belonging to them

Yua’s childhood advice genuinely transformed Mai:

> Seventy points can be enough.

But Mai does not merely retain the advice. She treats every subsequent development as an extension of Yua.

This creates a distorted chain:

-   Yua taught me to relax.

-   Therefore my happiness comes from Yua.

-   Yua became interested in fashion.

-   Therefore I must learn fashion.

-   Yua wants followers.

-   Therefore I must build an account.

-   Yua wants to win.

-   Therefore my account, magic, friends’ resources, and integrity may be sacrificed.

-   Keroppe says I might become her closest friend.

-   Therefore witchhood exists to secure that position.


Yua becomes the implied author of Mai’s whole life.

Yua is uncomfortable because this deprives her of an ordinary relationship. She cannot merely be:

-   an old friend;

-   someone who drifted away;

-   someone selfish;

-   someone who once did a good thing;

-   or someone Mai eventually outgrows.


She has been made into Mai’s foundational myth.

---

# Yua’s denial is partly true and partly defensive

Yua says she only visited Mai’s home because it was entertaining.

That probably contains truth. The relationship was not equally central to both girls.

But it does not necessarily mean her childhood kindness was false. The flashbacks show genuine warmth, play, and concern. Yua may now be minimizing the relationship because Mai’s interpretation has become intolerably large.

The same experiences can carry radically different weight:

-   For Yua, visiting Mai was one pleasant part of childhood.

-   For Mai, Yua’s visits made school and life survivable.


Neither memory has to be fabricated for the asymmetry to hurt.

Yua’s mistake is treating unequal significance as proof that the relationship meant nothing.

Mai’s mistake is treating profound significance as proof that Yua must accept permanent intimacy.

---

# Self-sacrifice can still violate another person’s boundaries

Mai insists:

> 「私がそうしたい」
> “I’m doing it because I want to.”

This invokes the self-authorship ethic established in Episode 3. Azu decides what body is right for Azu. Rinko decides what work she will accept. Why should Mai not decide how much she gives?

Episode 5 identifies a limit:

> **A self-chosen sacrifice can still place an unwanted role, debt, or obligation upon another person.**

Mai’s sacrifices tell Yua:

-   You are infinitely valuable.

-   You are responsible for the person I became.

-   Your happiness justifies my wrongdoing.

-   I must remain your closest person.

-   Rejecting my help means rejecting the meaning of my life.


Yua has not consented to being the object of that devotion.

Mai does not demand repayment in ordinary material terms. But the emotional structure implicitly demands that Yua validate the relationship’s magnitude.

---

# 「価値しかない」 and the persistence of numerical thinking

Episode 4 criticized Yua for turning people into follower numbers.

Mai appears to reject this worldview by assigning Yua infinite personal value. But she is still quantifying.

> 「価値しかない」
> “You are nothing but value.”

This sounds loving, yet it removes everything about Yua that does not fit Mai’s valuation.

Yua can be selfish, insecure, cruel, ordinary, unsuccessful, or simply unwilling to remain close. Mai’s language has no room for those realities because Yua is defined as pure value.

Yua turned people into numbers to determine whether they were useful.

Mai turns Yua into an absolute value to determine whether every sacrifice is justified.

Both approaches struggle to encounter the other person as an unstable, limited human being.

# The title: whose one hundred percent?

## 「それ100％の70％？」

The title does more than continue the numerical joke from Episode 4.

The crucial question is not merely whether seventy percent is sufficient. It is:

> **Seventy percent of what—and whose definition of one hundred?**

Mai believes she lives according to a moderate philosophy. She does not chase perfection. She accepts “good enough.”

But nearly everything in her life has been organized around Yua:

-   her philosophy;

-   fashion interests;

-   social-media work;

-   witch training;

-   use of magic;

-   and desired friendship hierarchy.


Mai may be living at seventy percent effort while giving Yua one hundred percent authority over the denominator.

Her “relaxed” life is still calibrated against someone else.

## Other meanings of the title

The phrase also echoes several numerical distortions:

-   Yua’s 6,861 followers were approximately seventy percent of ten thousand, but she presented them as essentially ten thousand.

-   Kumai offers seventy-point fashion through work requiring much greater expertise.

-   Yua once gave Mai one piece of advice; Mai interprets it as the source of virtually one hundred percent of her present life.

-   Yua receives one hundred percent of Mai’s account despite contributing none of its authorship.

-   Mai describes herself as an NPC while giving Yua the entire protagonist role.


Episode 4 asked whether aiming for seventy was acceptable.

Episode 5 asks whether the supposed seventy is even honest when the person has never defined her own one hundred.

---

# Keroppe’s recruitment of Mai

Mai reveals that Keroppe told her becoming a witch could allow her to become Yua’s closest friend.

This is one of the most ethically troubling recruitment pitches so far.

Keroppe offered:

-   Azu control over her desired embodiment.

-   Mai control over relational proximity.


A body can at least be directly transformed. Another person’s affection cannot ethically be guaranteed.

Keroppe appears to identify a girl’s deepest vulnerability and frame witch training as its solution. Even if he does not literally promise mind control, he encourages Mai to believe that accumulating magical status can solve an asymmetrical relationship.

The remote 99,999-point target then keeps each girl working toward the psychological need through which she was recruited.

---

# The Mapo economy becomes openly coercive

Episode 5 clarifies that Mapo serve all of the following functions:

-   wages;

-   promotion points;

-   magical fuel;

-   shop rent;

-   inventory costs;

-   utilities;

-   and the group’s continued employment.


At zero, they are dismissed.

This means the girls cannot simply save every point toward full witchhood. Their accumulated labor must constantly pay the operating expenses required to perform more labor.

The shop resembles a precarious business whose workers are also responsible for its capitalization.

Keroppe discloses the risk only when they are one point from termination.

Azu’s suspicion that the system is exploitative continues to be justified.

## The unpaid wish

Mai gives Yua the account, but the magical system awards nothing because the act occurred outside the shop.

This proves decisively that points do not measure moral good or customer satisfaction. They measure labor performed under authorized conditions.

The system does not ask:

> Did someone’s wish come true?

It asks:

> Was the approved magical service process used?

That distinction resembles platform labor, where socially useful activity remains uncompensated unless performed through the platform’s recognized transaction.

---

# The flower shop in austerity mode

The visual transformation of the shop is one of the episode’s strongest images.

Its flowers disappear. Furniture and ornamentation vanish. The light becomes cold and dark. The register remains as the central surviving object because the accounting mechanism outlasts the beauty and care it supposedly supports.

The sequence reveals that the flower shop’s emotional warmth is infrastructure.

Its welcoming appearance was sustained by prior labor.

This also parallels Mai:

-   Kumai’s approachable seventy-point advice conceals extraordinary labor.

-   The flower shop’s effortless fantasy conceals continuous resource consumption.

-   Yua’s fashionable effortlessness conceals desperation and calculation.


Nothing that appears naturally comfortable is actually costless.

---

# Mai and cheating

Mai says that if Yua is happy, the method does not matter.

This is the endpoint of her relational morality.

She:

-   steals communal points;

-   violates a one-person-one-vote rule;

-   gives away an identity trusted by 120,000 people;

-   endangers the other witches’ training;

-   and remains willing to repeat the behavior.


Her devotion has not merely become excessive. It has displaced every obligation except the one she feels toward Yua.

This also explains her earlier willingness to be called a thief. “Thief” is acceptable if theft serves Yua. The moral condemnation cannot reach her while Yua remains the higher value.

The episode does not romanticize this as pure loyalty.

---

# Yua’s wish

Yua’s apparent wishes include:

-   winning the contest;

-   obtaining Kumai’s audience;

-   escaping the impersonation scandal;

-   restoring her university life;

-   and avoiding humiliation.


The performance resolves something more fundamental.

Yua appears to choose to erase or abandon everything gained through the shortcut:

-   the flower-shop intervention;

-   the magical assistance;

-   the Kumai impersonation;

-   and the borrowed path to popularity.


She returns to posting selfies under her own identity.

The others infer that she has decided to try seriously through her own ability.

Her wish can therefore be formulated as:

> **Yua wishes to escape both the humiliating failure of the borrowed identity and the debt created by Mai’s total devotion, so that she can attempt to become valuable through a self she actually authored.**

This does not necessarily mean she has become morally mature. She may still be vain, status-conscious, and unfair to Mai.

But she rejects the stolen protagonist role.

# 「You Are My World」

## A song about devotion that knows it is unhealthy

The insert song is not a straightforward friendship anthem.

Its opening image is:

> 「僕の蕾の中で 溢れ出す思い出が
> 全部君の色に 染まってく」
> “Inside my bud, the overflowing memories are all dyed in your color.”

Mai’s heart-flower has not developed its own color. Every memory is absorbed into Yua.

The title follows:

> **You Are My World**

Not “you are part of my world.”

Not “we share a world.”

Yua is the world.

## Protagonist and NPC

The lyrics state:

> 「君が主役のストーリー」
> “It is a story in which you are the protagonist.”

> 「僕は所詮NPCだって 気づいてる」
> “I know that I’m ultimately only an NPC.”

The video-game imagery literalizes this:

-   Yua is labeled **1P**.

-   Mai is labeled **NPC**.

-   childhood memories appear as game environments;

-   clothing and identities become equipment;

-   progress is expressed through ranks and levels.


An NPC exists to serve the player’s experience. It may offer guidance, items, healing, or narrative motivation, but it is not expected to possess an independent story.

This is exactly how Mai treats herself.

She does not merely think Yua is more important. She imagines her own purpose as facilitating Yua’s playthrough.

## “How can I stay with you forever?”

The song asks:

> 「どうすれば君と一緒に
> ずっと過ごせるのだろう？」
> “What would let me remain with you forever?”

Mai’s decisions become possible answers:

-   study Yua’s interests;

-   become fashionable;

-   create an account;

-   provide followers;

-   cheat for her;

-   become a witch;

-   become indispensable.


The goal is not mutual discovery. It is preventing distance.

## “It’s fine if my feelings never reach you”

Mai then claims:

> 「この想いはきっと届かない
> それでもいいの」
> “These feelings probably will not reach you. Even so, that is fine.”

But the episode shows that it is not fine.

Mai has constructed her entire life around making the feelings material:

-   the account;

-   the votes;

-   the magic;

-   the flower shop;

-   and the song itself.


The claim that she expects nothing in return is contradicted by her need to become Yua’s number one.

## From one protagonist to two

The late lyric introduces a healthier possibility:

> 「僕たちなら 世界中愛す主役になれるさ」
> “The two of us can become protagonists loved throughout the world.”

For the first time, the grammar allows both girls to be protagonists.

This is the song’s possible way out:

> Mai does not need to dethrone Yua.
> She needs to stop defining herself as an NPC.

The performance does not fully accomplish that development, but it makes the alternative visible.

## “BITTER END”

The visual performance concludes with a game-like **BITTER END**, rather than an unqualified happy ending.

Yua apologizes and says:

> 「やっぱ重い」
> “It’s still too heavy.”

This is crucial.

The magical song communicates Mai’s feelings with spectacular force, but it does not compel Yua to reciprocate them. Emotional honesty does not automatically make the other person responsible for accepting the relationship.

Magic grants expression.

It does not grant ownership of another person.

---

# The performance centers Mai but fulfills Yua’s wish

This is one of the show’s most complicated interventions so far.

The song is overwhelmingly about Mai:

-   her memories;

-   her worship;

-   her NPC self-image;

-   and her longing to remain beside Yua.


But the customer is Yua.

The outcome is not that Yua finally understands how wonderful Mai is and embraces her. The outcome is that Yua rejects the borrowed identity and leaves.

The magical process therefore does not validate Mai’s devotion as the correct answer.

It reveals it clearly enough that Yua can answer honestly.

That honesty hurts Mai, but it also prevents their relationship from continuing through unspoken debt and deception.

---

# “Still too heavy” is not a failed resolution

Yua’s response can seem brutally dismissive after Mai’s vulnerable song. But the series needs that refusal.

Had Yua responded by accepting Mai as her closest friend, the episode would imply that sufficient emotional sincerity entitles a person to reciprocity.

Instead:

> Mai’s feelings are real.
> Yua’s boundary is also real.

Neither truth cancels the other.

This is consistent with Episode 3’s treatment of self-authorship. Azu may define her body, but she may not define Rinko’s. Mai may define the magnitude of her feelings, but she may not define Yua’s required response.

---

# The real resolution is uncertainty

After Yua leaves, Mai asks whether all her involvement was simply unwanted interference.

She assumes Yua no longer wants anything to do with her.

Kyouka—or the group’s more grounded reasoning—acknowledges that this may be true. They cannot know what Yua thinks.

The important question becomes:

> What does Mai want now?

Mai cannot answer.

She still wants to be Yua’s number one. She is also furious, humiliated, and hurt by how Yua treated her.

Yuina proposes:

> Put the question on hold.

Continue training. When Mai eventually becomes a witch, she can decide whether becoming Yua’s closest friend is still what she wants.

Mai calls this extremely arbitrary.

Yuina responds that arbitrariness may be acceptable:

> Friendship can operate through the mood and needs of the present rather than an eternal, fully articulated contract.

This is the healthier version of seventy points.

---

# Provisional friendship versus total friendship

Mai’s model of friendship demands:

-   permanence;

-   hierarchy;

-   complete devotion;

-   proof through sacrifice;

-   and the status of “number one.”


Yuina offers a lighter alternative:

-   remain together now;

-   admit uncertainty;

-   postpone irreversible decisions;

-   allow feelings to change;

-   and accept that the relationship need not be one hundred percent defined.


This is not emotional shallowness.

It is a refusal to make ordinary friendship carry the weight of absolute salvation.

The episode’s answer to Mai’s excessive devotion is not indifference. It is **provisional commitment**.

---

# Mai’s first desire independent of Yua

Mai finally says she does not want to stop witch training:

> 「わりとここ、落ち着くし」
> “I feel reasonably comfortable here.”

This may be the most important line in her arc so far.

She does not say:

-   the shop will help her regain Yua;

-   becoming a witch will make her Yua’s number one;

-   or she must continue because Keroppe promised something.


She says she likes being there.

It is modest language—characteristically around seventy percent—but it is hers.

The flower shop has become a place Mai values independently of the person around whom she previously organized everything.

---

# The celebratory drink

The girls decide to spend some newly earned Mapo on the elaborate drink they could not afford earlier.

Azu objects that immediately consuming their scarce resource is irresponsible.

# Cumulative reading after five episodes

The series has now moved through a clear ethical progression.

### Episode 1: Do not replace someone’s agency

Eiko does not want magic to accomplish her ambition for her.

### Episodes 2–3: Do not project your desired self onto someone else

Azu cannot assume Rinko wants the transformation Azu chose.

### Episodes 4–5: Do not turn love into entitlement or self-erasure

Yua cannot claim Mai’s authored identity because they are friends.

Mai cannot obligate Yua to become the center of a relationship merely because Mai’s devotion is sincere.

The broader thesis can now be refined:

> *Maebashi Witches* is about learning that self-authorship requires boundaries in both directions. A person has the right to choose her ambitions, body, image, work, and relationships—but not to use that choice to overwrite another person’s agency or erase her own.\*

Episode 5’s most important lesson is not “seventy percent is enough.”

It is:

> **You must first possess a self-defined one hundred percent before choosing what seventy percent means.**

## Yua wish / performance interpretation refinement

I think your reading is substantially right. The important refinement is that Yua’s wish is not merely “I want to undo the scandal.” It is closer to:

> **I want to erase the entire chain of borrowed success, indebtedness, and emotional exploitation that has made this relationship unbearable, and return to a point where I can act as myself.**

The insert-song character acting is what makes that interpretation persuasive. Yua does not leave the performance merely angry at Mai’s “heaviness.” She comes to understand, with increasing horror and guilt, what Mai has made of their relationship—and what Yua herself has taken from it.

# Yua’s emotional progression during the song

## 1\. She begins as a defensive observer

At the opening of the performance, Yua is standing apart from the witches. Her expression is guarded and slightly suspicious. She has already spent the preceding scene minimizing their childhood relationship and accusing Mai of making her feel perpetually indebted.

She is therefore prepared to interpret Mai’s behavior as another attempt to impose a narrative:

-   Yua saved Mai;

-   Mai owes everything to Yua;

-   Mai’s devotion proves their friendship is uniquely important;

-   Yua must acknowledge that importance.


Initially, she is not entering the song with a receptive “please heal me” posture. She is watching to determine what Mai is trying to make her accept.

## 2\. The childhood images break through her denial

The first major visual reconstruction shows the two girls playing games together as children.

Yua had just insisted that she visited mostly because Mai’s house had games, cosmetics, and interesting things. That was probably partly true. But the performance shows that the relationship also contained real warmth.

Yua’s expression softens because the song does not simply refute her. It presents the emotional truth of those memories as Mai experienced them.

The painful discovery is not:

> “Mai invented everything.”

It is:

> “The same ordinary childhood interactions that were casual to me became foundational to her.”

Yua can no longer dismiss Mai’s attachment as a manipulative fabrication. The attachment is real precisely because the memories themselves were real—just radically unequal in significance.

# The 1P/NPC sequence

The most devastating portion of the performance is the game metaphor:

-   Yua is labeled **1P**.

-   Mai is labeled **NPC**.

-   Yua’s story advances.

-   Mai exists to provide support, equipment, advice, and emotional reinforcement.

-   The lyrics explicitly call Yua the protagonist and Mai “only an NPC.”


Yua’s face during this section is not flattered.

She looks disturbed because the performance confirms the accusation she had been making in less precise language:

> Mai has not simply loved Yua. Mai has built a world in which Yua is entitled to be the only fully realized person.

That is initially favorable to Yua, but it is also an intolerable responsibility.

If Mai is an NPC, then every sacrifice appears natural:

-   give Yua the account;

-   spend the group’s Mapo;

-   cheat in the contest;

-   erase Mai’s authorship;

-   endanger Mai’s friendships;

-   become a witch solely to remain near Yua.


The song communicates that Mai has reduced herself willingly. Yua now sees that accepting Mai’s gifts means accepting that reduction.

That is one source of the guilt visible on her face.

# Why Yua’s guilt is complicated

Yua undoubtedly recognizes that she exploited Mai.

She:

-   used friendship as leverage to demand the account;

-   accepted labor she did not create;

-   appropriated Mai’s audience and identity;

-   blamed Mai when the impersonation failed;

-   and entered the shop expecting Mai to rescue her again.


The song makes it impossible for her to pretend that she was merely accepting a convenient gift from an equal friend. She has been consuming the work of someone who believes her own existence is secondary.

But guilt is only part of Yua’s reaction.

She is also frightened by what Mai’s devotion demands of her. Mai says she expects nothing, yet everything she has done implicitly asks Yua to validate the premise that:

> Yua is Mai’s world.

That places Yua in an emotionally impossible position. If she accepts the devotion, she reinforces Mai’s self-erasure. If she refuses it, she appears to reject the person whose life was shaped by loving her.

Her pained expression therefore contains at least three things at once:

-   remorse for exploiting Mai;

-   horror at the extent of Mai’s self-subordination;

-   and a desperate need to escape the role of Mai’s absolute value.


# The flower presentation

The close acting when Mai offers Yua the flower is the emotional center of the performance.

Mai approaches with an open, tender expression. She is not accusing Yua. She is presenting her devotion as a gift.

Yua initially looks directly at her, startled and emotionally exposed. She then lowers and averts her eyes.

That avoidance is crucial.

She does not reject the flower with contempt. She appears unable to receive it.

Accepting the flower would mean accepting everything placed inside it:

-   Mai’s childhood gratitude;

-   Mai’s anonymous labor;

-   Mai’s belief that Yua is the protagonist;

-   Mai’s desire to remain beside her forever;

-   and the proposal that they might now become protagonists together.


Yua has just understood that Mai’s generosity is not free. Not because Mai consciously demands repayment, but because the gift contains Mai’s whole self.

The lyric at that point moves toward:

> 「僕たちなら 世界中愛す主役になれるさ」
> “The two of us can become protagonists loved throughout the world.”

This is the song’s attempt to imagine a healthier relationship: not player and NPC, but two protagonists.

But Yua cannot simply accept that transition. The song has spent most of its duration revealing that Mai does not presently experience herself as an equal protagonist. The promise of equality is aspirational, not accomplished.

Yua understands that taking Mai’s hand at that moment would not automatically create mutuality.

# 「もう一人じゃないと 伝わらないかな？」

The final lyric asks, approximately:

> “Will it not get through that you’re no longer alone?”

In another context, this would be comforting. Here it is painfully double-edged.

Mai means:

> You do not have to struggle alone. I will remain beside you.

Yua hears something closer to:

> You cannot escape being the center of my world.

This is why her averted gaze is so important. The companionship Mai offers is sincere, but Yua experiences its absolute form as enclosing rather than liberating.

# 「ごめん。やっぱ重い」

Yua’s delivery after the performance should not be read as a snide dismissal.

She begins with:

> 「ごめん」
> “I’m sorry.”

Only then:

> 「やっぱ重い」
> “It’s still too heavy.”

The apology acknowledges that she has understood Mai.

That is what makes the refusal harsher but also more honest. She is not rejecting Mai because the song failed to communicate the devotion. She is rejecting the relationship in its current form because the song communicated it completely.

The magical performance succeeds.

The hoped-for reconciliation does not.

That distinction is essential.

# What Yua’s wish appears to be

Afterward, the girls observe:

> 「なかったことにした」
> “She made it so it never happened.”

They list:

-   coming to the shop;

-   using magic;

-   impersonating Kumai;

-   and the surrounding sequence of events.


Yua then resumes posting selfies under her own identity.

So your formulation—“I want to go back so none of this happened”—is very close to what the episode communicates.

I would be slightly more cautious about saying that she definitively **forgot everything**. The episode does not explicitly state:

> Yua’s memories were erased.

The Japanese **なかったことにする** can mean to annul, undo, erase, or treat something as though it never occurred. In a magical context, it may indeed represent a genuine timeline-like rollback, but the exact metaphysics are left unclear.

The safest reading is:

> **Yua uses the wish to erase the magical shortcut and its consequences, restoring a pre-impersonation path on which she must proceed under her own name.**

Whether she retains an emotional memory of Mai’s confession is deliberately uncertain.

That uncertainty improves the ending. If Yua remembers, the reset is an avoidance of direct responsibility. If she forgets, then Mai has finally communicated everything only for the recipient to erase the communication itself.

Both are bitter.

# Why the wish comes from guilt

Yua’s wish is not simply an honorable decision to compete fairly.

The visual acting suggests she reaches the decision because she can no longer tolerate what her success represents.

Kumai now means:

-   stolen authorship;

-   abused trust;

-   Mai’s self-erasure;

-   cheating;

-   a public falsehood;

-   and proof that Yua accepted a person’s unconditional devotion as a usable resource.


Continuing with the account would require Yua to live inside that knowledge.

She therefore does not merely return the account. She wants the whole contaminated route annulled.

That makes the wish partly restorative and partly avoidant:

### Restorative

She rejects stolen social capital and returns to building an identity through her own work.

### Avoidant

She avoids fully repairing the harm with Mai, the witches, and Kumai’s audience. Instead of living through the consequences, she wishes the route away.

This is consistent with Yua’s established personality. She repeatedly protects herself from visible failure by denying how much she wanted something. Her magical wish is an extreme extension of that defense:

> If the route has become intolerably humiliating, erase the route.

# The “Bitter End” screen

I agree completely that the end card is a direct statement about the unresolved relationship.

It does not say:

-   GOOD END;

-   HAPPY END;

-   TRUE END;

-   or GAME OVER.


It says:

> **BITTER END**

That is precise.

Something has been achieved:

-   Yua understands Mai.

-   Mai communicates her hidden emotional life.

-   Yua rejects the borrowed account.

-   The exploitative shortcut ends.

-   Mai’s devotion is no longer concealed.


But the relationship is not healed:

-   Yua still calls Mai too heavy.

-   Mai still wants to be Yua’s number one.

-   Yua does not reciprocate that intensity.

-   Mai is angry at herself for continuing to love her.

-   Neither girl knows whether they should remain connected.


The end card is also visually glitched and drained of the performance’s bright color. It resembles a completed game route whose result is valid but emotionally unsatisfying.

The route has ended.

The underlying attachment has not.

# Why “Bitter End” is better than “Bad End”

A bad ending would imply that the witches failed and should have produced a different outcome.

A bitter ending implies that the truthful outcome hurts.

The ethically correct resolution cannot be:

-   force Yua to love Mai equally;

-   validate Mai’s self-erasure;

-   let Yua keep the stolen identity;

-   or declare their asymmetry meaningless.


The magic therefore produces the painful answer:

> Mai’s feelings deserve to be understood, but understanding them does not obligate Yua to accept them.

Yua’s boundary is legitimate.

Her exploitation of Mai was not.

Mai’s love is sincere.

Its current form is unhealthy.

All of these things remain true simultaneously.

# The performance is partly Yua’s trial

Although Mai is the principal emotional voice of the song, Yua undergoes the decisive internal movement.

The sequence makes Yua witness:

1.  the childhood she minimized;

2.  the identity Mai built from it;

3.  the player/NPC hierarchy;

4.  the extent of Mai’s devotion;

5.  the possibility of becoming equal protagonists;

6.  and the flower containing everything Mai cannot say normally.


Yua is forced to decide what accepting that flower would mean.

Her refusal is not evidence that she learned nothing. It is evidence that she understood too much to accept it casually.

# Revised formulation of Yua’s wish

I would now state it as:

> **Yua wishes to erase the path on which she turned Mai’s love, labor, and self-erasure into instruments for her own status, thereby freeing herself from the stolen Kumai identity and the debt it represents. She chooses to restart under her own name, even if that means abandoning rather than resolving her relationship with Mai.**

The ending is bitter because this is a necessary act of self-authorship that is also an act of emotional flight.

And Yua’s pained avoidance when Mai offers the flower is the clearest indication that she knows exactly what she is fleeing.
