# Episode summary

## Imperfect photographs

The episode opens with the girls reviewing photographs from their recent experiences.

Azu hates several pictures of herself:

-   one catches her half-blinking;

-   one makes her look heavier;

-   another uses an angle she considers unacceptable.


She tells Yuina to burn the film and erase the photographs from existence.

Yuina refuses. Destroying the images would also destroy the memories and emotional significance attached to them. Kyouka agrees that a genuinely good photograph is not necessarily the one in which everyone appears most conventionally flattering.

The dispute ends when they reopen the flower shop after lunch.

## The successful flower shop has consumed their summer

The shop is now so busy that customers face a two-and-a-half-hour wait. The girls have become competent enough to fulfill several wishes in one day and have begun accumulating Mapo at a meaningful pace.

But they realize that summer vacation is nearly over and they have done virtually nothing except work.

Yuina wants all five to go somewhere together:

-   the mountains;

-   a festival;

-   a swimming pool;

-   or fireworks.


Azu says they should postpone recreation until they have completed training and become witches.

Yuina rejects the either-or structure. She wants to train **and** experience the summer that exists now.

Before they can settle the question, someone enters after the shop has supposedly closed.

It is Eiko, their first customer.

## Eiko’s dream has advanced without becoming simple

Eiko has been studying intensively and may qualify for a recommendation to a regional medical school.

The others initially celebrate this as the near-completion of the wish they helped awaken in Episode 1. Eiko, however, remains anxious:

-   her parents still oppose the plan;

-   the recommendation is not guaranteed;

-   she must continue studying;

-   and she does not know whether she can actually survive medical school.


She cannot easily express these fears to her peers because they are also examinees. Complaining from a comparatively strong academic position could sound privileged or self-indulgent.

Kyouka recognizes the loneliness of holding a difficult ambition and reveals that she plans to begin by running for student council president before eventually pursuing the Maebashi mayoralty.

Yuina assumes Eiko has returned because she needs another emotional push.

The witches transform and perform for her.

## The festival performance

The insert song is an exuberant summer-festival number about temporarily lowering the difficulty, recovering one’s energy, celebrating together, and refusing to let an unfair world make joy feel wasteful.

The visual production is unusually playful:

-   festival towers;

-   stalls;

-   lights;

-   game-like graphics;

-   oversized props;

-   carnival imagery;

-   and a deliberately chaotic sense of collective recreation.


Afterward, the others point out that the song may have reflected Yuina’s own desire for summer vacation more than Eiko’s stated anxiety.

Eiko nevertheless says that it helped and that she feels better.

But she does not disappear from the shop as fulfilled customers usually do.

## Eiko’s magical potential

Eiko can now see Keroppe.

Keroppe realizes that she possesses dormant magical aptitude—and claims that her potential is several times greater than that of the existing five witches.

Yuina immediately invites her to join:

> Six witches should be even more fun than five.

Kyouka slows the conversation and asks what Eiko herself wants.

Eiko hesitates. She has cram school, does not know whether she can manage witch training, and does not want to burden them. The group proposes a trial period in which she can observe and participate lightly before deciding.

She becomes a provisional:

> “apprentice witch apprentice.”

Azu appears reluctant but ultimately says she does not oppose it.

## Eiko observes the group

During lunch, the girls explain their daily shop routine and reveal how thick their internal culture has become:

-   Kyouka’s first experience with cup noodles;

-   Choco’s nicknames;

-   Azu often eating at home;

-   their communal food;

-   Mai’s Kumai account and increasingly successful game streaming;

-   and their accumulated habits around saving Mapo.


Eiko carefully writes things down. She apologizes for being inefficient and habitually needing notes.

Yuina asks how she feels about the shop. Eiko says that everyone looks happy.

The conversation returns to summer vacation. Eiko suggests a fireworks festival that avoids several of Azu’s objections:

-   no daytime sun;

-   no mountain dirt;

-   and no swimming.


Eiko cannot attend because of summer classes, but the five witches agree to go.

## Azu prepares for an outing she cannot make

Mai creates a detailed plan for the festival. Azu researches attractive food stalls and helps refine it, revealing that she is much more excited than her dismissive language suggests.

The others also recognize that Azu has never left the flower shop in her transformed body.

Outside the shop, she would return to the heavier body she keeps hidden from them. Maintaining her chosen appearance beyond the magical space would consume more Mapo than they possess.

Azu plans to cancel at the last minute.

Yuina and the others lure her back to the shop by falsely claiming Yuina has been injured. They do not force Azu outside or demand that she reveal why she cannot go. Instead, they bring the festival to her.

## The indoor fireworks

The girls buy the food Azu researched and use the magical space to create their own private fireworks display.

Azu objects:

> These are false fireworks. Real ones would be better.

Yuina answers that fireworks are still fireworks, and that who watches them with her matters far more than whether the display is physically authentic.

The five sit together beneath the magical display.

Yuina says she wants to repeat this:

-   next year;

-   the year after;

-   after their training has ended;

-   and even after they have become full witches.


She no longer wants the flower shop to exist merely as a temporary means toward individual goals. She wants to remain there with the others and continue making people’s heart-flowers bloom.

Azu says that of course this is acceptable and criticizes Yuina for needing to ask.

Yuina photographs them and announces that they have surpassed her previous emotional maximum.

Together, they proclaim:

> 「前橋ウィッチーズは永遠不滅」
> “The Maebashi Witches are forever.”

## Eiko’s wish

Eiko returns after her summer course.

The group assumes that she has decided to join them. Instead, she asks how Mapo are used through the register.

Then she says:

> 「わかったんだ、私の願い」
> “I figured out what my wish is.”

She spends all their accumulated Mapo.

The flower shop is violently overwritten by red, black, and electric-blue magic. Eiko takes control of the space, throws the five witches out, and says goodbye.

Keroppe announces that the shop has been hijacked.

They have twenty-four hours to reclaim it. Otherwise:

-   their training will fail;

-   they will return to being ordinary girls;

-   and every memory connected with witch training will be erased.


The girls realize that this means losing not only magic but their memories of one another.

# Core thesis

Episode 9 is about the danger hidden inside the desire for permanence.

The witches have finally reached the point where they no longer value the shop only as a route toward:

-   Azu’s chosen body;

-   Kyouka’s independence;

-   Choco’s financial security;

-   Mai’s relationship with Yua;

-   or Yuina’s ultimate photograph.


They value the shared life itself.

At that exact moment, the story introduces the possibility that the entire life could vanish from memory.

The central proposition is:

> **A relationship becomes most vulnerable at the moment people recognize that they want it to continue.**

---

# The title is both sincere and immediately contradicted

「永遠不滅」 means something like:

-   eternally indestructible;

-   immortal;

-   everlasting;

-   or forever imperishable.


The phrase is grandiose, adolescent, and sincere.

The girls even briefly joke over whether the more conventional expression should be **永久不滅** rather than **永遠不滅**. The linguistic precision does not matter to Yuina. She wants the promise.

But only moments later, Eiko threatens to erase the memories that make the promise intelligible.

The title therefore operates on several levels.

## As a declaration

The girls genuinely intend to remain together after training. This is the first explicit articulation of the flower shop as a desired future rather than a temporary workplace.

## As dramatic irony

Their declaration of immortality is followed almost immediately by the most severe termination clause Keroppe has disclosed.

## As a philosophical question

What would it mean for the Maebashi Witches to be “forever” if:

-   their powers vanish;

-   their shop disappears;

-   they forget the experiences;

-   or the group changes form?


The episode does not yet answer. It creates the question.

# The opening photo argument is direct foreshadowing

The first scene initially appears to be another Azu–Yuina disagreement over aesthetics.

It is actually the episode’s conceptual foundation.

## Azu wants control over representation

Azu does not want an imperfect visual record to survive.

Her reaction remains connected to her body-image history. A photograph can:

-   freeze an angle she hates;

-   make her body appear different from the one she wants to present;

-   and circulate a version of Azu she does not authorize.


Her demand to destroy the photographs is not pure vanity. It is an assertion of representational control.

Episode 3 established:

> Azu decides what counts as cute for Azu.

That principle logically includes photographs.

## Yuina wants historical preservation

Yuina believes the emotional value of a photograph exceeds whether everyone looks attractive.

For her, destroying the image destroys:

-   the moment;

-   the feeling;

-   and evidence that the shared experience occurred.


The line becomes much more serious once memory erasure is introduced.

Yuina’s camera may preserve what the mind can lose.

## Neither position is completely sufficient

Yuina is right that imperfect records can become emotionally precious.

Azu is also right that a photographer should not possess unlimited authority over another person’s image.

This tension has followed Yuina throughout the series. She often photographs people:

-   suddenly;

-   while they are emotionally exposed;

-   or before receiving explicit consent.


At the fireworks, Azu again complains about the surprise photograph.

Yuina’s archive may become crucial, but the series has not erased the ethical question of who controls the archive.

# Summer vacation versus deferred life

The episode repeatedly contrasts living now with postponing life until a future condition has been satisfied.

Azu’s position is:

> Finish training first. Enjoy life after becoming witches.

Yuina’s position is:

> Training matters, but this particular summer cannot be recovered later.

This is not simply irresponsible Yuina versus disciplined Azu.

Episodes 6–8 have already shown the danger of endless usefulness:

-   Choco uses every available hour for care work.

-   Kyouka tries to earn freedom through perfect social usefulness.

-   Mai subordinated her life to Yua’s happiness.

-   Azu spends magical stamina maintaining her appearance.

-   The flower shop itself has become a successful service operation consuming the girls’ vacation.


The girls have improved so much at emotional labor that they risk recreating the same structures from which the shop once offered refuge.

Yuina’s insistence upon recreation is therefore ethically serious:

> A future goal cannot be allowed to consume every present in which life was supposed to occur.

# The insert song is partly for the witches themselves

The performance offered to Eiko is unusually transparent in its projection.

Its emotional vocabulary concerns:

-   festival celebration;

-   temporary rest;

-   changing to an easier mode;

-   recovering before continuing;

-   and enjoying oneself with friends.


That is exactly what Yuina wants.

The others notice this immediately after the song.

Yet Eiko genuinely benefits from it.

This suggests a more mature model of the flower shop’s performance:

> A song does not have to emerge from a perfectly detached diagnosis of the customer. It can create a shared emotional field in which the customer’s need and the performers’ need overlap.

Eiko is exhausted by ambition.

The witches are exhausted by service.

Both need permission to pause without abandoning what they care about.

## A critical limitation

The song makes Eiko feel better, but it does not fulfill the wish that drew her back.

Her failure to disappear is the magical evidence.

The witches have become excellent at producing emotional uplift. They are still capable of confusing uplift with understanding.

That distinction becomes crucial at the end.

# Eiko’s return complicates the case-of-the-week structure

Eiko was the series’ first customer. Her return establishes that a fulfilled wish does not end a person’s story.

She followed the encouragement.

She studied.

She improved her prospects.

And she became more anxious.

That does not mean the original intervention failed. It means agency creates new burdens:

-   success becomes possible;

-   possibility creates responsibility;

-   and responsibility creates fear of failure.


Episode 1 could have ended with a simple message:

> Pursue your dream.

Episode 9 adds:

> Pursuing the dream does not make uncertainty disappear.

The customer can return because life continues after the moral lesson.

# The witches misread Eiko again

Episode 1 taught them not to assume that Eiko wanted magic to complete her ambition.

Episode 9 shows that the lesson remains incomplete.

When Eiko returns, they rapidly construct several explanations:

1.  She needs another encouragement song.

2.  She should become a witch.

3.  A trial membership will be good for her.

4.  Six members will necessarily produce more happiness.


Kyouka is the only one who consistently asks what Eiko wants.

Even then, the group never reaches the deeper wish before allowing her access to the system.

Eiko’s takeover is consequently not an arbitrary villain reveal. It is the result of the same interpretive failure that has structured the series:

> The witches see a recognizable role before they understand the person.

This time the role is not “troubled customer.”

It is “new friend.”

# Eiko as the rare prodigy

Mai previously asked why Keroppe assembled five merely adequate candidates instead of searching for one extraordinary talent.

Keroppe said that true diamonds in the rough were rare.

Eiko appears to be that rare candidate.

She reportedly possesses several times the magical potential of the existing members.

This creates an important structural challenge.

The five witches have developed value through:

-   incompatibility;

-   failure;

-   listening;

-   apology;

-   conflict;

-   and collective work.


Eiko possesses superior raw aptitude without having participated in that shared ethical education.

The takeover demonstrates:

> Magical power and readiness to hold the flower shop responsibly are not the same thing.

The five are weak enough to need one another.

Eiko may be strong enough to act alone.

That does not automatically make her freer or wiser.

# The sixth-member problem

Yuina assumes that six must be better than five because more people mean more fun.

But the episode’s visual and relational structure subtly places Eiko outside the existing unit.

During lunch she is:

-   listening;

-   writing;

-   learning nicknames;

-   recording habits;

-   and observing a culture that already has history.


The others sincerely welcome her, but they cannot make her share the memories that produced their intimacy.

She is entering after:

-   Azu and Rinko;

-   Mai and Yua;

-   Choco’s disclosure;

-   Kyouka’s collapse;

-   the Mapo crisis;

-   and multiple private rituals.


The phrase “Maebashi Witches are forever” refers emotionally to the five who endured those experiences.

Eiko may have been invited into the organization while remaining outside the foundational story.

That does not justify her actions. It helps explain why “more members means more happiness” is inadequate.

Belonging cannot be conferred administratively.

# Eiko’s note-taking is recontextualized

During lunch, Eiko writes down apparently trivial information:

-   food habits;

-   nicknames;

-   routines;

-   and group customs.


At first this reads as conscientious awkwardness. She is an anxious, methodical student who fears doing something incorrectly.

After the takeover, the same behavior becomes more ambiguous.

She has been learning:

-   the flower shop’s culture;

-   the Mapo economy;

-   the register;

-   the apprenticeship rules;

-   and the girls’ interpersonal vulnerabilities.


The episode does not establish that every note was part of a malicious plan. It does establish Eiko as someone who gathers information before acting.

Her final question about how Mapo are spent is the last missing operational detail.

# Azu and the impossibility of going outside

The fireworks storyline is the most emotionally complete section of the episode.

Azu wants to attend.

Her:

-   research into food;

-   attention to planning;

-   and eventual false illness


make that clear.

She cannot leave because doing so would expose the body she has kept private.

The others know that some serious reason exists, but they do not force her to explain it.

This is meaningful after the Choco–Kyouka arc.

The group has learned:

> Care does not require total disclosure.

They accommodate the boundary without demanding access to the history behind it.

## The deceptive invitation

They lie that Yuina has been injured because they know Azu will otherwise avoid the shop.

This is manipulative and should not be romanticized as perfectly ethical.

But the purpose is limited:

-   they do not force Azu outdoors;

-   they do not expose her body;

-   they do not use her secret publicly;

-   and they do not demand that she confess.


They deceive her into arriving at a place where her chosen self remains safe, then adapt the event around that constraint.

It is a flawed form of care, but substantially different from forcing disclosure “for her own good.”

# Fake fireworks and real feeling

Azu objects:

> These are fake.

The line directly recalls Episode 3, where she called her transformed body fake.

Yuina’s answer is one of the episode’s most important:

> The relational experience determines the emotional reality.

A magical firework is artificial.

A witch costume is artificial.

An idol performance is constructed.

A photograph is framed.

A flower-shop song is rehearsed and stylized.

None of those facts automatically make the resulting feeling false.

This is central to the entire series.

> **Artifice becomes authentic when it enables people to inhabit a relationship or desire that belongs to them.**

The indoor festival is not valuable because it perfectly simulates the public fireworks.

It is valuable because it allows Azu to participate without surrendering bodily authorship.

# The flower shop has become a home

Originally, the flower shop was:

-   Keroppe’s workplace;

-   the site of their training;

-   a point-generating system;

-   and a venue for customers.


By Episode 9, it has also become:

-   Kyouka’s alternative to her family home;

-   Choco’s protected “Choco time”;

-   Mai’s independent social world;

-   Azu’s only safe public embodiment;

-   and Yuina’s desired permanent community.


The private fireworks confirm this transformation.

They are no longer merely workers sharing a shift.

They are using the workplace as the place where their chosen selves can coexist.

This makes Eiko’s takeover resemble more than theft of a business.

It is a home invasion.

# Yuina finally identifies her own wish

Throughout the series, Yuina has lacked a clear reason to become a witch.

She has wanted:

-   emotional experiences;

-   photographs;

-   friendship;

-   novelty;

-   and excitement.


At the fireworks, that vagueness consolidates into a genuine desire:

> She wants to keep the flower shop and continue making heart-flowers bloom with these people.

This is the first time Yuina gives a concrete answer to the question:

> What do you want after training?

She does not want witchhood as abstract status.

She wants the practice and the relationships.

That is why the memory-erasure threat strikes her so violently. The thing she has finally recognized as her wish can be taken away before she has even named it for a full minute.

# “Forever” as a dangerous promise

The group promise is beautiful, but the series does not present permanence without tension.

Promises such as:

> “We will always be together”

can express genuine love.

They can also deny:

-   change;

-   separation;

-   conflicting futures;

-   and individual autonomy.


The previous arc with Mai and Yua demonstrated how “forever” can become emotional pressure when one person defines the relationship for both.

Episode 9’s version is healthier because all five participate willingly. Nevertheless, it remains an adolescent attempt to secure an uncertain future through language.

The ending immediately reminds them:

> Nothing stays fixed merely because people promise that it will.

The normal ending theme becomes especially pointed after the memory threat. Its lyrics emphasize:

-   change;

-   eventual goodbyes;

-   continuing beyond separation;

-   and trusting that no experience is wasted.


The episode title declares immortality.

The ending song answers with continuity through change rather than immunity from change.

# Eiko’s wish remains intentionally unknown

Episode 9 gives us enough information to identify the shape of Eiko’s decision, but not its final meaning.

What we know:

-   she did not return merely for encouragement;

-   the first performance did not fulfill her wish;

-   she possesses exceptional magical aptitude;

-   she asked to observe the witches;

-   she learned how the system operates;

-   she suggested the fireworks event but could not attend;

-   she returned immediately after the five affirmed their permanence;

-   and she deliberately spent all their Mapo to take the shop.


Several interpretations remain possible:

## She wants the flower shop itself

Eiko may desire the place, power, or possibility represented by the shop.

## She wants to interrupt the witches’ path

She may believe their training, promises, or magical dependence are harmful.

## She wants to test the bond

The act may challenge whether the group’s declaration survives the loss of institutional support.

## She wants something only a total takeover can enable

Her wish may require complete control of the magical space.

The episode does not justify choosing among these yet.

The responsible conclusion is:

> Eiko has transformed from a person asking permission to pursue her own future into a person willing to seize control of other people’s shared future.

That reversal is deliberate.

# The visual language of the takeover

The flower shop’s usual magic is:

-   floral;

-   circular;

-   ornamental;

-   pastel;

-   luminous;

-   and organized through blooming.


Eiko’s takeover is rendered through:

-   red and black brush strokes;

-   violent spatial distortion;

-   splattered pigment;

-   fractured architecture;

-   shadowed facial framing;

-   and blue energy cutting through the red field.


It does not resemble blooming.

It resembles:

-   overwriting;

-   vandalizing;

-   crossing out;

-   or painting over an existing image.


That is thematically precise.

Eiko does not simply enter the group’s space. Her wish writes itself across the space the five authored together.

# Memory erasure is the darkest employment term yet

Keroppe has repeatedly disclosed essential rules only after they become relevant:

-   how little Mapo customers may produce;

-   that Mapo can be spent;

-   that operating expenses are deducted;

-   that zero Mapo means dismissal;

-   and now that failed training erases all memories.


This is not informed consent.

The five girls agreed to witch training without knowing that failure could remove:

-   their relationships;

-   their personal growth;

-   their knowledge of customers;

-   and the meaning of months of life.


Keroppe casually says they should not be sad because they will not remember the loss.

That is a chilling moral position:

> If a person cannot consciously grieve what was removed, no harm occurred.

The girls reject it immediately.

# What would memory loss actually erase?

The stakes extend far beyond forgetting magical costumes.

Their witch experiences have changed them.

Azu learned:

-   not to impose her body ideals upon others;

-   and to use fashion as care.


Mai began:

-   separating herself from Yua;

-   reclaiming Kumai;

-   and forming independent friendships.


Choco:

-   asked for help;

-   redistributed family labor;

-   and made suffering speakable.


Kyouka:

-   recognized exploitation;

-   accepted imperfection;

-   and formed a self-authored political aspiration.


Yuina:

-   developed a more ethical understanding of listening, privacy, and care;

-   and discovered what she wants.


If the memories vanish, an unanswered question remains:

> Do the changes survive without conscious access to the events that produced them?

The external world may still bear effects:

-   Rinko may still reject degrading work.

-   Choco’s teachers and neighbors may still help.

-   Kumai’s audience may still exist.

-   Customers may still have made life decisions.


The witches could forget while the world remains altered by them.

That possibility complicates “forever.”

An experience can continue through its consequences even when its author no longer remembers it.

# Character updates

## Yuina

Episode 9 gives Yuina her clearest personal wish so far.

She wants:

-   the five-person relationship;

-   the shop;

-   continued emotional work;

-   and shared memories.


Her present-oriented philosophy also matures. She argues that training and summer life need not compete.

Her continuing flaw is intervention without permission:

-   she refuses to destroy Azu’s photographs;

-   ambushes the group with pictures;

-   and lies to bring Azu to the shop.


Her motives are loving, but she still assumes that emotional value can override another person’s stated boundary.

## Azu

Azu’s body-image anxiety remains active. She continues to monitor photographs and cannot leave the shop in her chosen body.

But she also:

-   openly contributes to the festival;

-   accepts an imperfect constructed version of the experience;

-   joins the permanence promise;

-   and treats continued friendship as obvious rather than threatening.


Her disagreement with Yuina over fake fireworks revisits the real/fake problem without forcing Azu to abandon her transformation.

## Kyouka

Kyouka now possesses an openly stated future goal and has begun taking practical steps toward it through student leadership.

She is also the member most consistently attentive to consent:

-   she asks what Eiko wants;

-   slows Yuina’s recruitment;

-   and accepts a provisional arrangement.


Her “mayor” nickname has become affectionate group language rather than purely an imposed role.

## Choco

Choco has reintegrated without being reduced to her hardship. The group continues treating her as comic, food-loving Choco-chan while also understanding the invisible labor behind her fatigue.

She is among the first to recognize Azu’s discomfort and participates in adapting the outing around it.

## Mai

Mai’s Kumai identity is now developing independently:

-   fashion advice continues;

-   game streaming is succeeding;

-   and Kyouka listens to her content.


This demonstrates that her post-Yua life is becoming social rather than merely solitary.

Mai is also highly attentive to Azu’s strain and helps design the accommodation without demanding disclosure.

## Eiko

Eiko moves from recurring customer to central destabilizing figure.

Her defining contradiction is now:

-   anxious about burdening others;

-   yet willing to endanger the entire group;

-   hesitant about joining;

-   yet decisive in taking control;

-   methodical and polite;

-   yet capable of visually violent magic.


Her prior passivity should now be reconsidered. Eiko has always possessed strong will. Episode 1 taught her to act upon it. Episode 9 reveals that agency itself is ethically neutral.

## Keroppe

Keroppe’s role becomes more disturbing.

He identifies Eiko’s aptitude but does not adequately supervise her access.

He reveals catastrophic employment conditions only after the crisis begins.

His casual tone toward memory erasure suggests that he regards the girls’ emotional continuity as less important than the formal success or failure of witch training.

# Cumulative reading after nine episodes

The first eight episodes taught the witches how not to overwrite other people:

-   do not replace another person’s agency;

-   do not project your desired body onto hers;

-   do not appropriate another person’s identity;

-   do not turn love into entitlement;

-   do not demand complete disclosure;

-   do not confuse care with total rescue.


Episode 9 applies those lessons to the survival of the group itself.

They have created a place where each girl can become more fully herself.

Now someone else’s wish has overwritten that place.

The cumulative thesis can be expanded:

> *Maebashi Witches* is about the creation of a shared emotional space whose legitimacy depends upon consent, listening, and self-authorship—and about how fragile that space becomes when power, management, or another person’s unexamined wish takes control of it.\*

Episode 9’s most important achievement is that it changes the final stakes.

The question is no longer:

> Will they become witches?

It is:

> **What are the Maebashi Witches if the shop, magic, and memories through which they became one another’s friends can all be removed?**

# Revised Episode 9 synthesis

The clips make Episode 9’s structure exceptionally tight.

## Opening

Azu wants to erase imperfect images.

Yuina argues that destroying images destroys memory.

## Middle

Eiko receives an emotionally successful but diagnostically incomplete performance.

The five create an artificial festival that becomes a genuine memory.

They promise permanence and take a photograph.

## Ending

Eiko identifies a wish and uses the group’s own magical system to overwrite their shared home.

Keroppe reveals that failure will erase the memories.

The photographs remain.

The episode is therefore organized around three different forms of authenticity:

### Material authenticity

Is the firework real?
Is Azu’s body natural?
Is the photograph flattering?
Is the stage constructed?

### Emotional authenticity

Did the people genuinely feel joy, belonging, relief, or love?

### Ethical authenticity

Did the experience respect the agency of everyone involved?

The artificial fireworks are emotionally authentic because they allow Azu to participate without exposing her body.

The candid photograph is emotionally authentic but remains ethically disputed because Azu did not consent to it.

Eiko’s wish is internally authentic—she truly wants it—but ethically destructive because it overwrites the others’ shared authorship.

That is the strongest refinement supplied by the clips:

> **The series is no longer asking only whether a constructed self or experience can be real. It is asking whether sincerity alone is enough.**

Episode 9 answers:

> No.
> A feeling may be completely sincere and still violate someone else’s world.
