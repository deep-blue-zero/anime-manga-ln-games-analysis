# Episode summary

## Mai at home

The episode opens in Mai’s home. Her father invites her to play a hunting game after her bath, while her mother says that Mai is currently more interested in fashion and makeup.

The parents then reminisce about **Yua**, an older neighborhood girl who frequently visited their home and was especially kind to Mai. Yua now attends university in Tokyo and has become noticeably fashionable.

In her bedroom, Mai studies Yua’s social-media profile before turning to an account called:

> **無難コーディネーター熊井ちゃん**
> “Safe-Choice Coordinator Kumai-chan”

Its slogan is:

> 「100点よりも70点」
> “Seventy points rather than one hundred.”

The account recommends accessible outfits designed to achieve a reliably presentable result rather than risk failure while pursuing perfection. Mai reacts enthusiastically to the account’s branding and philosophy.

## The 70-point debate

At the shop, Mai introduces Kumai-chan’s philosophy to the others.

Azu rejects it immediately:

> 「最初から70点目指してるやつは
> 絶対70点も取れないし」
> “Someone aiming for seventy from the beginning will never even score seventy.”

Choco explains that this is not a motivational parable about ambition. Fashion costs money. Kumai-chan gives people practical ways to look reasonably stylish without requiring wealth, exceptional taste, or a high-risk experiment.

Kyouka says that she personally prefers wearing what she likes, while Mai answers that what anyone seeks from fashion is an individual choice.

The conversation is interrupted when the girls notice that the magical register’s point total has decreased.

## The missing points

The girls initially suspect theft, although no ordinary outsider should be able to enter the shop or remove points.

Keroppe finally reveals a system he assumed they already understood: accumulated points can be spent to temporarily increase their magical power.

He illustrates this through a retro-video-game tutorial. The witches become pixel characters with status bars. Their ordinary magic is weak, costly, and physically exhausting, but point-enhanced magic becomes dramatically more powerful and does not drain their stamina.

The girls name the currency **マポ**, short for magical points.

Keroppe then reveals that the person secretly spending the communal points is Mai. She has been returning to the shop after everyone leaves and using small quantities for several days.

Mai admits it and apologizes, but her first apology is terse and defensive. After being pressed, she promises to work harder than everyone else to replace what she used.

Yuina is devastated. She had just argued that she would rather forget about the missing points than suspect one of her friends. When Keroppe casually confirms that Mai is the culprit, Yuina effectively freezes, then abruptly leaves the shop.

The others ask Mai why she used the points. She refuses to explain:

> 「使った理由はごめん 言いたくない」
> “I’m sorry, but I don’t want to say why I used them.”

> 「だから泥棒って思ってくれていい」
> “So you can think of me as a thief.”

She prefers accepting moral condemnation to revealing the private reason.

## Yua enters the shop

A customer arrives while Yuina is still absent. It is Yua, whom Mai immediately recognizes as **優愛姉ぇ**, “Yua-nee,” an affectionate childhood term resembling “big sister Yua.”

Yua initially does not recognize Mai. She remembers the younger Mai as:

-   quiet and plain;

-   constantly following behind her;

-   preoccupied with games;

-   and carefully writing game strategies in notebooks.


She is surprised by Mai’s fashionable appearance and shop work.

Mai remembers personal details about Yua, such as how much sugar she used to take in tea. Yua now declines it, saying that she avoids unnecessary sugar.

Yua speaks in a deliberately fashionable contemporary register, repeatedly describing things as **よき**. She has nearly ten thousand social-media followers and is participating in a university beauty contest. She claims that a senior entered her without permission, but admits that the contest may increase her following and possibly generate income.

She offers to promote the flower shop.

## Azu’s private judgment

Azu cannot tolerate Yua’s self-presentation and uses magic to freeze Mai and Yua while she vents to the others.

She identifies a familiar influencer performance:

-   claiming not to have sought a beauty contest;

-   mentioning follower numbers without being asked;

-   posing from the same flattering angle;

-   heavily editing selfies;

-   placing luxury items beside photogenic drinks;

-   and converting a modest following into sponsorship announcements.


Kyouka observes that Azu may be experiencing **同族嫌悪**, aversion to someone who resembles herself. Yua possesses some of the qualities Azu values—fashion, public image, social-media presence—but performs them in a form Azu finds dishonest and vulgar.

Azu nevertheless refuses to confront Yua directly because Yua is important to Mai:

> 「だってマイが聞いたら嫌でしょ」
> “Mai would hate hearing it.”

> 「大好きな友達なんだから」
> “Because she’s a friend Mai loves.”

This is a meaningful continuation of Azu’s Episode 3 growth. She still makes severe judgments, but she now recognizes that expressing every judgment can injure someone she cares about.

## Yuina returns

Yuina returns carrying freshly steamed manju. She explains that good food makes people feel that they have eaten something good, after which the points may seem less important. Her feet began moving before she had consciously decided to act.

She is still hurt, but responds by trying to raise the group’s emotional temperature rather than demanding an explanation from Mai.

Mai begins telling Yua that the girls are training as witches and can grant wishes. Yua is skeptical until they demonstrate magic.

## Magic as consumer convenience

Yua first asks them to remove a pimple. The magic works.

She then asks for an extremely customized melon-chocolate-vanilla cream drink. Yuina and Mai’s unenhanced magic produces a comically tiny version.

Mai asks permission to spend some of the remaining points. The others agree partly because Yua is important to her and partly because Yua’s promotion could supposedly bring future customers and replenish the resource.

Using point-enhanced magic, Yuina and Mai produce a huge drink. Yua then requests additional conveniences:

-   instantly charging her phone;

-   and creating an air purifier because she senses pollen.


The remaining points rapidly disappear.

This is the opposite of the flower shop’s original purpose. Rather than discovering an emotionally suppressed wish, the witches use collective magical labor to demonstrate utility and impress an influencer.

## Yua’s follower-number wish

Mai again asks Yua to let her grant a genuine wish. She suggests increasing Yua’s followers.

Yua proposes something more revealing:

> 「魔法で人の頭の上に
> フォロワー数表示するとかもできるの？」
> “Can magic display people’s follower counts above their heads?”

Her rationale is:

> 「最初からフォロワー数見えてたら
> つきあう人間選ぶの楽そうで よきだし」
> “If you could see follower counts from the start, it would be easier to choose which people to associate with.”

The magic activates.

Every person becomes a profile:

-   Kyouka: **Not found**

-   Azu: 328

-   Choco: 72

-   Yuina: 5

-   Yua: 6,861

-   Mai: **120,975**


But Mai’s number does not appear under her own name. It appears under:

> **無難コーディネーター熊井**
> “Safe-Choice Coordinator Kumai.”

Mai is Kumai-chan.

Yua had described herself as having nearly ten thousand followers, but her actual number is 6,861. She becomes deeply embarrassed when the magical display contradicts her self-presentation.

She then realizes that Mai—the supposedly plain childhood follower—is secretly an anonymous fashion authority with more than 120,000 followers.

Yua interprets Mai’s earlier questions as mockery:

> Was the popular Kumai-chan looking down on someone with fewer than ten thousand followers?

Mai denies it, but Yua leaves.

## Post-credits scene

After the ending, Yua calls Mai.

She asks:

> 「ねえ 私たち友達だよね」
> “We’re friends, right?”

Mai answers:

> 「うんうん もちろん」
> “Yes, of course.”

Yua then says:

> 「じゃあ 熊井のアカウント…
> 私にちょうだい」
> “Then give me the Kumai account.”

The episode cuts to the next title:

> 「それ100％の70％？」
> “Is that seventy percent of one hundred percent?”

# Central thesis

Episode 4 is about **numbers replacing relationships**.

The episode contains several forms of scoring:

-   fashion graded out of 100;

-   magical points;

-   game statistics;

-   follower counts;

-   beauty-contest rankings;

-   account metrics;

-   and the witches’ 99,999-point promotion goal.


Each system promises clarity. Instead, each system conceals something important.

Mai’s anonymous 120,975 followers do not reveal why she hides.

Yua’s 6,861 followers do not reveal whether anyone genuinely knows her.

Magical points do not measure the ethical quality of the wishes being granted.

A “70-point outfit” does not measure the sincerity or substance of its wearer.

The episode’s governing problem can be expressed as:

> **When people become scores, the score becomes easier to understand than the person—but much less true.**

---

# What Mai’s “70 points” actually means

Azu initially interprets the philosophy as mediocre ambition:

> People who deliberately aim low will accomplish less than they could.

That is not what Mai or Kumai-chan means.

The account’s purpose is practical accessibility. A person may not have:

-   a large fashion budget;

-   extensive knowledge;

-   an ideal body;

-   confidence in experimentation;

-   or enough time to construct a completely original style.


A reliable seventy-point outfit allows that person to participate without risking an obvious failure.

This distinguishes Mai’s philosophy from Azu’s. Azu treats fashion as highly personal self-construction and demands that it realize a strong aesthetic ideal. Mai treats fashion partly as a social technology: something that can help an ordinary person become acceptably presentable without consuming excessive money, effort, or anxiety.

Both positions are defensible.

The problem is not that Mai advocates seventy points. It is that she refuses to let anyone know how much ability lies behind her modest philosophy.

---

# The paradox of Kumai-chan

Mai’s supposedly unambitious account has 120,975 followers.

The practical advice outperforms the aspirational influencers.

This makes Kumai-chan an elegant expression of Mai’s character:

> She creates extraordinary success by helping other people avoid the demand to be extraordinary.

Her expertise is not the creation of singular haute-fashion visions. It is translating fashion into repeatable, low-risk choices for ordinary users.

That requires considerable skill:

-   understanding trends;

-   knowing what commonly fails;

-   estimating cost;

-   recognizing what works across many bodies and lifestyles;

-   and communicating without making the audience feel inadequate.


Mai’s “seventy” is therefore not evidence that her work is only seventy percent competent. It is a design target chosen by someone whose actual competence is much higher.

## Mai hides inside adequacy

Mai repeatedly presents herself as:

-   detached;

-   low-energy;

-   unambitious;

-   uninterested in recognition;

-   and satisfied with whatever causes the least difficulty.


Kumai reveals that this is incomplete.

She is capable of:

-   sustained work;

-   mass influence;

-   aesthetic judgment;

-   audience analysis;

-   and building a recognizable public voice.


But she does all of it anonymously.

Mai helps other people become visible while removing herself from the image.

Her 70-point philosophy may therefore also be a defensive philosophy:

> Do enough to succeed, but not so openly that anyone can evaluate the whole person behind the work.

---

# Why Mai stole the points

Episode 4 does **not yet disclose exactly what Mai used the points for**.

The reveal that she is Kumai strongly implies that the spending is connected to the account, but the episode does not show whether she used magic to:

-   create clothing or cosmetics;

-   generate photographs;

-   test outfit combinations;

-   produce account content;

-   maintain anonymity;

-   manipulate the account’s growth;

-   or accomplish something else entirely.


That uncertainty should remain part of the cumulative record.

What is clear is that Mai would rather accept being called a thief than explain the reason.

This means the hidden activity threatens something more important to her than the group’s moral judgment. The secret is not merely embarrassing; it protects the structure through which she is able to act.

---

# Theft, apology, and the 70-point moral response

Mai’s handling of the stolen points resembles her broader philosophy.

She gives the group enough accountability to continue functioning:

-   admits the act;

-   apologizes;

-   promises restitution;

-   accepts their anger.


But she withholds the explanation that would allow full reconciliation.

It is a **seventy-point apology**.

It is not false. Mai genuinely recognizes that she misused a communal resource. She does not invent an excuse or deny responsibility.

But it is incomplete. She offers repayment rather than vulnerability.

This reveals the limit of procedural repair. The group can balance the account mathematically, but the emotional breach remains.

Kyouka identifies that distinction when she says the reason matters because otherwise resentment will remain. Forgiveness and understanding are not interchangeable.

---

# Yuina’s first serious rupture of trust

Yuina’s response to the missing points initially reflects her characteristic moral generosity:

> She would rather abandon the investigation than suspect a friend.

This is emotionally admirable but structurally dangerous. Shared resources require accountability. Refusing to investigate does not make wrongdoing disappear.

When Keroppe reveals Mai, Yuina’s ideal of friendship abruptly collides with reality. Her comic “system crash” is important because Yuina has previously seemed almost immune to rejection and conflict.

Azu can insult her.

The group can criticize her.

She can embarrass herself and recover immediately.

But Mai’s betrayal reaches a different layer. Yuina had explicitly placed trust above evidence and is immediately proven wrong.

Her later response is to steam manju and repair the atmosphere. She does not demand disclosure or express anger. Her instinct is:

> Everyone feels bad, so create something that makes everyone feel better.

This is both her gift and a possible flaw.

Yuina can transform pain into care with extraordinary speed. But she may also use care to avoid remaining with unresolved hurt.

The manju restores social motion. It does not resolve the theft.

---

# Keroppe stages a public confession

Keroppe already knows that Mai has been using the points.

Rather than confront her privately, he constructs an elaborate tutorial and allows the girls to accuse one another before naming her. He says that he created an opportunity for Mai to “吐く”—to confess—while making a frog-themed **ゲロゲロ** pun.

This is another example of Keroppe behaving as a producer rather than a caretaker.

He wants the hidden conflict exposed because the group cannot develop while it remains concealed. But his method prioritizes dramatic revelation over psychological safety.

He also bears responsibility for the situation. He apparently gave every girl access to a shared consumable resource without explaining:

-   that it could be spent;

-   how transactions would be recorded;

-   whether individual use was permitted;

-   or how the team should govern it.


The magical workplace has no budget, authorization system, or transparent ledger.

Azu’s suspicion that Keroppe is running an exploitative and poorly explained labor arrangement continues to look justified.

---

# Points change the meaning of magical labor

Until Episode 4, points appeared to be deferred compensation: complete emotional labor now and gradually progress toward becoming full witches.

The episode reveals that points are also consumable power.

This creates a serious collective-action problem:

> Every point spent on convenience delays everyone’s promotion.

The resource is simultaneously:

-   wages;

-   experience;

-   fuel;

-   capital;

-   and access to easier labor.


That design encourages conflict between immediate need and long-term advancement.

The phrase:

> 「ご利用は計画的に」
> “Use responsibly.”

echoes consumer-credit warnings. Magical power becomes something one can impulsively consume and later regret.

The girls spend nearly all their remaining points on:

-   a large drink;

-   phone charging;

-   air purification;

-   and a follower-count display.


The labor performed for Eiko and Rinko is converted into consumer convenience for Yua.

The episode is quietly harsh about this. Emotional work accumulated the points; status-seeking burns them.

---

# Magic as a cheat code

Keroppe explains point-enhanced magic through old game consoles and an RPG-like interface. Mai immediately understands and controls the system better than the others because gaming is part of her suppressed identity.

Her childhood notebooks contained game strategies. Her bedroom still contains gaming objects. Her father still expects her to enjoy playing.

The apparently fashionable Mai has not replaced the gamer Mai. She has hidden her.

This creates another parallel with Azu:

-   Azu uses magic to maintain a chosen body.

-   Mai may use magic to maintain a chosen public identity.

-   Both fear that the mechanism will make the result seem fraudulent.


Mai previously called magic “too much of a cheat.” Episode 4 reveals that she has secretly been using the cheat.

That is not necessarily simple hypocrisy. She may call it a cheat precisely because she knows how tempting and compromising it is.

---

# Yua’s constructed “effortlessness”

Yua presents herself as someone for whom success merely happens:

-   a senior entered her in the beauty contest;

-   she participates only because she has to;

-   she has “almost” ten thousand followers;

-   promotion might casually produce money;

-   she is naturally more fashionable now;

-   and she avoids unnecessary sugar.


Every accomplishment is described in a way that minimizes visible desire.

She wants recognition, but she does not want to be seen wanting it.

That resembles Azu’s criticism of influencer culture: ambition must be performed as accidental desirability.

The beauty contest is especially revealing. Yua says that she entered against her will, but then openly calculates its value in followers and income. The disavowal protects her from failure:

-   If she wins, she is naturally desirable.

-   If she loses, she never cared.


Her persona is built to receive validation without admitting dependence upon it.

---

# 6,861 as “nearly ten thousand”

Yua’s actual follower count is 6,861.

This is not a trivial following. The shame comes from the difference between the actual number and the number she implied.

Numerically, 6,861 is approximately 69 percent of 10,000—almost exactly the episode’s symbolic seventy points.

This creates a sharp contrast between Mai and Yua.

### Mai’s seventy

Mai openly offers a realistic seventy as seventy. Its value lies in honesty, accessibility, and reliability.

### Yua’s seventy

Yua presents approximately seventy percent as if it were virtually one hundred percent. Its purpose is prestige.

The issue is therefore not whether seventy points are enough.

It is whether the person is honest about what the seventy represents.

---

# Why Yua asks to see follower numbers

Yua does not merely ask for more followers.

She asks for people to become visibly sortable by social value.

> 「つきあう人間選ぶの楽そう」
> “It would make choosing people to associate with easier.”

This is the episode’s most morally revealing line.

Yua imagines relationships as investments. A person’s social value should be immediately legible so that she can avoid wasting time on low-status connections.

Magic literalizes that worldview by placing glowing numbers over everyone’s head.

The image is deliberately dehumanizing. The girls cease to be:

-   Kyouka;

-   Azu;

-   Choco;

-   Yuina;

-   and Mai.


They become profiles.

Kyouka’s **Not found** is particularly significant. Yua reacts as if a person without social media is barely intelligible:

> 「ウソ どうやって生きてんの」
> “Seriously? How do you live?”

For Yua, absence from the metric system resembles absence from society.

---

# The magic exposes the wrong person

Yua believes the follower display will give her power over other people.

Instead, it exposes her.

This follows a recurring pattern in the series:

-   Eiko’s wish reveals the witches’ assumptions about ambition.

-   Rinko’s body reveals Azu’s wound.

-   Yua’s desire to rank others reveals her own insecurity and dishonesty.

-   Mai’s effort to grant Yua’s wish reveals Mai’s hidden identity.


The customer asks magic to make other people transparent. Magic makes the customer and witch transparent instead.

---

# Mai and Yua’s reversed hierarchy

Their childhood relationship appears to have had a clear structure:

-   Yua was the admired older girl.

-   Mai followed behind her.

-   Yua was socially confident.

-   Mai was quiet and absorbed in games.

-   Yua offered attention and guidance.

-   Mai remembered every detail.


Adult Mai still calls her **Yua-nee** and treats her as someone special.

Yua also continues to understand Mai through that hierarchy. She is surprised that Mai became fashionable and works in an attractive shop. She describes the younger Mai almost as a harmless dependent.

The Kumai reveal reverses that relationship.

Mai is not merely somewhat more successful online. She has nearly eighteen times Yua’s following and is an authority in the exact field through which Yua now constructs her identity.

Yua’s anger is therefore not only about being lied to.

It is a status injury.

The childhood follower has surpassed the admired older sister without asking for her recognition.

---

# Was Mai mocking Yua?

Yua asks whether Mai secretly looked down on her when discussing follower growth.

There is no evidence that Mai was mocking her.

Mai:

-   sincerely admires Yua;

-   remembers her preferences;

-   wants to grant her wish;

-   spends communal resources for her;

-   asks the others for permission;

-   and appears desperate for Yua to believe in her.


But Yua’s interpretation is psychologically understandable.

Mai concealed a major public identity while allowing Yua to boast about a much smaller following. Once exposed, every previous exchange can look like a private joke at Yua’s expense.

Mai’s secrecy creates space for that interpretation even though contempt was not her intention.

This is another limit of anonymity:

> Hiding protects Mai from being judged, but it also denies people the context necessary to interpret her behavior accurately.

---

# The post-credits demand

The post-credits scene substantially changes how Yua’s departure should be read.

She begins with:

> 「私たち友達だよね」
> “We’re friends, right?”

This sounds like a request for reassurance. Once Mai confirms it, Yua turns the affirmation into leverage:

> 「じゃあ 熊井のアカウント 私にちょうだい」
> “Then give me the Kumai account.”

The structure is coercive:

> If you are really my friend, surrender your achievement to me.

Yua converts childhood intimacy into a claim over Mai’s labor and social capital.

Her demand may arise from panic, humiliation, or fear about the beauty contest rather than cold calculation. But regardless of motive, it reveals that she treats friendship as transferable value.

She earlier wanted visible follower counts so she could choose useful relationships.

Now she attempts to extract a high-value account from an existing relationship.

The post-credits scene is therefore not an optional cliffhanger. It confirms that Yua’s worldview has already entered her relationship with Mai.

---

# What is Yua’s actual wish?

Her explicit requests are not necessarily her heart’s wish:

-   remove a pimple;

-   create a drink;

-   charge a phone;

-   clean the air;

-   show follower counts;

-   transfer the Kumai account.


These are tools.

Her deeper wish likely concerns **social worth and relational hierarchy**.

A provisional formulation is:

> **Yua wants external proof that she is important enough to deserve attention, opportunity, and advantageous relationships—and she wants to preserve her position as the admired person in Mai’s life.**

The Kumai account would provide:

-   followers;

-   authority;

-   beauty-contest influence;

-   sponsorship value;

-   and restoration of superiority over Mai.


But receiving it would not resolve the underlying fear. It would merely transfer Mai’s number to Yua.

The series has repeatedly rejected magical shortcuts that bypass personal agency. An account transfer would be the purest shortcut so far: social credibility without authorship.

---

# Azu’s development

Azu’s analysis of Yua is cruelly phrased but largely accurate. More importantly, she does not deliver it to Yua.

She stops time and vents privately because she anticipates a fight. When reminded that Yua is precious to Mai, she accepts that harming Yua would harm Mai.

This is significant progress from Episode 2, when Azu treated her immediate judgment of Rinko as something she had the right to announce publicly.

Azu now distinguishes:

-   having a judgment;

-   the possibility that the judgment contains truth;

-   and having the right to weaponize it.


Kyouka’s suggestion of **同族嫌悪** is also insightful. Azu and Yua both:

-   care intensely about image;

-   understand brands;

-   use social media;

-   fear visible inadequacy;

-   and construct attractive selves.


The difference is that Azu despises indirect performance. She would rather state an ugly desire openly than pretend not to possess it.

---

# Kyouka as the episode’s moral diagnostician

Kyouka again provides the most measured interpretation.

She says that validation-seeking is not unique to Yua; everyone possesses it. That prevents Azu’s critique from becoming a simplistic division between vain influencers and morally pure people.

Kyouka also insists on learning why Mai used the points. She understands that:

-   an apology can be accepted;

-   restitution can be promised;

-   and the relationship can still remain damaged by withheld meaning.


Her lack of a social-media account further distinguishes her. She is not immune to recognition needs, but she has not outsourced her social legibility to a public metric.

---

# Yuina and the five-follower paradox

Yuina has only five followers.

She is also the girl most capable of changing the emotional direction of a room.

The contrast is probably intentional:

> Yua possesses thousands of followers but struggles to relate without ranking.
> Yuina possesses five followers but creates immediate embodied connection.

Yuina’s influence is difficult to quantify because it occurs through attention, food, photographs, impulsive encouragement, and participation.

Nevertheless, she is not immune to Yua’s status. She calls Yua a celebrity and is eager to impress her. She helps spend precious magical points on increasingly trivial demonstrations.

Yuina’s openness makes her resistant to hierarchy, but not resistant to glamour.

---

# The episode’s absence of a song

Episode 4 never reaches a performance because there is no shared understanding of what should bloom.

The witches do not know:

-   why Mai stole the points;

-   what Mai truly feels toward Yua;

-   what Yua actually wants;

-   whether Yua values Mai apart from utility;

-   or whether the account represents Mai’s authentic aspiration or defensive refuge.


A song would prematurely impose emotional coherence.

Instead, the episode ends with a transactional demand.

The movement from Episodes 1–4 is now:

### Episode 1

A customer’s apparent desired outcome conceals a wish for encouragement.

### Episodes 2–3

A witch projects a wish onto a customer and must learn self-determination.

### Episode 4

A customer treats magic, people, and friendship as tools for social optimization, while the witch hides the achievement through which she could define herself.

The obstacle is no longer merely misinterpreting a wish.

It is determining whether the stated wish deserves to be granted at all.

---

# The “real” and “fake” motif continues

Episode 3 complicated authenticity through Azu’s magically altered body.

Episode 4 does the same through Mai’s anonymous account.

Kumai is technically a false name, but the account expresses real knowledge, real labor, and a real philosophy.

Mai’s visible indifference may be more false than the pseudonym.

Yua uses her real face and name, but inflates her numbers and performs accidental ambition.

The series is consistently refusing the easy equation:

> natural and public = authentic
> constructed and hidden = false

Instead, authenticity depends upon who authors the construction and whether it expresses or conceals desire.

---

# Cumulative character update: Mai

Mai can now be understood as someone who manages exposure through controlled underachievement.

Her defining qualities are not mere laziness:

-   She is highly competent.

-   She notices social systems and distributed costs.

-   She dislikes emotional spectacle.

-   She avoids making herself the center.

-   She prefers practical solutions.

-   She takes responsibility for actions but resists explaining herself.

-   She creates meaningful work anonymously.

-   She fears being known more than being disliked.


Her “seventy points” is simultaneously:

-   an ethical commitment to accessibility;

-   a rejection of perfectionism;

-   a practical aesthetic philosophy;

-   and a hiding place.


Her arc will likely require learning that claiming authorship does not obligate her to become a fully exposed, hundred-point public personality.

---

# Cumulative reading after four episodes

The series’ governing thesis can now be expanded:

> *Maebashi Witches* examines how people construct acceptable selves under social pressure, and how every attempt to interpret or grant a wish is distorted by the helper’s own wounds and by external systems that assign numerical value to bodies, ambitions, labor, popularity, and relationships.

Episode 4 adds a new distinction:

> **“Good enough” can be humane when it frees people from perfectionism, but destructive when it becomes a method for hiding desire, disguising inequality, or presenting seventy percent as one hundred.**

Mai’s seventy-point fashion helps people.

Her seventy-point emotional disclosure damages trust.

Yua’s seventy-percent follower count is not shameful.

Her need to present it as nearly one hundred—and to judge relationships through the number—is the problem.

## Audiovisual clip refinement

These clips confirm the earlier Episode 4 reading, but they make the emotional dynamics considerably sharper. In particular, Mai’s apology is more complicated than simple evasiveness, Yua’s influencer persona is more consciously performed than the stills alone suggested, and the post-credits demand is a mixture of genuine attachment panic and deliberate emotional coercion.

# Clip 1: Mai’s theft, apology, and Yuina’s breakdown

## Keroppe turns the betrayal into entertainment

Keroppe does not merely reveal that Mai spent the points. He stages the revelation as a courtroom game, complete with visual gimmicks and a frog pun:

> 「マイがゲロるタイミングをつくってあげたつもり」
> “I thought I was giving Mai an opportunity to spit it out.”

He then remarks that Mai is quite an **役者**, an actor, because she listened to his entire explanation without confessing.

That wording matters. Keroppe almost admires Mai’s ability to conceal herself. He treats a rupture of trust as a dramatic test of character rather than an administrative failure that he should have stopped.

He knew for several days that Mai was secretly consuming the group’s shared resource. Instead of:

-   disabling her access;

-   warning her privately;

-   informing the group;

-   or establishing spending rules,


he waited for the most theatrically effective moment.

This strengthens the producer reading of Keroppe. He seems to believe that hidden tensions should be allowed to develop until they can become useful “content” for the group’s maturation.

## Mai’s first apology is procedural

Mai’s initial:

> 「はい、ごめんなさい」

is clipped and emotionally closed. It resembles an acknowledgment entered into a record rather than an attempt to repair trust.

When Azu reacts, Mai immediately says:

> 「だから謝ってんじゃん」
> “I already apologized.”

Her assumption is that the required action has been completed:

1.  Admit the act.

2.  Say “sorry.”

3.  Promise repayment.

4.  End the discussion.


That is almost a literal **70-point apology**. It satisfies the minimum procedural requirements, but it does not offer the vulnerability necessary for emotional reconciliation.

When challenged further, she becomes defensive:

> 「土下座でもしろってこと？」
> “What, do you want me to prostrate myself?”

Mai experiences continued questioning as an attempt to extract an excessive performance of remorse. She resists emotional spectacle just as she resists striving for a flashy “100-point” exterior.

## Her second apology is sincere—but deliberately incomplete

After Yuina leaves and Kyouka asks why she used the points, Mai’s voice and posture change substantially. She becomes quieter, lowers her eyes, and no longer fights over the formal adequacy of the apology.

She says:

> 「使った理由はごめん、言いたくない」
> “I’m sorry, but I don’t want to say why I used them.”

> 「だから泥棒って思ってくれていい」
> “So you can think of me as a thief.”

Then she gives a fuller apology:

> 「みんなのマポなのに、使ったのは悪かったと思ってます」
> “They were everyone’s points, and I know using them was wrong.”

This apology is genuine. The clip does not support reading Mai as indifferent to the harm.

But she still draws a hard boundary around her motive. She would rather accept a morally ugly, simplified identity—“thief”—than allow the others to see what she was protecting.

That is both self-punishment and control.

By saying “think of me as a thief,” Mai chooses the narrative under which she will be condemned. The others can judge the act, but they cannot interpret the vulnerable self behind it.

This is exactly how Mai operates through Kumai:

> She permits people access to the useful product while withholding access to its creator.

## Yuina’s reaction is less like forgiveness than flight

Yuina’s freeze is presented comically, but the clip makes clear that she is genuinely overwhelmed. Her normal conversational momentum stops completely. She cannot turn the betrayal into words.

She then bolts from the shop while speaking in absurdly formal samurai language:

> 「拙者、すぐ戻りますゆえ」
> “This humble warrior shall return shortly.”

That is not calm moral generosity. It is an emergency defensive maneuver.

Yuina cannot remain in the room with her anger and disappointment, so she converts them into action: leave, obtain manju, return with something capable of improving everyone’s mood.

The later food offering is therefore doing two things:

-   caring for the group;

-   regulating Yuina’s own distress.


Yuina is extraordinarily good at transforming pain into kindness, but the clip also exposes the possible cost of that gift. She may move toward repair so quickly that she never articulates what injured her.

# Clip 2: Yua’s persona and Mai’s regression

## Yua enters the shop as a content creator before she enters as a customer

Yua is already filming or composing an image when she arrives. When one of the girls enters her frame, her first reaction is essentially:

> “You’re in the shot.”

This is a very efficient introduction. Yua initially experiences the flower shop as an aesthetic backdrop whose boundaries she controls.

The world is continuously being organized into:

-   usable images;

-   flattering angles;

-   social-media material;

-   and people who either improve or interfere with the frame.


That does not make every subsequent expression of warmth false. It means image management is her default mode of encountering the environment.

## Mai becomes younger around Yua

Mai’s vocal register changes noticeably when she recognizes her. Her ordinarily dry, low-energy delivery becomes lighter, more eager, and deferential:

> 「優愛姉ぇ」

She does not relate to Yua as an equal contemporary friend. She returns to the role of the younger girl who followed Yua to school, remembered how much sugar she used, and admired her social confidence.

This regression explains why Mai has such difficulty challenging her later. The title **Yua-nee** contains both affection and hierarchy.

Mai may possess the vastly more successful fashion account, but emotionally she still occupies the subordinate childhood position.

## Yua’s affection contains casual superiority

Yua appears genuinely pleased to meet Mai, but her memories are organized around a “before and after” narrative:

-   Mai was plain.

-   Mai followed behind her.

-   Mai only talked about games.

-   Mai filled notebooks with game strategies.

-   Now Mai has surprisingly become fashionable.


Yua does not seem to intend these recollections as insults. She even explains that she means Mai has changed in a “good” way.

But the structure remains hierarchical. Yua understands herself as the socially developed older girl witnessing her former dependent’s successful glow-up.

That is why the Kumai reveal is so destabilizing. It does not merely show that Mai has changed. It reveals that Mai has quietly surpassed Yua within the field Yua now uses to define her own value.

## The humblebrag is clearly a performance

Yua’s:

> 「私フォロワー1万弱で、大したことないけどさ」
> “I have just under ten thousand followers—not that it’s a big deal.”

is delivered with practiced casualness.

She then immediately provides a comparison target: another beauty-contest candidate with forty thousand followers. Likewise, she claims that a senior entered her in the contest without permission, while explaining its possible benefits for followers and income.

The pattern is:

> Make the achievement visible while verbally denying ownership of the ambition.

Yua wants the prestige of striving without the vulnerability of being recognized as someone who badly wants to win.

If she succeeds, it confirms her natural desirability.

If she fails, she can say she was never seriously invested.

## Azu’s rant is informed, not random prejudice

Azu’s social-media critique comes out at high speed and with extraordinary specificity:

-   identical selfie angles;

-   heavy editing;

-   branded objects beside photogenic drinks;

-   indirect boasting;

-   sponsorship announcements;

-   and sudden ambassador titles.


She recognizes the template because she inhabits the same visual culture.

Kyouka’s diagnosis of **同族嫌悪** is therefore accurate. Yua resembles a version of Azu who has converted self-presentation into visible social prestige.

Azu’s contempt is still excessive, but it is not ignorant. She can see the labor and insecurity beneath Yua’s supposedly effortless image because she performs related labor herself.

## Azu’s restraint is genuine character growth

The most important part of her rant is not the insult. It is why she freezes time before delivering it:

> 「だってマイが聞いたら嫌でしょ」
> “Mai would hate hearing it.”

> 「大好きな友達なんだから」
> “Because Yua is a friend Mai loves.”

Episode 2 Azu believed that recognizing an ugly judgment gave her the right to speak it directly into Rinko’s face.

Episode 4 Azu now distinguishes:

-   thinking something;

-   believing it may contain truth;

-   and having the right to say it where it will injure someone.


She still vents viciously in private, but she is considering the relational consequences. That is real progress without an implausible personality replacement.

# Clip 3: Magic, exposure, and Yua’s collapse

## Yua treats magic as a friction-removal service

Her requests escalate casually:

-   produce a larger drink;

-   charge the phone;

-   remove pollen;

-   display follower counts.


She does not initially treat magic as sacred, emotionally significant, or even especially mysterious. Once convinced that it works, she treats it like an application capable of optimizing minor inconveniences.

Mai is so eager to retain Yua’s approval that she assists in this misuse of the group’s shared resource.

The relationship has already become asymmetrical service labor before Yua explicitly demands the account.

## The follower request sounds casual because the hierarchy feels normal to her

Yua’s desire to display follower counts over everyone’s head is not delivered like a villainous confession. She says it brightly, almost playfully:

> 「つきあう人間選ぶの楽そうで、よきだし」
> “It would make it easier to choose who to associate with.”

That lightness makes the worldview more revealing.

Yua does not think she has said something shocking. She takes for granted that relationships can be optimized by identifying which people possess useful social capital.

Azu’s immediate:

> 「クズみ強っ」
> “That’s intensely scummy.”

provides the moral judgment that Yua herself lacks.

## The magic does to Yua what she wanted to do to everyone else

Yua asks for radical transparency because she assumes it will increase her control.

Instead, her own number becomes visible:

> **6,861**

Her panic is immediate. Her speech accelerates, her composure collapses, and she repeatedly tries to stop the display.

She attempts the technically defensible but socially absurd claim:

> 「四捨五入すれば1万というか」
> “If you round it, it’s basically ten thousand.”

Six thousand eight hundred sixty-one really does round to ten thousand at the ten-thousands place. But that mathematical technicality exposes the episode’s entire conflict:

> Yua takes roughly seventy percent and presents it as one hundred.

There is nothing inherently shameful about 6,861 followers. The humiliation comes from the inflation and from the importance she has assigned to the metric.

## The Kumai reveal produces status reversal before it produces betrayal

Mai’s number is not merely higher:

> **120,975**

It is attached to the identity of a recognized fashion authority.

Yua discovers that the girl she remembered as a plain, game-obsessed follower has nearly eighteen times her audience and possesses authority within the exact cultural domain Yua now performs.

This destroys their inherited hierarchy in a single image.

## Yua’s accusation reveals how she imagines high-status people think

Yua asks:

> 「どんな気持ちで言ってたの」
> “What were you feeling when you said those things?”

She then imagines Mai’s hidden thought:

> “This nobody doesn’t even have ten thousand followers, and she thinks she can promote us?”

There is no evidence Mai thought anything like this.

Yua is projecting her own metric-based worldview onto Mai. She assumes that someone with 120,000 followers must naturally look down on someone with 6,861, because Yua herself proposed using follower counts to decide which people were worth knowing.

This parallels Azu’s mistake with Rinko:

-   Azu invented Rinko’s motives from her body.

-   Yua invents Mai’s contempt from her status.


Both assume that other people morally interpret hierarchy the same way they do.

## 「全然よきじゃない」

Yua’s final line before leaving is especially effective:

> 「全然よきじゃない」
> “This isn’t good at all.”

Her omnipresent **よき** has functioned like a personal branding device. It keeps everything:

-   light;

-   fashionable;

-   noncommittal;

-   and socially smooth.


When the persona collapses, she still lacks a different vocabulary for expressing betrayal. She negates the catchphrase itself.

The line is not merely “I dislike this.” It means:

> The interpretive system through which I have been declaring everything acceptable has failed.

# Clip 4: The post-credits call

The audio and staging make the call more coercive than a text-only reading suggests.

## The opening question is probably sincere

Yua asks softly:

> 「ねえ、私たち友達だよね」
> “We’re friends, right?”

There is real insecurity inside the question. The Kumai reveal has made her reinterpret their entire relationship. She genuinely wants confirmation that Mai’s affection was not an elaborate act of condescension.

Mai answers immediately and brightly:

> 「うんうん、もちろん」
> “Yes, of course.”

Her relief is audible. She believes reconciliation has begun.

## Yua consciously turns reassurance into leverage

After securing Mai’s affirmation, Yua says, with a deliberate pause:

> 「じゃあ、熊井のアカウント……」
> “Then, the Kumai account…”

> 「私にちょうだい」
> “Give it to me.”

The **じゃあ**—“then”—makes the logic explicit:

> If we are friends, you should give me the account.

This is emotional blackmail, although not the behavior of a cold mastermind. It emerges from humiliation, attachment panic, envy, and a desperate desire to restore the earlier hierarchy.

Yua has recovered enough composure to formulate the demand calmly. She is no longer speaking in the uncontrolled panic of the follower reveal. She knows that what she is asking requires relational leverage.

## She asks for more than followers

Yua does not ask Mai to use magic to increase her follower number.

She asks for **the Kumai account**.

That includes:

-   the audience;

-   the account history;

-   its accumulated trust;

-   the fashion expertise associated with it;

-   its recognizable voice;

-   its commercial value;

-   and the labor through which Mai built it.


She wants to wear Mai’s authored identity as though it were another fashionable persona.

This is a direct violation of the self-authorship ethic established in Episode 3.

Azu insists:

> “Azu decides what is cute for Azu.”

Rinko decides what kind of modeling work can represent Rinko.

Mai created Kumai as a controlled form through which her real expertise could exist.

Yua now attempts to appropriate that form wholesale.

## The childhood photograph is the unstated threat

After the demand, the scene turns toward an old image of Mai and Yua together.

The visual implication is:

> What is more valuable to Mai—the account or this relationship?

Yua may not openly threaten to end the friendship, but the conditional structure makes the threat legible. Refusal could be interpreted as proof that Mai never truly cared.

That is why the demand is likely to be so difficult for Mai. Kumai is her labor and hidden self, while Yua is connected to a childhood bond that still governs her emotionally.

# The point theft and account demand mirror each other

The episode’s two betrayals are structurally related.

Mai secretly takes a communal resource because she cannot explain what she needs it for.

Yua asks Mai to surrender a private resource as proof of friendship.

Both involve the conversion of trust into access.

But there is a significant difference:

-   Mai knows that using the points was wrong and accepts restitution.

-   Yua presently treats friendship as grounds for entitlement.


Yua’s request also intensifies Mai’s earlier offense. Mai took small amounts of shared capital over several days. Yua asks for the complete transfer of Mai’s accumulated social and creative capital in one act.

# Revised interpretation of Yua’s wish

After the clips, I would formulate Yua’s underlying wish as:

> **Yua wants assurance that she remains desirable, socially important, and uniquely valued by Mai even after discovering that Mai has surpassed her. Because she cannot separate personal worth from visible hierarchy, she attempts to restore that security by acquiring Mai’s account.**

The account is not the true wish. It is the proposed mechanism.

What she actually wants is:

-   not to feel ordinary;

-   not to be the lower-status member of the relationship;

-   not to believe Mai secretly judged her;

-   and not to lose the identity of admired older sister.


Her tragic error is that she seeks relational reassurance by demanding the erasure of the very self Mai finally created.

That makes the Episode 4 conflict more than a warning about social-media vanity. It is about what happens when one person can only understand love through hierarchy:

> If Mai loves Yua, Mai must remain below her—or surrender the proof that she is not.

These four clips provide the missing performance context for Episode 4; no further scene is necessary to establish its emotional structure.
