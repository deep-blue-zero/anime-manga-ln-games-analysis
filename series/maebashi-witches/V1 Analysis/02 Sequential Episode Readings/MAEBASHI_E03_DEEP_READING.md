# Episode summary

## Azu’s recruitment

The episode opens before the events of Episode 1.

Azu is shown at school in a visibly heavier body, surrounded by faceless classmates and online comments:

> 「デブが生意気」
> “A fat girl has some nerve.”

> 「4万出す前に、4キロ痩せろよな」
> “Before spending forty thousand yen, lose four kilograms.”

They mock her for carrying an expensive-looking phone case while failing to meet their beauty standards. The problem is not merely that they consider her unattractive. They treat fashion itself as something she has not earned the right to enjoy.

Azu eventually stops attending school. Keroppe enters her room and asks whether this “petty bullying” is why she has withdrawn. He then offers her a highly targeted proposition:

> If what she creates and sees inside her smartphone could become real, what would she do?

He brings her into the magical space, where her body is transformed into the slim, fashionable Azu we have known since Episode 1. Facing the mirror, she says:

> 「ずっと会いたかった」
> “I’ve always wanted to meet you.”

> 「これがなりたかったアズ」
> “This is the Azu I wanted to become.”

> 「本当のアズなんだ」
> “This is the real Azu.”

The scene then returns to her emotional collapse after insulting Rinko. Yuina’s earlier claim—that achieving something through one’s own effort is what makes it emotionally meaningful—echoes in her head. Azu angrily rejects everyone and anticipates being lectured.

## Repairing the flower shop’s harm

The girls cannot enter Azu’s private door; Keroppe suddenly invokes privacy despite having originally entered her bedroom without permission.

Rinko then clarifies that she had not come seeking miraculous weight loss—or any miraculous intervention. She literally wanted a bouquet for a retiring teacher at her school reunion.

This is the worst possible confirmation of how thoroughly Azu misread her.

Yuina and the others hurriedly prepare an unusually large bouquet. Kyouka helps when Yuina struggles with the arrangement, and the girls formally apologize and refuse payment.

Rinko explains that she has had her body type since childhood because of her constitution. Other people cannot know that simply by looking at her, yet people like Azu have always been present.

Yuina answers:

> 「そんな子、どこにでもいちゃダメです」
> “People like that shouldn’t be everywhere.”

Rinko responds that the world would be better if it contained more people like Yuina.

## Rinko’s reunion

The bouquet reaches its destination, but the reunion demonstrates that professional success has not removed Rinko’s stigma.

Former classmates still call her **ブー子**, a pig-based childhood nickname. They express surprise that she became a model and comment that they would be too embarrassed to wear the kind of outfit she wore in an advertisement.

Their language is framed as casual reunion conversation rather than overt aggression. That makes it more insidious. Rinko is expected to smile because she has ostensibly “succeeded,” even as the people around her continue defining that success through her body.

Her former teacher offers another well-intentioned but inadequate consolation:

> 「見た目のことなんて、年を重ねたら気にならなくなるのよ」
> “As you get older, you stop worrying about appearance.”

Rinko politely agrees, although appearance remains integral to her work and the former classmates have just demonstrated that the social judgment has not disappeared.

Later, someone close to Rinko calls her and says she should not have attended. They ask whether she has responded to a new modeling offer. Rinko says she will decide by the next day.

## The group’s response to Azu

When Azu returns to the shop, Kyouka and the others confront her. She grudgingly admits that she knows what she did was wrong.

Yuina then stops the group from continuing to punish her:

> 「これ以上、アズちゃん怒っていいのは凛子さんだけだからさ」
> “From this point on, Rinko is the only person who has the right to be angry with Azu.”

Yuina does not declare Azu innocent. She distinguishes between accountability to the person harmed and collective retaliation by bystanders.

The group begins distributing more flyers, hoping Rinko will return so Azu can apologize. Azu refuses to go outside.

Her refusal now has a concrete reason. The slim Azu we have seen is an ongoing magical transformation. Outside the shop, where apprentice magic does not function, she would have to expose the body she has hidden from both school and her online audience.

Meanwhile, the others question Yuina’s gentleness. They argue that when a person who behaves badly receives patience and accommodation, everyone else bears the cost.

Yuina answers:

> 「悪いやつに悪いことしたら、うちらも悪いやつになっちゃうよ」
> “If we do something bad to a bad person, then we become bad people too.”

She then destabilizes the category itself. If a “bad person” is someone who has done something wrong, Yuina—who constantly makes mistakes—must also be bad.

The others laugh, but her point is philosophically significant:

> Wrong actions require repair, but a wrong action does not reveal an immutable category of human being.

## Rinko returns

Rinko finds the shop again while Azu is alone.

Azu finally tells her the truth about the magical flower shop. Unfortunately, she still assumes that Rinko wants weight loss:

> 「魔法でスリムになれますよ」
> “Magic can make you slim.”

> 「努力しないで、シンデレラ体重です」
> “You can reach ‘Cinderella weight’ without effort.”

Rinko directly challenges the assumption:

> 「それを、私が願ってるって思うの？」
> “You think that’s what I wish for?”

She then asks whether Azu used to be fat and whether Azu sees her as an indolent woman who simply chose to remain that way.

Azu attempts to deny it, but Rinko has been thinking about Azu’s expression:

> Azu was hurting Rinko while visibly hurting herself.

## Azu’s wound

The episode reveals more of Azu’s past.

She once tried to shop for clothes, only to be told that the store carried nothing in her size. Male classmates mocked her weight, thighs, and femininity. One jokes that another boy dressed as a girl would look better than she did.

Rinko tells her:

> 「時がたって、平気なフリはできるけど、言われたことって一生消えない」
> “With time, you can learn to pretend you’re fine, but the things people said never disappear.”

> 「時間が解決なんてないし」
> “Time doesn’t simply solve it.”

Azu rejects Rinko’s attempt to identify with her:

> 「アズはあなたと一緒じゃない」
> “Azu isn’t the same as you.”

Rinko can expose herself publicly as a model. Azu feels incapable of that. The only thing she can confidently display is an apparently prestigious phone case purchased secondhand through a resale app.

Her social presence is built from:

-   magical bodily alteration;

-   luxury-coded accessories;

-   carefully selected photographs;

-   resale purchases presented as new;

-   and a personality that never admits uncertainty.


Azu states the rule governing this construction:

> 「結局世の中、かわいい子が正解で正義じゃん」
> “In the end, cute girls are the correct answer. They’re justice.”

Rinko tells her that she is already cute. Azu rejects the compliment:

> 「これ偽物だし」
> “This is fake.”

> 「本物のアズも、今のアズも、全然かわいくなくて、ブスで無理」
> “The real Azu and the Azu now—neither is cute. They’re ugly. Impossible.”

The girl who initially called her transformed body her “real self” no longer feels secure in either body.

## Rinko’s incomplete self-acceptance

Azu says that she can never become as cool as Rinko.

Rinko rejects that idealization. She has only recently begun to like herself, and she no longer strongly wishes to lose weight, but this is not effortless self-love.

She has swallowed humiliation, disappointment, and compromised expectations. She repeatedly tells herself that things are fine:

> 「人生こんなもんかなって思ってる」
> “I tell myself that life is probably just like this.”

> 「大丈夫、大丈夫って、自分に必死に言い聞かせてる」
> “I desperately keep telling myself, ‘It’s okay, it’s okay.’”

She has technically achieved her dream of becoming a model. She also has another offer, a supportive partner, and continuing employment. Yet the modeling work is moving further from the image of the model she wanted to become.

She wonders whether wanting anything more would be greedy.

Azu responds:

> 「無理」
> “Impossible.”

But this time, she is not rejecting Rinko.

She is rejecting a world in which ordinary dignity and meaningful work are treated as luxuries:

> 「そんな当たり前のことが贅沢になっちゃう世の中とか、ほんと無理」
> “A world where something that ordinary becomes a luxury is genuinely impossible.”

Azu begins crying on Rinko’s behalf, then denies it because crying would ruin her makeup.

She finally asks to make Rinko’s heart-flower bloom.

# Core thesis

Episode 3 develops the series’ central ethic into something more complicated than “accept yourself”:

> **No body, aspiration, or presentation has one universally correct form. What matters is whether the person inhabiting it has the authority to decide what kind of self she wants to present.**

This permits apparently opposite choices:

-   Rinko may remain plus-size and reject magical slimming.

-   Azu may continue using magic to alter her body.

-   Rinko may reject work that publicly exploits her size.

-   Azu may continue valuing fashion and deliberate self-construction.

-   Neither choice becomes a rule the other person must obey.


The episode does not designate an untouched “natural body” as the only authentic one.

Its actual position is:

> **The authentic choice is the choice whose meaning belongs to the person making it.**

# The episode title

## 「怠慢と書いて、ありのままと読む」

The title imitates the Japanese construction “write it as X, read it as Y.”

Azu looks at Rinko and **writes** her body as:

> 怠慢 — laziness, negligence, insufficient effort.

But the episode asks her to **read** Rinko as:

> ありのまま — as she is, according to her own life and self-understanding.

The title does not mean that every current condition must be preserved without change. Azu continues transforming herself. Rinko changes her professional decision. Both women act.

“Being yourself” here is not passivity. It is the right to determine what changes and what does not.

# Azu’s two bodies and the unstable “real Azu”

Episode 3 reveals that Azu’s current appearance is not simply an anime-design abstraction. It is part of the plot.

The original Azu was heavier. Keroppe offered her a magical body resembling the fashionable self she had constructed on her phone. While she remains an apprentice, maintaining that form consumes magical energy and appears possible only inside the shop.

That explains several earlier details:

-   Azu’s urgency about becoming a full witch;

-   her horror at needing ninety-one years to reach 99,999 points;

-   her insistence that becoming a witch after youth would be meaningless;

-   her refusal to distribute flyers outside;

-   and Keroppe’s later warning that she loses more stamina each day than the other girls.


Azu does not merely want magical powers.

> She wants permanent control over her embodiment.

Becoming a full witch would allow her to inhabit the body she calls the “real Azu” anywhere, rather than only within Keroppe’s controlled workplace.

This also makes Keroppe’s recruitment ethically darker. He found a bullied, school-avoidant adolescent, identified the idealized self on her smartphone, offered to make it physically real, and then attached that promise to a remote performance target with hidden expenses.

He converts insecurity into labor.

## Real and fake

Azu’s language is deliberately contradictory.

When she first transforms:

> “This is the real Azu.”

When speaking to Rinko:

> “This is fake.”

The visual language makes the split explicit through “Real/Fake” images, mirrors, smartphone screens, filtered photographs, and duplicate versions of Azu.

The episode does not resolve which body is real because **Azu herself cannot maintain a stable answer**.

Her transformed appearance expresses a real desire. It is not meaningless merely because magic created it. But the desire arose within a social environment that taught her that only thin, expensive, photogenic girls possess legitimacy.

Her chosen appearance is simultaneously:

-   sincere self-expression;

-   escape from abuse;

-   conformity to an oppressive standard;

-   personal artistic construction;

-   and an exhausting defensive mechanism.


The episode respects her choice without pretending that the choice developed under conditions of freedom.

# The transformation is empowerment—and beauty labor

After the performance, Keroppe remarks that Azu still intends to maintain her current body despite everything that happened with Rinko. He also warns that this continuously drains her stamina.

Azu answers:

> 「アズのかわいいはアズが決めるから」
> “Azu decides what counts as cute for Azu.”

That is the correct culmination of the episode’s argument, but it leaves an intentional tension.

Azu is allowed to transform herself. The story does not impose a moral lesson in which she must return to her original body to prove that she has healed.

At the same time, the transformation literally consumes her life energy.

The mechanism resembles beauty labor:

-   it creates social confidence;

-   it allows desired clothing and presentation;

-   it can feel genuinely expressive;

-   but it must be continuously maintained;

-   and the cost is disproportionately borne by the girl whose body failed the norm.


Whether Azu will eventually change her magical form, stop maintaining it, or simply develop a healthier relationship with it remains a major question.

---

# Rinko is not a completed body-positivity ideal

Rinko is not written as someone who has effortlessly transcended insecurity.

She can pose publicly, work as a model, maintain a relationship, and compassionately recognize Azu’s pain. Nevertheless, she admits that her apparent confidence partly consists of repeating:

> “It’s okay.”

The episode thereby avoids a simplistic contrast:

-   insecure teenager Azu;

-   perfectly self-loving adult Rinko.


Rinko has become more comfortable with herself, but she has also learned to swallow humiliations. Her maturity includes accommodation and resignation.

This explains why Azu becomes the person who helps her. Rinko can recognize Azu’s wound, but Azu can recognize the suppressed anger inside Rinko’s acceptance.

Their exchange is reciprocal:

> Rinko sees that Azu’s cruelty is self-directed pain.
> Azu sees that Rinko’s composure is partly self-denial.

Neither is simply therapist or patient.

# Success does not erase stigma

Rinko’s reunion is essential because it tests a conventional success narrative.

She became a professional model. One might expect that achievement to retroactively prove her bullies wrong. Instead, her classmates incorporate the achievement into the original humiliation.

They still use the pig nickname. They treat her advertisement as embarrassing spectacle. Her visibility becomes something astonishing precisely because they still think her body should exclude her from visibility.

The teacher’s reassurance that appearance matters less with age also fails. It individualizes the problem:

> Eventually, Rinko should stop caring.

But the reunion shows that the surrounding people have not stopped judging.

Rinko’s pain is therefore not merely low self-esteem. It is a rational response to continuing social treatment.

---

# The modeling offer and the limits of representation

Rinko’s new offer initially appears to confirm that her career is advancing. The proposal, however, centers on a pig-themed costume and pig wordplay.

She is being offered visibility because the employer believes her body can function as a joke.

This creates a sophisticated distinction:

> Representation is not automatically dignity.

A plus-size model appearing publicly may be socially meaningful. But a company can also commodify that body through humiliation while congratulating itself for inclusion.

Rinko has been tempted to accept because:

-   modeling opportunities are valuable;

-   she already believes asking for more may be greedy;

-   and refusing work could threaten the success she has struggled to achieve.


After the magical performance, she declines:

> 「豚の衣装、着たくありませんから」
> “I don’t want to wear a pig costume.”

The wish is not fulfilled by changing her body or automatically improving her career. It is fulfilled when she recognizes that she is allowed to reject an opportunity that violates the kind of model she wishes to be.

Episode 1 encouraged Eiko to say **yes** to a difficult future.

Episode 3 enables Rinko to say **no** to a compromised one.

Both are exercises of agency.

# The changing meaning of 「無理」

Azu’s repeated **無理** is one of the episode’s strongest pieces of character writing.

Initially it means:

-   I reject Yuina.

-   I reject the group.

-   I reject Rinko.

-   I reject fat people.

-   I reject the body I once had.

-   I reject emotional exposure.


During the conversation, it becomes:

> 「ブスで無理」
> “I’m ugly. I’m impossible.”

The word reveals that Azu’s hostility toward others is an extension of how she speaks to herself.

Then it changes again:

> A world where ordinary dignity is considered extravagant is “impossible.”

Azu’s defining negative expression becomes a moral refusal.

She does not suddenly become gentle. Her emotional grammar remains confrontational. What changes is the target:

> She stops directing disgust at Rinko’s existence and directs it at the social structure that taught both of them to settle for humiliation.

Even her denial of tears—

> 「泣かない。メーク崩れるとか無理」
> “I’m not crying. Ruining my makeup would be impossible.”

—allows vulnerability to pass through her established persona without requiring the persona to disappear.

# 「シャボン・テンション！」

## The song’s central argument

The opening declaration is:

> 「どんなワタシがいいかって決めれるのは
> 世界中でワタシしかいないから！」
> “The only person in the entire world who gets to decide what kind of me is right is me.”

The song begins in a hyper-saturated commercial city filled with:

-   billboards;

-   advertisements;

-   colorful pinups;

-   social-media interfaces;

-   live-stream framing;

-   balloons;

-   and interchangeable “cute” mascots.


It resembles the aspirational world inside Azu’s phone.

The lyrics initially tempt the audience with instant construction:

> 「インスタントに正解、創っちゃいましょうか？」
> “Shall we instantly create the correct answer?”

The resulting self receives praise:

> “Cute!”
> “Amazing!”
> “It doesn’t even look like me!”

The final compliment exposes the problem. A transformation can satisfy every surrounding standard while producing estrangement from the person being praised.

## The kaleidoscope

The lyric then asks:

> 「どれがワタシ？」
> “Which one is me?”

The “correct” self multiplies into a kaleidoscopic maze. Each version can be optimized for a different audience, trend, brand, photograph, or social expectation.

The problem is not that self-fashioning is artificial. Fashion is inherently constructed.

The problem is losing authorship among countless externally approved constructions.

## Balloons and bubbles

The visual world is full of balloons, while the song title invokes soap bubbles.

Both are:

-   colorful;

-   reflective;

-   inflated;

-   attractive;

-   fragile;

-   and dependent upon maintaining a delicate surface.


That is Azu’s existing identity. She has inflated an image from clothes, accessories, magic, and online validation. It is beautiful, but any insult can puncture it.

The song does not tell her to make the bubble stronger.

It says to burst it deliberately:

> 「弾けさせて、花吹雪にしよう！」
> “Burst it and turn it into a shower of flowers!”

Fragility becomes material for expression rather than something that must be hidden.

## The monochrome section

The bright commercial city gives way to a predominantly black-and-white world. Mocking faces become dark balloons and spiked objects. Rinko and Azu are reduced to bodies viewed through hostile judgment.

Small traces of the witches’ identifying colors remain.

This visual shift suggests that normative judgment does not merely impose one color. It removes color entirely. It turns people into:

-   weight;

-   size;

-   market category;

-   photograph;

-   joke;

-   before-and-after comparison.


The witches’ colored accents gradually re-enter the image as the song transforms judgment into chosen style.

## One runway

The song rejects the infinite marketplace of possible selves:

> 「たった一つのランウェイでいい、歩き出すんだ」
> “One runway is enough. Start walking.”

The line does not mean that only one permanent identity exists. It means that the person does not need universal approval or every available path.

Rinko needs one runway on which she can appear without being reduced to a pig joke.

Azu needs one version of herself she can inhabit because she chose it, not because it won an imaginary universal competition.

## Complexes as thorns

The final lyric is:

> 「コンプレックスが棘だったら？
> 飾り付けるんだ、スタイルに」
> “If your complexes are thorns, decorate them into your style.”

The wish leaves Rinko holding a cactus.

That is a remarkably precise visual conclusion. The cactus does not eliminate its thorns. Its thorns are integrated into the form through which it survives and becomes recognizable.

The episode does not promise that hurtful words disappear. Rinko explicitly says they do not.

It proposes transformation of meaning:

> The wound can remain without retaining sole authority over the person’s identity.

# Fashion changes from weapon to care

In Episode 2, Azu used fashion knowledge to rank and humiliate Yuina.

In Episode 3, she gives Yuina a pair of unused shoes and insists that good styling requires attention to the entire outfit.

Azu revises her earlier insult:

> 「百歩譲って、ペラペラはいいけど」
> “I’ll concede that the flimsy clothes can stay.”

> 「服はペラペラでも、気持ちは厚くなきゃ無理」
> “Even if the clothes are flimsy, the feelings have to be substantial.”

She has recognized what Episode 2 demonstrated: Yuina’s inexpensive clothing does not prove emotional shallowness.

Azu still cares about fashion, quality, and coordination. The story does not erase the interest that partly enabled her cruelty. It changes its social function.

Fashion becomes:

-   a gift;

-   shared expertise;

-   an invitation to collaboration;

-   and Azu’s indirect method of apologizing.


She remains unable to call Yuina a friend, but she can help her become the “summer fashionable girl” she wants to be.

---

# Azu’s resentment of Yuina remains

The episode does not resolve their relationship through instant friendship.

Azu explains why Yuina continues to irritate her:

-   Yuina photographs well without filters.

-   She eats large quantities of manju without apparently changing body type.

-   She is “reasonably” blessed in multiple ways.

-   She does not maximize those advantages.

-   She can afford to approach everyone with easy kindness.


Azu describes Yuina’s kindness as something like:

> the generosity of a person who already has enough.

This is not entirely fair, but it is perceptive. Yuina’s openness is partly enabled by having escaped the forms of stigma that structured Azu’s life.

Yuina does not deny the disparity. She answers by asking Azu to use her expertise:

> 「うちを全部アズちゃん色に染めて、夏のオシャガールにして」
> “Dye me completely in Azu’s colors and make me a fashionable summer girl.”

She converts Azu’s envy into a role Azu can positively occupy.

Azu’s response remains:

> 「無理」

Their relationship advances without becoming frictionless.

---

# Yuina’s ethics

Yuina’s handling of Azu reveals a distinctive moral orientation.

She separates:

-   a person;

-   a harmful action;

-   accountability;

-   and retaliation.


She does not argue that Azu’s prejudice was harmless. She arranges for an apology and seeks Rinko. But she resists turning punishment into group entertainment.

Her reasoning is unsophisticated in expression but substantial in implication:

> Everyone makes mistakes. If committing harm permanently makes someone a “bad person,” moral categories become incapable of accommodating repair.

This helps explain why Yuina is effective at the shop. She creates space in which people can act after failure.

There is nevertheless a legitimate criticism. Mai and the others correctly observe that accommodating Azu imposes costs on them. Yuina’s refusal to condemn can become a tendency to absorb conflict without recognizing how much labor she asks from everyone else.

The episode does not completely settle that dispute.

---

# The ordinary bouquet matters

Before any magical resolution, the witches perform ordinary reparative labor:

-   they listen to what Rinko actually requested;

-   prepare a bouquet;

-   help one another arrange it;

-   apologize;

-   and refuse payment.


This is an important response to the consent problems in Episodes 1 and 2.

Magic is not the first thing Rinko needed. She needed competent service and recognition of the harm already done.

The ordinary bouquet also becomes part of her difficult reunion. The flower shop cannot protect her from every insult. Its first act is not miraculous healing but helping her honor a teacher despite the painful environment around that relationship.

---

# Magic through mutual recognition

The Episode 2 performance failed because Azu had no sincere wish to support Rinko.

Episode 3’s performance begins only after Rinko and Azu understand one another.

But the understanding is not unilateral. Rinko effectively counsels Azu before Azu grants Rinko’s wish. The customer helps reveal the witch’s heart; the witch then identifies the resignation the customer has not fully admitted.

Their roles become reciprocal.

This confirms that the shop is not a place where psychologically complete girls dispense wisdom to troubled adults.

> The magical intervention emerges from two incomplete people recognizing related wounds without collapsing their differences.

Azu explicitly says they are not the same. That boundary is important. Empathy does not require pretending that two lives are interchangeable.

---

# Magic as body technology and exploitative labor

Azu’s backstory gives the 99,999-point system much higher stakes.

Keroppe did not recruit her with a generic promise of adventure. He offered access to the body she believed would allow her to live.

Then the episode reveals more hidden charges:

-   uniforms;

-   information about Rinko;

-   flowers;

-   Yuina’s instant change of clothes;

-   transporting Azu’s belongings;

-   and general management.


The group briefly sees approximately 400 points, only for Keroppe to deduct almost everything and leave them with 46.

At that pace, becoming witches would still take almost thirteen years.

The comedy conceals a severe power imbalance:

> Keroppe controls the workplace, magic, information, accounting, promotion criteria, and access to Azu’s desired embodiment.

He also recruits emotionally vulnerable girls by offering solutions tailored to their wounds.

The series continues to play this as producer and workplace satire, but the implications are increasingly uncomfortable.

# Character updates

## Azu

Episode 3 establishes her as the most psychologically exposed character so far.

Her fashion obsession, hostility, magical ambition, school withdrawal, social-media behavior, body judgment, and urgency all arise from one interconnected structure:

> She believes social existence requires a visually correct self.

Her growth is not learning that appearances never matter. She knows from experience that they do.

Her first step is learning that:

-   appearance does not reveal effort or moral worth;

-   another person’s accommodation is not necessarily contentment;

-   her own chosen transformation cannot become a rule imposed on others;

-   and beauty knowledge can be used to support rather than rank.


She apologizes to Rinko, helps her reject a demeaning offer, and gives Yuina shoes. But she continues maintaining the magical body and continues resenting Yuina.

The arc advances without pretending she is healed.

## Rinko

Rinko becomes the series’ most developed customer so far.

She is:

-   professionally visible;

-   romantically supported;

-   compassionate;

-   still vulnerable to old humiliation;

-   increasingly able to like herself;

-   and tempted to mistake resignation for maturity.


Her wish is not self-love in the abstract. It is permission to demand that her work reflect the model she wants to be.

## Yuina

Yuina’s warmth gains an ethical framework. She rejects permanent moral labeling and focuses on creating conditions for repair.

She also discovers a personal motive for continuing witch training:

> When the customer and witches become emotionally happy, she may finally capture her ultimate photograph.

She calls this a win-win arrangement.

That is endearing but potentially important. Yuina is not wholly selfless. Other people’s breakthroughs also become material for her photography project. The series should eventually address consent and whether every meaningful moment should be captured.

## Kyouka

Kyouka demonstrates practical competence by helping with the bouquet and direct moral clarity when confronting Azu.

She does not indulge cruelty, but unlike Yuina, she is attentive to the burden that a difficult group member places on everyone else.

## Mai

Mai continues thinking in terms of distributed costs and fairness. She questions why everyone else should suffer while the person who caused the problem receives exceptional patience.

The post-credits scene reveals a more private ideology:

> 「魔法ってチートすぎ」
> “Magic is too much of a cheat.”

> 「点数にこだわるとか、ダサすぎ」
> “Being obsessed with scores is so lame.”

This leads directly into the next episode title, 「100点よりも70点」—“Seventy points rather than one hundred.”

Her 70-point philosophy may be more than laziness. It appears to reject both miraculous shortcuts and systems that reduce life to numerical performance.

## Choco

Choco remains the group’s emotional and comic accelerator, but the physical exhaustion after the performance emphasizes that magic has real bodily cost even for girls who appear endlessly energetic.

## Keroppe

Keroppe becomes considerably more suspect.

He:

-   recruited Azu through her deepest insecurity;

-   controls access to the body she wants;

-   withholds the economic terms of training;

-   charges for unrequested services;

-   and benefits from the girls’ magical labor.


He does respect Azu’s closed door in this episode and warns her about physical strain, so he is not presented as purely malicious. But his system is exploitative regardless of his intentions.

# Cumulative reading after three episodes

The first three episodes now form a coherent progression.

### Episode 1: Agency

The witches initially try to give Eiko an outcome. They learn that she wants encouragement to pursue the outcome herself.

### Episode 2: Projection

Azu sees Rinko’s body and invents a wish based on her own prejudice.

### Episode 3: Self-determination

Azu and Rinko discover that neither weight loss nor passive acceptance is universally correct. Each person must determine the form of her own life and presentation.

The broader thesis can now be refined:

> *Maebashi Witches* is about girls who attempt to make other people’s wishes visible, only to discover that every interpretation is filtered through their own wounds, privileges, aspirations, and theories of what a correct life looks like.

The flower shop does not simply grant wishes.

It creates encounters in which people can distinguish:

-   desire from social expectation;

-   acceptance from resignation;

-   transformation from coercion;

-   kindness from moral superiority;

-   and a chosen self from a supposedly “correct” one.


Episode 3 is the strongest confirmation so far that the series intends its magical-girl and idol-performance language to carry substantial ethical and psychological weight.

## Rinko wish interpretation refinement

Your reading is plausible, and I think it points closer to the heart of Rinko’s wish than simply calling it “self-acceptance.”

My strongest formulation after watching the clips is:

> **Rinko’s wish is to recover authorship over how her body participates in fashion—to stop accepting visibility that treats her as a joke and begin pursuing the kind of model, style, and possibly fashion-making she actually wanted.**

Creating her own fashion brand for women with bodies like hers would be a very logical concrete realization of that wish. Episode 3 does not explicitly establish that as her plan, but the song and imagery leave the possibility open quite deliberately.

# Rinko had already achieved partial self-acceptance

The conversation makes clear that Rinko’s central problem is not simply:

> “I hate my body and wish I could accept it.”

She tells Azu:

> 「そりゃ今は 自分がやっと好きになれてきて」
> “By now, I’ve finally started to like myself.”

> 「痩せたいとか そこまで思わないよ」
> “I don’t particularly feel that I need to lose weight anymore.”

She has already done much of the emotional work that a conventional body-positivity story might treat as its conclusion. She has:

-   entered modeling;

-   become publicly visible;

-   found someone who understands her;

-   and begun liking herself.


Her actual crisis begins **after** that achievement.

She has become a model, but the work available to her is moving farther away from the model she wanted to become:

> 「なりたかったモデルの姿とは離れてくけど」
> “Though it’s moving away from the kind of model I wanted to be…”

The pig-costume offer is the culmination of that problem. The industry technically gives her visibility, but only by making her body the premise of a joke.

Rinko has therefore accepted her body more successfully than she has defended her ambition.

# Her deeper problem is resignation

Rinko repeatedly describes a life in which she has learned to tell herself that what she has is sufficient:

> 「人生こんなもんかなって思ってる」
> “I tell myself that life is probably just like this.”

> 「大丈夫 大丈夫って 自分に必死に言い聞かせてる」
> “I desperately keep telling myself, ‘It’s okay, it’s okay.’”

> 「正直 これ以上は贅沢なのかなって」
> “Honestly, maybe wanting anything more would be asking too much.”

Her self-acceptance has become entangled with **lowering her expectations**.

That is why Azu’s response is so important. Azu does not tell her to love herself more. She immediately rejects the idea that ordinary professional dignity should count as an extravagant demand:

> 「そんな当たり前のことが贅沢になっちゃう世の中とか、ほんと無理」
> “A world where something that ordinary becomes a luxury is genuinely impossible.”

The wish the song uncovers is therefore not merely:

> “Let me feel okay about who I am.”

It is closer to:

> **“Let me stop treating ‘okay’ as the highest life I am allowed to want.”**

# Why the fashion-brand interpretation is plausible

Several elements support your interpretation.

## 「マイブランドのファッション」

The song moves through:

> 「たった一つのランウェイでいい 歩き出すんだ」
> “One runway is enough—start walking.”

> 「このワタシがいい！」
> “This is the me I want!”

> 「マイブランドのファッション」
> “My-brand fashion.”

**マイブランド** can refer literally to one’s own brand or label. In a more figurative pop-lyric sense, it can also mean one’s personal brand, self-defined style, or fashion that belongs to one’s own identity.

The episode does not tell us decisively which meaning is intended. But because Rinko is a professional model and the song is built around runways, fashion imagery, commercial signage, and styled bodies, the literal fashion-label resonance is difficult to dismiss.

## The song gives Rinko a bespoke image

Rinko does not merely appear in her normal clothes while being reassured that she is beautiful. She is reimagined in a highly designed, avant-garde black outfit covered in crystalline spikes and thorn-like forms.

That costume directly visualizes:

> 「コンプレックスが棘だったら？
> 飾り付けるんだ、スタイルに」

> “If your complexes are thorns, decorate them into your style.”

The body feature that the world uses to stigmatize her is not hidden, corrected, or treated neutrally. It becomes the starting point for a new aesthetic language.

That is much closer to **design** than passive acceptance.

The magical sequence effectively asks:

> What kind of fashion might emerge if Rinko’s body were treated as a creative premise rather than a commercial punchline?

A brand designed for people with similar bodies would be a natural answer.

## Existing fashion structures have failed both women

Azu’s earlier experience in the clothing store is crucial:

> 「当店にはお客様に合うサイズは…」
> “Our store does not carry a size suitable for you…”

Rinko’s professional experience repeats the exclusion in another form. She is permitted into fashion, but the industry marks her as novelty casting rather than treating her as a model capable of carrying aspirational clothing.

Together, the two stories expose two related gaps:

-   people like the former Azu cannot find clothing made for them;

-   people like Rinko can be hired, but often only to perform their difference.


A fashion line created by Rinko—or perhaps eventually influenced by Azu’s styling knowledge—would address both problems. It would give similar women clothing and imagery not designed around shame, concealment, or comedy.

That would be an unusually coherent extension of the episode’s themes.

# Why I would not yet call the brand literal canon

There are also important limits.

Rinko never says:

-   that she wants to become a designer;

-   that she wants to establish a company;

-   that she wants to make clothes for other plus-size women;

-   or that she has a specific entrepreneurial plan.


Her immediate post-song action is much narrower:

> 「例のオファーですけど お断りします」
> “About that offer—I’m declining it.”

> 「豚の衣装 着たくありませんから」
> “Because I don’t want to wear a pig costume.”

She then says:

> 「かっこいい姿 あの子たちにも見せたいし」
> “I want to show those girls a cool version of me, too.”

Her first recovered desire is therefore still located inside modeling. She wants to appear as a **cool fashion figure**, not as a humiliating novelty.

So I would distinguish three levels:

1.  **Explicit immediate wish:**
    Refuse the degrading pig-costume job.

2.  **Underlying vocational wish:**
    Become the model she genuinely wanted to be, rather than settling for any work that happens to offer visibility.

3.  **Possible future realization:**
    Create or participate in a fashion brand that gives plus-size people dignified, self-authored style.


The third is well supported as a thematic possibility, but not yet confirmed as Rinko’s concrete intention.

# “My brand” may also mean that Rinko herself is the brand

There is another reading that partly overlaps with yours.

The lyric places:

> 「このワタシがいい！」
> “This is the me I want!”

immediately beside:

> 「マイブランドのファッション」

This may mean that Rinko does not need an institution to certify which version of her is marketable. **Rinko herself becomes the brand.**

In that reading, “my brand” means:

-   her own image;

-   her own aesthetic;

-   her own professional identity;

-   her own conditions for visibility.


That remains commercially relevant—models are often treated as image-bearing public personas—but does not require her to manufacture clothing.

The two readings could eventually converge. A person who first establishes a distinct public fashion identity may later build a literal label around it.

# Clip 1: Azu’s transformation

The audio makes Azu’s first transformation considerably more sympathetic and emotionally complicated.

Before transforming, her voice is quiet, hesitant, and defensive. Her reaction to Keroppe is not the sharp “Azu persona” we know from the shop. She stammers, recoils, and repeatedly says that his presence is impossible.

After transforming, she does not initially sound boastful. She sounds overwhelmed.

Her lines—

> 「ずっと会いたかった」
> “I’ve always wanted to meet you.”

> 「これがなりたかったアズ」
> “This is the Azu I wanted to become.”

> 「本当のアズなんだ」
> “This is the real Azu.”

—are delivered with tenderness and near-reverence.

The wording treats the desired Azu as someone who already existed but whom she had been prevented from meeting. The transformation feels less like obtaining a better disguise and more like bodily recognition.

That does not remove the social origins of her desired appearance. Her ideal was formed within a violently fatphobic environment. But the scene takes her identification with the transformed body seriously.

## A partial correction regarding Keroppe

Keroppe discloses more of the cost than the still-image reading suggested.

He tells Azu before she commits that:

-   she can only maintain the transformed appearance inside the shop while training;

-   using the magic will continuously drain her stamina;

-   but after becoming a full witch, she can use the appearance wherever she goes.


So this is not a complete bait-and-switch. Azu understands that the transformation is physically costly and geographically restricted during training.

What Keroppe apparently does **not** explain is how long promotion may take, how few points they will earn, or how extensively he will deduct operational costs.

His manipulation is therefore less “conceal every downside” and more:

> Find an emotionally vulnerable girl, offer the exact body she desperately wants, reveal enough cost to make the bargain formally comprehensible, but leave the true scale of the obligation obscure.

## Yuina’s remembered line destabilizes the transformation

When Azu recalls Yuina saying that becoming something through one’s own effort makes the achievement meaningful, Azu erupts:

> 「どいつもこいつも無理」
> 「もう全部無理」

The delivery is much less composed than her public insults. It is a near-childish emotional breakdown.

Yuina’s idea threatens her because Azu’s desired body came through magic. If value only belongs to achievements earned through ordinary effort, then the self she called “real” could be dismissed as fraudulent.

That is why she later says:

> 「これ偽物だし」
> “This is fake.”

The conflict is not simply real body versus false body. It is the fear that neither body can support a lovable identity.

# Clip 2: Rinko and Azu’s conversation

## Azu initially tries to repair the wrong problem

Azu’s offer—

> 「魔法でスリムになれますよ」
> “Magic can make you slim.”

—sounds like an awkward, brittle sales presentation. She is attempting to be helpful without abandoning the assumption that produced the original harm.

That is psychologically credible. Azu has recognized that insulting Rinko was wrong, but she has not yet recognized that her entire interpretation of Rinko was wrong.

She thinks the repair is:

> “I should offer the transformation politely rather than condemning her for wanting it.”

Rinko instead asks:

> 「それを 私が願ってるって思うの？」
> “You think that’s what I wish for?”

Her delivery is controlled and direct, not explosively angry. She forces Azu to examine the assumption rather than merely apologizing for its rude expression.

## Rinko recognizes pain before biography

Rinko’s key observation is:

> 「私を傷つけながら 自分も傷ついてて」
> “While hurting me, you were hurting yourself too.”

She does not initially know Azu’s history. She reads it through Azu’s expression.

This is the inverse of Azu’s earlier error:

-   Azu sees Rinko’s body and invents a moral biography.

-   Rinko sees Azu’s pain and cautiously asks whether there may be a related wound.


Rinko’s empathy is inferential but not presumptuous. She frames it as a question.

## 「時間が解決なんてない」

Rinko’s line that time does not solve these experiences carries considerable weight in the performance. It is delivered with quiet certainty rather than dramatic anguish.

She has learned to function, but the insults remain available to memory. That prepares us to understand her later “self-acceptance” as something ongoing and effortful rather than a completed enlightenment.

## Azu’s worldview breaks apart while she states it

Azu’s declaration—

> 「結局世の中 かわいい子が正解で正義じゃん」
> “In the end, cute girls are the correct answer and justice.”

—is separated by pauses. She struggles to produce the words.

It does not sound like a triumphant philosophy. It sounds like a rule she hates but believes experience has proven.

When Rinko calls her cute, Azu rejects both versions of herself:

> 「本物のアズも 今のアズも
> 全然かわいくなくて ブスで無理」

The transformed body once allowed her to meet the “real Azu.” Now she calls the transformed appearance fake while also calling her original self ugly.

The deeper problem is no longer body shape alone. It is that she cannot imagine a self whose worth survives knowledge of how that self was produced.

## Rinko’s confession changes the relationship

Rinko does not remain the perfectly confident adult counseling a teenager. Her voice becomes quieter and more tired when she admits that she repeatedly tells herself everything is okay.

That admission is what allows Azu to see her.

Azu previously envied Rinko’s ability to present herself publicly. Once Rinko reveals that confidence and resignation coexist, Azu realizes that Rinko’s apparent strength has its own cost.

## The final 「無理」 is compassion in Azu’s native language

When Rinko wonders whether asking for more is too greedy, Azu answers immediately:

> 「無理」

The emotional target of the word has completely changed.

Earlier, it meant:

-   your body is unacceptable;

-   your presumed wish is unacceptable;

-   I cannot tolerate you.


Here it means:

> I cannot tolerate a world that convinced you dignity was excessive.

Her voice cracks into tears while she becomes angrier on Rinko’s behalf. Azu does not need to become verbally gentle to demonstrate moral growth. Her existing aggression is redirected toward injustice.

That is much more convincing than replacing her established personality with generic kindness.

# Clip 3: 「シャボン・テンション！」

The full performance confirms that the song is about **self-authorship**, not passive reassurance.

## The musical role of Azu

Azu’s sharper conversational register becomes forceful and purposeful in song. Her attack gives the performance a more confrontational quality than Episode 1’s encouragement song.

This is not merely:

> “You are beautiful, so feel better.”

It sounds like a challenge to choose, reject, burst, and reconstruct.

The other voices give the song a polished ensemble surface, but Azu supplies its ideological force. Her judgmental decisiveness becomes useful once she is defending Rinko’s right to define herself.

## From colorful commerce to monochrome judgment

The sequence begins inside a hyper-colorful visual economy:

-   signage;

-   pinups;

-   screens;

-   balloons;

-   instantly generated “correct” selves;

-   praise such as “cute” and “amazing.”


It then collapses into black and white.

That transition suggests that the endless production of colorful acceptable images ultimately reduces real people to simple judgments:

-   real or fake;

-   cute or ugly;

-   thin or fat;

-   marketable or embarrassing.


The sequence regains color only as the witches and Rinko begin treating those judgments as materials rather than verdicts.

## Rinko’s thorn costume

Rinko’s black outfit is essential to understanding the wish.

It does not minimize or neutralize her body. It gives her a severe, glamorous, intentionally imposing silhouette. Its crystalline spikes directly embody the lyric about turning complexes that function as thorns into style.

She is no longer being dressed **as a pig because of her body**.

She is being styled as an avant-garde fashion figure whose visual power belongs to her.

That makes the song’s resolution vocational and aesthetic, not only psychological.

# My final interpretation of Rinko’s wish

I would describe it in full as:

> **Rinko wishes to stop confusing self-acceptance with accepting whatever role society offers her. She wants the freedom to present her body through fashion on her own terms, pursue the cool and dignified model she originally imagined, and transform the feature used to restrict her into part of a self-authored style.**

Your fashion-brand idea fits naturally at the outer edge of that wish.

A literal brand for similarly built people would accomplish all of the following:

-   provide sizes denied to girls like the former Azu;

-   give plus-size models work that is not based on humiliation;

-   turn stigmatized bodily features into deliberate design;

-   allow Rinko to move from being selected by an industry to creating the conditions of selection;

-   and convert private self-acceptance into public opportunity for others.


Episode 3 has not yet said that she will do it. But **「マイブランドのファッション」**, the custom thorn outfit, and the emphasis on one self-chosen runway make it a grounded interpretation rather than an unsupported invention.

For the cumulative record, I would preserve it as:

> **Possible implied future: Rinko may eventually establish or inspire a plus-size fashion brand, although Episode 3 explicitly confirms only her decision to reject degrading work and reclaim her intended modeling identity.**
