# Episode 1: 「現状維持で何が悪いっ！！」

## Episode summary

The episode opens **in medias res** with Yuina presenting a blue rose to a girl named Zen Eiko. The flower-shop line already sounds ceremonial:

> 「ここは あなただけのお花屋さん」
> “This is a flower shop just for you.”

Yuina and the others then sing for Eiko. Before the audience understands who these girls are or why they are doing this, the episode briefly presents the polished magical performance that will eventually become its climax.

It then pulls backward into disorder.

Yuina is a cheerful but directionless high-school student living a reasonably pleasant life in Maebashi. She enjoys photography and has reserved the central position on her bedroom photo wall for the most emotionally powerful photograph of her life—her imagined **“エモエモ最強マックス”** picture. She is not miserable, but she describes her daily life as more or less ordinary: not exactly boring, simply lacking something decisive.

Keroppe appears inside her wardrobe and invites her to become a witch. He claims he has been looking for someone with greater emotional “temperature” because the existing candidates are too gloomy. Yuina, highly susceptible to momentum, follows him into a white liminal space and meets:

-   Azu, aggressive and intensely invested in becoming a witch;

-   Kyouka, restrained and conciliatory;

-   Choco, exuberant and linguistically chaotic;

-   Mai, dry, realistic, and inclined to follow the majority.


Keroppe explains their work. The five must open a magical shop, fulfill troubled customers’ wishes, and accumulate points at a register. When they reach 99,999 points, they will become full witches and gain the ability to use magic outside the shop. Until then, magic functions only inside this special commercial space.

The girls join hands and collectively imagine the shop. It becomes a flower shop because Yuina has the most concrete image: she once wanted to become a florist as a child. It also contains a stage because she likes karaoke. Her half-forgotten childhood fantasy becomes the architecture everyone else must inhabit.

Their first customer is Eiko, an academically successful student torn over her future. She appears to be facing pressure to choose a safe, locally accessible educational path, while privately considering the far more demanding possibility of medical school.

The girls immediately make a mess of helping her. Yuina announces that they will fulfill Eiko’s wish before actually understanding it. Azu resents Yuina for taking control despite having arrived last. The group fights in front of Eiko, who leaves.

During the ensuing argument, the girls debate what Eiko actually needs:

-   Should magic make her a medical student?

-   Should it make her a doctor?

-   Would that be good for her?

-   Is the dream realistic?

-   Is staying where she is really so bad?


Yuina eventually distinguishes between **achieving Eiko’s goal for her** and **supporting Eiko’s decision to pursue it**. Becoming a doctor is meaningful precisely because Eiko must work toward it herself. What Eiko lacks is not magical admission to a university, but an emotional constituency: someone willing to say that her difficult choice is worth attempting.

Keroppe rewinds the encounter. The girls greet Eiko again, this time with humility. Yuina offers the blue rose, whose flower meaning is identified in the episode as **「不可能はない」—“nothing is impossible.”** They sing an encouragement song whose decisive lines say that no miracle actually needs to occur; what matters is believing in the self who dared to wish.

The episode ends with the five exhausted by magic, sharing Yuina’s homemade manju, and only partially overcoming their hostility. Azu still rejects Yuina, but Yuina now believes that being a witch with these girls may lead to the photograph—and the life—she has been waiting for.

# Provisional series thesis

Based on Episode 1, my initial reading of *Maebashi Witches* is:

> **This is a story about girls who do not grant dreams so much as make suppressed wishes socially speakable. Magic does not replace agency. It creates a performance through which someone can recognize, choose, and pursue her own desire.**

The flower is already inside the customer. The witches’ task is not to plant it but to make it bloom.

That distinction appears to be the show’s central ethical premise.

# The title’s argument: what is wrong with maintaining the status quo?

The episode title is phrased as an angry defense:

> **「現状維持で何が悪いっ！！」**
> “What’s wrong with maintaining the status quo?!”

The episode’s answer is more intelligent than “change is always good.”

Mai and Azu are right that stability has value. Medical school is expensive, difficult, and socially punishing. Eiko already possesses a respectable route. Staying local and choosing a more attainable option is not inherently cowardly. Yuina herself explicitly says that maintaining the status quo is not bad.

Her objection is different:

> 「けど 現状維持って 何も変わらないってことだよ」

The problem is not stability. It is **inertia disguised as choice**.

The episode distinguishes:

-   freely choosing an ordinary life;

-   remaining in an ordinary life because fear, family pressure, money, expectations, or imagined inadequacy have made alternatives feel unspeakable.


Nothing is wrong with the first. The witches intervene against the second.

This is also Yuina’s own condition. She is not suffering intensely. Her life is “普通にまあ”—reasonably fine. But she has not actively chosen its shape. Her blank photograph space and Eiko’s undecided future express the same condition: **a life awaiting authorship**.

# The blue rose and the circular structure

The episode begins with the successful blue-rose scene, then spends most of its running time revealing how badly the girls initially mishandled it.

That circular construction is important. The beautiful magical performance is not presented as spontaneous purity. It is the result of:

-   conflict;

-   failed listening;

-   interpersonal resentment;

-   ethical debate;

-   apology;

-   rehearsal;

-   and a literal redo.


The polished magical-girl image is therefore revealed as **constructed performance**.

This is especially relevant to the idol component. The audience first sees the finished enchantment. The narrative then reveals the emotional labor and ugly group process required to produce it. The episode does not oppose authenticity to performance. It suggests that a carefully shaped performance can become the vehicle through which authentic feeling finally reaches someone.

The blue rose also changes meaning over the episode.

At first it is merely beautiful and unusual. At the climax it becomes a message:

> Your goal is difficult.
> We cannot accomplish it for you.
> We can stand here and say that pursuing it is not absurd.

# Magic that refuses to perform a miracle

The most philosophically important lyric is:

> 「奇跡など 起こらなくたっていいの」
> “It’s all right even if no miracle occurs.”

That is a startling line for a magical-girl performance.

The song does not promise that Eiko will enter medical school. It does not promise that effort guarantees success. It refuses the dishonest motivational claim that sincere desire bends the world automatically.

The next idea explains the song’s actual promise: someday Eiko may be able to love the self who dared to wish.

Magic therefore functions as **permission rather than omnipotence**.

The witches cannot make the desired life risk-free. They can help Eiko recognize that it is hers to attempt. Their supernatural intervention restores subjective agency rather than dictating an objective outcome.

That keeps the episode from becoming a simplistic “believe in your dreams and success is certain” story. Its claim is narrower and more defensible:

> A dream can be worth choosing even when failure remains possible.

# The flower shop as a theory of desire

The official introduction calls the setting a “singing and dancing magical flower shop.” [![](https://www.google.com/s2/favicons?domain=https://www.maebashi-witches.com&sz=128)オリジナルアニメ「前橋ウィッチーズ」公式サイト](https://www.maebashi-witches.com/story/) But Episode 1 establishes that it is not primarily a retail flower shop.

It is a mechanism for externalizing interior desire.

The recurring phrase is:

> 「心の奥にある あなたのお花咲かせる場所」
> “A place where the flower deep inside your heart can bloom.”

This language assumes that the customer’s real wish already exists. The witch cannot manufacture it. She must perceive it without confusing it with:

-   social status;

-   practical outcomes;

-   family expectations;

-   objective “specs”;

-   or her own preferred solution.


The first attempt fails because the girls treat Eiko as a problem to solve. They know her name, school, grades, beauty, and potential. They immediately turn her into a profile. Everyone around Eiko already seems to be doing something similar: evaluating which future best fits her abilities.

The successful intervention begins when Yuina stops asking what outcome would be impressive and asks what Eiko wants to do herself.

## An ethical tension worth watching

The episode does not completely resolve the ethics of this process.

Eiko never explicitly requests magical counseling. Keroppe reveals personal information about her. Yuina intrudes into her uncertainty, and the group initially attempts to decide her future for her. Even the successful performance is an intervention staged by near-strangers.

The episode recognizes part of this problem by having their first attempt collapse. It teaches them not to impose the result. But the witches still decide that they have correctly interpreted Eiko’s hidden need.

That may be an intentional tension:

> How do you help someone articulate an unspoken wish without turning your interpretation into another form of pressure?

This should be tracked across later episodes. A series about fulfilling wishes will become much more interesting if it keeps distinguishing empathy from projection.

# Magic as emotional service work

The setting deliberately combines magical wonder with mundane commercial systems:

-   a storefront;

-   customers;

-   a register;

-   points;

-   performance quotas;

-   opening hours;

-   service failures;

-   and a mascot-supervisor.


The girls are essentially being trained in **magical emotional labor**.

This arrangement is comic, but it also has thematic consequences. Their advancement depends on satisfying customers. Care has been gamified. An invisible emotional process has been converted into points at a cash register.

That creates a built-in temptation to:

-   identify the customer’s “problem” quickly;

-   offer a spectacular solution;

-   collect the reward;

-   and move on.


Their failure with Eiko is therefore not merely adolescent incompetence. It reveals the danger of treating human fulfillment as a measurable service outcome.

The episode’s corrective is slow interpretation. They earn the right to perform only after arguing about what helping actually means.

There is also an obvious idol-industry analogy. The girls can use their full magic only within a designated venue. They must satisfy an audience through appearance, song, dance, emotional recognition, and a curated fantasy. The “customer” comes with an unspoken emotional need; the performers must create an experience that lets her leave changed.

# Maebashi: magic without escape

One of the episode’s best jokes occurs when Keroppe opens the magical doorway onto locations such as Rose Avenue, the prefectural-office area, and the Green Dome.

Yuina’s response is essentially:

> They’re all local.

The otherworld does not transport these girls away from provincial life. It keeps opening more deeply into Maebashi.

That suggests an important series-level idea:

> **The ordinary local world is not what magic must escape. It is the material from which magic is made.**

Yuina’s flower shop comes from a childhood wish. The stage comes from karaoke. The door appears among familiar city locations. Eiko’s problem is not a demonic curse but an ordinary educational decision. The fantasy vocabulary enlarges local adolescent life rather than replacing it.

The official introduction likewise emphasizes a first-year student in Maebashi whose merely “ordinary” routine becomes transformed by Keroppe and the other girls. This gives the series a potentially distinctive position among idol and magical-girl works. Its miracle is not Tokyo, celebrity, or departure. It is discovering that enough psychic and social depth already exists in the local everyday world.

# Yuina: the ordinary girl as emotional catalyst

Yuina is not introduced with a grand dream.

She likes photography and karaoke. She once wanted to run a flower shop. She follows trends without mastering them. She talks too much, moves before thinking, and admits that her daily life is merely “fine.” The official profile similarly describes her as bright, photograph-obsessed, casually trend-following, and capable of changing those around her through a comparatively flat, unhierarchical perspective. [![](https://www.google.com/s2/favicons?domain=https://www.maebashi-witches.com&sz=128)オリジナルアニメ「前橋ウィッチーズ」公式サイト](https://www.maebashi-witches.com/character/yuina/)

Her apparent shallowness is already functioning as a strength.

## 1\. She does not rank desires by prestige

The others debate whether Eiko’s aspiration is realistic, expensive, socially difficult, or appropriate to her abilities. Yuina’s reasoning is far less sophisticated, but it reaches the more important question:

> Does Eiko want to try?

Yuina does not know enough to impose a comprehensive life plan. That ignorance allows her to return to the subjective core.

## 2\. She is generative rather than authoritative

Yuina does not lead through competence. She produces possibilities.

Her half-formed childhood idea generates the flower shop. Her love of karaoke generates the stage. Her language generates the blue-rose ritual. Her enthusiasm gives the group enough emotional temperature to act.

Keroppe recruits her specifically because the other girls are “a little dark.” Yuina’s function is not intellectual superiority; it is **affective ignition**.

## 3\. Her flaw is premature certainty

She decides they will grant Eiko’s wish before asking Eiko what it is. She takes control without noticing that Azu has spent much longer waiting for this opportunity. Her friendliness can become occupation of shared space.

The show therefore avoids making her warmth automatically correct. Yuina must learn that good intentions do not equal understanding.

## 4\. Photography begins as distance

Yuina wants the greatest photograph of her life. She has left a physical blank at the center of her photo wall for it.

At first, this suggests that she expects significance to arrive as something she can capture. Photography lets her stand slightly outside experience and convert it into an image.

By the end, she has spent her available shots documenting an event she helped create. She begins moving from:

> waiting to photograph an extraordinary life

toward:

> participating in the production of an extraordinary moment.

Her likely deeper arc is not simply becoming a witch. It is learning to become the author and participant of the “emotion” she previously wanted only to photograph.

# Azu: hostility as seriousness

Azu makes the roughest first impression. She is insulting, territorial, fashion-conscious, and openly contemptuous of Yuina. Her official description confirms her sensitivity to fashion and trends, extreme likes and dislikes, harsh mouth, and greater commitment than anyone else to completing the witch training and obtaining her wish. [![](https://www.google.com/s2/favicons?domain=https://www.maebashi-witches.com&sz=128)オリジナルアニメ「前橋ウィッチーズ」公式サイト](https://www.maebashi-witches.com/character/azu/)

Her anger in Episode 1 is not wholly irrational.

She was recruited earlier. She has been waiting for training to begin. Then Yuina arrives and, almost immediately:

-   determines the shop’s form;

-   generates the stage;

-   occupies the center;

-   speaks for the group;

-   and receives Keroppe’s attention.


From Azu’s perspective, the least committed member is being rewarded for expressive spontaneity while Azu’s preparation is invisible.

Her complaint therefore contains a serious ensemble question:

> Does a group unconsciously center the person who can articulate desire most vividly, even when someone else has invested more?

Azu also supplies the episode’s harshest social realism. She raises cost, educational difficulty, gender discrimination, and the possibility that a high-achieving girl may have an easier life by remaining where she is already valued. She speaks cruelly, but she is not stupid. Her negativity notices structural obstacles that Yuina’s optimism ignores.

Her probable role is to prevent the series’ encouragement ethic from becoming sentimental.

# Kyouka: mediation as provisional leadership

Kyouka is the first person who successfully translates between Yuina and Azu.

She observes that Yuina is not necessarily being favored arbitrarily. Yuina had the clearest mental image, so the magical space responded to her. Kyouka then admits that she, like Azu, genuinely wants to become a witch but had not formed an equally concrete answer.

That is a strong act of mediation because she does not tell Azu simply to calm down. She:

1.  identifies the factual source of the imbalance;

2.  acknowledges Azu’s underlying seriousness;

3.  admits her own inadequacy;

4.  redirects the group toward their common task.


Her official profile calls her the group’s most conventional, reliable observer while also noting high ideals and sheltered naïveté. [![](https://www.google.com/s2/favicons?domain=https://www.maebashi-witches.com&sz=128)オリジナルアニメ「前橋ウィッチーズ」公式サイト](https://www.maebashi-witches.com/character/kyoka/)

Episode 1 presents the reliable side first. She is the ensemble’s early stabilizer, although her own deeper desire remains unrevealed.

# Choco: dream language without embarrassment

Choco’s contribution is initially comic, but she supplies one of the episode’s moral pivots:

> 「夢が見れるってステキだよ」
> “Being able to dream is wonderful.”

This sounds simplistic beside the others’ arguments about cost and feasibility. Yet the episode ultimately sides with the emotional principle inside it.

Choco does not prove that Eiko will succeed. She protects the legitimacy of wanting.

Her language turns nearly every condition into a “Choco-chan” state—happy, exhausted, hungry, deflated. That makes her a walking emotional register. Feelings are not hidden or intellectualized; they become playful labels and bodily states.

The official profile describes her as an exceptionally high-energy mood maker who sometimes appears to lose power and drift into sleep or blankness. # Mai: defensive realism and the safety of consensus

Mai is the most openly cautious member.

She questions whether becoming a doctor is actually right for Eiko. She notices the difficulty and cost. She thinks maintaining a comfortable life may be rational. Once the group reaches consensus, however, she says she is the type to be swept along by the larger current.

The official profile describes her as a cool realist who constantly considers social expectations and what is proportionate to her abilities; her motto is “70 points rather than 100,” and she follows the majority rather than reveal her true feelings. [![](https://www.google.com/s2/favicons?domain=https://www.maebashi-witches.com&sz=128)オリジナルアニメ「前橋ウィッチーズ」公式サイト](https://www.maebashi-witches.com/character/mai/)

Episode 1 already makes the danger visible. Mai’s flexibility helps the group function, but it also allows her to avoid ownership of a position. She does not force her desire onto others because she may be reluctant to admit that she possesses one.

Eiko’s conflict is therefore likely relevant to Mai: both are tempted by the safety of an externally approved life.

# Keroppe: mascot, producer, and manipulator

Keroppe is not a neutral magical mentor.

He recruits Yuina partly as if casting an entertainment unit:

> the existing members are too dark;
> a warmer character type is missing.

He openly refers to character balance. He praises self-production. He withholds important information, including how physically exhausting magic will be. He insults the girls and enjoys provoking them, especially Azu. The official profile describes him as a mysterious familiar whose real motive for collecting magical power is unknown and who habitually mocks the group. [![](https://www.google.com/s2/favicons?domain=https://www.maebashi-witches.com&sz=128)オリジナルアニメ「前橋ウィッチーズ」公式サイト](https://www.maebashi-witches.com/character/keroppe/)

He therefore occupies several roles at once:

-   magical mascot;

-   producer;

-   casting director;

-   retail supervisor;

-   game-system administrator;

-   and unreliable adult.


His pedagogical method is also revealing. He can apparently identify Eiko’s condition, but refuses to provide the answer. He forces the girls to debate it. He is manipulating them, but he is also making them produce their own ethics of assistance.

The repeated **“魔女ガチャ”** joke is particularly suggestive. The girls are treated like pulls from a roster—perhaps good, perhaps defective, assembled for complementary attributes. This lightly satirizes the production of color-coded entertainment ensembles even as the anime itself creates one.

# The ensemble: not friendship, but forced collaboration

The episode refuses to pretend the five immediately like one another.

They are brought together because none possesses sufficient magical power alone. Keroppe explicitly says that five are needed to open the shop. Their first bond is therefore not friendship but **functional insufficiency**.

Each possesses something the others lack:

The first magical act requires them to hold hands. The symbolism is straightforward, but the episode complicates it: physical connection occurs before emotional trust.

Their magic works because they cooperate mechanically. Their customer work fails because they have not yet learned to interpret one another.

That is a promising ensemble structure. The series is not merely asking whether they can combine powers. It is asking whether girls with incompatible theories of life can develop a shared practice of care.

# Visual reading

The visual material reinforces the episode’s movement from **blankness to authored saturation**.

## 1\. White liminal space

The recruitment room is nearly empty: white floors, sketchlike stairways, disconnected colored doors, and minimal architecture. It resembles an unfinished stage or design draft.

The girls exist there as disconnected color-coded figures. They have not yet created a common world.

## 2\. The shop as psychic collage

Once they hold hands, the sterile space erupts into floral mandalas, golden ornament, stairs, arches, roses, display counters, and a stage. The shop is visually excessive because it is not rational architecture. It is accumulated adolescent fantasy.

The inverted floral ceiling emphasizes that this space does not obey ordinary physical orientation. Desire has become environment.

## 3\. Everyday Maebashi against spectacular interiority

Exterior scenes use recognizable streets, homes, shops, transit, school routes, and city landmarks. The magical shop is overwhelmingly saturated by comparison.

The effect is not that the city is ugly and fantasy is beautiful. The magical space repeatedly opens directly onto the city. The two worlds interpenetrate. Maebashi supplies the people, problems, memories, food, visual references, and childhood fantasies from which magic is constructed.

## 4\. The performance as interior world

The climax shifts into a dark blue and violet concert space filled with floral and circular magical geometry. Each girl receives a coordinated but individualized costume and color identity. Eiko’s ordinary school uniform remains visually distinct.

This staging places the witches inside a consciously produced spectacle while preserving Eiko as the recipient. The sequence is less a literal concert than an emotional chamber created for one audience member.

That fulfills the opening line: a flower shop “just for you.”

# What Episode 1 establishes about the anime

*Maebashi Witches* initially presents itself as a hybrid of:

-   magical-girl fiction;

-   idol performance;

-   local coming-of-age drama;

-   ensemble comedy;

-   customer-of-the-week emotional storytelling;

-   and workplace/service fiction.


But the mixture is already coherent.

All of those forms concern **transformation through performance**:

-   The magical girl transforms herself.

-   The idol transforms feeling into a public song.

-   The shop transforms private need into a service encounter.

-   The camera transforms experience into memory.

-   The flower transforms an invisible desire into visible form.

-   The local city transforms from “ordinary” environment into a site of miracle.


The episode’s strongest idea is that none of these transformations is automatic. They require interpretation, collaboration, and the willingness to fail once before trying again.

# Initial critical judgment

Episode 1 is stronger than a generic “five girls become magical idols” premiere.

Its greatest strength is that its wish-of-the-week plot contains an actual ethical distinction:

> **Helping someone is not the same as producing the outcome you think she ought to want.**

Its second strength is the friction inside the cast. Azu’s resentment, Mai’s conformity, Kyouka’s mediation, Choco’s emotional simplicity, and Yuina’s intrusive warmth are not merely personality decorations. They generate different theories of desire and responsibility.

The largest risk is that later episodes could flatten this into a formula:

1.  customer arrives;

2.  hidden problem is discovered;

3.  song solves it;

4.  points increase.


The premiere avoids that flatness because the girls misread Eiko, fight, and have to reconsider the ethics of their intervention. The series will become substantially richer if later wishes remain ambiguous and if the witches’ own needs contaminate their interpretations.

For now, the working thesis I would carry forward is:

> **Maebashi Witches is about emotionally unfinished girls learning to help other people author their futures—and discovering that every wish they interpret exposes something unresolved in themselves.**

This is an excellent foundation for the remaining episode bundles.
