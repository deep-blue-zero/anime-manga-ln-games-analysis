## Extracted V1 deep reading

Spoilers for *One Punch Man* Vols. 4–6 only.

# One Punch Man Vols. 4–6: Second Batch Deep Reading

## Working thesis

Volumes 1–3 established Saitama as a hero whose strength has already outrun narrative meaning. Volumes 4–6 expand the problem outward:

> **Saitama is not merely unrecognized because nobody has seen him. He is unrecognized because society actively misprocesses heroism through ranks, rumor, resentment, spectacle, bureaucracy, and public emotion.**

This batch is where *One Punch Man* becomes much more than “bored invincible man punches monsters.” It now has a proper social world. Saitama’s problem is no longer only existential boredom. It is also **misrecognition**: he saves people, but the story of what happened gets captured by crowds, rank hierarchies, media assumptions, jealous heroes, and institutional logic.

The meteor arc and Deep Sea King arc form a pair:

-   In the **meteor arc**, Saitama saves a city but is blamed for the damage.

-   In the **Deep Sea King arc**, Saitama saves everyone but deliberately lets himself be misread so the defeated heroes are not humiliated.


That movement matters. Saitama is still deadpan, irritated, and socially awkward, but these volumes prove he is not morally shallow. He may not care much about applause, but he understands when reputation matters for other people.

The batch also introduces the S-Class world, Amai Mask, Mumen Rider, Silver Fang, Metal Knight, Puri-Puri Prisoner, Tatsumaki, and the Dark Matter Thieves. The scale rises from neighborhood monsters to meteor disaster to citywide massacre to alien invasion. Yet Saitama remains the same man: grocery bags, blank face, cape, and one punch.

That mismatch is the engine.

# 1\. Comprehensive Summary

## Volume 4: Meteor, public blame, and the sea threat

Volume 4 begins with the giant meteor arc. Saitama has recently become a professional hero, and Genos is still trying to understand what being Saitama’s disciple actually means. A massive meteor is on a collision course with Z-City, and the Hero Association classifies it as a catastrophic threat. The disaster-response system activates. Heroes are called, civilians flee, and the S-Class begins to matter as an institutional category.

The meteor sequence introduces three crucial S-Class figures or attitudes.

**Genos** treats the situation as a mission of self-sacrificial intervention. He calculates, deploys upgraded firepower, and pours everything he has into stopping the meteor. He fails, but his failure is heroic in the old battle-manga sense: effort, sincerity, risk, and a willingness to burn himself out for others.

**Metal Knight** appears through a remotely operated weapon system. He is powerful, but his stance is coldly experimental. He is not framed as rushing in out of civic feeling. He is testing weapons under disaster conditions. This is the first major sign that S-Class power does not automatically mean heroic spirit.

**Silver Fang / Bang** appears as an old master who understands scale, danger, and the limits of even extraordinary human technique. He is calm, observant, and unlike most characters, he begins to sense that Saitama may be something beyond ordinary classification.

Saitama arrives and destroys the meteor. But because he does not vaporize it cleanly, fragments rain down across the city. He has prevented annihilation, but the city still suffers enormous damage. This creates one of the series’ first major public-recognition crises. Saitama is not celebrated as the savior. He becomes a convenient target for displaced anger.

Tanktop Tiger and Tanktop Black Hole exploit the situation. They weaponize the crowd’s resentment and accuse Saitama of being responsible for the damage. Saitama’s response is sharp: he is not especially interested in defending himself through emotional performance. He essentially tells the crowd that he did what he did, and if they want to blame him, that is their business. This is not a polished superhero response. It is socially abrasive, but morally clarifying. He refuses to pretend that saving them entitles them to a comforting narrative.

The volume then transitions into the sea threat. A group of monsters from the sea invade, and Stinger initially appears as a competent A-Class hero, defeating multiple sea creatures through determination and skill. But then the Deep Sea King appears, and the tone shifts. Deep Sea King is not just another monster of the week. He is the first villain in the manga who systematically exposes the gap between heroic image and heroic capacity.

He beats Stinger. He beats Lightning Max. He beats Puri-Puri Prisoner after Puri-Puri breaks out of prison to fight him. Sonic engages him as well, though the conditions of the fight are comic and compromised. The arc escalates toward the shelter, where civilians gather under the assumption that the Hero Association’s protective infrastructure and professional heroes will save them.

This matters because the shelter becomes a stage for the central question of the arc:

> What does heroism look like when everyone is watching and the hero cannot win?

Volume 4 ends with Deep Sea King still active and the crisis unresolved.

## Volume 5: Mumen Rider, Genos’s sacrifice, Saitama’s reputation choice, and B-Class

Volume 5 continues the Deep Sea King arc and gives the early series its strongest moral counterpoint to Saitama: **Mumen Rider**.

Deep Sea King reaches the shelter and threatens the civilians directly. Genos arrives and fights him. This is one of Genos’s most important early moments. Until now, much of Genos’s identity has been revenge, discipline, admiration for Saitama, and the desire to grow stronger. Against Deep Sea King, he acts not for revenge or self-improvement, but to protect a child. When the monster targets a little girl, Genos shields her and takes severe damage. His body is ruined, but the action clarifies his heroic core.

Then Mumen Rider arrives.

Mumen Rider knows he cannot win. The story does not pretend otherwise. He is C-Class Rank 1, not because he is secretly monstrous, but because he is dutiful, persistent, and honest about small-scale heroism. Against Deep Sea King, he is physically outclassed to an almost absurd degree. But he still charges. His speech is not “I will defeat you.” It is closer to “I know I cannot win, but I have to stand here anyway.”

That is one of the most important philosophical moments in these volumes. Mumen Rider proves that *One Punch Man* is not a simple power-worship story. The manga can laugh at weak heroes, but it does not despise courage without power. In fact, Mumen Rider’s hopeless stand is one of the purest expressions of heroism so far.

Saitama arrives and kills Deep Sea King with one punch.

But the crowd cannot process what happened. One man voices the resentment that the manga has been building toward: maybe the other heroes weakened the monster first; maybe Saitama only got lucky; maybe he is taking credit. This is the Deep Sea King arc’s version of the meteor crowd. Public perception looks for a story that fits its emotional needs.

Saitama makes a crucial choice. Rather than defend his own reputation, he loudly pretends to be the kind of cheap opportunist the skeptic imagines: someone who let the other heroes exhaust the monster and then claimed the kill. This redirects the crowd’s anger toward him and preserves the honor of the defeated heroes.

This is a major Saitama moment. He is often described as not caring about reputation, but the sharper point is that he can choose how reputation is spent. In the meteor arc, he refuses to submit to public blame. In the Deep Sea King arc, he strategically accepts blame because it protects others. That is not apathy. It is rough, anti-sentimental moral judgment.

After the Deep Sea King arc, Saitama’s status changes. He receives official credit, but distorted credit. He moves upward, yet his social position remains unstable. Genos continues to reflect on him as master, but also begins seeing that Saitama’s heroism is not only strength. Mumen Rider sends a thank-you letter, which gives Saitama one of the few forms of recognition that actually matters: not mass applause, but concrete gratitude from someone who understood.

The later part of the volume moves toward B-Class and the politics of hero ranking. Amai Mask appears as an important gatekeeper figure, and the Hero Association’s internal logic becomes more visible. The association is not only about responding to disasters. It is about image, promotion, celebrity, public trust, class boundaries, and control over what kind of person gets to be seen as top-tier.

The bonus material around “things that cannot be bought” also matters more than it first appears. Saitama’s mundane life—money, food, drinks, small purchases—keeps cutting against the vast heroic scale around him. The strongest man alive is still constrained by ordinary lack and ordinary routine. That contrast remains central.

## Volume 6: S-Class, prophecy, alien invasion, and the first cosmic mirror

Volume 6 expands the world dramatically.

It begins with Saitama, Genos, and Bang. Bang’s martial art, **流水岩砕拳 / Water Stream Rock Smashing Fist**, is introduced as a serious traditional discipline. Bang recognizes Saitama’s absurd strength in a way many others cannot, and he is interested in him partly as an old master confronting something outside ordinary martial logic.

The S-Class meeting is one of the most important worldbuilding chapters so far. The manga finally gives us the Hero Association’s elite in one room: Tatsumaki, Atomic Samurai, Child Emperor, Metal Bat, Pig God, Tanktop Master, Watchdog Man, Flashy Flash, Zombieman, Drive Knight, Superalloy Darkshine, King, Metal Knight by absence, Blast by absence, Bang, Genos, Puri-Puri Prisoner, and others. The room is full of power, but not unity.

This is key. S-Class is not presented as a noble order of knights. It is a collection of eccentric, arrogant, disciplined, strange, unstable, secretive, or barely cooperative individuals whose power exceeds ordinary management. The Hero Association needs them, but it does not fully control them.

The meeting introduces Shibabawa’s prophecy: “Earth is in danger.” The prophecy sharply raises the scale. Up to this point, disasters have been city-threatening, region-threatening, or institution-threatening. Now the language is planetary.

Before the prophecy can be properly digested, an alien ship arrives over A-City. The Dark Matter Thieves attack. A-City is devastated almost instantly, but the Hero Association headquarters survives because of its extreme defensive construction. This visual contrast matters: the civilian city is erased, while the institution’s fortress remains. The association can protect itself better than it can protect the city around it.

The S-Class heroes split into two fields of action.

Outside, several heroes battle **Melzargard**, a regenerating alien warrior with multiple bodies or heads and hidden cores. This fight lets the manga show S-Class teamwork and difference. Atomic Samurai cuts, Bang strikes, Puri-Puri Prisoner brawls, Metal Bat swings, and others observe or intervene. Melzargard’s regeneration forces them to think beyond simple damage. He is not Saitama’s kind of enemy; he is an enemy designed for tactical group combat.

Inside the ship, Saitama wanders. This is one of the funniest and most telling uses of him so far. While S-Class heroes engage in a high-stakes battle outside, Saitama treats the alien flagship like an annoying building full of people who will not explain where the boss is. The chapter titles around “men who do not listen” and “are you stupid?” fit the comedy: alien commanders, heroes, monsters, and Saitama are all operating from incompatible assumptions.

Saitama encounters powerful alien fighters. Their scale, psychic abilities, regeneration, and command structure are impressive by the story’s normal standards. But Saitama is not impressed. He defeats them with the same combination of deadpan irritation and absurd power that has defined him from the start.

The most important setup comes with **Boros**. Boros is introduced as the leader of the Dark Matter Thieves, a galactic conqueror who has come because of a prophecy promising a worthy opponent. Even before the proper fight, the mirror is obvious:

> Boros crosses the universe seeking the same thing Saitama dreams of: a real fight.

That is the batch’s major endpoint. Saitama’s existential boredom is no longer just a local gag. The story has introduced a cosmic-scale version of the same hunger.

# 2\. Deep Thematic Analysis

## A. Misrecognition becomes the central social problem

Volumes 1–3 already showed that Saitama was invisible to the world. Volumes 4–6 make that invisibility more active and more painful.

The meteor and Deep Sea King arcs both ask:

> What happens after the hero saves people but the public story goes wrong?

The meteor crowd blames Saitama because they still suffered. From a human perspective, this is not entirely irrational. Their homes are destroyed; their lives are disrupted; they saw a meteor become fragments. People in pain often search for the nearest visible cause. Saitama becomes that cause.

The Deep Sea King crowd does something different. They do not blame Saitama for damage. They resent the possibility that their emotional investment in the beaten heroes was misplaced. The defeated heroes suffered, bled, and failed in front of them. Then Saitama arrives and ends the threat in one hit. That makes the entire emotional structure awkward. If the monster was so easy for Saitama, were the other heroes weak? Were their sacrifices meaningless?

The crowd cannot hold the truth cleanly:

-   Deep Sea King was terrifying.

-   The defeated heroes were brave.

-   Saitama was incomparably stronger.

-   Their suffering still mattered.


So a simpler lie appears: Saitama is a fraud.

Saitama chooses to inhabit that lie for the sake of the others. That is one of the first moments where his relationship to recognition becomes ethically complex rather than merely comic.

## B. Heroism without power versus power without recognition

Mumen Rider is the crucial figure here.

He is not strong enough to win. He is not secretly S-Class. He has no hidden transformation. The manga refuses to reward his courage with a power-up. That refusal is important. Mumen Rider’s heroism is not validated by sudden effectiveness. It is validated by willingness.

Saitama is the opposite extreme: power without recognition. Mumen Rider is recognition-worthy heroism without power.

Together they create one of the central tensions of *One Punch Man*:

> Heroism is not identical to strength.
> But strength still matters when people need saving.

The manga avoids a sentimental answer. Mumen Rider is morally magnificent, but he would have died without Saitama. Saitama is physically decisive, but the public cannot understand what he is. The story needs both of them to define heroism properly.

## C. Public emotion is unstable and easily redirected

The crowd scenes in these volumes are brutal.

People cheer, panic, blame, doubt, resent, and reverse themselves rapidly. The manga does not portray civilians as evil. It portrays them as emotionally vulnerable under disaster conditions. They need heroes, but they also need a story that makes the disaster intelligible.

That is why rumor and rank matter. Most people cannot directly evaluate heroic action. They rely on labels:

-   C-Class

-   B-Class

-   S-Class

-   celebrity

-   known hero

-   criminal

-   monster

-   fraud

-   savior


Saitama’s problem is that he violates the labels. Mumen Rider’s problem is that his label underrates his spirit but accurately reflects his combat limits. Amai Mask’s power is that he understands labels as public reality.

## D. S-Class is not moral elevation

The move from C/B-Class concerns to S-Class worldbuilding could have been simple escalation: now we meet the strongest good guys. Instead, the manga makes S-Class morally and socially messy.

S-Class heroes are impressive, but they are not uniformly admirable. They are proud, weird, absent, dismissive, vain, violent, secretive, or socially dysfunctional. Some are deeply heroic. Some are ambiguous. Some are dangerous tools. Some are barely integrated into the institution around them.

This continues the manga’s argument that rank measures capacity, not moral clarity.

Genos is S-Class because he is obviously powerful.
Mumen Rider is C-Class despite being spiritually heroic.
Saitama is B-Class despite being functionally supreme.
Metal Knight is S-Class but ethically suspect.
Amai Mask is A-Class but exercises enormous cultural power.

The rank system is real, but not true in the deeper sense.

## E. The scale of threat rises, but Saitama’s inner problem does not change

The batch escalates from meteor to Deep Sea King to alien invasion. Structurally, these would be huge arc endpoints in another manga. In *One Punch Man*, the escalation proves the opposite: no matter how large the threat becomes, Saitama’s basic condition remains unchanged.

That is why Boros matters even before his full fight. Boros is the first enemy whose motivation directly mirrors Saitama’s boredom. He is not merely invading Earth for resources or conquest. He has come because prophecy promised him someone who could make him feel alive.

Saitama wanted Subterraneans who could challenge him. Boros crossed space for the same reason.

The implication is sharp: the search for a worthy opponent can become either melancholy or imperial violence. Saitama becomes a bored hobby hero. Boros becomes a conqueror. Same hunger, very different ethics.

# 3\. Character Updates

## Saitama

This batch deepens Saitama considerably.

He is still funny because he is blunt, bored, cheap, and emotionally flat. But Volumes 4–6 prove that he is not simply apathetic. His moral intelligence is rough but real.

Key developments:

-   He saves Z-City from the meteor but refuses to perform gratitude politics.

-   He understands that the crowd’s anger is emotionally stupid but socially real.

-   He kills Deep Sea King instantly, then intentionally damages his own reputation to protect other heroes.

-   He treats Mumen Rider’s gratitude more seriously than public applause.

-   He remains almost immune to intimidation, whether from S-Class heroes, aliens, monsters, or institutional settings.

-   He repeatedly ignores speeches, not only for comedy but because he does not grant grandeur automatic authority.


The most important update:

> Saitama does not need recognition to act, but he is not indifferent to what recognition does to others.

That is why the Deep Sea King lie matters. It shows that Saitama can use his own invisibility as a shield for people whose reputations are more fragile.

## Genos

Genos develops from obsessive disciple into a more recognizably heroic protector.

Against the meteor, he embodies effort under impossible conditions. Against Deep Sea King, he makes a clear protective sacrifice. He is still too serious, too eager to stake everything, and still prone to being outmatched by threats that Saitama trivializes. But these defeats are not empty. They build his moral profile.

Genos’s mistake is not cowardice or vanity. It is over-identification with usefulness. He wants to be worthy of Saitama, worthy of S-Class, worthy of his revenge quest, worthy of survival. The Deep Sea King arc shows that even when he loses, he can still act heroically.

## Mumen Rider

Mumen Rider is the moral center of this batch.

He is weak by the setting’s combat standards, but his heroism is unusually pure. He does not mistake himself for strong. He does not posture. He does not try to convert weakness into ideology. He knows he cannot win and goes anyway because someone has to stand between the monster and the civilians.

He exposes a truth the ranking system cannot express:

> There are heroes who cannot win, but whose willingness to stand still matters.

That does not make him secretly the “best” hero in a practical disaster-response sense. A city cannot survive on Mumen Rider alone. But a hero society without people like him would be spiritually rotten.

## Silver Fang / Bang

Bang becomes the first S-Class hero who feels truly perceptive about Saitama. He is old, disciplined, and grounded in martial tradition. Unlike Genos, he does not worship Saitama with youthful intensity. Unlike the Hero Association, he does not reduce him to rank. He watches.

Bang’s role is important because he brings a form of wisdom that is neither bureaucratic nor technological. He understands strength through body, practice, age, and intuition. That lets him recognize anomalies more cleanly than the institution can.

## Metal Knight

Metal Knight is introduced as power without trust.

He has immense technology, but his first major action is framed as weapons testing rather than rescue. This does not yet make him a villain, but it makes him one of the clearest examples of the series’ suspicion toward instrumental power.

He represents the question:

> What if a hero’s power is real, but his relationship to the public good is opaque?

## Tanktop Tiger and Tanktop Black Hole

These two are minor in combat terms but important socially. They show how weak or middling heroes may police status through resentment, crowd manipulation, and rank anxiety. They are not monsters, but they behave parasitically within hero society.

Their attack on Saitama after the meteor shows that professional heroism can reproduce petty hierarchy. They cannot defeat major threats, but they can try to control public interpretation.

## Puri-Puri Prisoner

Puri-Puri Prisoner introduces one of the series’ strangest tensions: a criminal, predatory-coded hero who is nevertheless brave in battle and counted among S-Class. His presence makes the Hero Association morally bizarre. It needs power badly enough to incorporate deeply compromised figures.

At the same time, his fight with Deep Sea King is not cowardly. He goes, fights, loses, and survives. The manga often uses him as a joke, but structurally he also expands the question of whether a “hero” is defined by public utility, personal morality, combat power, or institutional classification.

## Amai Mask

Amai Mask is introduced as a gatekeeper of heroic image.

He is crucial because he complicates the rank hierarchy. Officially, he is A-Class Rank 1, below S-Class. Socially, he has enormous power. He understands popularity, aesthetics, public legitimacy, and the symbolic meaning of promotion.

He is the opposite of Saitama in one key respect:

> Saitama has reality without image.
> Amai Mask has image as power.

That makes him one of the most important early figures for the manga’s recognition politics.

## Tatsumaki

Tatsumaki’s introduction immediately communicates overwhelming power and terrible social grace. She is childish, arrogant, insulting, and genuinely terrifying. She helps define S-Class as a space where immense ability does not necessarily produce maturity.

She also introduces psychic power as one of the setting’s major non-physical power types.

## Boros

In Volume 6, Boros is mostly setup, but it is excellent setup. His core function is already visible: he is Saitama’s cosmic mirror. He is a being who became so strong that ordinary conquest no longer satisfied him. He seeks prophecy, challenge, and a worthy opponent.

The difference is that Saitama’s boredom has not made him predatory. Boros’s boredom has.

# 4\. Worldbuilding Updates

Volumes 4–6 expand the world in several major ways.

## Disaster levels become more meaningful

The meteor and Deep Sea King arcs make disaster levels feel like public language rather than just flavor text. Threats are classified, announced, and filtered through response protocols. The public understands danger through these categories.

The key point is that classification helps organize response, but does not guarantee understanding. A Dragon-level meteor, a Demon-level monster, and a planetary prophecy all produce different kinds of panic.

## Hero Association infrastructure

The Hero Association is now seen as a physical and bureaucratic institution:

-   exams;

-   promotion systems;

-   headquarters;

-   emergency meetings;

-   disaster response;

-   rank management;

-   public communication;

-   S-Class mobilization;

-   fortified facilities.


The A-City headquarters surviving the alien bombardment is especially important. It shows the Association’s technological and financial power, but also creates a troubling image: the institution survives while the surrounding city is obliterated.

## S-Class as unstable elite

S-Class becomes a major worldbuilding category. It is not just “top heroes.” It is a semi-autonomous elite class with wildly different power sources and personalities.

The batch introduces or foregrounds:

-   martial arts;

-   cyborg weaponry;

-   psychic power;

-   swordsmanship;

-   brute strength;

-   regeneration;

-   remote weapons systems;

-   child genius technology;

-   celebrity power;

-   possibly unknown or absent supreme figures like Blast.


The elite hero world is plural and unstable.

## Prophecy enters the setting

Shibabawa’s prophecy adds a new genre element. Until now, the series has mostly parodied action, superhero, monster, and disaster genres. Prophecy introduces apocalyptic fantasy logic. It also creates institutional panic: a vague prediction becomes a policy problem.

## Aliens and cosmic scale

The Dark Matter Thieves expand the setting beyond Earth. This is a huge move structurally. The world is not merely full of monsters and strange humans; it exists in a universe where alien conquerors operate at planetary scale.

Yet the comedy remains: Saitama handles cosmic invaders with the same irritation he brings to local problems.

# 5\. New Motifs and Symbols

## Debris

The meteor arc is full of fragments. Saitama saves the city by breaking the meteor, but the broken pieces still damage everything. This is a perfect image for partial salvation.

The motif returns in social form: truth itself breaks into fragments. The crowd sees damage, not salvation. Recognition fragments just like the meteor.

## Rain

The Deep Sea King arc is built around rain.

Rain empowers the sea monster, darkens the city, isolates civilians, and gives the arc its mood of damp despair. It also makes Saitama’s entrance feel anti-heroic: he is late partly because ordinary weather and ordinary inconvenience still exist for him.

Rain here means pressure. It is the environment itself turning hostile.

## The shelter

The shelter symbolizes institutional safety. It is where civilians gather because the system says they will be protected. Deep Sea King entering the shelter violates that promise. The place of safety becomes a stage of helplessness.

This is one of the manga’s sharper images: society can build shelters, but someone still has to stand at the door.

## The bicycle

Mumen Rider’s bicycle is one of the strongest symbols in the early series. It is ridiculous next to cyborg bodies, alien ships, meteor weapons, and psychic powers. But it is also pure. He arrives by effort, not spectacle.

The bicycle is heroism scaled to ordinary human capacity.

## Letters and thanks

Mumen Rider’s thank-you letter is quiet but important. In a world of rankings, rumors, crowds, and exams, a handwritten expression of gratitude feels more real than mass recognition.

Saitama does not need fame, but he is not immune to being genuinely thanked.

## The S-Class table

The meeting table in Volume 6 is a visual symbol of institutional power trying to contain uncontainable people. The heroes sit together, but they are not unified. The table suggests order; the people around it suggest volatility.

## The alien ship

The Dark Matter ship is the largest visual object in the manga so far. It symbolizes cosmic escalation, but also bureaucratic failure: all the meeting, prophecy, and response structure cannot prevent A-City from being erased. The ship dwarfs human systems.

Then Saitama walks through it like an annoyed tenant looking for the superintendent.

# 6\. Power System Observations

This batch dramatically expands the power ecology.

## Saitama remains outside the system

The meteor, Deep Sea King, and alien ship all confirm the same rule:

> Saitama is not at the top of the power system. He is outside the power system.

That distinction matters. The manga can introduce Dragon-level disasters, S-Class elites, psychic powers, regeneration cores, alien conquerors, and world-ending prophecies. Saitama does not merely rank above them; he breaks the meaning of ranking.

## S-Class power is diverse

S-Class is not one kind of strength. It includes:

-   Bang’s martial mastery;

-   Genos’s cyborg firepower;

-   Tatsumaki’s psychic force;

-   Atomic Samurai’s sword technique;

-   Metal Knight’s remote robotics;

-   Puri-Puri Prisoner’s brute physicality;

-   Metal Bat’s fighting spirit and bat combat;

-   Pig God’s strange bodily capacity;

-   Watchdog Man’s animalistic style;

-   Zombieman’s implied regeneration;

-   Child Emperor’s intelligence and tools;

-   Drive Knight’s unknown mechanics;

-   King’s reputation;

-   Blast’s absence.


This diversity matters because it prevents the world from collapsing into one simple power scale. The ranking system tries to order them, but the actual powers are qualitatively different.

## Regeneration and hidden cores

Melzargard introduces a more tactical kind of enemy. He cannot simply be damaged conventionally; he must be understood. The heroes must discover the relationship between his bodies and his cores.

This is important because it creates fights Saitama is not structurally needed for. S-Class heroes can have meaningful tactical combat against enemies who would be irrelevant to Saitama.

## Psychic power

Tatsumaki and alien psychics introduce psychokinesis as a major force. Psychic power expands the setting beyond physical strength, martial arts, and technology. It also gives the manga a visual language of invisible force, floating debris, and spatial distortion.

## Disaster levels are useful but incomplete

Threat levels help describe public danger, but they do not map cleanly onto narrative or moral importance. Deep Sea King is lower-scale than the meteor or alien invasion, but his arc is more morally revealing. The meteor is physically massive, but socially simple. Boros’s invasion is cosmic, but Saitama’s personal problem remains unchanged.

# 7\. Satirical Targets

## Public ingratitude and crowd psychology

The meteor and Deep Sea King arcs both satirize how quickly gratitude can become accusation. The public wants safety, but when safety is incomplete or confusing, it looks for someone to blame.

The satire is not “ordinary people are stupid” in a simple sense. It is sharper: disaster creates emotional needs that truth does not always satisfy.

## Hero ranking culture

These volumes intensify the critique of ranking. Rank determines status, but not truth. Saitama is under-ranked. Mumen Rider is low-ranked but morally luminous. Amai Mask is not S-Class but has enormous cultural authority. Metal Knight is S-Class but ethically opaque.

The rank system is useful enough to persist and flawed enough to mislead.

## Celebrity gatekeeping

Amai Mask introduces heroism as brand management. He is concerned with who gets to ascend, how heroes look, and how the public understands them. He is not simply vain; he embodies a real social function. That makes the satire stronger. Image management is absurd, but in a hero society, it may also be politically necessary.

## Disaster bureaucracy

The Hero Association responds to threats through alerts, meetings, classifications, shelters, and mobilization. Some of this works. Much of it is inadequate. The satire is not anti-institutional in a flat way; it shows that institutions are necessary but structurally slow, status-conscious, and vulnerable to misclassification.

## Techno-instrumental heroism

Metal Knight is the main target here. Power used at a distance, with unclear motives and experimental logic, is treated with suspicion. The manga asks whether saving people is still the point when disaster becomes a testing ground.

## Apocalyptic prophecy

Shibabawa’s prophecy satirizes the institutional panic caused by vague but credible doom. “Earth is in danger” is maximally serious and minimally actionable. The Hero Association must respond, but the information is almost useless.

## Villain grandeur

Deep Sea King, alien commanders, and Boros all bring theatrical menace. The manga allows them to be visually impressive and genuinely threatening to others, then places Saitama beside them as a deflationary force.

The satire is not that villains are never dangerous. They are very dangerous. The joke is that danger is relative, and Saitama ruins their genre.

# 8\. Philosophical Questions Raised

## Does heroism require effectiveness?

Mumen Rider forces this question. His courage is real, but he cannot win. The manga honors him without pretending courage alone saves the day. That creates a mature answer:

> Heroism requires willingness, but society also needs effective power.

Neither Mumen Rider nor Saitama alone fully defines heroism. One gives moral clarity; the other gives decisive capacity.

## What does a hero owe the public?

Saitama saves people but refuses to flatter them. After the meteor, he does not comfort the crowd. After Deep Sea King, he lies to protect the other heroes. This suggests that a hero’s duty is not identical to public emotional satisfaction.

## Is reputation part of heroism?

The Deep Sea King arc says yes, but indirectly. Saitama sacrifices his own reputation because the defeated heroes’ reputations matter. If the public loses faith in all heroes, the social fabric weakens. Reputation is not the essence of heroism, but it affects whether heroism can function socially.

## Can institutions recognize the exceptional?

The Hero Association keeps failing to interpret Saitama. This raises a broader problem: institutions need categories, but truly exceptional cases may exceed the categories that make institutions work.

## What is the moral difference between Saitama and Boros?

Both are too strong. Both are bored. Both want a real fight. But Saitama still helps people; Boros conquers worlds. The batch sets up one of the series’ most important ethical contrasts:

> The desire for challenge is not itself evil.
> What matters is what you are willing to do to satisfy it.

## Is power socially meaningful without witnesses?

Saitama’s power is factually supreme, but socially unstable. Genos, Bang, Mumen Rider, and a few others begin to serve as witnesses. The manga is slowly building a tiny counter-public around Saitama: people who know, even if the world does not.

# 9\. Literary and Visual Observations

Murata’s art becomes even more important in this batch. The meteor, rain, Sea King shelter, S-Class meeting, alien bombardment, and Dark Matter ship all show how much the manga depends on visual seriousness.

The comedy only works because the threats look real. Deep Sea King is not lazily drawn. The meteor is not a scribble. The alien ship is not a gag object. Murata renders them with enough grandeur that Saitama’s flatness becomes metaphysical comedy.

Several visual patterns stand out:

-   **Saitama’s blank face beside catastrophe**: his simplicity becomes more absurd as the scale grows.

-   **Crowd faces**: the public is drawn as a collective emotional organism, shifting from fear to hope to resentment.

-   **Rain texture**: the Deep Sea King arc uses weather as emotional pressure.

-   **Huge institutional spaces**: Hero Association rooms make individuals look like pieces in a system.

-   **Alien scale**: the ship’s mass over the city creates the first true cosmic visual.

-   **Impact contrast**: Genos’s attacks are complex and technical; Saitama’s attacks are visually simple but ontologically final.


The batch also uses structural repetition very effectively. Again and again, characters prepare speeches, analyses, classifications, or techniques. Then Saitama ignores the terms of the scene. The title “men who do not listen” is not just a joke. Saitama often refuses the narrative frame other characters try to impose.

# 10\. Comparative Notes for Future Discussions

## Compared to My Hero Academia

These volumes sharpen the contrast with *My Hero Academia*.

*MHA* is deeply invested in the social function of heroic symbols. All Might’s smile matters because public trust matters. *One Punch Man* is much more cynical about public trust: the crowd can misread, resent, and scapegoat the savior almost immediately.

But the two works overlap around one issue:

> Heroism is not reducible to strength.

Mumen Rider is an especially useful comparison to early Deku. Both act despite lacking sufficient power. The difference is that *MHA* rewards Deku’s heroic nature with inherited power. *OPM* does not reward Mumen Rider that way. His weakness remains. His heroism is therefore purer but less practically triumphant.

## Compared to Solo Leveling

Jinwoo’s world ranks people by power, and the drama comes from him breaking the fixed ranking system through growth. Saitama also breaks ranking systems, but without growth. He is already beyond them.

Jinwoo’s misrecognition is temporary; eventually the world must acknowledge him.

Saitama’s misrecognition is structural and comic; even when evidence appears, the social story bends away from truth.

## Compared to To Be Hero X

This batch makes the *To Be Hero X* comparison stronger.

*To Be Hero X* asks how public belief manufactures heroic reality. *One Punch Man* shows a world where public belief constantly fails to track heroic reality. Saitama’s problem is not that belief gives him power; it is that belief cannot see power when it lacks the right image.

Amai Mask would fit very naturally into a *To Be Hero X* discussion because he understands heroism as public image management. Saitama is the opposite: pure heroic fact without adequate public authorship.

## Compared to Mob Psycho 100

Saitama and Mob remain strong mirrors as ONE protagonists. Both possess overwhelming power that does not solve ordinary human problems.

Mob’s challenge is emotional growth and self-acceptance.

Saitama’s challenge is meaning, recognition, and the problem of being finished before the story begins.

Mumen Rider also resembles Reigen in an odd structural way: both are weak or fraudulent relative to the supernatural/power scale, but both reveal that human courage, timing, and moral presence can matter even in worlds dominated by overwhelming force.

## Compared to traditional superhero fiction

Mumen Rider is the “with great power comes great responsibility” formula inverted:

> He has almost no power, but still accepts responsibility.

Saitama inverts it the other way:

> He has absolute power, but almost no conventional heroic image.

Amai Mask represents the superhero as celebrity brand. Metal Knight represents the superhero as military-industrial asset. Bang represents the superhero as martial elder. Genos represents the superhero as tragic cybernetic avenger. The series is now building a full taxonomy of hero types.

# Batch Conclusion

Volumes 4–6 are where *One Punch Man* becomes a genuine social satire of heroism.

The first batch asked what happens when a hero is too strong to enjoy battle. This batch asks what happens when that same hero enters a society that cannot correctly interpret him.

The meteor arc shows that saving people does not guarantee gratitude.
The Deep Sea King arc shows that losing heroes can still be heroic.
Mumen Rider proves that heroism can exist without power.
Genos proves that defeat can still contain sacrifice.
Amai Mask proves that image can become its own kind of authority.
S-Class proves that strength creates hierarchy, not moral unity.
Boros introduces the frightening possibility that Saitama’s boredom, under different ethical conditions, could become cosmic violence.

The strongest formulation for this batch is:

> **Saitama is the strongest hero, Mumen Rider is the clearest hero, and the Hero Association is the system that cannot fully understand either of them.**

That is the foundation the next batch will inherit.
