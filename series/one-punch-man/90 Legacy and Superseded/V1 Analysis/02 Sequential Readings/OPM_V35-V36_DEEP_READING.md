## Extracted V1 deep reading

# *One Punch Man* Volumes 35–36 — Deep Reading and Analysis

## Scope

This reading covers the Japanese tankōbon material in:

**Volume 35:** 176「会っちゃいけない奴」 through 181「震源」, plus 「怖くないよ」
**Volume 36:** 182「超常とリスク」 through 188「取引」, plus 「最初の勇気」

I am treating 35–36 as one continuous unit, but preserving the distinction between what each volume accomplishes. Nothing from Volume 37 or later serialization is being used.

The decision to supply the later Amai Mask material in Japanese also makes sense. After 36, the project will retain an essentially uninterrupted Japanese-language evidentiary chain for the material where voice and self-presentation become especially important.

---

# I. Executive thesis: after saving the universe, *One Punch Man* turns to the politics of living together

Volumes 31–34 pushed *One Punch Man* to almost absurd cosmological limits. Garou acquires divine power. Saitama and Garou leave Earth. Jupiter is physically devastated. Causality itself is reversed.

Volumes 35–36 respond by doing something much more interesting than simply beginning another escalation.

They ask:

> **After overwhelming power has saved the world, how are people supposed to live together?**

The central problems become classification, belonging, autonomy, protection, institutional legitimacy, and voluntary association.

Who counts as a person?

Who gets to decide that?

When does protection become possession?

What is the difference between having followers and having people who freely choose to stand beside you?

Does weakness define a person's future?

How much moral obligation do we owe a mere acquaintance?

What happens when public confidence is maintained through a useful lie?

And what kind of society responds to catastrophe by turning monsters, heroes, refugees, and psychic bodies into commodities?

The strongest combined formulation is:

> **Volumes 35–36 move One Punch Man from the problem of overwhelming power to the problem of social classification: who is a hero, who is a monster, who belongs to whom, who may rely on whom, and whether institutions built around strength can recognize personhood without turning it into property.**

Saitama becomes extraordinarily important in this transition because his ethics are almost aggressively anti-categorical.

He does not ask what something *is supposed to be*.

He asks what it is actually doing.

That simple instinct increasingly places him in opposition to the world's institutions.

---

# II. Volume 35: aftermath becomes structure

Volume 35 is very deliberately an **aftermath volume**.

But aftermath does not mean inactivity. The Monster Association catastrophe has changed the political economy of hero society. The dead are still being recovered. The Hero Association is fortifying itself. Its financial weakness is becoming visible. Strange surviving monsters have to be classified. Hero rankings are shifting. Hidden organizations smell opportunity.

The war is over, but everything created by the war is still moving.

## 176「会っちゃいけない奴」 — heroism after victory

The opening is important because it refuses to pretend that defeating the boss ends a disaster.

Pig God continues participating in rescue and recovery despite his own condition. Bodies and survivors still have to be found. This returns *One Punch Man* to a recurring distinction:

> **winning the battle and doing the work of heroism are not identical.**

Saitama meanwhile ends up moving through the devastated Z-City landscape with Black Sperm and Rover.

That looks comic, but it begins one of the most consequential ideas of the two-volume batch.

These are, categorically, monsters.

Black Sperm was an extraordinarily dangerous Monster Association cadre. Rover was a major combat threat. The Hero Association's normal answer to either is obvious: extermination.

Saitama does something else.

He responds to their **current conduct**.

Rover behaves increasingly like an animal. Black Sperm, reduced and terrified, behaves opportunistically rather than as an immediate existential threat. Saitama does not construct a theory of monster rehabilitation. He simply does not see a reason to kill something that presently is not attacking anyone.

This develops something already visible in his treatment of Manako:

> **Saitama's moral categories are behavioral rather than ontological.**

That difference will become much more politically significant later in Volume 35.

---

# III. 177「新居」 — the Hero Association builds a fortress, Saitama brings monsters inside it

「新居」—“New Home”—is a very good chapter title because the word *home* becomes ironic almost immediately.

Saitama has now become:

**A級39位ヒーロー ハゲマント**
A-Class Rank 39 Hero, Caped Baldy.

He consequently receives access to the Hero Association's enormous fortified headquarters/residential environment.

The Association's answer to catastrophe is revealing.

It does not primarily become more democratic, transparent, community-oriented, or ethically sophisticated.

It becomes **more secure**.

The headquarters is increasingly a fortress and protected residential environment. Elite heroes function not merely as emergency responders but as the security infrastructure surrounding people wealthy or privileged enough to live close to concentrated heroic power.

This is an important development in the institutional satire.

Earlier *One Punch Man* criticized:

-   rankings;

-   celebrity;

-   donor politics;

-   bureaucratic blindness;

-   public misrecognition.


Now it begins criticizing something more structural:

> **the privatization of safety.**

Monster attacks make ordinary cities increasingly precarious. The Hero Association responds by concentrating protection inside a hardened enclave.

Heroic power becomes real estate value.

And then Saitama arrives with **two monsters**.

That is almost perfect.

Metal Knight's automated defense apparatus detects Black Sperm and Rover correctly according to its taxonomy.

They are monsters.

The machine therefore attacks.

Saitama destroys the security system.

The joke reveals a genuine moral problem:

> **The automated classifier is factually correct and ethically inadequate.**

It knows what species/category Black Sperm and Rover belong to.

It does not know what they are presently doing.

Saitama has less information and better judgment.

This gives us one of the clearest contrasts yet between Saitama's practical ethics and institutional ethics:

**Institution:** Monster → threat → eliminate.
**Saitama:** Is it attacking anybody? No? Then why are we killing it?

That is not universal pacifism. Saitama kills active threats without much hesitation.

It is **anti-essentialism**.

---

# IV. 178「秘匿情報」 — truth exists, but no institution possesses all of it

「秘匿情報」—“Confidential Information”—widens the problem from physical security to information.

Several people now know fragments of what really happened during the Monster Association disaster.

Genos knows the impossible Saitama story.

Blast possesses knowledge of God and the larger cosmic conflict.

Flashy Flash has seen Saitama behave in ways incompatible with his nominal rank.

Bofoi/Metal Knight now has surveillance data showing that the newly promoted A-Class hero is capable of destroying supposedly formidable security infrastructure with absurd ease.

The Hero Association possesses reports.

But there is no single coherent institutional understanding of reality.

This is very important after Volume 34.

Genos's surviving core made him the **archive of an erased history**. Volume 35 follows through on that role: Genos is still the person attempting to translate Saitama's impossible reality into communicable information.

Yet testimony does not automatically become accepted truth.

People interpret through preexisting models.

Flash interprets Saitama through elite competition.

Bofoi interprets him through strategic threat assessment.

Genos interprets him through discipleship.

The executives interpret events through institutional risk.

Saitama himself barely cares.

So the chapter establishes a new epistemic problem:

> **The truth about power exists, but hero society lacks a common language capable of processing it.**

This has always been true of Saitama, but after Garou the consequences are larger. The institution's most important reality is effectively becoming classified folklore.

---

# V. 179「華」 — A-Class hierarchy meets the man who does not understand why hierarchy matters

Saitama's introduction to his A-Class neighbors is an excellent miniature version of the whole series.

Forte, Butterfly DX, Chain'n'Toad, and the others are not cosmic beings. They live much closer to the ordinary professional ecology of hero work.

That gives them recognizable concerns:

rank, territory, seniority, reputation, competence, professional etiquette.

Saitama has entered their social level without internalizing any of its norms.

The resulting friction is revealing.

Forte wants to establish the new arrival's place. That is perfectly understandable inside the Association's professional culture.

Saitama does not really grasp why any of this should matter.

The sequence also uses one of *One Punch Man*'s favorite formal tricks: heroic self-seriousness is punctured by mundane contingency. Forte's martial seriousness does not culminate in some grand proof of skill. Ordinary urban reality intrudes.

The point is not that Forte is worthless.

It is that the narrative refuses to let his identity become cosmically important merely because he believes it should be.

Yet this relationship changes by Volume 36.

Saitama's new neighbors begin skeptical of him.

They end with much greater respect.

That is an important change in Saitama's recognition pattern.

For years, he has been institutionally and publicly misrecognized. These volumes begin creating **small pockets of empirical recognition** around him.

Not universal fame.

Not the Symbol of Peace.

Just people nearby thinking:

> Something about this guy is seriously abnormal.

That is probably a much healthier form of recognition for Saitama than celebrity would be.

---

# VI. 180「訪問者」 — the darkest institutional development yet: monsterhood becomes legal non-personhood

This is where Volume 35 becomes considerably more severe.

Fubuki goes to the Hero Association's special detention facility to see Psykos.

Psykos is no longer simply a defeated enemy. She is a prisoner, a psychic research asset, and—most importantly—a **legally dehumanized body**.

The Tsukuyomi representative and Association personnel discuss the scientific value of her psychic transformation. Her temporary ability to fight Tatsumaki at extraordinary levels makes her biologically and psychically valuable.

Then the manga gives us one of the ugliest lines in the Association material so far.

Because Psykos has been officially classified as a monster:

**怪人認定して人権は剥奪してありますから**

Her human rights have been stripped.

Therefore:

**どんな実験でもご自由にどうぞ**

Essentially, conduct whatever experiments you like.

This is a major escalation in *One Punch Man*'s institutional critique.

Earlier, “monster” was primarily a disaster category.

Now it is also a **legal category of non-personhood**.

That changes everything.

The Monster Association's existence makes this especially uncomfortable because many monsters are transformed humans. The story has spent dozens of volumes destabilizing the human/monster boundary:

Garou attempts monsterization.

Amai Mask is increasingly suspicious.

Phoenix Man's identity is entangled with costume and role.

Manako behaves sympathetically.

Rover becomes doglike.

Black Sperm can become a terrified survivor.

Monster cells can transform people.

Psykos herself is human-origin.

And yet the Association's legal framework effectively says:

> Once classified as 怪人, personhood ends.

This is the exact opposite of Saitama's ethics.

The contrast is almost too clean:

> **Saitama shelters dangerous former enemies because they are presently behaving peacefully.
> The Hero Association removes a human-derived prisoner's rights because a classification says she is a monster.**

This is one of the most important developments in the manga's philosophy of monsterhood to date.

---

# VII. Fubuki and Psykos: Volume 35 radically enlarges Fubuki's history

The school flashback in 181「震源」 is enormously valuable.

It reveals that Fubuki's faction-building tendencies predate the Hero Association.

She was already **会長 / kaichō**, the president or chair of a psychic-oriented school organization. Psykos addresses her in that role.

This complicates our earlier Fubuki interpretation.

We previously framed the Blizzard Group primarily as a compensation for living beneath Tatsumaki:

> If Fubuki cannot be at the top of hero society, she creates a smaller hierarchy where she can be at the top.

That remains true.

But Volume 35 shows that **group organization is also much more fundamental to her personality**.

Young Fubuki already thinks socially.

She gathers.

Organizes.

Absorbs rival groups.

Builds structures.

Psykos herself talks about enlarging their organization and eventually exerting influence far beyond school.

This makes Fubuki and Tatsumaki even more interesting as sisters.

Tatsumaki's instinct is:

> become powerful enough that nobody is necessary.

Fubuki's instinct is:

> organize people until collective structure compensates for individual weakness.

That opposition existed before professional heroism.

The Hero Association did not create it.

---

# VIII. Psykos: future sight as epistemic trauma

Psykos's flashback is also one of the most significant character additions in Volume 35.

Her ideological collapse does not simply begin from hatred or desire for power.

She researches a new psychic capability:

**未来を見通す“第三の眼”**
a “third eye” capable of seeing the future.

Then she sees something.

The manga deliberately does not fully reveal what.

What matters is what seeing it does to her.

Her language collapses into apocalyptic disgust. Humanity becomes, in her view, a species that merely eats, excretes, reproduces, multiplies, and consumes the planet without achieving meaningful “harmony.”

Her conclusion becomes exterminatory:

humanity must diminish;

the world must be “cleansed”;

the current order has no meaning.

This gives Psykos a fascinating epistemological problem.

> **What if knowledge does not enlighten the observer but destroys her capacity to live among ordinary people?**

Her future vision becomes the origin of ideological monstrosity.

This is not unlike several other *One Punch Man* villains, but with a different mechanism.

Garou takes a real social wound and universalizes it into absolute evil.

Homeless Emperor takes resentment and interprets divine selection as moral justification.

Psykos sees something true—or at least something she experiences as true—about the future and converts that revelation into a total judgment against humanity.

The missing content of the vision is therefore almost less important than its effect.

She cannot psychologically integrate what she sees.

Knowledge becomes radicalization.

Fubuki asks, in essence:

> **What did you see?**

Volume 35 does not answer.

That unresolved question is wisely preserved.

---

# IX. Fubuki/Psykos as mirror organizers

Their shared past changes the Monster Association retrospectively.

Psykos did not simply become a psychic villain from nowhere.

She learned to think in terms of organization, hierarchy, recruitment, intelligence, and group power in the same social world as Fubuki.

This creates a striking parallel:

**Fubuki → Blizzard Group**
**Psykos → Monster Association**

Obviously the moral difference is enormous.

But structurally, both are people who answer vulnerability by building organizations around themselves.

Fubuki uses organization to stabilize identity and collective security.

Psykos escalates organization into manipulation, monster cultivation, and ideological domination.

The Monster Association therefore becomes, in part, a grotesque answer to the same social instinct that produces Fubuki's faction.

That is much richer than “good psychic sister versus evil psychic schoolmate.”

---

# X. Volume 36: power becomes a relationship problem

Volume 35 establishes the institutional and psychic framework.

Volume 36 turns it into a direct character confrontation.

The central triangle becomes:

**Tatsumaki — Fubuki — Saitama**

And the governing question is:

> **Can Tatsumaki protect Fubuki without owning the conditions under which Fubuki is allowed to live?**

The answer, at the beginning, is no.

By the end, the answer becomes:

> perhaps slightly more than before.

That is the right degree of movement. Tatsumaki is not suddenly “healed.”

---

# XI. 182「超常とリスク」 — supernatural power as institutional risk

The Tsukuyomi material puts psychic power into a different frame.

To Fubuki and Tatsumaki, psychic ability is identity.

To Psykos, it becomes revelation and ideology.

To the secret psychic organization, it is research capital.

To the Hero Association, it is simultaneously strategic value and liability.

Hence the title:

**超常とリスク — “The Supernatural and Risk.”**

This is another way Volume 35–36 moves beyond simple power scaling.

The question is not only:

> How strong is an esper?

It is:

> Who owns an esper's body?
> Who studies it?
> Who regulates it?
> Who profits from it?
> Who gets classified as dangerous?

That turns psychic power into a political resource.

Tsukuyomi consequently matters not just as a new enemy faction, but as evidence that **human organizations can be every bit as objectifying as monster organizations**.

---

# XII. Saitama's intervention: practical ethics against family mythology

Saitama's response to Tatsumaki is characteristically unimpressive as rhetoric and excellent as ethics.

He sees Tatsumaki's treatment of Fubuki and basically says:

this is too much;

you're both heroes;

why are you destroying each other?

There is no speech about childhood trauma.

No theory of sibling attachment.

No diagnosis.

No grand heroic principle.

He sees conduct and asks whether it makes sense.

That is Saitama's moral intelligence.

He is extraordinarily poor at symbolic self-presentation but often very good at stripping symbolic justification away from behavior.

---

# XIII. 183「外でやれ！」 — one of the most Saitama chapter titles imaginable

Tatsumaki's psychic conflict has the potential to become a massive superhero spectacle.

Saitama's response:

**外でやれ！**

Do it outside.

This sounds like a parent responding to children fighting in the house.

And that is exactly why it is so effective.

The manga takes city-shattering psychic force and subjects it to ordinary civic morality:

> Don't break everybody else's stuff.

This will eventually become Saitama's clearest ethical statement to Tatsumaki.

The chapter also contains one of the most important relational jokes.

Tatsumaki demands to know what Saitama is to Fubuki.

He rejects the possibilities and eventually calls her:

**ただの知り合い**

“Just an acquaintance.”

Fubuki's reaction makes clear that this does not correspond to how *she* experiences the relationship.

But the phrase becomes more important later.

---

# XIV. “Just an acquaintance” becomes a theory of moral obligation

When Saitama eventually confronts Tatsumaki more directly, he invokes the phrase again:

**フブキのただの知り合いの力だぜ**

It is the power of “just an acquaintance of Fubuki.”

This is quietly one of Saitama's most philosophically revealing lines in the entire manga.

Tatsumaki's worldview is possessive.

Fubuki is her sister.

Therefore Tatsumaki has responsibility.

Therefore Tatsumaki has authority.

Therefore Tatsumaki may decide what relationships are safe for Fubuki.

Saitama's answer is radically less hierarchical:

> I don't need to own Fubuki.
> I don't need to be her master.
> I don't need to be her best friend.
> I don't need blood relation.
> I can be “just an acquaintance” and still have reason to intervene when someone is hurting her.

That is almost the opposite of possessive care.

It implies:

> **Relationship creates concern without creating ownership.**

This is exactly the ethical correction both psychic sisters need.

Fubuki has historically tended to convert relationship into hierarchy.

Tatsumaki converts relationship into protective control.

Saitama demonstrates relationship without either.

---

# XV. 184「目撃」 — power becomes publicly visible but not publicly intelligible

The Saitama–Tatsumaki conflict travels through inhabited space.

People witness impossible events.

Buildings and terrain are damaged.

The Hero Association attempts to make sense of what is happening.

Again, reality is visible without being legible.

Saitama also repeatedly pays attention to people, vehicles, buildings, and collateral conditions in a way that contrasts with Tatsumaki's intoxication with finally having someone she cannot simply overwhelm.

This is an important distinction.

Saitama is vastly stronger than Tatsumaki.

But he is the person more worried about the social environment.

Absolute power has not made him cosmically self-important.

That is one reason his strength remains morally safer than hers.

---

# XVI. 185「取り込み中」 — Feather and Erika as a miniature version of the main argument

The Feather subplot initially looks like a comedic side adventure interrupting the psychic battle.

It is thematically very well placed.

Feather has attempted to rescue Erika from criminals.

He learns that she had been coerced into deceiving him.

His response is not:

> You betrayed me, so you belong with them.

He understands coercion.

He refuses to turn her lack of freedom into moral condemnation.

His response moves toward:

> forget what happened; from here, live the freedom you wanted.

That miniature drama sits directly beside Tatsumaki's problem.

Both Feather and Tatsumaki claim protective relationships.

But Feather's successful form of protection ends in **returning freedom**.

Tatsumaki's damaged form ends in **restricting freedom**.

This makes the apparently minor gang subplot a useful ethical echo.

---

# XVII. Sonic: rivalry mythology meets a stupid physical world

Sonic's intervention is classic *One Punch Man*.

He sees Saitama in an ambiguous situation and immediately converts it into the continuing epic of Sonic versus Saitama.

Reality does not cooperate.

Tatsumaki treats him as an irritation.

His elaborate speed/technique drama is punctured by mundane accident.

This preserves Sonic's established structural function:

> **He keeps trying to force Saitama into a rival narrative that Saitama refuses to inhabit.**

Garou eventually becomes important enough that Saitama must engage his self-mythology.

Sonic remains almost gloriously incapable of achieving that status.

The world itself mocks the rivalry.

---

# XVIII. 187「未知数」 — the most important Tatsumaki chapter so far

「未知数」—“Unknown Quantity”—is the centerpiece of Volume 36.

At first glance, the unknown quantity is Saitama.

Tatsumaki cannot calculate him.

Psychic force that should move anything does not behave normally against him.

He repeatedly emerges unharmed.

She cannot find the limit.

But the chapter's deeper unknown quantity is **relationship**.

Tatsumaki knows how to measure psychic force.

She does not know how to measure what another person can mean without being either dependent or threatening.

---

# XIX. Tatsumaki enjoys herself — and that matters enormously

The narration gives us a remarkable insight.

Tatsumaki begins by telling herself that she is testing Saitama.

Then she gets carried away.

Why?

Because, outside life-or-death struggle, she has essentially never had someone against whom she can release her full power safely.

The text explicitly frames the fight as **fun** for her.

And she had believed:

> **only Blast could make something like this possible.**

That is a major psychological revelation.

Tatsumaki's problem has not simply been:

> nobody is as strong as me.

It has been:

> **I cannot express the full extent of what I am around other people without becoming dangerous to them.**

That is a relationship problem.

Saitama's invulnerability creates an unusual form of safety.

She can push.

He does not break.

She can escalate.

He does not fear her.

She can be excessive without immediately destroying the person in front of her.

Saitama therefore gives Tatsumaki something he himself almost never receives from battle:

> **play.**

This is a fascinating inversion.

Saitama wants someone capable of making battle meaningful through danger.

Tatsumaki discovers someone capable of making battle enjoyable through **safety**.

Those are opposite emotional problems produced by overwhelming strength.

---

# XX. Tatsumaki's deepest contradiction: she claims to need no one because she still needs Blast

The Blast material significantly revises our Tatsumaki deep dive.

Previously, the strongest formulation was:

> Tatsumaki learned that dependence is dangerous, so she made total self-reliance into her survival philosophy.

That remains correct but incomplete.

Volume 36 reveals the hidden underside.

Tatsumaki thinks:

> **フブキを過酷な運命に引きずり込んだのも私**
> I was the one who dragged Fubuki into this harsh fate.

And:

> **私は誰にも頼らず フブキを守る責任が——**
> I have the responsibility to protect Fubuki without relying on anyone.

That is exactly the Tatsumaki we already understood.

But then the manga reveals something more vulnerable.

Blast was:

> **タツマキを自由にしてくれた大人**

The adult who set Tatsumaki free.

And she developed:

> **ブラストに過度に期待を寄せる**

Excessive expectations toward Blast.

Her buried wishes are painfully childlike:

Blast should recognize how strong she has become.

Blast should show her a better way to live.

She wants to tell him:

**「ねえ ブラスト 私頑張ったよ」**
Hey, Blast. I worked hard.

But:

**「だけどうまくいかないの…」**
It isn't going well.

And, devastatingly:

**「ブラスト 私どうしたらいい…？」**
Blast, what should I do?

This changes the psychology considerably.

Tatsumaki is not a person who eliminated dependency.

She is a person who **concentrated dependency into one absent, idealized exceptional man** while rejecting dependence on everyone else.

Her emotional structure becomes:

> Ordinary people cannot be relied upon.
> I must be strong.
> I must protect Fubuki myself.
> But somewhere, Blast will eventually return and tell me I did well and show me what comes next.

That is not true independence.

It is **displaced dependency**.

And because Blast remains distant, the ideal cannot be corrected by ordinary human imperfection.

---

# XXI. The cut from Blast to Saitama

Visually, the sequence becomes particularly strong.

Tatsumaki's internal image of Blast and her unresolved request for guidance gives way to Saitama's almost comically blank face.

This should not be reduced to:

> Saitama is the new Blast.

That would be too neat and too romanticized.

The relationship is different.

But formally, the manga is very clearly placing Saitama into the space occupied by Tatsumaki's need for an adult who can:

-   survive her;

-   see her;

-   not exploit her;

-   not fear her;

-   and give her uncomplicated recognition.


And Saitama's response could hardly be less mythic.

He checks whether she is okay.

He tells her not to overdo it.

He says she is really impressive.

Then he gives her the moral rule she actually needs.

---

# XXII. Saitama's minimal ethic of freedom

His advice to Tatsumaki is almost hilariously small relative to the scale of her trauma:

> You're an esper or whatever—basically live how you want.

But if you're going to rampage:

> **人のもん壊すな**

Don't break other people's stuff.

And if she can manage that:

> **大したヒーローだと思うぜ お前は**

He thinks she's a great hero.

This is quintessential Saitama.

He does not require Tatsumaki to:

be nicer;

stop being psychic;

become emotionally normal;

submit to authority;

join a group;

repent for existing;

or become dependent.

His ethic is essentially:

> **Be what you are. Just respect the boundary where your freedom starts destroying other people's lives.**

This is probably the clearest expression yet of Saitama's **non-domination ethic**.

It is extraordinarily permissive about identity and extraordinarily firm about harm.

For Tatsumaki, that is almost the perfect corrective.

She has spent her life thinking power must either dominate or protect.

Saitama says power can simply exist freely—as long as it respects other people.

---

# XXIII. The head pat: ordinary recognition versus mythic salvation

The head pat deserves attention.

Tatsumaki has just internally regressed toward the child who wants Blast to acknowledge her.

Saitama gives her simple approval.

This could easily have been sentimentalized.

Instead, because Saitama is Saitama, it remains mundane.

That is what makes it work.

Blast is an ideal.

Saitama is a guy standing there.

Blast represents the extraordinary rescuer whom Tatsumaki has built into a psychological myth.

Saitama gives her something smaller:

> **immediate, ordinary recognition from someone who has nothing to gain from flattering her.**

That may ultimately be healthier.

Again, I would not romanticize this or turn it into hidden courtship. Its strongest function here is psychological and ethical.

---

# XXIV. Fubuki's dangerous overcorrection: “Then I'll fight alone too”

After everything that has happened, Fubuki initially draws exactly the wrong lesson.

She effectively tells Tatsumaki:

> You were right.
> My understanding was naive.
> I'll fight alone like you do.

This is extremely important.

Character growth is not linear.

Fubuki recognizes a flaw in her dependence on group structure—but responds by swinging toward Tatsumaki's pathological extreme.

That sets up the next development.

---

# XXV. The Blizzard Group chooses Fubuki

Earlier in Volume 36, Blizzard Group members openly confront the cost of following Fubuki.

They are being dragged into conflicts involving S-Class heroes, psychic conspiracies, and threats far beyond ordinary B-Class expectations.

Some naturally ask whether continuing is sane.

That matters enormously.

Because for perhaps the first time, the story emphasizes that **leaving is possible**.

Other members respond differently.

Their conclusion is not:

> Fubuki is entitled to our loyalty.

It becomes:

> We're too weak.

And:

> those who actually intend to get stronger should follow.

Later, facing Tatsumaki's hostility, they explicitly choose to remain with Fubuki and become strong enough to stand there.

That changes the moral meaning of the Blizzard Group.

Earlier:

> Fubuki gathered people to secure her position.

Now increasingly:

> **people are choosing Fubuki despite knowing the cost.**

The distinction is profound.

A faction becomes a community when membership can be refused.

Fubuki's mature leadership therefore does not require dissolving the Blizzard Group.

It requires transforming it from:

**possession → consent**

and from:

**followers as proof of Fubuki's worth → followers who possess reasons of their own.**

That is precisely what Volume 36 begins to accomplish.

---

# XXVI. Tatsumaki also changes—slightly

Tatsumaki does not suddenly approve of the Blizzard Group.

That would be implausible.

She still regards them as weak.

She still threatens them.

She still tells them that if anything happens to Fubuki, she will never forgive them.

But she does something she previously struggled to do:

> **she permits the relationship to continue.**

That is small, but for Tatsumaki it is not trivial.

Earlier logic:

> Fubuki's connections are vulnerabilities. Remove them.

Emerging logic:

> I still distrust these people, but if they insist on standing beside her, they are responsible for becoming capable enough to do so.

That is the beginning of recognizing Fubuki's relational autonomy.

Not completion.

Beginning.

---

# XXVII. Saitama's most important new statement about strength

When Tatsumaki dismisses the Blizzard Group as weak, Saitama gives one of the strongest additions yet to his philosophy of power:

> **俺も筋トレ始める迄はレベル狼にだって勝てなかったんだ**

Before he started training, even Saitama couldn't beat a Wolf-level threat.

And:

> **今弱くても そいつの限界がどこかなんて誰にもわかんないもんな**

Even if someone is weak now, nobody knows where that person's limit really is.

This is enormous for interpreting Saitama.

Saitama is not merely indifferent to power ranking.

He is **anti-essentialist about weakness**.

Tatsumaki sees the strong and weak as relatively fixed categories.

Saitama's own life makes such thinking absurd.

He was weak.

Then he became Saitama.

Therefore:

> **present weakness tells you almost nothing about someone's ultimate possibility.**

This distinguishes Saitama from both contemptuous elitism and sentimental underdog worship.

He does not say:

> weakness itself is noble.

He says:

> weakness is a condition, not an essence.

That is a much stronger position.

---

# XXVIII. 188「取引」 — Fubuki is becoming a better leader, not a morally innocent one

The final chapter prevents Fubuki's development from becoming too clean.

She is still **Fubuki**.

She understands leverage.

She understands secrets.

She understands institutional embarrassment.

She finds evidence of the Hero Association's hidden dealings involving monster remains and outside organizations.

She then approaches McCoy with a proposition.

The Association has a huge public-relations problem.

Tatsumaki has wrecked enormous amounts of property.

Security has failed.

Dangerous monster material has been stored in headquarters.

External transactions could become scandalous.

Fubuki realizes that all of these facts can be turned into a different public story.

If the monster remains are presented as active threats and Tatsumaki is credited with defeating them, then:

-   the damage becomes justified;

-   Tatsumaki's rampage becomes heroism;

-   the Association's elite security narrative is restored;

-   public confidence improves;

-   and punishment for Tatsumaki can disappear.


McCoy recognizes exactly what she is doing.

And he likes it.

This is Fubuki's political intelligence in its most mature and morally ambiguous form.

She is not becoming Saitama.

She is becoming a more capable Fubuki.

---

# XXIX. Fubuki as institutional operator

This deserves emphasis because it improves her deep-dive considerably.

Fubuki possesses something Tatsumaki largely lacks:

> **institutional intelligence.**

Tatsumaki understands force.

Saitama understands immediate moral reality.

Fubuki understands systems of people:

who has leverage;

who is embarrassed;

what information matters;

what story the public will believe;

what the Association needs;

where interests overlap;

what can be traded.

That talent was corrupting when primarily used to control a personal hierarchy.

But the talent itself is not bad.

Volume 36 shows it being redirected.

She uses manipulation to preserve people and relationships rather than merely status.

Still ethically gray.

Still recognizably Fubuki.

Much better characterization than having her simply become “nice.”

---

# XXX. The manufactured Tatsumaki story: public truth remains broken

Fubuki's deal works.

The public is shown a heroic narrative:

Tatsumaki faced dangerous Demon-level monsters around Hero Association headquarters and defeated them.

People praise the S-Class hero.

The official story is useful.

It is also false.

This creates a fascinating comparison with King.

King's false reputation largely emerges accidentally from collective misunderstanding.

Fubuki and McCoy **deliberately author a false heroic narrative** because it produces desirable institutional consequences.

This sets up a more mature question than “truth good, lie bad.”

The lie:

protects the Association;

restores public confidence;

protects Tatsumaki;

hides corruption;

erases accountability;

misrepresents reality.

All at once.

That is how institutional narratives actually become ethically difficult.

The lie is functional.

That is precisely why it is dangerous.

---

# XXXI. Tatsumaki's institutional power

Tatsumaki's subsequent interaction with Association leadership adds another layer.

She is not simply an employee.

She is too valuable.

The Association may nominally exercise authority over S-Class heroes, but Tatsumaki's actual relationship with it is closer to strategic mutual dependency.

She can cause extraordinary damage and still negotiate from strength because everyone knows how irreplaceable she is.

This gives us another form of power:

Saitama has physical power without institutional status.

King has reputational power without physical power.

Fubuki has relational/institutional leverage.

Tatsumaki has both immense physical power and enormous bargaining power derived from institutional dependence.

This makes the Association less a hierarchical command system than a coalition trying to contain and utilize anomalies.

---

# XXXII. Child Emperor discovers the rot

Volume 36 closes the institutional thread very deliberately.

Child Emperor begins investigating.

What he discovers is not merely one questionable transaction.

His reaction is essentially:

> this place is full of improperly concealed wrongdoing.

That ending matters.

The Hero Association's post-war legitimacy crisis is not an abstract future possibility anymore.

A highly intelligent hero inside the organization is beginning to see the mismatch between:

**what the Association publicly claims to be**

and

**what its internal systems actually do.**

That is one of the clearest forward-looking threads established by these volumes.

I will leave it there, because anything beyond that belongs to Volume 37.

---

# XXXIII. The two bonus chapters are thematically much more useful than expected

## Volume 35 — 「怖くないよ」

The comedic treatment of Black Sperm and Rover continues the main volume's monster-category destabilization.

The former apex threats are gradually absorbed into absurd everyday rhythms: food, pet-like behavior, hierarchy, fear, practical management.

It reinforces the main-volume argument:

> **post-conflict identity is not necessarily identical to wartime identity.**

The monster label may be historically accurate.

It is not always sufficient for determining what to do next.

## Volume 36 — 「最初の勇気」

This is the more important bonus.

Glasses encounters a younger person being bullied and intervenes.

The key point is that he does not present himself as fearlessly superior.

He tells the victim, essentially:

> I understand being afraid.
> I feel fear too.

And encourages him to tell someone what is happening.

That act of telling becomes:

**最初の勇気 — the first courage.**

This is beautifully placed after a volume involving Tatsumaki and Saitama.

The main chapters contain almost unimaginable power.

The bonus says heroism may begin with:

> admitting you're scared and telling another person.

That belongs on the same ethical axis as Mumen Rider.

Power is useful.

Courage begins earlier.

# XXXIV. Visual and formal analysis: from Jupiter to an apartment hallway

The shift in visual scale after Volume 34 is excellent.

Volume 34 gives us Io, Jupiter, cosmic portals, radiation, causality.

Volume 35 gives us:

**Room 104.**

Shoes.

Hallways.

Neighbors.

Repair bills.

Security systems.

Pet problems.

The joke is obvious, but the literary function is serious.

*One Punch Man* refuses to allow cosmic power to become the permanent register of the story.

It brings the sublime back into ordinary life.

The universe was nearly destroyed.

Saitama still needs clothes.

Buildings still belong to somebody.

People still live underneath the psychic battlefield.

That is why 「外でやれ！」 is almost a thesis statement for this entire phase.

---

# XXXV. Architecture as a motif: everyone wants containment

The Hero Association headquarters is particularly interesting visually.

These volumes are full of:

-   cells;

-   underground floors;

-   security doors;

-   fortified walls;

-   apartments;

-   laboratories;

-   observation rooms;

-   containment spaces;

-   restricted information;

-   locked bodies.


Everyone is trying to contain something.

The Association contains monsters.

Tsukuyomi wants to contain psychic bodies.

Tatsumaki wants to contain Fubuki's relationships.

Fubuki historically contained her followers within hierarchy.

Secret information is contained institutionally.

And Saitama repeatedly screws up containment.

He brings monsters home.

Breaks automated security.

Pulls Tatsumaki outside.

Crosses boundaries.

Refuses classification.

He is narratively anti-containment.

Not because he has a theory of liberation.

Because many of the walls simply look stupid to him.

# XXXVI. Psykos's future vision is visually anti-human

The rendering of Psykos's breakdown deserves mention.

The clean social world of the school flashback gives way to increasingly dense, ugly, almost organic visual abstraction.

The future ceases to look like a normal human environment.

It becomes:

swirls;

darkness;

screaming texture;

unstable bodily imagery.

The art communicates that Psykos has not simply learned an unpleasant fact.

Her perceptual world has become uninhabitable.

She no longer sees individual human beings.

She sees species-level infestation.

That visual loss of individuality parallels her ideological loss of human moral scale.

---

# XXXVII. The Saitama–Tatsumaki fight visually changes what a “battle” can mean

Their fight is not structured primarily around the question:

> Who wins?

That question is basically answered immediately.

Tatsumaki cannot meaningfully hurt him.

The visual escalation instead asks:

> How much can she express?

The terrain becomes an emotional meter.

As Tatsumaki allows herself to escalate, the world bends around her.

Then Saitama walks out unharmed.

The emotional climax is not a final punch.

It is conversation.

That is exactly appropriate.

A conventional battle resolves disagreement by demonstrating superiority.

Saitama is already superior.

Therefore superiority cannot be the resolution.

The fight has to become relational.

# XXXVIII. Major character-model updates

## Saitama

Volumes 35–36 make several major additions to the existing Saitama deep dive.

The strongest new formulation is:

> **Saitama believes both identity and relationship should remain minimally coercive: people should be allowed to become what they can become, relationships need not confer ownership, and power becomes morally problematic chiefly when it starts overriding other people's lives.**

Three new ideas are particularly important.

**“Just an acquaintance” morality:** moral concern does not require possession or intimacy.

**Weakness is not destiny:** nobody knows another person's upper limit.

**Freedom plus a harm boundary:** live as you want; don't wreck other people's lives/property.

These are among his most explicit philosophical statements in the manga.

---

## Fubuki

The previous deep dive needs a meaningful refinement.

Earlier formulation:

> Fubuki turns inferiority into hierarchy, then gradually learns that leadership need not mean possession.

Volumes 35–36 strongly validate that—but make it more sophisticated.

Her group-building instinct predates professional heroism.

She possesses genuine institutional and political intelligence.

Her members increasingly acquire the ability to choose whether to remain.

She experiments with the wrong solution—Tatsumaki-style solitude—before her followers reject it.

She uses morally gray political manipulation to protect people.

The updated formulation is:

> **Fubuki's mature problem is no longer whether she should have followers. It is whether she can build a group in which remaining is voluntary, weakness is developable, and leadership creates responsibility rather than ownership.**

That is substantially better than simply “Fubuki learns friendship.”

---

## Tatsumaki

Her profile receives the largest revision.

Previous:

> overwhelming protective power hardened into isolation.

Still correct.

But now:

> **Tatsumaki's radical self-reliance conceals an unresolved dependence on an idealized rescuer. She does not eliminate her need for recognition; she reserves it almost entirely for Blast.**

This explains much of her emotional rigidity.

Saitama matters because he supplies something she thought only Blast could supply:

a person strong enough not to be endangered by her full self.

But unlike Blast, Saitama gives her no grand doctrine.

Only ordinary recognition and a boundary against harming others.

Her tentative tolerance of Fubuki's group is consequently meaningful.

Not healing.

Movement.

---

## Psykos

Psykos becomes dramatically more interesting.

She is now:

a former member of Fubuki's psychic social world;

a weaker psychic relative to young Fubuki;

an organizer;

a researcher;

a future-seer;

a person radicalized by what she believes she sees;

a defeated enemy whose legal personhood has now been removed.

That last transition is particularly important:

> **The story takes a character who dehumanized humanity at species scale and places her inside an institution that legally dehumanizes her in return.**

That is not moral equivalence.

But it is very deliberate irony.

---

# XXXIX. The deepest thematic synthesis: classification is the new antagonist

The Monster Association arc was full of characters asking:

> What makes someone a monster?

Volumes 35–36 convert that philosophical question into social practice.

The answer matters because classifications now determine:

who can be killed;

who retains rights;

who becomes research material;

who may live inside a protected city;

who is trustworthy;

who counts as weak;

who is allowed to have companions;

who receives public credit.

The series increasingly distrusts every system that lets a label completely substitute for attention to the actual person.

That is why Saitama is so disruptive.

He keeps seeing the inconvenient individual underneath the category.

Monster? Maybe it's behaving like a dog.

Weak B-Class follower? Maybe they can become stronger.

Terrifying S-Class psychic? Maybe she's exhausted and needs someone to ask whether she's okay.

“Just an acquaintance”? Still somebody worth helping.

This gives us an excellent new overarching formulation:

> **Saitama's greatest social virtue may be that he is nearly impossible to make respect a category more than the person standing in front of him.**

---

# XL. What Volumes 35–36 do to the full-series thesis

Through Volume 34, the strongest synthesis was:

> **Power can end a fight. It cannot, by itself, tell a person what to live for.**

Volumes 35–36 add a second clause:

> **And after the fight ends, power still has to learn how to live beside other people.**

That may be the real transition into post-Monster-Association *One Punch Man*.

Garou's arc asks whether overwhelming power can create justice.

The answer is no.

The psychic-sisters material asks something more intimate:

> Can overwhelming power create healthy relationships?

Again, not by itself.

Tatsumaki can save cities but cannot automatically understand Fubuki.

Fubuki can organize people but cannot automatically make organization non-coercive.

The Hero Association can protect civilization but cannot automatically respect personhood.

Saitama's strange advantage is not merely that he is stronger.

It is that he has almost no desire to possess what his strength allows him to possess.

---

# Final synthesis

Volumes 35–36 are considerably more important than their “post-Garou transition arc” reputation might suggest.

They represent a genuine thematic shift.

The Monster Association war shattered the world's physical order. These volumes begin exposing the moral and institutional order underneath it.

The Hero Association responds to catastrophe through fortification, secrecy, commodification, and useful propaganda.

Psykos reveals that knowledge without human scale can become nihilism.

Fubuki confronts whether community can survive once control is removed.

Tatsumaki confronts whether protection can exist without isolation and ownership.

And Saitama, almost accidentally, supplies the simplest answer in the entire volume:

> You don't need to own someone to care about them.
> Being weak now does not mean being weak forever.
> Live however you want—but don't destroy other people's lives in the process.

The most concise formulation I would preserve for the authoritative corpus is:

> **Volumes 35–36 are about power learning boundaries. Fubuki must learn that belonging is meaningful only when people can choose it; Tatsumaki must learn that protection does not grant ownership; the Hero Association demonstrates what happens when classification replaces personhood; and Saitama, precisely because he cares so little about hierarchy, becomes the character best able to recognize freedom without confusing it with abandonment.**

And for Tatsumaki specifically, I would now revise the earlier character thesis to:

> **Tatsumaki is overwhelming protective power hardened into isolation, but Volume 36 reveals that her isolation is not true independence: beneath it survives the child who still expects Blast to return, recognize how hard she has tried, and tell her how to live. Saitama's importance is that he begins offering something less mythic and potentially healthier—recognition without ownership, safety without dependence, and a relationship strong enough to withstand her without trying to control her.**

That is probably the largest interpretive gain from these two volumes.
