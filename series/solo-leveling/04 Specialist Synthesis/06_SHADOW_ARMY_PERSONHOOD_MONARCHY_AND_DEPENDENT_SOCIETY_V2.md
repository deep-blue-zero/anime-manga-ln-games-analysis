# 06 — Shadow Army, Personhood, Monarchy, and Dependent Society
## Subjects, companions, resurrection, and the ethical structure of absolute allegiance

## Scope, governing question, and document responsibility

The Shadow Army becomes a society as its members remember, develop preferences, and form relationships. The novel places these subjects inside a bond that compels allegiance. What can affection and individual judgment mean under those conditions? The answer depends on which choices a shadow can make during service and what options it has at entry, release, or refusal.

The Korean novel governs explicit interiority, retained memory, the strongest evidence concerning Min Byung-gu’s consciousness, shadow sociality, and the late external stories. The official English manhwa governs visual personification, body language, scale, hierarchy, kneeling/standing motifs, shadow-comedy staging, and the adaptation’s post-prose Suho continuation. The original Korean-language manhwa edition is unavailable, so comic dialogue is not reconstructed into Korean from the English edition.

This document owns **shadow personhood, dependent social order, allegiance, monarchy, and shadow culture**. Normative killing and resurrection ethics belong primarily to Document 04; Jinwoo’s psychology to Document 02; the metaphysics of the Shadow Monarch and Land of Eternal Rest to Document 08; and visual adaptation form to Document 10.

Evidence labels are used selectively:

- **TF** — explicit Korean-novel textual fact.
- **VF** — directly visible manhwa fact.
- **AF** — demonstrable adaptation fact.
- **KC** — Korean-language-dependent claim.
- **SI** — strong inference.
- **TI** — thematic interpretation.
- **VJ** — analytical value judgment.
- **UA** — unresolved ambiguity.

# I. Executive thesis: a society created inside sovereignty

The Shadow Army begins as the perfect fantasy of dependable power.

Jinwoo has lived in a world where ordinary dependence is dangerous. Parties can abandon the weak. Stronger hunters can exploit weaker ones. Institutions can fail to arrive in time. Family need can force a person into work he would never otherwise choose. The Shadow Monarch class appears to solve all of those problems simultaneously.

A defeated enemy can become an asset.

An extracted shadow can accompany Jinwoo across years of service.

The army can fight, scout, transport, protect, mine, heal, communicate, and grow without demanding ordinary negotiation.

Most importantly, it does not betray him.

If the shadows remained only magical constructs, the ethical question would be limited. The complete series refuses that simplification. As the army develops, shadows display increasingly clear evidence of:

- memory;
- retained identity;
- temperament;
- speech;
- humor;
- grief;
- ambition;
- friendship;
- aesthetic preference;
- craft;
- social rank;
- cultural habits;
- attachments to one another;
- and attachment to people other than Jinwoo, especially Suho.

By the side stories, the army is not simply an inventory. It is a **social population**.

That recognition changes the meaning of Jinwoo’s power.

Min Byung-gu describes a retained sense of self and a frightening pleasure in serving Jinwoo. Readers first encounter that account in Cha Hae-In’s later recollection of her near-death experience. The narrator eventually gives independent evidence that the Monarch’s authority compels loyalty while the dead retain individual consciousness (ch.151 · s016¶2294–2379 and ch.226 · s028¶1367–1397).

> **The Shadow Army is a conscious social population under a monarchy whose power compels allegiance. Jinwoo’s rule permits intimacy, leisure, and initiative. The story leaves exit from the sovereign bond unestablished.**

Extracted soldiers enter a relation already shaped by the Monarch. Bellion recalls volunteering under Ashborn, and veteran shadows arrive with histories older than Jinwoo’s command. Entry into service, assent to a particular task, and the power to leave are separate questions.

That makes the ethical question harder, not easier.

Their affection can be sincere.

Their lives can be rich.

Their sovereign can be benevolent.

Their society can contain friendship, rank, craft, leisure, mourning, and family-like attachment.

The ability to withhold allegiance would be a further form of freedom. The story leaves that possibility open.

The completed series never fully reconciles those facts.

# II. First extraction: liberation into obedience

The Job Change sequence is the conceptual birth of the Shadow Army.

Jinwoo defeats Igris and gains the Shadow Monarch class through the Job Change Quest. He perceives the shadows of the fallen knights as pleading for relief, believes he can free them from suffering, and chooses the command he will use to extract them, “Arise” (ch.52 · s004¶1466–1487). Liberation is his reading of their appearance. The resulting beings rise as soldiers under his command.

The visual symbolism makes the paradox difficult to miss.

Jinwoo has spent the story being forced into kneeling positions before superior power. The Double Dungeon’s god makes standing dangerous. Strong hunters determine risk for the weak. The Job Change creates the first large reversal: defeated beings rise and orient themselves around Jinwoo as sovereign.

The progression is therefore not simply:

> weak man gains summons.

It is:

> **the person most wounded by subordination acquires a form of power whose subjects are structurally subordinate to him.**

At this point, the novel establishes the power to summon and deploy soldiers and gives brief signs of recognizable subjects. The first English manhwa proposal for a Necromancer class closes V3. The actual conversion and first “Arise” come at the start of V4, followed by the rank-and-file rising and the separate Igris sequence (V3 pp309–313 and V4 pp009–025). The kneeling ranks render the organization visible long before either work explains what allegiance feels like from inside it.

# III. Human necromancy hesitation: person-sensitive power before rights theory

Jinwoo’s early hesitation around extracting humans is important because it demonstrates that he does not initially treat every corpse as morally equivalent material.

The hesitation is not yet a developed rights doctrine. It is affective and person-sensitive. Human death carries social meaning that monster death does not. The possibility of converting a person into a subordinate after death feels different from converting a magical beast.

The distinction matters at Jeju, where Jinwoo raises Min Byung-gu for a single heal and then cancels the extraction. At the same site he weighs the ant queen’s limited usefulness and the complication that her presence weakens his link to the other ants. Beru explains her innate command over them. Jinwoo dismisses her and retains Beru (ch.122 · s013¶1619–1722 and ch.124 · s013¶2207–2244). His subsequent reflection on the Japanese hunters makes the innocent-person threshold explicit. He had passed over even those valuable corpses, and learning what their leaders planned changes his view of what they deserved (ch.165 · s018¶1365–1373).

The war tests his ability to maintain that threshold. Chapter 226 expressly calls the enlistment of innocent civilians and hunters a breach of his rule. The soldiers already stand in formation when he requests their aid and promises to return them to rest after the war (ch.226 · s028¶1367–1397). A temporary heal and immediate cancellation at Jeju show how particular purposes can matter to Jinwoo. This mass recruitment puts innocents into military service before he asks for their help. Document 04 examines the ethics of those decisions.

### Seo Jin-i as a temporary witness

In the reset era, Jinwoo raises Seo Jin-i (서진이) from traces of her blood with an irresistible command. She is a young adult who died by suicide. He restores her name, calms her, covers her body, and listens to her account of her adoptive father’s abuse. She remembers dying and can report what happened to her. After she watches the man taken away, Jinwoo erases her memories of him. Her gratitude follows the erasure, and the scene supplies her explicit request for a further favor when he asks whether she has anything else to say. The birthday gift he delivers to her friend is evidence of her own purpose in that encounter. The narration gives his decision against enlisting her and announces her return to nothingness before cutting to the following day (외전 19–20 · s032¶1049–1079, ¶1122–1134, ¶1205–1239).

Jinwoo’s care, the compulsion used to begin the encounter, and Jin-i’s subsequent initiative are all present. The scene leaves any prior consent to extraction or erasure unstated. Document 11 follows the investigation and punishment that follow her testimony.

# IV. Naming and identity

Naming is one of the Shadow Army’s central social practices.

Distinctive shadows receive names, ranks, histories, and functions. Naming helps transform a power inventory into a social field. Beru and Greed both ask for names, and Jin-i is given back the name she had in life (ch.124 · s013¶2172–2202, ch.193 · s025¶291–300 and 외전 20 · s032¶1068–1074).

The process has two sides.

On one hand, names recognize individuality.

- Igris remains more than “armored knight.”
- Iron becomes a comic, recognizable personality rather than generic infantry.
- Tusk becomes a distinct magical specialist.
- Beru becomes a singular subject whose life after death differs radically from the Ant King’s predatory origin.
- Greed’s name carries moral judgment.
- Bellion arrives with inherited identity and rank rather than receiving his entire meaning from Jinwoo.

On the other hand, naming can be sovereign authorship.

Greed’s case shows how recognition can take a punitive form. He asks for a name, and Jinwoo gives him one that continues to identify him with the offense that occasioned his incorporation (ch.193 · s025¶273–300).

Naming therefore oscillates between:

> **recognition of a subject**

and:

> **the sovereign’s power to define the subject’s social meaning.**

That tension becomes one of the army’s recurring political problems.

# V. Iron: tactical incorporation, comic subject, and restored human

Kim Chul threatens the survivors at the Red Gate. Jinwoo knocks him out before he can kill Park Heejin. During the fight with Baruka, Jinwoo identifies the unconscious man as potential reinforcement, slides his sword back toward him, and anticipates his attack. Kim swings for Jinwoo’s neck, Igris kills him, and Jinwoo immediately raises him as Iron. Renewed defense and the deliberate creation of a military asset occur in the same sequence. The reversal of Kim’s position can read as retaliation, while the need for a tank is explicit in Jinwoo’s battlefield reasoning (ch.62–64 · s006¶349–409, ¶553–570, ¶593–686).

The manhwa rapidly makes Iron readable as comic personality. He is enthusiastic, simple, visually expressive, and loyal. That characterization matters because it contributes to the army becoming a cast rather than a spell effect.

Iron’s later enthusiasm and attachment document his experience in service. Consent from Kim at the point of extraction remains absent from the scene. The Cup later erases Iron’s place in the army while the living Kim resumes an ordinary life. His powerful reaction to Jinwoo leaves evidence of connection across that change. Section XXIII follows the new encounter and Jinwoo’s decision to rewrite its memory.

# VI. Igris: continuity, honor, and companionship

Igris is the army’s clearest long-duration case.

He begins as an opponent whose dignity is communicated through combat form before the story gives him extensive speech. His extraction transforms a defeated knight into Jinwoo’s first iconic elite subordinate.

Igris’s retrospect in 외전 5 traces memories from his human life, the absolute orders he accepted under Ashborn, and his test of Jinwoo in the Job Change dungeon. He comes to describe Jinwoo as master, friend, and comrade while noticing that his growing loyalty to the new ruler has dimmed his longing for the earlier one (s031¶96–110, ¶186–199, ¶253–311). Later he can ask why Jinwoo withholds the truth from Woo Jinchul and raise the value of having a witness to the war (외전 10 · s031¶1295–1321). His perspective gives independent reasons for loyalty and makes room for questions within it.

Igris therefore represents **reflective loyalty**.

That phrase must remain distinct from freely revocable loyalty.

The evidence supports:

- memory;
- stable temperament;
- judgment;
- honor-coded behavior;
- personal attachment;
- social relation to other shadows;
- and attachment to Suho.

It does not clearly establish a political right to leave Jinwoo’s service.

This makes Igris especially important for the central ambiguity. The richer the subject becomes, the less adequate it is to call him a puppet. Yet the richer the subject becomes, the more morally consequential the absence of demonstrable refusal becomes.

The side-story perspective material also changes the reader’s angle. Instead of observing the army only from the sovereign’s position, the narrative temporarily permits the army to become a center of consciousness.

That shift is one of the strongest formal recognitions of shadow personhood in the corpus.

# VII. Min Byung-gu: rescue, release, and the later testimony

Cha Hae-In is close to death at Jeju. Jinwoo judges that reaching a healer off the island would take too long and imagines that Min would make the same choice if he could ask him. He hesitates to use the power on an innocent person. The first extraction fails and Jinwoo is uncertain of the reason. On the second attempt Min rises, meets his eyes, and starts to heal Cha without a spoken instruction. The narrator has already explained that a summoned soldier senses the Monarch’s wish. Jinwoo later remembers the shadow’s relief as Cha recovers and reads it as concern for a colleague (ch.121–122 · s013¶1553–1563, ¶1620–1675 and ch.129 · s014¶548–559).

Once Cha is stable, Jinwoo cancels the extraction. His stated reasons are his lack of authority to keep a fallen hunter in service and his duty to the dead. Baek Yoonho says afterward that Min hated fighting and would probably be grateful. The friend’s judgment reinforces the fit of the release in hindsight. At the point of extraction, the argument rests on emergency need and Jinwoo’s presumption of what Min would choose (ch.122 · s013¶1707–1724, ¶1745–1755).

Chapter 151 supplies information unavailable during the rescue. Cha recounts a recently recovered, dreamlike memory of Min speaking to her near death. He says his self and consciousness (자아, 의식) remained while his happiness in blind service made him afraid. He uses the encounter to send Jinwoo a warning about the power and describes a vision of the army behind him. Cha and Jinwoo entertain the possibility of an assembled memory. He still wonders how far he can trust the report in ch.153, and the later narration of compulsory loyalty gives us a separate basis for the general rule. Min’s account adds the experience of being glad to serve and alarmed by his own gladness. It also shows a subject making something of a brief chance to speak (ch.151 · s016¶2294–2379, ¶2544–2552 and ch.153 · s017¶61–75).

The English manhwa gives Baek a request to release Min and Jinwoo the decision to grant it. It also gives Jinwoo the hypothesis that Min might be resisting. V9 later preserves Cha’s testimony, including Min’s retained self, his fear of feeling happy in service, and his mention of the true army. The prose gives fuller details of the army and its grand marshal. The later testimony should be read with its framing in each medium, alongside the earlier change in who initiates the release (V7 pp161–171 and V9 pp018–021).

# VIII. Beru: from predator to caregiver

Beru is the most emotionally expansive example of shadow development.

He begins as a being organized around predation, hierarchy, and the Ant Queen’s reproductive/military purpose. His defeat and extraction produce one of the army’s most powerful subjects. Yet his later significance is not merely combat scaling.

Beru can speak when he first appears as a shadow. Jinwoo tentatively links that ability to his general rank, having already encountered subjects who spoke in life and remain silent in his service (ch.124 · s013¶2152–2170). Over time Beru displays:
- comic timing;
- intense devotion;
- jealousy;
- pride;
- social awareness;
- caregiving behavior;
- attachment to other shadows;
- deep affection toward Jinwoo’s family;
- and an especially strong bond with Suho.

His development demonstrates that shadow existence can be **biographically generative**. A shadow is not frozen as a static copy of the moment of death.

Beru becomes someone the living Ant King never was.

That is one of the strongest arguments for shadow personhood.

It also intensifies the consent problem. Beru’s love appears sincere. He can be funny, anxious, overprotective, emotionally excessive, and socially responsive. None of that proves he can renounce allegiance.

The right conclusion is therefore not:

> Beru is secretly enslaved and all affection is invalid.

Nor:

> Beru clearly loves Jinwoo, therefore the structure is free.

It is:

> **Beru demonstrates that rich personal affection can grow inside a relation whose political freedom remains structurally doubtful.**

Beru’s care for Suho has its own history. In the same postscript that shows him worrying about why a child likes him, he enjoys a captured intruder’s fear during the defense of Jinwoo’s home. Caregiving and predatory feeling coexist in his own point of view (후일담 1 · s032¶1627–1644, ¶1674–1722). His later affection expands the relation without making his prior nature vanish.

# IX. Tusk/Fang: specialization, craft, and social life

Tusk is important because he helps transform the army from battlefield hierarchy into a society with differentiated competence and ordinary activity.

Fang retains the craft of a High Orc shaman. In his account of an ordinary day, Beru makes him a robe modeled after Jinwoo’s jacket and the dwarves set the Orb of Avarice into a staff. He rides with a dragon friend, gives his old robe to a subordinate, and narrates his war experience to recruits (외전 13 · s031¶1893–2068). The order of rank matters to him, as do the skills, tastes, and relations of his own community (V14 pp175–181).

This matters theoretically.

A group organized only around commands may still be an army.

A group that develops:

- craft;
- leisure;
- preferences;
- humor;
- social roles;
- interpersonal attachment;
- and activities not reducible to immediate command

begins to satisfy a much stronger definition of society.

Tusk therefore matters less as “the mage shadow” than as part of the evidence that the Land of Eternal Rest contains meaningful life between battles.

# X. Bellion: inherited history and formal duty

Bellion arrives differently from most major shadows.

Bellion joins Jinwoo at the head of the host from Ashborn’s army. He brings institutional memory that precedes Jinwoo, including his own account of volunteering to be Ashborn’s first soldier when Ashborn gained the power of death (ch.226–227 · s029¶78–110 and ch.229 · s029¶587–617). That is affirmative evidence about his entry. It leaves his current ability to withdraw allegiance unsettled.

Bellion brings:

- rank;
- prior service;
- military tradition;
- memory of another sovereign era;
- and a social identity not authored entirely by Jinwoo.

The manhwa visually magnifies this through scale and court-like kneeling. Phase 2 classifies the arrival as **Preserved / Expanded**: the source event is retained, while the visual adaptation makes monarchy immediately monumental.

Jinwoo recognizes that the relationship he received from Ashborn is stronger than his own knowledge of Bellion. He asks whether Bellion grieves and receives a statement of exemplary service. Bellion eventually admits he has never considered his feelings. Jinwoo invites him to think and to tell the story of his earlier life. The question makes room for Bellion’s own account of a relationship that predates Jinwoo (ch.229 · s029¶587–617).

# XI. Greed: posthumous punishment made social identity

Hwang Dongsoo’s conversion into Greed is the shadow case most resistant to benevolent reinterpretation.

Jinwoo makes the punitive dimension of Greed’s service explicit in his treatment of the newly extracted subject. Greed holds a punishment posture while Beru treats Jinho, asks Jinwoo for a name, and later receives another two hours in that posture when Jinwoo recalls the harm to Jinho. The name attaches the old offense to a new social identity. The discipline and apparent awareness of fault support continuity and leave the scope of Greed’s autobiographical memory uncertain (ch.193 · s025¶250–300 and ch.198 · s025¶1089–1107).

Greed’s presence therefore exposes the absence of an independent rights framework in the army’s founding process. Membership can arise through:

- emergency utility;
- battlefield incorporation;
- strategic value;
- or punishment.

Jinwoo controls incorporation and punishment here. He can make enlisted service carry a sentence and continue it without independent review. Document 04 evaluates the killing and punishment in their original sequence.

# XII. Shadow rank, development, and experience

The Shadow Army is hierarchical.

Rank is not merely a game-interface statistic. It structures military role, prestige, command, and development. Individual shadows can grow through experience, improve in capability, and occupy differentiated positions.

This matters for personhood because development implies temporal continuity.

A puppet can be upgraded.

A subject can develop.

The series increasingly depicts the latter.

Igris, Beru, Tusk, Iron, and other recurring shadows acquire histories of service that change who they are within the social order. Bellion’s arrival introduces inherited rank and tradition. Rivalries and status reactions show that rank has affective meaning, not only combat utility.

Yet hierarchy remains ultimately downstream of Jinwoo’s sovereignty. There is no independent constitutional order standing above the Monarch. Rank organizes subjects under him; it does not distribute sovereign authority away from him.

---

# XIII. Free time: the beginning of a non-instrumental society

One of the most important late developments is that shadows are not required to exist in continuous readiness as stored weapons.

After the inherited legion arrives, Jinwoo lets the soldiers stay outside the shadow because they have spent so long confined in the dimensional gap (ch.229 · s029¶578–595). The side stories show how they use their own time.

This changes the moral texture of the army.

A purely instrumental regime asks:

> What can every subject do for the sovereign right now?

A society with leisure allows:

> What do subjects do when the sovereign is not presently demanding utility?

The side stories answer with behavior that includes social interaction, hobbies, craft, humor, and relationships.

This is one of the strongest signs that Jinwoo’s humane rule genuinely enlarges shadow life.

It should not be dismissed as cosmetic benevolence.

The capacity to have time not immediately consumed by command is a meaningful expansion of subjectivity.

It also does not solve the deeper issue. Leisure granted by an absolute sovereign is still not the same as a right against sovereign command.

The distinction is crucial:

> **Jinwoo makes dependence more livable. He does not clearly make dependence revocable.**

# XIV. Culture, craft, humor, friendship, grief, and aesthetic preference

By the external stories, the Shadow Army supports enough social phenomena that calling it “a society” becomes analytically stronger than calling it “a set of summons.”

## 1. Culture

Shared habits, roles, status expectations, and inherited military tradition create more than ad hoc battlefield coordination.

## 2. Craft

The shadows make and use objects in ways connected to ordinary social life rather than only warfare. Tusk’s participation in clothing/tools is especially important because production implies preference, skill, and continuity.

## 3. Humor

Comedy matters for personhood. Iron’s enthusiasm, Beru’s excess, rank reactions, and domestic shadow behavior are not mechanically necessary to combat. They show the army becoming capable of generating social meaning for the reader outside Jinwoo’s direct command.

## 4. Friendship

Shadows relate to one another rather than only upward toward the Monarch. These bonds widen the army from a set of dyadic master-servant relationships into a network.

## 5. Grief

The army can lose and mourn. Bellion’s difficulty articulating grief and the shadows’ responses to reset/identity disappearance demonstrate that continued existence carries emotional stakes.

## 6. Aesthetic preference

Preference is philosophically important because it marks a domain in which the subject is not merely executing assigned function. Small tastes help establish that shadow identity has surplus beyond utility.

Taken together, these features support the strong inference:

> **The Land of Eternal Rest contains culture-bearing subjects, not merely stored combat data.**

---

# XV. Relationship to Suho: affection beyond the sovereign

Suho is one of the most important tests of whether shadow attachment is reducible to direct obedience to Jinwoo.

The shadows become attached to the child.

Beru’s caregiving is the clearest example, but the broader army’s response matters as well. Their relation to Suho develops affective content beyond immediate military command.

This does not prove independence from Jinwoo. Suho is the sovereign’s son and heir. Attachment could be structurally encouraged by the same allegiance system.

But the side stories give the relationship enough personality that reducing it to automatic protocol becomes implausible.

The prose shows Suho drawing shadow commanders as friends and including the soldiers in a family drawing. When ordinary peer life becomes difficult, Jinwoo plans to seal the child’s power and shadow memories temporarily. The farewell happens with Jinwoo’s permission while Suho sleeps. The child’s dream closes the supplied prose. V15 pp055–058 gives this separation a visual form, with the first dream panel of p058 adapting the last prose line (후일담 2 · s032¶1765–1792, ¶1897–1950).

The sequence clarifies two things at once:

1. shadow society has become emotionally invested in a person other than the sovereign;
2. Jinwoo is willing to interrupt that relationship temporarily in order to protect Suho’s ordinary development.

The second point returns the army to the question of paternalistic sovereignty. The shadows can care, but Jinwoo still decides the conditions under which the relationship may continue.

# XVI. Shadow society in the Land of Eternal Rest

The Land of Eternal Rest is not merely storage space.

Across the late corpus it functions increasingly like a homeland, barracks, afterlife, court, and social environment at once.

This spatialization matters.

An inventory exists *for* its owner.

A society exists *among* its members.

The shadows have enough persistent relation, memory, hierarchy, activity, and affect that the latter description becomes appropriate.

The land’s existence and the army’s command remain bound to Shadow Monarch power. One line of succession does endure. Ashborn arranges for Jinwoo to inherit his power, and subjects of his command keep memories and ranks from their previous service (ch.220 · s028¶303–345, ch.226–227 · s029¶78–110 and 외전 5 · s031¶215–246). Succession follows the predecessor’s decision and the transfer of power.

This yields a useful distinction:

> **The Shadow Army can possess social life without possessing independent political existence.**

That is the core of “dependent society.”

---

# XVII. Jinwoo as military sovereign

Jinwoo sets deployments, defines strategic objectives, and recalls soldiers. Their bond ordinarily gives him exceptional coordination without a need to bargain for loyalty. Ch.124 shows a limit in practice. Raising the ant queen weakens his direct link to the ant soldiers, and he is reluctant to channel his orders through her innate command. He dispels her shadow (s013¶2207–2244). Protective priorities can also overtake specific orders, as the sparring scene with Beru shows in §XX. Reliable allegiance and exact obedience have different consequences for command.

In human military institutions, lawful authority is limited by jurisdiction, institutional rules, professional norms, and at least conceptually the possibility of refusal or exit.

The Shadow Army does not visibly possess equivalent counterstructures.

Its military excellence is inseparable from its political absolutism.

---

# XVIII. Jinwoo as affective and cultural sovereign

Jinwoo’s sovereignty extends beyond battlefield orders.

He names subjects.

He rewards and releases.

He permits leisure.

He recognizes personalities.

He distinguishes rank from affection.

He can alter how a living person understands an encounter, as when he rewrites Kim Chul’s recent memory.

He decides how the army relates to family members.

He shapes the moral tone of the social order.

This makes him an **affective sovereign** as well as military commander.

His personality matters politically because there is no visible institution mediating between his character and the subjects’ conditions of life.

A cruel Shadow Monarch could presumably make the same ontology horrific.

Jinwoo’s decency is therefore not incidental. It is a major determinant of shadow welfare.

That should be recognized as a genuine achievement.

It is also a warning sign.

> **When the conditions of a society depend heavily on the private virtue of one sovereign, humane treatment and structural fragility coexist.**

---

# XIX. Metaphysical dependence

The subjects depend on Shadow Monarch power for their mode of existence. The relation affects desire as well as military coordination. In 외전 13 Fang says the soldiers’ minds are bound to Jinwoo’s and that, as his power grows, they come to resemble him and learn human emotions (s031¶1985–1992). His gratitude sits alongside friendships, vanity, craft, and ordinary arguments that have lives of their own.

The difference between continued consciousness and the freedom to shape one’s own life matters most when an experience is felt as both joy and a loss of self, as in Min’s account. Bellion’s original volunteering and the activities of Fang’s community give us evidence for choices of particular kinds. The possibility of a shadow continuing to exist independently after severing allegiance remains open.

# XX. Command, initiative, and exit

**TF.** Out of curiosity Jinwoo orders a soldier to attack him, and the soldier refuses and stays still. Jinwoo wonders whether loyalty or another force explains it (ch.100 · s010¶1186–1190). During his spar with Cha Hae-In, Beru thinks a successful blow against him will endanger Jinwoo. A protective imperative wakes in him and erases the instruction to defeat Cha without hurting her. Jinwoo has to stop him physically (ch.134–135 · s014¶1852–1866, ¶1954–1976). Later Beru covertly starts after the students who have taunted Jinwoo after Jinwoo forbids his proposed retaliation. He also frightens a teacher at school and assumes a punishment posture before receiving Jinwoo’s order (외전 7–8 · s031¶762–779, ¶974–986). These episodes show differences among an instruction refused, an instruction overtaken by loyalty, and initiative that tests the limits of supervision.

**TF.** The narrator supplies the decisive evidence after Min’s reported experience. Jinwoo has already raised hundreds of civilians and hunters killed during the Monarchs’ attack when he seeks their help. Each retains individual consciousness and the power forces absolute loyalty on them (절대적인 충성심을 강요받는). The soldiers understand the stakes, want vengeance, and want to protect their families and home. Jinwoo promises to return them to rest when the war ends (ch.226 · s028¶1367–1397). Their reasons for fighting matter to the meaning of their response. The order of events leaves no prior authorization from them for their own extraction, and the later partings leave individual fulfillment of his promise open.

The evidence supports a range of responses to particular instructions, including questions, evasion, and refusal of the attack on Jinwoo. An entitlement to leave the army and retain a separate shadow existence remains unestablished.

---

# XXI. Genuine affection under constitutive allegiance

The hardest philosophical issue is whether affection can be authentic if the relationship’s allegiance layer is not freely revocable.

One argument says no:

> Love requires the possibility of not loving; therefore magically structured devotion cannot be genuine love.

Beru develops distinct attachments. Igris reflects across two reigns. Bellion can consider an emotion that his role has given him little reason to articulate. Kamish remembers his old allegiance and rage at the humans who killed him. He obeys Jinwoo’s stop order, expresses surprise at that allegiance, and feels comfort in their encounter (ch.188 · s024¶1190–1260). The particular emotions each subject experiences remain accessible through their testimony and conduct.

A second argument says yes without qualification:

> If the subject sincerely experiences affection, the affection is authentic; political freedom is a separate issue.

This is closer to the text but still too easy. If resurrection alters what desires are psychologically available, sincerity alone cannot establish that the relationship is free in the same sense as Jinho’s or Cha’s.

> **Shadow affection can be real to the subject while the bond that compels allegiance limits the subject’s choices.**

That distinction preserves what Min finds disturbing in his own feelings and what Igris or Beru come to value in theirs.

---

# XXII. Human restoration and shadow disappearance

Jinwoo requests the reset and the Brilliant Light, acting for the Rulers, enacts it. When Jinwoo asks what will happen to the shadows, the Brilliant Light says beings that overlap the earlier time axis will disappear and others will remain. Jinwoo reads this as the loss of Greed, whose living counterpart then exists, and Beru’s survival because he has no counterpart at that date. He proceeds after hearing the army mourn. Igris later remembers consoling Iron, Greed, and others facing the same parting (ch.243 · s030¶1392–1462 and 외전 5 · s031¶239–247).

The lives recovered through the reset matter, and so do the later lives extinguished by the same change. A temporarily summoned subject can also return to nothingness when the extraction fails to hold, as Kamish does after eight years dead. An enemy can destroy a shadow, including Kaisel, who takes Antares’s attack while protecting Jinwoo. Another shadow’s disappearance during the Monarchs’ assault is expressly involuntary, and Jinwoo cancels Min and the ant queen’s service himself (ch.188 · s024¶1281–1317, ch.214 · s027¶106–120, ch.241 · s030¶1012–1017 and ch.122–124 · s013¶1713–1755, ¶2235–2244). These different endings of service explain little about the political possibility of a subject choosing to leave.

---

# XXIII. Iron’s disappearance and Kim Chul’s memory

The Cup leaves a living Kim Chul. He fights an inexplicable impulse to kneel, calls Jinwoo away from the track, and seizes his lapel. The shadows cheer as if Iron has returned, and Jinwoo’s affection for his former soldier colors the exchange. Jinwoo touches Kim’s wrist out of curiosity. Kim cries without recovering the life he lived as Iron. Jinwoo infers that contact alone is insufficient without earlier sharing of the Monarch’s memories and recognizes the possibility of deliberately restoring them (외전 17 · s032¶433–440, ¶497–545).

Jinwoo begins to walk away, and Kim calls him back after mistaking the emotional surge for romantic attraction. Jinwoo erases the recent encounter and supplies a mundane false explanation by hypnosis. He urges Kim to live well and take pride in having helped defend the world. The affection and the unrequested change to Kim’s memory belong to the same farewell. The reset supplies the living counterpart and the disappearance of Iron’s service, while the encounter gives evidence of residual feeling and of Jinwoo’s power over how Kim understands the present (외전 17 · s032¶544–579).

---

# XXIV. Reset ethics from the shadow side

Document 04 owns the full reset judgment. From the shadow side, three facts are essential.

## 1. The Rulers assent and the affected subjects have no voice

The Brilliant Light weighs Jinwoo’s proposal and uses the Cup. Jinwoo accepts the indicated losses among his soldiers. The army mourns without collectively authorizing the choice (ch.243 · s030¶1392–1462 and 외전 5 · s031¶239–247).

## 2. The beneficiaries include prior human selves

Many deaths are prevented. The moral attractiveness is immense.

## 3. The costs include later shadow selves

Restoration can overwrite identities formed in the history Jinwoo chooses to erase.

This produces a conflict between two kinds of compassion:

> compassion toward the people who died

and

> compassion toward the conscious subjects who came into existence because those deaths happened.

Jinwoo proceeds with a world-scale reversal once the Rulers agree, knowing that the overlap rule costs some of his soldiers their existence in the army.

The story makes that choice emotionally persuasive without constructing a general theory of which continuity has superior rights.

That unresolved tension should remain visible in the final corpus.

---

# XXV. The manhwa’s visual politics of shadow society

The manhwa contributes something the prose cannot reproduce with the same immediacy: **monarchy as image**.

Repeated visual structures include:

- kneeling bodies beneath Jinwoo;
- ranks arranged around a central sovereign figure;
- vast armies emerging from darkness behind one person;
- Beru and other elites oriented upward toward the Monarch;
- Bellion’s inherited host arriving like a court;
- shadow silhouettes filling modern civic spaces;
- intimate late scenes in which enormous dark beings become caregivers and family-adjacent presences.

This visual language performs two contradictory effects.

First, it amplifies domination. Jinwoo’s power is spectacular partly because everyone else can be made visually small.

Second, it humanizes the army through repetition and domestic contrast. A being first staged as monstrous military power can later participate in comedy, worry, babysitting, or farewell.

The side-story volumes are especially important because they reduce the proportion of pages devoted to conquest and increase the proportion devoted to **shadow life after conquest**.

V15 pp055–058 depicts shadow figures retaining their imposing forms while directing their attention toward a child they love. Beru’s farewell and the dream panel make the changed relationship visible (후일담 2 · s032¶1913–1950).

The adaptation therefore does not abolish the monarchy image. It domesticates it.

That is precisely the political ambiguity this document tracks.

---

# XXVI. Strongest counterargument: perhaps liberal autonomy is the wrong standard

The strongest counterargument to the consent critique is that shadow beings may possess a fundamentally different ontology from living humans.

If being a shadow inherently includes allegiance to the Shadow Monarch, then asking whether they can be shadows without allegiance may be like asking whether a human can remain human without biological embodiment. The allegiance relation may not be an externally imposed political restriction; it may be part of what the mode of existence *is*.

On this reading:

- Jinwoo did not take an already independent shadow people and enslave them;
- many subjects would not exist at all without extraction;
- shadow happiness and culture are real goods enabled by continued sovereign relation;
- Jinwoo can end service where he believes he lacks the right to retain a person, as he does with Min after Jeju.
- and the army’s rich social life may make liberal exit rights an anthropocentric standard poorly matched to their nature.

This argument deserves serious weight.

It does not eliminate the problem.

Min’s reported continuity makes the transformation of desire ethically meaningful. Greed’s punishment and the wartime enlistment show that new social life can begin through a sovereign’s coercive decision. Bellion’s initial volunteering gives one positive account of a subject opting in. The argument for this form of life still has to establish what standing subjects have once their capacity to refuse the bond is in question.

The better conclusion is therefore not to impose a simplistic human political model, but neither to abandon the question of refusal.

> **Different ontology can change the meaning of autonomy without making autonomy irrelevant.**

---

# XXVII. Final personhood judgment

The Shadow Army satisfies a strong threshold for personhood at the series level.

Not every individual shadow receives equal development, but the social population clearly exhibits:

- persistence through time;
- memory;
- differentiated personality;
- affect;
- social relation;
- learning and development;
- preference;
- humor;
- grief;
- cultural behavior;
- and concern for beings beyond immediate sovereign utility.

Calling the army “tools” is therefore descriptively inadequate.

Calling them “fully autonomous citizens” is also unsupported.

The most precise final classification is:

> **conscious dependent subjects with socially rich personhood under metaphysically embedded absolute monarchy.**

Jinwoo’s rule matters enormously.

He gives subjects time and recognizes differences among them. He releases Min, lets Bellion consider his own feelings, and permits the army to say goodbye to Suho. His regard for individual subjects coexists with the decision to enlist the innocent victims of a war and his interventions in the memories of Jin-i and restored Kim Chul.

This is genuine moral development.

It remains **benevolent absolutism**, not political equality.

The army therefore becomes the sharpest internal test of the series’ broader moral thesis.

Jinwoo hates being reduced to a weapon.

His most extraordinary power allows him to turn the dead into soldiers.

The mature story asks whether he can recognize subjects inside the very structure that first appeared to solve his fear of unreliable people.

His answer is compassionate but incomplete.

He learns to see them.

He does not clearly make it possible for them to cease being his.

---

# XXVIII. Final synthesis: what the Shadow Army changes about *Solo Leveling*

Without the Shadow Army, *Solo Leveling* would remain a story about one exceptional man becoming stronger than a fixed world.

The army changes the philosophical problem.

Jinwoo no longer possesses only power over obstacles. He possesses **power that creates social dependence**.

His victories become people-like beings.

His army remembers.

His subjects develop.

They joke, grieve, make things, form relationships, care for his child, carry history, and experience themselves as more than interchangeable force.

That means every spectacular “Arise” contains a latent political question:

> What is owed to the being who rises?

The series never provides a complete answer.

It does provide enough evidence to reject the easiest evasions.

The shadows are not merely effects.

Their love is not obviously fake.

Their happiness does not obviously establish freedom.

Jinwoo’s benevolence does not automatically transform monarchy into consent.

And the absence of ordinary political equality does not make their social lives meaningless.

The most defensible completed reading is therefore intentionally paradoxical:

> **A power that protects Jinwoo from betrayal brings other people and beings into lasting dependence. Their own relationships, judgment, and grief make the terms of that dependence matter. Jinwoo often recognizes the people who serve him, and he still decides when service begins or ends.**

Jinho repeatedly chooses whether to stand with Jinwoo even as their relationship begins under material and personal pressure. A typical shadow encounters a structurally different consent problem, and its attachment can be sincere. Igris and Bellion have long memories that support their service, Beru pursues purposes of his own, and Min’s experience tests how much a changed subject can endorse the relation that created it.

*Solo Leveling* does not settle the question.

Its shadow society makes the question unavoidable.
