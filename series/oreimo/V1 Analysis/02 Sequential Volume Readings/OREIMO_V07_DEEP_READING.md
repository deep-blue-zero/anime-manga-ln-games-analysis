Volume 7 is the clearest structural turning point so far: a **counterfeit Kirino romance** forces genuine emotional disclosures, then gives way to the series’ first actual romantic relationship.

# *Oreimo* Volume 7 Deep Reading

## The counterfeit couple and the first real romance

Volume 7 begins by apparently confirming the franchise’s most provocative possibility.

Kyousuke waits for his girlfriend. Kirino arrives, links arms with him, calls him **京介**, receives his praise, and walks beside him like an inexperienced but affectionate lover. The narration describes her beauty and Kyousuke’s pride in being seen with her before revealing that this is only a performance.

The revelation does not render the opening meaningless. It establishes the volume’s governing mechanism:

> **Characters speak their most emotionally truthful words while ostensibly playing false roles.**

Kirino’s fake-romance request forces the siblings to:

-   behave as an exclusive couple;

-   describe what they value in one another;

-   confront how others perceive their intimacy;

-   simulate romantic loss;

-   and discover that neither can remain emotionally neutral when the other appears to choose someone else.


Yet Volume 7 does not conclude that the siblings are already consciously in love. Instead, it gives that emotional pressure a socially viable alternative: Kuroneko directly confesses, Kyousuke accepts, and they become an actual couple.

The volume’s central thesis is therefore:

> **Kyousuke and Kirino possess an attachment whose exclusivity increasingly resembles romance, but they remain unable—or unwilling—to identify it as such. Kuroneko becomes the first person to transform similarly intense recognition into an explicit, reciprocal romantic choice.**

---

# 1\. Narrative structure

Volume 7 has five major movements.

## 1\. Kirino’s counterfeit boyfriend

A powerful fashion executive, Fujima Misaki, wants to recruit Kirino as an exclusive model and take her to Europe.

Kirino wants to refuse without jeopardizing her existing modeling relationships. Under pressure, she claims that she has a boyfriend and values her time with him too much to leave.

Because Kyousuke is the only boy whom she can safely recruit, she asks him to play the role.

## 2\. The fake date

Misaki is supposedly monitoring the couple to determine whether the relationship is real.

Kyousuke and Kirino therefore:

-   link arms;

-   use first names;

-   see a film;

-   visit a sweets shop;

-   answer questions about what they like in one another;

-   and take romantic purikura photographs.


During the date, they encounter:

-   Manami;

-   Kanako and Bridget;

-   and finally Kuroneko.


Each witness interprets the performance differently, forcing the siblings to experience how their intimacy looks from outside.

## 3\. The broader romantic inquiry

Ayase interrogates Kyousuke about Kirino’s possible boyfriend.

Meanwhile, Kyousuke and Kōhei Akagi investigate Sena’s false claim that she has begun dating someone. Sena eventually admits that she lied to provoke a reaction from her brother.

This sibling parallel becomes a key to Kirino’s later behavior.

## 4\. Comiket and shared authorship

Kyousuke, Kirino, Kuroneko, and Saori collaborate on a *Maschera* dōjinshi under Kuroneko’s circle name, **神聖黒猫騎士団**.

The work sells out.

At Comiket they meet Mikagami Kōki, an attractive, successful young model and designer who shares Kirino’s professional and otaku worlds.

Kirino then announces that Mikagami is her real boyfriend.

## 5\. The rupture and first real confession

Kyousuke cannot accept Kirino’s supposed relationship. He finally tells her and Mikagami that no man can care for Kirino more than he does.

Kirino reveals that the romance was another performance. Mikagami explains that she wanted Kyousuke to notice something, but Kirino cannot articulate what.

After the crisis is repaired, Kuroneko meets Kyousuke behind the school and directly says:

> **「私と付き合ってください」**
> “Please go out with me.”

Within days, they become lovers.

---

# 2\. The fake boyfriend request is plausible precisely because Kyousuke is family

Kirino’s practical problem is credible.

She needs a boy who:

-   will not exploit the situation;

-   can keep her secrets;

-   understands both her public and private identities;

-   knows why she does not want to leave Japan again;

-   and will reliably take her side against a powerful adult.


No actual suitor possesses all of these qualifications.

Kyousuke does.

The fake romance therefore depends on a paradox:

> The person safest to impersonate Kirino’s boyfriend is the person socially prohibited from becoming one.

His family status lets him enter a space that would otherwise require romantic intimacy.

This continues the pattern established in America. Kirino contacted Kyousuke because he was not a friend. Here she chooses him because he is not a legitimate romantic candidate.

The socially excluded category becomes the source of privileged access.

---

# 3\. Kirino’s first wording is unnecessarily ambiguous

Kirino does not initially say:

> Pretend to be my boyfriend.

She says:

> **「あんた、あたしの彼氏になってよ」**
> “Become my boyfriend.”

She then looks upward nervously and asks:

> **「あの……だ、ダメ？」**
> “Um…is that no good?”

Kyousuke reacts with fear and asks whether she is in love with him.

Kirino’s face changes from red to pale, then to furious red. She insists that she does not like him and says he should have waited for the explanation.

At the literal plot level, this is comic miscommunication.

Psychologically, the wording creates a test.

Kirino receives an answer to an unasked question:

> How would Kyousuke react if I really asked him to become my boyfriend?

His initial response is recoil and disbelief.

Her later accusation—

> **「あのときは気持ち悪いみたいな顔したくせに！」**
> “You looked disgusted back then!”

—shows that she remembers the reaction as emotionally consequential.

The text does not prove that the original request was a disguised sincere confession. The practical reason is real.

But Kirino’s phrasing allows a practical request and a private experiment to coexist.

---

# 4\. Playing a boyfriend allows Kyousuke to say “I love Kirino”

When Misaki offers money for Kyousuke to leave Kirino, he declares:

> **「なぜなら俺は、桐乃を愛しているから！」**
> “Because I love Kirino!”

The statement is delivered as part of the performance.

Its emotional content is nevertheless not entirely false.

Kyousuke has already demonstrated that he:

-   crossed the world for Kirino;

-   cannot tolerate her absence;

-   considers her precious family;

-   and wants her happiness within his reach.


The false role permits him to translate this attachment into a form intelligible to an outsider.

This repeats a pattern from Volume 2, when his fake incest confession allowed him to say that he loved his sister.

The difference is one of escalation.

In Volume 2:

-   the incest performance was outrageous farce;

-   its purpose was to redirect Ayase’s suspicion;

-   and Kyousuke immediately treated it as absurd.


In Volume 7:

-   the romantic role lasts an entire date;

-   requires sustained physical and emotional behavior;

-   and begins producing real jealousy among the surrounding heroines.


The performance is becoming more structurally serious even while remaining explicitly false.

---

# 5\. Physical intimacy remains awkward rather than natural

Kyousuke embraces Kirino, places an arm around her shoulders, and pulls her close in order to convince Misaki.

Kirino stiffens, blushes, and complains that his touch is sexual.

Kyousuke insists that minor contact should be acceptable between siblings and claims to possess no sexual interest in her body.

This interaction supports several simultaneous conclusions.

### The siblings are not accustomed to affectionate touch

Their relationship has developed mainly through:

-   conversation;

-   shared media;

-   intervention;

-   arguments;

-   and symbolic objects.


Physical tenderness remains unfamiliar.

### The romantic role makes ordinary touch newly charged

An arm around a sister may be familial.

An arm around someone presented as a girlfriend acquires another meaning, even when the bodies are the same.

The discomfort therefore does not necessarily reveal preexisting desire. It reveals the power of the role to reorganize perception.

### The franchise eroticizes what the characters deny

The narration and illustrations invite attention to:

-   bodily proximity;

-   embarrassment;

-   Kirino’s physical maturity;

-   and Kyousuke’s awareness of contact.


The novels continue insisting on a distinction between:

-   fictional or genre-coded eroticism;

-   and the characters’ conscious intentions.


But Volume 7 deliberately narrows the distance between the two.

---

# 6\. The fake date uncovers genuine compatibility

The date succeeds whenever Kyousuke stops trying to imitate generic romance and attends to the actual Kirino.

He initially draws upon eroge conventions:

-   stock dialogue;

-   standard date locations;

-   romantic-film logic;

-   and scripted affectionate behavior.


Kirino immediately recognizes and rejects the borrowed lines.

She observes that game romance is an easy mode compared with dealing with a real person.

This is a characteristically self-aware statement from a series deeply structured by heroine-route conventions.

## Kyousuke chooses the children’s anime

Kirino initially selects a romance film because that is what a dating girl is “supposed” to choose.

Kyousuke remembers that she genuinely wants to see the children’s anime *Little Sisters* and buys tickets for it instead.

She objects briefly, then becomes ecstatic.

After the film, Kirino forgets:

-   the surveillance;

-   the fake-couple role;

-   and her fashionable public restraint.


She launches into detailed fan commentary.

Kyousuke finds her enthusiasm excessive, but he is pleased because he selected something that made her happy.

The scene repeats the basic structure of their relationship:

> Kirino performs the socially expected desire; Kyousuke recognizes the concealed one.

This is why his recognition matters uniquely. He can often identify what she wants beneath the version she believes a situation requires.

---

# 7\. “What do you like about each other?”

Bridget asks the counterfeit couple why they fell in love.

Unlike Misaki, she is not interrogating them strategically. Her innocence gives the question an emotional force neither sibling can easily evade.

Kirino says she likes Kyousuke because he is:

-   **優しい** — kind;

-   and **結構頼りになる** — fairly dependable.


The qualification **結構** and her hesitant delivery preserve embarrassment, but the content is sincere.

These are the qualities around which their reconstructed relationship has been built.

Kyousuke says Kirino is:

-   a hard worker;

-   attractive;

-   and someone whose effort he recognizes.


His praise draws directly on Volumes 3 and 4, in which he learned that her achievements were not effortless.

The two are ostensibly inventing a romance.

Yet neither chooses invented qualities.

Their counterfeit couple is constructed from true judgments.

---

# 8\. Manami sees the siblings crossing a boundary

Manami encounters Kyousuke and Kirino:

-   arm in arm;

-   calling each other by given names;

-   and appearing indistinguishable from a romantic couple.


Kyousuke is immediately distressed.

Kirino pulls him away before he can explain and later takes pleasure in imagining how they looked.

Her reaction is significant.

She is not merely committed to deceiving Misaki. She enjoys that Manami has seen the image.

Possible motives include:

-   hostility toward Manami;

-   revenge for Manami’s domestic intimacy with Kyousuke;

-   satisfaction in claiming public priority;

-   desire to unsettle the ordinary future Manami represents;

-   or romantic jealousy.


Volume 7 does not conclusively choose among these.

What is clear is that Kirino treats the fake-couple image as a weapon against Manami.

## Manami’s perspective remains largely withheld

The narration does not grant Manami an explicit emotional monologue.

She later passes the information to Ayase, showing that she does not dismiss what she saw.

Her restraint should not be mistaken for lack of perception.

Manami has repeatedly understood the siblings more clearly than they understand themselves. Seeing them enact romance makes visible something she may already suspect:

> Their relationship is no longer contained comfortably within ordinary sibling distance.

---

# 9\. The purikura makes the counterfeit route materially real

Kyousuke and Kirino take couple-themed purikura photographs.

The images contain:

-   heart motifs;

-   romantic captions;

-   exaggerated visual intimacy;

-   and permanent evidence of the role.


They divide the sheet, each taking one half.

Later, Kirino throws hers at Kyousuke during an argument. Rather than discarding it, he retains it.

The purikura performs a function similar to Kirino’s collection:

> It turns an emotionally ambiguous experience into an object that can be preserved after the stated purpose has ended.

The date was fake.

The memory is not.

The illustration of the cheerful couple-style photograph intensifies this contradiction. It packages the siblings as a romantic pair even while the prose insists that the image was produced strategically.

The franchise can therefore market the route without yet requiring the characters to choose it.

---

# 10\. Kuroneko becomes the injured third party

Kuroneko sees Kyousuke and Kirino leaving the purikura booth together.

Kirino:

-   calls him **京介**;

-   holds his arm;

-   refuses to clarify immediately;

-   and permits Kuroneko to believe that they may be dating.


Kuroneko’s shock demonstrates that the cheek kiss in Volume 5 was not a disposable joke. She has genuine emotional investment.

The scene also shows that Kirino understands Kuroneko’s feelings—or at least suspects them strongly enough to provoke her.

## Kirino’s behavior is cruel

Even if the date has a practical purpose, Kirino does not need to inflict the image on Kuroneko.

She uses ambiguity to:

-   assert priority over Kyousuke;

-   punish Kuroneko;

-   test her reaction;

-   and perhaps retaliate against the closeness developed during Kirino’s absence.


The volume does not absolve this as cute jealousy.

Later events show that the damage is serious enough to threaten the group.

Kirino’s vulnerability explains the aggression. It does not make the aggression harmless.

---

# 11\. “A little sister and a girlfriend are not that different”

After the exhausting date, Kyousuke observes:

> **「妹と彼女ってたいして変わんねえな」**
> A little sister and a girlfriend are not that different.

His reasoning is practical:

-   both are difficult to understand;

-   both demand attention;

-   both become angry when misunderstood;

-   both require him to infer hidden desires;

-   and both are impossible to ignore.


Kirino reacts with intense disgust and anger.

Her response reveals that the distinction matters greatly to her.

From Kyousuke’s perspective, the statement reduces relationships to the labor they demand from him.

From Kirino’s perspective, it may erase:

-   exclusivity;

-   erotic recognition;

-   chosen partnership;

-   and the special status implied by becoming someone’s girlfriend.


The line exposes one limitation in Kyousuke’s relational worldview.

He understands intimacy primarily through:

-   helping;

-   interpreting;

-   protecting;

-   and being needed.


He has less developed language for differentiating romantic desire from other kinds of care.

This limitation will matter when Kuroneko confesses.

---

# 12\. “Next time I’ll ask my real boyfriend”

When Kyousuke says he never wants to impersonate Kirino’s boyfriend again, she answers:

> **「もういい。次からは、本当の彼氏に頼むから」**
> “Fine. Next time I’ll ask my real boyfriend.”

The statement is superficially retaliatory.

The Akagi sibling episode later provides an explicit interpretive model.

Sena admits that her own boyfriend claim was:

> **本心とぜんぜん違う台詞**
> words completely different from what she truly felt.

She said them because:

-   she was angry;

-   her brother had hurt her;

-   and she wanted to provoke evidence that he cared.


Kirino’s sentence strongly resembles this structure.

She has just been told:

-   the role was intolerable;

-   Kyousuke wants no repetition;

-   and little-sister intimacy is functionally equivalent to girlfriend intimacy anyway.


Her “real boyfriend” threat becomes a way of asking:

> Would you care if I gave this role to someone else?

Kyousuke does care, but he cannot yet say so.

---

# 13\. Ayase’s interrogation reveals hostile trust

Ayase learns of the purikura from Manami and summons Kyousuke.

Her interrogation includes comic violence and suspicion that the siblings may be in an incestuous relationship.

But she also reveals that, before knowing him personally, she thought he seemed like a kind older brother and hoped to get along with him.

This revises the apparent inevitability of their antagonism.

Ayase’s current hostility developed through:

-   fear for Kirino;

-   Kyousuke’s repeated deceptions;

-   embarrassment;

-   moral panic;

-   and increasing personal attachment.


## She asks Kyousuke to investigate Kirino

Ayase knows that Kirino receives considerable male attention:

-   from models;

-   designers;

-   older industry figures;

-   and boys outside Ayase’s immediate awareness.


Yet she asks Kyousuke to investigate the part of Kirino’s life beyond her surveillance.

This is a major admission.

Ayase recognizes that Kyousuke has access to a Kirino she cannot reach.

Her idealized friendship once demanded total knowledge. Now she acknowledges an irreducible family interior.

Kyousuke’s privileged position is becoming visible to the surrounding cast.

---

# 14\. The Akagi siblings provide the explanatory mirror

Sena claims she has a boyfriend, sending Kōhei into an intrusive investigation.

The claim is false.

She made it because her brother had angered and embarrassed her, and she wanted to provoke his reaction.

This episode is broad comedy, but structurally precise.

It demonstrates how a younger sister may use supposed romantic independence to test an older brother’s attachment.

## Kōhei admits possessiveness

Kōhei knows that Sena will eventually:

-   date;

-   leave the family home;

-   and build a life beyond him.


He also knows that a good brother should wish for her happiness.

Yet he admits that the thought hurts and that his reaction is not purely selfless.

This gives Kyousuke a socially recognizable framework for his own feelings.

A brother may feel:

-   protective;

-   displaced;

-   jealous;

-   worried;

-   and irrationally hostile toward a sister’s partner


without consciously desiring the sister romantically.

The father later provides the adult version of the same reaction.

## But Kyousuke’s intensity exceeds the ordinary template

The parallels legitimize some of his response.

They do not explain everything.

Kyousuke has already:

-   crossed the world for Kirino;

-   imagined her fictional substitute;

-   become distressed by her social independence;

-   and participated in a sustained romantic performance with her.


Volume 7 uses ordinary family possessiveness as a foundation while building an increasingly exceptional form on top of it.

---

# 15\. Kyousuke recognizes that he wants the present to continue

The Akagi conversation leads Kyousuke to consider what happens when a younger sister forms a relationship outside the family.

He realizes that he loves the everyday life created during the past year:

-   Kirino’s noise through the wall;

-   arguments;

-   consultations;

-   otaku friends;

-   shared outings;

-   and his role in her life.


He does not want this structure changed by another man.

This is not simply fear that Kirino will be mistreated.

It is fear of replacement.

The insight refines the “need to be needed” hypothesis.

Kyousuke’s concern is increasingly:

> I do not want the relationship that gives my life meaning to become secondary to someone else.

That desire is psychologically understandable and ethically possessive.

---

# 16\. The dōjin project creates a shared world

Kyousuke organizes the group around a collaborative *Maschera* dōjinshi.

Their roles reflect their established strengths:

-   Kirino writes prose.

-   Kuroneko creates manga and visual design.

-   Saori provides illustrations and production knowledge.

-   Kyousuke contributes cosplay photographs and coordination.


The project is important because no single person owns it entirely.

Unlike Kirino’s cellphone novel or Kuroneko’s game proposal, it is a collectively authored object.

## Kyousuke becomes an actual participant

Kyousuke can no longer plausibly describe himself as someone merely dragged to otaku events.

He:

-   proposes the project;

-   coordinates work;

-   wears costumes;

-   contributes content;

-   sells at Comiket;

-   and emotionally invests in the result.


He recognizes that he has crossed to **こちら側**—this side.

His normal life now includes active otaku creation.

This confirms that Kirino’s world has become his world, even though their tastes remain different.

## Their first sale

The group’s reaction to the first customer is one of the volume’s emotional high points.

The purchase confirms that:

-   their combined labor reached someone;

-   an object created privately has entered public circulation;

-   and friendship produced something that did not previously exist.


Kuroneko, who had experienced rejection and unsold work, receives particular validation.

The fifty copies eventually sell out.

The success is modest, credible, and more emotionally satisfying than Kirino’s spectacular cellphone-novel success because all four have shared ownership of the outcome.

---

# 17\. Collaboration temporarily balances the group

The dōjin project provides a model of relationship different from the competitive heroine system.

Each member possesses a distinct function.

Nobody must become another person’s substitute.

The group succeeds because:

-   Kirino’s energy does not replace Kuroneko’s craft;

-   Kuroneko’s aesthetic control does not erase Kirino’s popular instinct;

-   Saori’s infrastructure does not make the others passive;

-   and Kyousuke’s role is not limited to rescuing someone in crisis.


This is perhaps the healthiest social structure *Oreimo* has produced so far.

Kyousuke is useful, but not indispensable in the old heroic sense.

His usefulness is collaborative.

That healthy balance makes Kirino’s later fake-boyfriend announcement particularly destructive. It introduces exclusivity into a system whose strength came from differentiated participation.

---

# 18\. The white-dress Kuroneko

Kirino chooses a white summer dress for Kuroneko.

When Kuroneko wears it, Kyousuke is visibly struck by her beauty and calls her a **白猫**—white cat—in contrast with her normal black gothic appearance.

The change does not replace Kuroneko’s identity. It reveals another possible version.

This continues Volume 6’s interest in performed and alternative selves.

But the scene is also unambiguously romantic.

Kyousuke:

-   looks at Kuroneko as a girl;

-   expresses attraction;

-   becomes embarrassed;

-   and experiences her chosen presentation as intimate.


The illustration foregrounds Kuroneko’s softness and vulnerability rather than gothic theatricality.

## Kirino’s position is contradictory

Kirino selected the outfit, effectively helping produce Kuroneko’s romantic appeal.

Yet she becomes unsettled by Kyousuke’s reaction and by evidence that the two have developed private familiarity:

-   real names;

-   school life;

-   game-club knowledge;

-   and time together during her absence.


Kirino wants her worlds integrated, but integration means surrendering exclusive control over how the people inside them relate.

That remains difficult for her.

---

# 19\. Mikagami is a deliberately constructed “male Kirino”

Mikagami Kōki is almost implausibly qualified.

He is:

-   young;

-   attractive;

-   professionally successful;

-   fashionable;

-   wealthy;

-   creatively accomplished;

-   familiar with Kirino’s industry;

-   and deeply knowledgeable about otaku media.


He also cross-cosplays and moves freely between public fashion prestige and private subcultural enthusiasm.

Structurally, he is a male analogue to Kirino.

He possesses the combination Kyousuke lacks:

-   professional parity;

-   aesthetic sophistication;

-   shared ambition;

-   shared fandom;

-   and social compatibility.


This makes him a uniquely threatening rival.

## He can recognize Kirino without requiring translation

Kirino has revealed her otaku identity to Mikagami.

That is a major step.

Until now, Kyousuke’s privileged status partly depended on being one of the few people who knew both:

-   the public Kirino;

-   and the hidden fan.


Mikagami appears capable of accepting both without scandal.

He therefore tests whether Kyousuke’s position depends only on secret knowledge.

The answer is no.

Mikagami may share Kirino’s worlds, but he does not share their history.

---

# 20\. Kirino’s fake real boyfriend

At the post-Comiket gathering, Kirino announces that Mikagami is her boyfriend and claims that they have kissed.

The performance is more harmful than the earlier fake date.

The first deception was directed at:

-   Misaki;

-   a business pressure;

-   and strangers.


The second is directed at:

-   Kyousuke;

-   Kuroneko;

-   Saori;

-   and the community Kirino values.


Its purpose is emotional rather than practical.

## What Kirino may be testing

The text leaves Kirino’s final sentence incomplete, so her precise intention should remain partly unresolved.

The evidence strongly suggests several overlapping motives:

-   she wants Kyousuke to display possessiveness openly;

-   she wants him to recognize that she is not interchangeable with his other girls;

-   she is retaliating for his initial recoil from the boyfriend request;

-   she is reacting to his closeness with Kuroneko and Manami;

-   she wants evidence that he will choose her emotionally;

-   and she may be trying to understand her own feelings through his reaction.


These are interpretations, not a completed confession.

What is directly supported is Mikagami’s conclusion:

> **「桐乃さんは、お兄さんに、気付いて欲しかった」**
> Kirino wanted her brother to notice something.

---

# 21\. Kirino weaponizes romantic hierarchy

Kirino presents Mikagami as superior to Kyousuke in almost every socially legible category:

-   appearance;

-   money;

-   talent;

-   professional status;

-   shared interests;

-   attentiveness;

-   and romantic desirability.


Her praise is also an attack.

She effectively asks:

> What claim could you possibly have against someone objectively better suited to me?

This targets Kyousuke’s deepest insecurity.

He has always considered himself:

-   ordinary;

-   mediocre;

-   less successful;

-   and incapable of matching Kirino’s exceptionalism.


Mikagami embodies the man Kyousuke imagines Kirino should choose if relationships follow rational compatibility.

Kyousuke must therefore decide whether his attachment can be defended without claiming superiority in ordinary terms.

---

# 22\. Kuroneko refuses to remain inside the performance

Kuroneko responds to the supposed romance with cold anger and leaves.

She calls Kirino a foolish child.

Her departure is not simply jealousy.

Kuroneko understands that Kirino is manipulating the room.

She refuses to:

-   compete inside a fabricated scenario;

-   reward Kirino’s cruelty;

-   or pretend that the lie has not damaged their friendship.


This preserves her dignity.

She has already stated that she is not a substitute for Kirino. Volume 7 shows the inverse:

> She will not permit Kirino to reduce her to a disposable rival whose pain exists only to clarify the siblings’ feelings.

The later reconciliation does not erase the injury. It demonstrates that their friendship can survive serious conflict without pretending the conflict was harmless.

---

# 23\. Saori confronts the collapse of her community

Kirino’s announcement nearly realizes Saori’s central fear.

The group she created begins to split:

-   Kuroneko leaves.

-   Kirino becomes defensive.

-   Kyousuke is immobilized.

-   the collaborative success of Comiket is immediately overshadowed by romantic hierarchy.


Saori cannot repair this through her usual comic mediation.

She reveals her uncostumed face to Kyousuke and asks him to help.

This is a significant development after Volume 6.

Saori no longer tries to preserve the group through her performance alone.

She asks another member to share responsibility.

Kyousuke had once depended on Saori to create social opportunities for Kirino. Now Saori depends on Kyousuke to protect the community they all created.

---

# 24\. The father becomes Kyousuke’s mirror

Kirino brings Mikagami home.

Their mother behaves with relative openness. Their father hides because he cannot emotionally tolerate the possibility that his daughter has a boyfriend.

Kyousuke initially recognizes the father’s behavior as irrational.

He argues that:

-   Kirino is old enough to make choices;

-   a parent cannot simply reject every possible partner;

-   and she must eventually bear responsibility for her own life.


Then he recognizes that he feels essentially the same way.

## Familial possessiveness is treated seriously

The father’s reaction supplies a socially conventional explanation for Kyousuke’s distress.

A father or older brother may feel that a daughter or sister is being “taken away.”

This vocabulary is culturally familiar but ethically revealing.

It treats the young woman as moving between male domains:

-   father;

-   brother;

-   boyfriend;

-   husband.


The novel uses this pattern sympathetically but does not fully challenge its possessive structure.

Kirino is nominally free to choose, yet the emotional climax centers on which man can claim to care for her most.

---

# 25\. Kyousuke finally tells Kirino the selfish truth

Kyousuke initially attempts to behave rationally.

He knows:

-   Kirino can choose her own partner;

-   opposing the relationship is hypocritical;

-   and he would reject equivalent interference in his own romantic life.


He apologizes for his irrationality.

Then he states it anyway.

He tells Kirino that he does not want her dating another man because it feels as though someone is taking his sister.

To Mikagami, he says:

> **「おまえには桐乃はやらん」**
> “I won’t give Kirino to you.”

And:

> **「おまえより俺の方が桐乃を大切にする！」**
> “I’ll treasure Kirino more than you will!”

The language is possessive, competitive, and emotionally exclusive.

## He admits jealousy

Kyousuke no longer hides behind safety arguments.

He admits that:

-   Kirino is unbearably cute to him;

-   he worries about her;

-   he resents the idea of another man occupying his place;

-   and he cannot calmly surrender the present relationship.


He still says he hates her.

The contradiction is no longer rhetorical camouflage alone. It has become his most honest formulation:

> I dislike much of who you are, and I cannot bear to lose you.

---

# 26\. Is Kyousuke’s declaration romantic?

The answer after Volume 7 remains: **structurally, increasingly yes; consciously, not yet clearly**.

## Familial evidence

Kyousuke frames his feeling through:

-   the father comparison;

-   Kōhei’s sibling possessiveness;

-   lifelong protection;

-   and his identity as Kirino’s brother.


He does not ask Kirino to date him.

He does not identify sexual desire.

He describes the feared loss as another man taking away his sister.

## Romantic structure

At the same time, his declaration contains elements usually associated with romantic exclusivity:

-   no other man should have her;

-   he will care for her most;

-   another partner threatens his primary position;

-   and he wants his relationship to remain uniquely privileged.


The volume deliberately refuses to resolve the overlap.

It shows how extreme sibling intimacy can become structurally similar to romance before the participants consciously cross the category boundary.

---

# 27\. Kirino’s rage reveals the double standard

Kirino responds:

> **「自分は地味子とか、あの黒いのとかといちゃついてるくせに！」**
> “You flirt with the plain girl and that black one yourself!”

This is the clearest expression of her jealousy so far.

She objects that Kyousuke claims freedom to form intimate relationships while denying the same freedom to her.

Her grievance is ethically legitimate.

Kyousuke wants:

-   Manami’s constant availability;

-   Kuroneko’s romantic interest;

-   and Kirino’s continued emotional priority.


That arrangement is unfair.

## “Why now?”

Kirino also says:

> **「なんでいまさら！」**
> “Why only now?”

And:

> **「あのときは気持ち悪いみたいな顔したくせに！」**

These lines suggest that she previously offered or exposed something Kyousuke failed to recognize.

The strongest immediate referent is his recoil when she first said “become my boyfriend.”

But the emotion may also gather earlier missed invitations:

-   her gratitude;

-   the album;

-   the proxy eroge;

-   the purikura;

-   and repeated attempts to make him articulate how much she matters.


The exact reference remains unresolved because Kirino cannot finish her explanation.

---

# 28\. “It is already too late”

Kirino says:

> **「もう遅い」**
> “It’s too late.”

The line is crucial but ambiguous.

Possible meanings include:

-   too late for Kyousuke to object after rejecting the fake-boyfriend possibility;

-   too late to claim exclusive sibling priority after becoming close to Kuroneko;

-   too late to undo the hurt caused by misunderstanding her;

-   or too late for the siblings to return to uncomplicated categories.


The volume does not yet establish that she means:

> It is too late for us to become lovers.

That would overstate the available evidence.

What the line clearly establishes is temporal grievance.

Kirino believes Kyousuke has recognized something only after another person appears to take her away.

---

# 29\. Kyousuke stops Kirino from finishing

Mikagami tells Kirino to explain what she wanted Kyousuke to notice.

She begins:

> **「あんたが……」**
> “You…”

But she cannot continue.

Kyousuke tells her not to force herself and pats her head as her older brother.

The gesture is tender.

It is also evasive.

## Protection prevents articulation

Kyousuke sees Kirino in distress and responds according to the lifelong habit established in Volume 6:

-   stop the pain;

-   protect her;

-   restore the sibling role.


But this interrupts the possibility that she might define the relationship differently.

His brotherly care becomes the mechanism that preserves ambiguity.

This is a central paradox:

> Kyousuke’s identity as protective brother creates the intimacy from which romantic possibility develops, then prevents that possibility from being spoken.

He would rather soothe Kirino than hear a truth that might destroy the category through which he knows how to care for her.

---

# 30\. Mikagami cannot reproduce the sibling bond

Mikagami explains that he was originally encouraged by Misaki to persuade Kirino to accept the European opportunity.

He participated in the boyfriend performance but was unable to divide the siblings.

He says, in effect:

> Someone like him could not tear their relationship apart.

Mikagami possesses nearly every surface qualification Kyousuke lacks.

But the volume’s conclusion is that compatibility cannot replace history.

Kyousuke knows:

-   Kirino’s humiliations;

-   failures;

-   hobbies;

-   cruelty;

-   effort;

-   loneliness;

-   and childhood.


He has been:

-   enemy;

-   witness;

-   protector;

-   audience;

-   collaborator;

-   and family.


Mikagami can resemble Kirino.

He cannot reproduce the relational system through which she became integrated.

This strongly supports the hypothesis that privileged access, not objective compatibility, drives the central bond.

---

# 31\. Mikagami is not defeated as a romantic villain

Mikagami is unusually decent.

He does not:

-   manipulate Kirino for sex;

-   exploit her secrets;

-   mock Kyousuke;

-   or insist on a romantic claim.


He recognizes that Kirino’s performance concerns Kyousuke more than himself.

He also admits his own attachment to a fictional heroine, placing him inside the volume’s broader discussion of otaku desire rather than outside it as a purely conventional rival.

His narrative function is not to be exposed as unworthy.

It is to show that even a highly suitable alternative cannot replace the particular relationship already formed.

That makes Kyousuke’s rejection more revealing.

He cannot justify himself by proving Mikagami bad.

He must admit:

> I oppose this because I selfishly want Kirino to remain mine in some sense.

---

# 32\. Kuroneko chooses direct speech

After the false romance is exposed and she reconciles with Kirino, Kuroneko meets Kyousuke behind the school.

This is the same relational space in which she gave him the cheek kiss before America.

She describes her confession through chūnibyō language:

-   the earlier kiss was a curse;

-   the curse must be overwritten;

-   and a stronger spell will replace it.


But beneath the performance, her words are completely direct:

> **「私と付き合ってください」**
> “Please go out with me.”

Unlike Kirino, Kuroneko does not:

-   recruit a fake boyfriend;

-   stage a jealousy test;

-   ask Kyousuke to infer her desire;

-   or withdraw the proposition before he can answer.


Her theatrical language supports directness rather than replacing it.

This is the mature form of performed identity Volume 6 prepared.

> Kuroneko uses the character she authored to say something the ordinary Ruri finds difficult—but she still says it.

---

# 33\. Kyousuke’s response confirms genuine romantic desire

Kyousuke’s reaction is physically and emotionally unmistakable.

He experiences:

-   intense joy;

-   accelerated heartbeat;

-   bodily weakness;

-   and the shock of receiving his first direct confession.


Within days, they begin dating.

This is important for evaluating the heroine structure.

Kuroneko is not merely:

-   an obstacle delaying Kirino;

-   a substitute during Kirino’s absence;

-   or a route that exists only theoretically.


Kyousuke chooses her.

The relationship is textually real.

It grows from:

-   creative collaboration;

-   shared vulnerability;

-   mutual recognition of envy;

-   school proximity;

-   private time;

-   direct romantic attraction;

-   and Kuroneko’s ability to challenge his rescuer identity.


Any later failure of this relationship must therefore be explained as the collapse of a meaningful possibility, not the removal of a false route.

---

# 34\. Why Kuroneko’s intimacy is healthier at this point

Kuroneko’s relationship with Kyousuke offers several qualities that the sibling bond lacks.

## Direct differentiation

She refuses to be treated as Kirino’s substitute.

## Respect for agency

She tells Kyousuke not to steal her responsibility.

## Reciprocal vulnerability

Both have witnessed the other’s insecurity and failure.

## Collaboration

They create together rather than existing only through rescue.

## Articulated desire

Kuroneko directly asks for a relationship.

## Social viability

Their romance does not violate family boundaries and can be recognized publicly.

This does not mean Kuroneko is perfectly healthy or uncomplicated. She is jealous, theatrical, controlling in her own ways, and deeply entangled with Kirino.

But after Volume 7, hers is clearly the more reciprocal and communicatively mature romantic relationship.

---

# 35\. Kirino and Kuroneko as rival and friend

Kuroneko’s confession comes only after reconciliation with Kirino.

That sequencing matters.

She does not simply defeat Kirino or seize Kyousuke while the friendship remains broken.

The relationship between the two girls must first become survivable again.

This preserves Kuroneko as a subject who values:

-   Kirino;

-   Kyousuke;

-   and her own romantic desire.


She refuses the heroine economy’s simplest demand that only one attachment may remain meaningful.

Yet the conflict cannot be fully neutralized.

By dating Kyousuke, Kuroneko alters:

-   her friendship with Kirino;

-   the group’s power balance;

-   and the siblings’ access to one another.


Volume 7 resolves the immediate lie but opens a more difficult real triangle.

---

# 36\. Otaku culture as romantic and social infrastructure

Volume 7’s relationships remain inseparable from fandom.

Otaku culture provides:

-   the eroge vocabulary through which fake romance is understood;

-   the children’s anime through which Kyousuke recognizes Kirino’s real desire;

-   the dōjin project that binds the group;

-   cosplay through which Kyousuke contributes creatively;

-   Comiket as the meeting point of Kirino’s communities;

-   Mikagami’s cross-performed identity;

-   and Kuroneko’s chūnibyō language of confession.


The volume confirms that fandom is not decorative setting.

It is the infrastructure through which:

-   people meet;

-   produce;

-   compete;

-   become jealous;

-   and confess.


## The incest dōjin mirror

The volume also includes meta-level material in which fictionalized sibling figures are erotically repackaged.

This mirrors what *Oreimo* itself is doing:

-   observing a psychologically complex sibling bond;

-   converting its charged moments into consumable heroine imagery;

-   and inviting readers to speculate about incest before the characters consciously do.


The work displays considerable awareness of its own commercial mode without ceasing to participate in it.

---

# 37\. Kyousuke’s “ordinary life” now includes creation

At the beginning of the series, Kyousuke defined ordinary life by absence:

-   no intense hobbies;

-   no ambition;

-   no unusual friends;

-   no public risk.


By Volume 7, his ordinary happiness contains:

-   Comiket;

-   cosplay;

-   dōjin production;

-   game clubs;

-   gothic-lolita juniors;

-   professional models;

-   and complicated emotional commitments.


He still wants continuity.

But the content of the continuity has been transformed.

The problem is no longer that Kyousuke fears all change.

He fears change that threatens the relationships through which his expanded life has gained meaning.

This makes him more alive and more possessive simultaneously.

---

# 38\. Japanese-language observations

## **彼氏 / 彼氏のふり / 本当の彼氏**

The volume repeatedly shifts among three formulations:

-   **彼氏** — boyfriend;

-   **彼氏のふり** — pretending to be a boyfriend;

-   **本当の彼氏** — real boyfriend.


The entire plot depends on instability among them.

The fake role produces real feelings.

The supposed real boyfriend is also fake.

The first genuinely real romance appears only when Kuroneko asks Kyousuke to **付き合う**.

## **付き合う**

**付き合う** can mean:

-   associate with;

-   accompany;

-   maintain relations;

-   or date romantically.


By the volume’s ending, context removes the ambiguity.

Kuroneko’s **私と付き合ってください** is a formal romantic request, and the narration confirms that they become **恋人同士**.

The series has finally crossed from route implication to an actual route.

## **取られる / やる / 渡す**

Kyousuke’s fear is repeatedly framed through possession and transfer:

-   Kirino being taken;

-   refusing to give her to Mikagami;

-   another man receiving her.


These terms express familiar family protectiveness while exposing its patriarchal logic.

Kirino is treated as someone whose movement between relationships threatens male custodianship.

## **大切**

Kyousuke says he will care for Kirino more than Mikagami can.

**大切にする** means:

-   treasure;

-   value;

-   treat carefully;

-   protect.


It does not itself specify romantic love.

Its exclusivist comparison, however, gives it romantic force.

## **気付いて欲しかった**

Mikagami’s phrase avoids specifying the object:

> Kirino wanted Kyousuke to notice.

The missing grammatical object mirrors the missing emotional definition.

She wants recognition before she can provide explanation.

## **もう遅い**

Kirino’s **もう遅い** marks belated recognition.

Kyousuke’s emotional response arrives only under threat of loss.

The phrase should be retained as unresolved rather than translated into a definitive rejected confession.

## **あのとき**

**あのとき**—“back then”—creates a private temporal reference that Kyousuke does not fully understand.

Its ambiguity indicates that Kirino has been tracking moments of relational possibility more carefully than he has.

## **呪い / 上書き**

Kuroneko’s curse metaphor transforms confession into authored ritual.

**上書き**—overwrite—draws on digital and game vocabulary:

-   the prior ambiguous kiss is replaced;

-   the old save state is superseded;

-   and a new explicit relationship is written over it.


Unlike Kyousuke’s earlier “one save file” regret, Kuroneko actively creates the new state.

---

# 39\. Illustration analysis

Volume 7’s illustrations visibly compete between two routes.

## The cover and opening images

Kyousuke and Kirino are presented in close, couple-like proximity. The visual paratext sells the fake romance before its explanation.

The effect is intentionally misleading but not dishonest: the entire volume concerns whether performance can reveal truth.

## Manami’s encounter

The illustration captures the shock of seeing the siblings arm in arm.

It makes Manami the visual witness to a boundary crossing that Kyousuke and Kirino are still describing as strategy.

## The purikura

The heart-framed couple photograph converts the fake route into a collectible romantic image.

Within the story it becomes a private keepsake.

Outside it, it is franchise material explicitly packaging the siblings as a couple.

## Ayase

The interrogation material converts moral alarm and jealousy into heroine comedy, continuing Ayase’s mixture of threatening authority and flustered attachment.

## Kuroneko in white

Her white dress creates a visual alternative to her familiar gothic identity.

The image is softer and more conventionally romantic, paralleling her movement toward direct confession.

## The collaborative group

The Comiket illustrations emphasize the cast as a creative ensemble rather than a romantic competition.

This is the most socially expansive visual register in the volume.

## The sibling confrontation

The emotional image of Kirino crying while Kyousuke reaches toward or comforts her returns them to the brother–sister protector structure just as her unspoken desire threatens to exceed it.

## The final Kuroneko image

The confession is framed with romantic seriousness.

Unlike Kirino’s performative couple images, Kuroneko’s final scene corresponds to a relationship the narration immediately confirms as real.

# 40\. Character baselines after Volume 7

## Kyousuke

Kyousuke has reached the point where he can no longer treat romance as something happening around him.

He:

-   wants clarification from Kuroneko;

-   feels threatened by Kirino’s supposed boyfriend;

-   admits possessiveness;

-   participates actively in otaku creation;

-   and accepts a direct confession.


His emotional life now contains both:

-   an actual girlfriend;

-   and a sister whose romantic independence he cannot tolerate calmly.


## Kirino

Kirino’s need has changed from acceptance toward recognition of priority.

She already has:

-   friends who know her hobbies;

-   professional success;

-   family support;

-   and multiple integrated communities.


What she now seeks from Kyousuke appears to be confirmation that she occupies a unique emotional position.

Her method is manipulative:

-   fake romance;

-   jealousy;

-   lies about kissing;

-   and injury to Kuroneko and Saori.


The volume makes her desire psychologically intelligible without treating her behavior as harmless.

## Kuroneko

Kuroneko becomes the first actual romantic partner.

She retains:

-   creative autonomy;

-   friendship with Kirino;

-   her chūnibyō voice;

-   and the ability to confront Kyousuke.


Her confession is a culmination of her growth from isolated creator to someone capable of asking directly for shared life.

## Saori

Saori’s community role becomes reciprocal.

She no longer functions as the sole invisible maintainer. She asks Kyousuke to protect the group, demonstrating trust and vulnerability.

## Manami

Manami becomes a silent witness to the siblings’ romanticized performance.

Her information reaches Ayase, and her earlier structural opposition to the Kousaka bond becomes more relevant.

She remains socially ordinary but is no longer narratively outside the conflict.

## Ayase

Ayase combines:

-   moral alarm;

-   hostility;

-   personal jealousy;

-   and trust in Kyousuke’s unique access to Kirino.


Her admission that she once expected to like him reinforces her status as a plausible alternative route rather than pure comic antagonist.

## Mikagami

Mikagami represents objective compatibility without shared history.

His decency prevents Kyousuke from disguising possessiveness as moral judgment.

## Kōhei and Sena

The Akagi siblings provide a comic but meaningful model of sibling provocation, jealousy, and direct emotional acknowledgment.

Their relative openness highlights the Kousakas’ dependence on elaborate performances.

# 41\. Hypothesis status after Volume 7

### Hypothesis 1

**Characters require performed or embarrassing relationships to disclose what they want.**

**Decisively confirmed.**

Kirino’s fake romances function as emotional experiments. Kuroneko’s theatrical curse language enables a real confession.

### Hypothesis 2

**Otaku culture is the first confessional space.**

**Further strengthened.**

Fandom now supports collective authorship, romantic self-understanding, identity performance, and explicit relationship formation.

### Hypothesis 3

**Kyousuke’s normality is defensive, and he needs to be needed.**

**Confirmed and refined again.**

His strongest fear is now clearly emotional replacement.

He does not only want to help Kirino. He wants his position to remain uniquely important.

### Hypothesis 4

**Kirino wants one person who accepts every compartment of her identity.**

**Expanded toward emotional priority.**

Kyousuke already accepts her multiplicity. Volume 7 suggests that acceptance is no longer sufficient.

She wants him to notice what her place in his emotional hierarchy means.

Whether she already wants romantic exclusivity remains unresolved because she cannot complete the thought.

### Hypothesis 5

**Shared secrecy creates access no outsider can reproduce.**

**Strongly supported.**

Mikagami can match Kirino socially, aesthetically, professionally, and subculturally. He still cannot reproduce the sibling bond.

### Hypothesis 6

**The heroines represent meaningful alternative futures.**

**Decisively confirmed.**

Kyousuke and Kuroneko become a real couple.

Her route is not hypothetical, decorative, or merely delaying.

It represents a reciprocal and socially viable future.

### Hypothesis 7

**The ending may grant impossible desire temporary reality while preserving social structure.**

Still not directly testable.

But Volume 7 establishes the central future conflict:

-   Kyousuke has entered a legitimate romantic relationship.

-   His bond with Kirino remains emotionally competitive with that relationship.

-   Kirino’s unarticulated need appears to exceed ordinary sibling recognition.

# 42\. Critical assessment

Volume 7 is one of the series’ most structurally important volumes and one of its most ethically unstable.

## Strengths

The father and Akagi parallels prevent Kyousuke’s possessiveness from being treated as automatically romantic.

The Comiket project gives the ensemble genuine creative life beyond heroine competition.

Kuroneko’s confession is earned through several volumes of:

-   vulnerability;

-   collaboration;

-   attraction;

-   and mutual challenge.


Mikagami’s decency forces Kyousuke into unusually honest self-exposure.

Kirino’s rage is also revealing because it identifies the unfairness in Kyousuke’s demand:

> He wants romantic freedom for himself and emotional exclusivity from her.

## Weaknesses

Kirino’s manipulation is severe.

She:

-   lies to her closest friends;

-   claims physical intimacy that did not occur;

-   humiliates Kuroneko;

-   destabilizes Saori’s community;

-   and uses Mikagami instrumentally.


The volume treats the resulting confrontation as productive, but the emotional cost is real.

Kyousuke’s physical and verbal possessiveness is also strongly romanticized. His refusal to “give” Kirino away is framed as moving sincerity without fully challenging its implication that her autonomy is secondary to his claim.

Mikagami is more functional than naturalistic. He is designed almost too perfectly as:

-   male Kirino;

-   ideal suitor;

-   and emotional test.


But this artificiality fits the route-based structure. He is less a fully autonomous rival than a deliberately constructed option used to expose why objective compatibility cannot settle intimate choice.

The ending’s rapid transition into the Kuroneko relationship also leaves the sibling confrontation unresolved rather than completed. That is intentional, but it means the real romance begins with an unprocessed emotional third party already inside it.

---

# Volume 7 verdict

Volume 7 answers the question raised at the end of Volume 6:

Kirino does not literally ask Kyousuke to become her boyfriend.

She asks him to perform the role so that she can preserve the integrated life she chose in Japan.

Yet the performance does more than solve a professional problem.

It makes the siblings practice:

-   exclusivity;

-   praise;

-   physical closeness;

-   first-name intimacy;

-   romantic display;

-   and jealousy.


The fake date reveals truths neither could state directly.

Kirino genuinely believes Kyousuke is kind and dependable.

Kyousuke genuinely admires her effort and beauty.

Both preserve the purikura evidence.

Both react strongly when the role appears transferable.

The later Mikagami deception pushes the experiment further. Kyousuke finally admits:

> I do not want another man to take you.
> I believe I will care for you more.
> I want the life we have now to remain mine.

These are psychologically coherent expressions of extreme sibling attachment.

They are also structurally romantic.

Kirino appears to want him to recognize that overlap, but she cannot say what she wants him to notice. Kyousuke responds by restoring the brother role before she can complete the sentence.

Then Kuroneko does what the Kousaka siblings cannot.

She says:

> **「私と付き合ってください」**

There is no practical pretext.

No fake partner.

No jealousy experiment.

No need for Kyousuke to deduce the request.

Kuroneko converts performed identity into direct romantic speech, and Kyousuke chooses her.

The most important conclusion after Volume 7 is therefore:

> **The series’ first real romance is not the sibling relationship. It is Kuroneko’s route—and it is convincing precisely because it offers directness, reciprocity, and differentiated identity where the Kousaka bond still relies on manipulation, inference, and family obligation.**

But that does not resolve the central sibling structure.

It makes it more dangerous.

Kyousuke now has a girlfriend, yet his declaration to Kirino has established that he cannot accept her moving beyond his emotional control. Kirino has apparently accepted Kuroneko’s relationship, yet she has also revealed jealousy and a need that remains unnamed.

Volume 8 must therefore test whether Kyousuke and Kuroneko’s healthier intimacy can exist independently—or whether the sibling bond has already become the hidden condition governing every romantic route around it.
