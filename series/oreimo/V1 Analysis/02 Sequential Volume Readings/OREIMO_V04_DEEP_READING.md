# *Oreimo* Volume 4 Deep Reading

## Gratitude, separation, and the life consultation that contains no problem to solve

Volume 4 completes the first major movement of *Oreimo*. The earlier books established a recurring structure:

1.  Kirino reveals a problem.

2.  Kyousuke interprets what she cannot say.

3.  He intervenes.

4.  The crisis is resolved.

5.  Their ordinary hostility resumes, but with greater intimacy underneath it.


Volume 4 asks what happens when that structure reaches its endpoint. Kirino announces a **最後の人生相談**—a final life consultation—but the eventual consultation contains no external crisis. She asks Kyousuke to buy games, plays with him, shows him things she values, and secures a promise that he will continue protecting them.

Only after she has left for America does Kyousuke understand:

> The final consultation was not a problem to solve. It was Kirino’s indirect way of saying goodbye, revealing where she came from, and confirming that their relationship would survive her absence.

The volume’s central thesis is therefore:

> **Kyousuke and Kirino no longer need a crisis to matter to one another, but they still need the language of crisis because neither can directly admit how much the relationship has come to mean.**

Volume 4 is also where Kyousuke’s understanding of **普通** undergoes its first genuine ideological revision. Ordinary life stops meaning an unchanged life protected from unusual people. It becomes a life capable of incorporating change without losing its grounding.

---

## 1\. Narrative structure

The volume consists of four loosely connected episodes whose emotional logic becomes clear only in retrospect.

### 1\. Ayase’s present and Kanako’s performance

Ayase wants to congratulate Kirino for her athletic success and repair the residual strain between them. She decides that the best present should come from Kirino’s otaku interests, even though she still dislikes those interests.

The chosen object is a unique *Meruru* figure available only as the prize in an official cosplay competition. Ayase refuses to wear the revealing costume herself, so she manipulates Kanako into entering. Kanako unexpectedly proves to be an outstanding singer, dancer, and performer and wins the competition.

### 2\. Manami enters the Kousaka household

Kyousuke invites Manami to his home while their parents are away. She cleans, cooks, and moves through the house with startling domestic ease.

Kirino reacts with immediate hostility. She eventually plants an eroge scene and Kyousuke’s hidden adult magazines in his room so that Manami will discover them.

Manami does not reject him. She responds with gentle teasing, asking whether she should start calling him **おにぃちゃん**.

### 3\. The apology party

Saori and Kuroneko learn what Kirino has done and compel her to make amends. They arrange what outwardly appears to be a celebration for Kirino’s novel but is actually intended to cheer Kyousuke up and create an opportunity for Kirino to apologize.

Their elaborate entertainment repeatedly humiliates Kyousuke, causing the plan to collapse. When he genuinely prepares to leave, Kirino finally says:

-   **この前はごめんなさい**

-   **いつも、ありがとう**


She gives him a present—appropriately and absurdly, an imōto eroge—and explicitly acknowledges everything he has done for her.

### 4\. The final consultation and departure

Kirino behaves with unusual gentleness toward her family, asks Kyousuke to buy two games at a midnight sale, plays them with him, and reveals a private collection of personal keepsakes. Among these are old report cards and race badges showing that she was once a mediocre student and slow runner who improved through years of effort.

She asks Kyousuke to continue protecting everything important to her. He agrees because he is her brother.

She then says:

> **「じゃあね、兄貴」**

# 2\. Kyousuke finally admits that he envies Kirino

The opening is one of the most important pieces of narration in the series so far.

Kyousuke no longer softens his resentment into generic irritation. He admits that during the plagiarism crisis he initially thought:

> Kirino deserved the setback. It served her right.

He resents:

-   her appearance;

-   academic success;

-   athletic talent;

-   professional opportunities;

-   self-confidence;

-   and repeated ability to succeed at things he has never attempted.


This clarifies his earlier refrain that he “hates” his sister. It is not simply comic denial of concealed affection. The resentment is real.

Yet the same opening concludes:

> **俺はあいつの、兄貴なんだよ。**

“I’m her brother.”

This does not mean that sibling duty erases his jealousy. It means that “brother” has become an identity strong enough to act **through** contradictory feelings.

Kyousuke can simultaneously:

-   envy Kirino;

-   enjoy seeing her humbled;

-   resent the cost of helping her;

-   admire her;

-   protect her;

-   and feel responsible for her.


Volume 4’s emotional maturity lies partly in refusing to purify him. He is not revealed to have secretly loved everything about Kirino from the beginning. His care has moral significance precisely because it is not effortless.

> **Being her brother becomes something he chooses to do, not merely a feeling he possesses.**

---

# 3\. Kyousuke revises his ideology of normality

Volume 3 had begun exposing the defensive character of Kyousuke’s “ordinary life.” Volume 4 makes the revision explicit.

He admits that:

> Saying an ordinary life is enough does not mean laziness is admirable.

He recognizes that he had used ideas such as:

-   “ordinary is best”;

-   “I am only a mediocre person”;

-   “extraordinary effort is not for me”;

-   and “there is no reason to take unnecessary risks”


as excuses not to test himself.

He begins studying more seriously. This is not because he has suddenly adopted Kirino’s relentless ambition. Rather, he separates two things he had previously confused:

1.  valuing an ordinary life;

2.  avoiding effort because failure might threaten his ordinary self-image.


The distinction is crucial.

Kyousuke does not need to become exceptional. He does need to take responsibility for how he lives.

## The epilogue completes the change

After Kirino leaves, Kyousuke again introduces himself as an ordinary high-school student. But the content of “ordinary” has changed radically.

His current life contains:

-   eroge;

-   dōjinshi;

-   Akihabara events;

-   otaku friends;

-   a childhood friend;

-   memories of Comiket;

-   and responsibility for Kirino’s hidden collection.


He now observes that everyone tends to regard themselves as normal and fear people who differ from them. His conclusion is effectively:

> A life can remain ordinary even while its contents change.

At the beginning of the series, normality meant static conformity.

By the end of Volume 4, it means maintaining one’s footing amid transformation.

That is a far wider and more tolerant category—not a rejection of ordinary life, but a rejection of using “ordinary” to exclude whatever one has not yet encountered. ÄåüEæ}èGòt)(Æ╟ë┴ìZÉ│12-05-24)-6

# 4\. The entire volume is organized around gifts

Each chapter contains someone trying to give another person something they cannot communicate directly.

-   Ayase gives Kirino the *Meruru* figure.

-   Kanako gives an audience a convincing performance.

-   Manami gives the Kousaka household domestic care.

-   Saori and Kuroneko create a party for Kyousuke.

-   Kirino gives Kyousuke an apology and an eroge.

-   Kyousuke gives Kirino his continued protection.

-   Kirino shows Kyousuke the evidence of her past effort.

-   The final consultation gives them one last ordinary evening together.


These gifts are imperfect.

They are often accompanied by:

-   deception;

-   embarrassment;

-   hostility;

-   miscommunication;

-   or comedy.


But that imperfection is the point. Characters in *Oreimo* rarely communicate affection in transparent declarations. They use objects, labor, performance, insults, and shared media as intermediaries.

Volume 4 is therefore less about “learning to say thank you” than about the difficulty of making gratitude legible to another person.

---

# 5\. Ayase: continued love without conversion

Ayase’s opening request develops the compromise reached in Volume 2.

She still does not approve of Kirino’s interests. She carefully distinguishes between material she considers tolerable and material she continues to regard as objectionable. But she now:

-   occasionally discusses less sexualized anime with Kirino;

-   accepts that these interests matter to her;

-   and wants the present to satisfy Kirino rather than Ayase’s own standards.


This is precisely what **折り合いを付けないまま** looks like in practice.

Ayase has not changed her beliefs to eliminate disagreement. She has changed her conduct so that disagreement does not invalidate the friendship.

## She now depends on Kyousuke as Kirino’s interpreter

Ayase asks:

> **「お兄さん、ご相談があります」**

The consultation structure has spread beyond Kirino.

Ayase recognizes that Kyousuke understands aspects of Kirino that she does not. This is especially significant because she once regarded him as the corrupting influence standing between herself and the “real” Kirino.

She now comes to him precisely because his access to Kirino’s hidden self is useful.

That gives Kyousuke a new social function:

> He mediates between Kirino’s separate worlds.

He can translate:

-   respectable friendship into otaku gifts;

-   subcultural knowledge into language Ayase accepts;

-   and Kirino’s hidden desires into actions other people can perform.


---

# 6\. Ayase’s care remains controlling

Ayase’s determination to obtain the perfect present is affectionate, but her method of recruiting Kanako reveals the darker side of that affection.

She:

-   conceals the true nature of the competition;

-   tells Kanako it is effectively a model audition;

-   arranges for Kanako’s ordinary clothes to be driven away;

-   flatters her strategically;

-   and leaves her with no socially viable way to withdraw.


Ayase’s goal is generous. Her means are coercive.

This is consistent with her characterization rather than an isolated gag. Ayase often assumes that once she has identified the morally correct outcome, manipulation is permissible.

In Volume 2, she tried to force Kirino back into the identity she considered healthy.

Here she forces Kanako into a performance because she has decided:

-   it will benefit Kanako’s career;

-   it will obtain Kirino’s present;

-   and therefore resistance can be overridden.


Her development is genuine, but not a complete softening. She has broadened what she can tolerate while retaining a strong desire to manage other people.

---

# 7\. Kanako complicates the series’ theory of authenticity

Volume 3 ended by contrasting Kirino’s “real” creative continuation with Setsuna’s imitation. Volume 4 immediately introduces a different problem.

Kanako knows almost nothing about *Meruru*. She initially despises:

-   cosplay;

-   otaku audiences;

-   and the embarrassment of the costume.


Nevertheless, she becomes the most convincing Meruru at the competition.

She succeeds because she already possesses:

-   vocal skill;

-   dance training;

-   stage confidence;

-   improvisational ability;

-   a strong appetite for attention;

-   and a physical resemblance to the character.


She reproduces the opening sequence after seeing it only briefly, adapts existing choreography, sings expertly, and gives the audience the experience of seeing Meruru embodied.

## The “real Meruru” need not privately love Meruru

Kanako’s success revises Volume 3’s formulation that “the real cannot lose to the fake.”

Here, authenticity is produced through performance.

The crowd does not know:

-   that Kanako mocks them backstage;

-   that she is not a fan;

-   that she smokes;

-   or that she entered under false pretenses.


What they recognize is a convincing public image.

This suggests several forms of authenticity operating in the series:

-   **biographical authenticity:** the work grows from the creator’s experience;

-   **emotional authenticity:** a person sincerely expresses attachment;

-   **performative authenticity:** the performer successfully creates the desired experience;

-   **social authenticity:** an audience collectively recognizes the performance as true enough.


Kanako lacks the first two but excels at the third and receives the fourth.

The volume therefore understands idol and character culture as a space where a carefully produced fiction can become experientially real.

## Kanako enjoys worship, not fandom

She admits that she liked:

-   being praised;

-   making people happy;

-   and feeling that the audience almost worshipped her.


She continues to consider their language ridiculous, but she finds their devotion cute and gratifying.

This places her on a plausible path toward entertainment work. She does not need to share the audience’s fantasy to understand how to generate it.

That is a sharp observation about character performance: the person who creates a fantasy and the audience who consumes it need not believe in it in the same way.

# 8\. Otaku culture as a performance and consumer economy

The cosplay competition broadens the novels’ cultural map.

Volume 1 emphasized hidden taste and community.

Volume 2 emphasized collective participation at Comiket.

Volume 3 emphasized amateur creation and professional publication.

Volume 4 emphasizes:

-   official franchise events;

-   voice actors;

-   cosplay competition;

-   character embodiment;

-   collectible scarcity;

-   audience response;

-   aspiring idol labor;

-   and commercial conversion of fan desire.


The unique *Meruru* figure is valuable because the franchise and audience agree that it is valuable. Ayase and Kyousuke must enter the event’s system to obtain it.

The episode also implicates *Oreimo* itself. It stages:

-   attractive girls in costumes;

-   competitive heroine visibility;

-   fan spectacle;

-   and rare character goods


while being a commercial character franchise doing much the same thing.

The narrative can recognize the strangeness of character consumption while profiting from it.

---

# 9\. Kuroneko and Kyousuke are forming an independent relationship

Before the gift hunt, Kyousuke meets Kuroneko alone. Their interaction is quieter than her usual exchanges with Kirino.

Kuroneko:

-   thanks him for supporting her after the publisher rejected her manuscript;

-   admits that his encouragement mattered;

-   discusses submitting work again;

-   asks whether he would accompany her;

-   and considers collaborative creation after previously working alone.


This is a major route-development scene.

Their relationship now contains experiences that do not belong to Kirino:

-   the publisher confrontation;

-   Kuroneko’s rejection;

-   Kyousuke’s praise of her effort;

-   their shared secret;

-   and her future creative ambitions.


Kuroneko can recognize Kyousuke’s insecurity because she has experienced the same comparison with Kirino. They are linked not only through affection for Kirino but through the wound of feeling inferior to her.

## “兄さん” becomes contested

Kuroneko continues calling him **兄さん**, a residue of their false sibling cover from Volume 3.

Kyousuke admits that he does not dislike it. The term gives their relationship:

-   warmth;

-   teasing intimacy;

-   and an ambiguous family-romantic flavor.


Kirino strongly objects.

She accuses Kyousuke of paying improper attention to her friends, especially Kuroneko, and resents Kuroneko’s claim on a sibling-coded address.

The exact nature of Kirino’s reaction is not yet settled. It may include:

-   possessiveness toward Kuroneko;

-   possessiveness toward Kyousuke;

-   discomfort that her private relationships are becoming independent of her;

-   embarrassment over the sibling-role play;

-   or emerging romantic jealousy.


Volume 4 supports the presence of possessiveness. It does not yet prove its final category.

## Kuroneko’s “two months” foreshadows a new social relation

She tells Kyousuke that she will stop calling him **兄さん** in two months. The epilogue reveals why: she enters his school and greets him as **先輩**.

The change moves their relationship from Kirino’s social world into Kyousuke’s everyday world.

Kuroneko is no longer merely “his sister’s friend.” She has become:

-   his school junior;

-   his creative confidante;

-   and the heroine positioned to occupy the narrative space created by Kirino’s departure.


---

# 10\. Manami enters the Kousaka home

The Manami episode makes her alternative future physically visible.

Until now, Manami has represented ordinary domestic continuity primarily through:

-   her family home;

-   shared studying;

-   food;

-   neighborhood familiarity;

-   and Kyousuke’s imagination.


In Volume 4 she crosses into the Kousaka household.

She immediately:

-   cleans effectively;

-   organizes the living room;

-   removes stains Kirino could not remove;

-   prepares a complete meal;

-   remembers Kyousuke’s favorite foods;

-   and makes the house feel like the Tamura home.


Kyousuke thinks:

> He may have wanted someone like Manami as a mother.

This line captures both the attraction and limitation of their relationship.

Manami gives him:

-   comfort;

-   care;

-   predictability;

-   and freedom from effort.


But Kyousuke often experiences her primarily as maternal infrastructure rather than as an autonomous young woman with desires of her own.

He enjoys what she provides without asking what it costs or what she expects.

That helps explain why their connection is emotionally deep but romantically stalled.

---

# 11\. Kirino experiences Manami as an invasion

Kirino’s hostility is immediate and disproportionate.

She objects that:

-   Kyousuke brought a girl home while their parents were absent;

-   Manami is touching and reorganizing family space;

-   she is cooking for him;

-   she is acting as though she belongs there;

-   and Kyousuke is visibly relaxed and satisfied.


The domestic imagery matters. Manami does not merely enter Kyousuke’s bedroom as a potential girlfriend. She enters the house as someone capable of becoming part of its daily structure.

Kirino treats her like an unwanted daughter-in-law while the narration repeatedly jokes that the scene resembles:

-   a hostile sister-in-law;

-   a new bride;

-   or a household test.


The comedy externalizes a real relational threat:

> Manami represents a future in which someone else becomes Kyousuke’s primary domestic attachment.

## Kirino’s motive is intentionally ambiguous

Kyousuke notes that the situation resembles a standard galgame jealousy event but immediately rejects that reading because Kirino and Manami supposedly do not fit the template.

The novel is more knowing than its narrator.

Kirino’s hostility might arise from:

-   old resentment connected to their childhood;

-   fear of losing Kyousuke’s attention;

-   dislike of Manami’s domestic authority;

-   protectiveness of family territory;

-   possessiveness unrelated to romance;

-   or an emerging romantic component.


Volume 4 does not yet supply enough information to choose conclusively.

What is clear is that Kirino is not indifferent to Kyousuke’s possible intimacy with another girl.

---

# 12\. Manami accepts Kyousuke’s embarrassing private self

Kirino’s retaliation is severe.

She places:

-   an explicit little-sister eroge scene on Kyousuke’s computer;

-   and his hidden adult magazines across his room


so that Manami will see them.

This reproduces Kirino’s own original nightmare: involuntary exposure of stigmatized taste to someone whose opinion matters.

Manami’s response is the inverse of Ayase’s response in Volume 2.

She does not:

-   condemn him;

-   declare him corrupted;

-   end the friendship;

-   or demand an explanation.


She notices that many of the magazines feature girls with glasses and then gently asks whether she should call him **おにぃちゃん**.

The joke humiliates Kyousuke, but it also demonstrates extraordinary relational resilience.

> Manami can absorb evidence that contradicts Kyousuke’s preferred public image without treating him as a different person.

In that respect, she offers exactly the kind of unconditional continuity Kirino previously needed from Kyousuke.

## But acceptance is not full understanding

Manami’s response is charitable and nonjudgmental, but she does not necessarily understand the actual situation:

-   which items belong to Kyousuke;

-   which belong to Kirino;

-   what his tastes mean;

-   or why the scene was arranged.


Her acceptance is grounded less in subcultural comprehension than in long familiarity.

She trusts Kyousuke before interpreting the evidence.

That makes her a powerful alternative model of intimacy:

-   Kuroneko understands through shared abnormality.

-   Manami accepts through accumulated familiarity.

-   Kirino depends on privileged secrecy and active participation.

# 13\. Kirino’s sabotage is not harmless tsundere behavior

The volume ultimately requires Kirino to apologize because her action is a genuine violation.

She:

-   invades Kyousuke’s private space;

-   exposes his sexual material;

-   weaponizes stigma;

-   attempts to disrupt a friendship;

-   and does to him a version of what she most fears being done to herself.


The online chat afterward shows that she initially treats the episode as comedy. Kuroneko helped suggest a trap, though Kirino escalated and executed it. Saori is appalled and insists they repair the damage.

This matters because the novel does not completely excuse Kirino through jealousy or embarrassment. Her vulnerability may explain her aggression, but it does not make the aggression acceptable.

Kyousuke later articulates the boundary clearly:

> Being family or friends does not mean they may hurt him without consequence.

This is an important counterweight to his rescuer identity. He is not required to tolerate everything merely because he cares.

# 14\. Online language reveals another layer of performed identity

The chat transcript shows Kirino, Kuroneko, and Saori communicating in stylized internet voices.

Kirino uses:

-   abbreviations;

-   repeated `ｗ`;

-   emoticons;

-   casual aggression;

-   and rapid contemporary slang.


Kuroneko transports her theatrical diction online, including the intentionally impossible reading of “千葉” as **せんよう**.

Saori adopts a refined **お嬢さま** register rather than her offline pseudo-otaku masculine speech.

Kuroneko immediately recognizes that Saori is performing a different internet personality.

This complicates the idea that online life simply frees the hidden “real self.”

Online identity can permit disclosure, but it also produces new performances.

The relevant question is not:

> Which voice is fake?

It is:

> What can each voice allow the person to do?

Saori’s voices let her:

-   manage groups;

-   soften confrontation;

-   assume authority;

-   and maintain emotional distance from her unperformed self.

# 15\. The apology party: care fails because it remains disguised

Saori organizes the party to:

-   improve Kyousuke’s mood;

-   make Kirino apologize;

-   and let the group thank him.


But nobody tells Kyousuke this.

Instead, the party is disguised as a celebration for Kirino. The participants perform comedy and embarrassing “entertainment”:

-   maid costumes;

-   sexually charged jokes;

-   Kuroneko’s manga portraying Kirino as a secret brocon;

-   and escalating humiliation.


The intended care becomes indistinguishable from another attack.

This is not accidental to the volume’s theme.

> **Affection that remains completely encoded can fail to reach its recipient.**

Kyousuke has spent three volumes interpreting Kirino’s indirect language. Here he reaches his limit.

He says he is leaving because:

-   he is already hurt;

-   nobody has explained the situation;

-   his friends appear to be worsening it;

-   and he feels unnecessary.


His line—

> **「俺なんかいてもいなくてもいいだろう」**

—reveals more than anger.

The deepest fear is not simply that they dislike him. It is that his role in this group may be dispensable.

---

# 16\. Kirino apologizes directly

Faced with Kyousuke actually leaving, Kirino abandons indirection.

She bows and says:

> **「この前はごめんなさいっ！」**

Then:

> **「……いつも、ありがとう」**

These are ordinary phrases, but they are extraordinary in Kirino’s voice.

Her usual vulnerability pattern is:

1.  disguise request as command;

2.  disguise gratitude as irritation;

3.  let Kyousuke infer the rest.


Here she recognizes that inference is insufficient:

> **“You won’t understand unless I say it.”**

She explicitly recounts what Kyousuke has made possible:

-   defending her collection from their father;

-   reconciling her with Ayase;

-   meeting Saori;

-   meeting Kuroneko;

-   and escaping the loneliness of having no one with whom to discuss what she loves.


This is the first full retrospective account of what Kyousuke means to her.

## Kyousuke cries because his labor has been seen

Kyousuke’s tears are not really about the physical present.

They arise because Kirino has confirmed that:

-   she noticed;

-   she remembers;

-   his interventions mattered;

-   and he was not merely an interchangeable tool.


His need to be needed is no longer just inference. The emotional intensity of his reaction shows that recognition from Kirino has become deeply important to his self-conception.

He finally admits:

> Being thanked by his sister makes him happy enough to cry.

This is not yet romantic desire. It is the emotional payoff of a relationship reconstructed through labor.

---

# 17\. The eroge present perfectly expresses Kirino

Kirino’s gift is 『妹×妹～しすこんラブすとーりぃ～』.

The choice is absurd but psychologically exact.

She gives Kyousuke:

-   an object she personally values;

-   a medium through which they first reconnected;

-   another experience they can share;

-   and a symbolic substitute for herself.


She says, in effect:

> Treat it as though it were me and take care of it.

The object combines:

-   gratitude;

-   self-reference;

-   otaku enthusiasm;

-   sibling provocation;

-   and inability to select a conventionally sentimental gift.


A normal present might have expressed less of Kirino.

Kyousuke recognizes this. Despite the comic collapse of the scene, he understands the sincerity inside the inappropriate form.

For a moment, he places his hand on her head and thinks they might look like an ordinary brother and sister.

That line is significant because the series does not define ordinary sibling intimacy as the absence of strangeness. Their most ordinary moment occurs through an eroge.

---

# 18\. The narrative rejects game-like emotional progression

After the party, Kyousuke explicitly reflects on eroge route logic.

If their lives followed game rules:

-   the apology would raise Kirino’s affection score;

-   a threshold would be crossed;

-   her behavior would switch from tsun to dere;

-   and the relationship would enter a new route phase.


But reality does not work that way.

Historical resentment remains.

Kyousuke still dislikes Kirino. Kirino remains abrasive. One meaningful gesture cannot erase years of hostility.

This is an important qualification to the series’ route structure:

> *Oreimo* uses game conventions while insisting that human relationships cannot be reduced entirely to affection flags and completed events.

Yet the novel also knows that its readers are tracking precisely those flags. It simultaneously resists and participates in the system.

# 19\. The afterword makes route logic explicit

The volume’s afterword announces a questionnaire project through which reader responses could influence the direction of future story development, describing it as a:

> **読者投票型のルート分岐シナリオ**

This is highly significant paratext.

By Volume 4, the heroine-route structure is not merely an interpretive analogy. The franchise explicitly invites readers to imagine alternative paths and influence which possibilities receive narrative development.

That means the eventual central romance should not be read as obviously inevitable from the outset.

At this stage, the text has actively cultivated several possibilities:

-   Manami as domestic ordinary continuity;

-   Kuroneko as shared creativity and reciprocal vulnerability;

-   Ayase as moral conflict transformed into attachment;

-   Kirino as the central sibling relationship reorganizing Kyousuke’s life.


The commercial structure benefits from keeping all of them emotionally plausible.

# 20\. The midnight eroge sale normalizes Kyousuke’s altered world

Kirino’s final practical request sends Kyousuke to a midnight eroge release.

The scene contains familiar comic estrangement:

-   cold weather;

-   unusual customers;

-   embarrassing merchandise;

-   spectators treating the queue as strange;

-   and Kyousuke insisting that he does not belong there.


But his attitude has changed.

He observes that everyone in line wants the creators’ work so badly that they cannot tolerate waiting until morning. Having recently met professional creators and publishers, he imagines how gratifying that anticipation must be to the people who made the games.

He is not merely mocking consumers. He can now see the reciprocal structure between:

-   creators;

-   works;

-   anticipation;

-   and audiences.


## Akagi gives him a parallel brotherhood

Kyousuke meets his apparently conventional classmate Akagi, who is buying a male–male game for his younger sister.

Each boy initially assumes the other must be sexually deviant. Each has an embarrassing explanation involving an otaku sister.

The scene has two important effects.

First, it punctures Kyousuke’s belief that his situation is uniquely abnormal. Other socially conventional boys may also possess hidden relationships to female fandom.

Second, it expands the series’ image of female otaku culture beyond Kirino:

-   female fandom includes BL and male–male erotic games;

-   brothers may be recruited into those interests;

-   and public respectability reveals little about private media life.


Kyousuke and Akagi establish solidarity through mutual nonexposure. Hidden fandom produces another community, this time between brothers.

---

# 21\. Kirino’s last consultation reenacts the first

The final consultation deliberately returns to the structure of Volume 1.

Kirino:

-   calls Kyousuke into her room;

-   asks him to acquire an eroge;

-   makes him play with her;

-   opens a hidden collection;

-   and struggles to explain why the objects matter.


But the function has changed.

## First consultation

Kirino needed:

-   someone to protect her secret;

-   someone to listen;

-   someone to share the hobby;

-   and help finding friends.


## Final consultation

Kirino needs:

-   one last period of shared time;

-   confirmation that Kyousuke will preserve what she leaves behind;

-   the chance to reveal something about her origin;

-   and assurance that the bond will continue without her physical presence.


The first consultation creates the relationship.

The final consultation tests whether the relationship can survive separation.

# 22\. The report cards dismantle the myth of effortless Kirino

Kirino reveals that she was once:

-   slow at running;

-   average in school;

-   and marked **がんばりましょう** in physical education.


Her badges show gradual progression:

-   sixth place;

-   sixth place;

-   fifth;

-   fourth;

-   third;

-   second.


Her grades improve similarly over several years.

This is the answer to Kyousuke’s jealousy at the beginning of the volume.

He has treated Kirino’s superiority as natural because that interpretation protects him:

-   if she was born exceptional, his own passivity is understandable;

-   if she became exceptional through sustained effort, he must reconsider himself.


Volume 3 taught him to notice her labor in writing.

Volume 4 reveals that the pattern extends back through childhood.

## Kirino’s motivation is not noble or elegant

She explains that she began because something made her intensely angry. When discouraged, she looks at the old records until she feels:

> **ナメんなバカ**

“Don’t underestimate me, idiot.”

Her ambition originates in:

-   resentment;

-   humiliation;

-   stubbornness;

-   and refusal to remain inferior.


This makes her achievement more human rather than less admirable.

Her polished confidence is built upon remembered inadequacy.

## She chooses to show Kyousuke the unpolished origin

Kirino has never told others because the story conflicts with her preferred image. It is:

-   muddy;

-   childish;

-   ordinary;

-   and embarrassing.


By revealing it, she gives Kyousuke access to something deeper than her fandom.

She is no longer showing only what she loves. She is showing how she became the person he envies.

That is an escalation of intimacy.

---

# 23\. The unopened album

Kirino also tries to show Kyousuke an album. He refuses because he assumes it contains some new form of extreme sexual material.

The volume never reveals its contents.

We should therefore resist confidently asserting that it contains childhood photographs or any particular secret. What the text does support is:

-   Kirino considers it important;

-   she is disappointed when Kyousuke refuses;

-   it belongs among deeply personal objects rather than ordinary merchandise;

-   and Kyousuke later feels that declining may have been the wrong choice.


He describes the sensation as though he selected the wrong dialogue option.

This is one of Volume 4’s most effective uses of route language.

Kyousuke has become skilled at solving explicit crises, but he still fails to recognize a quiet invitation to intimacy when it is not framed as a problem.

The unresolved album becomes the symbol of what remains unread between them.

---

# 24\. “Keep protecting these”

Kirino tells Kyousuke that the entire collection is precious and reminds him that he promised to help protect it.

He answers that he will continue even after the consultations end because:

> **「俺は、おまえの兄貴だしな」**

The promise has two levels.

### Literal

Kyousuke becomes caretaker of:

-   the games;

-   dōjinshi;

-   merchandise;

-   personal keepsakes;

-   and the hidden storage space.


### Symbolic

He is entrusted with:

-   Kirino’s private identity;

-   the memories they created;

-   the people she introduced into his life;

-   and the knowledge of who she is beneath her public success.


Kirino’s collection is no longer merely evidence of shame. It becomes an archive of their relationship.

When Kyousuke promises to protect it, he answers the true consultation without realizing what the question was.

That is why Kirino says:

> **「うん……大丈夫」**

She has established that what matters will remain in his care while she is gone.

---

# 25\. Kirino’s farewell is both tender and cruel

Kirino says:

> **「じゃあね、兄貴」**

Kyousuke hears it as a slightly unusual goodnight. Only afterward does its meaning become apparent.

Her failure to tell him directly that she is leaving should not be romanticized without qualification.

It is understandable because:

-   she cannot tolerate direct vulnerability;

-   she may fear his objection;

-   she does not want to weaken her decision;

-   she wants their last interaction to remain enjoyable;

-   and she trusts that the symbolic consultation communicated what she could not say.


But it is also cruel.

She denies Kyousuke:

-   the chance to ask questions;

-   the chance to express anger or sadness;

-   the chance to see her off;

-   and the chance to participate knowingly in a major transition.


Kirino retains total control over the separation.

The same independence that makes her admirable makes intimacy with her difficult. She moves ahead, decides alone, and asks others to understand after the fact.

---

# 26\. Kirino leaves as an autonomous person

The departure strongly revises any interpretation that Kyousuke has become the condition of Kirino’s functioning.

She leaves because she has:

-   a major athletic opportunity;

-   the discipline to pursue it;

-   sufficient modeling and royalty income to fund it;

-   the will to persuade her parents;

-   and confidence that her private world will survive in Japan.


She does not remain near Kyousuke because she needs him to manage every aspect of her life.

In fact, one function of the first four volumes may have been to make departure possible.

Before Volume 1, Kirino’s hidden identity was isolated and precarious.

By Volume 4:

-   her father has become more accepting;

-   Ayase knows the truth and remains her friend;

-   Saori and Kuroneko form a genuine community;

-   her collection has a protector;

-   and Kyousuke understands her.


She can leave without feeling that absence will erase the selves she constructed at home.

> Kyousuke’s support does not merely bind Kirino to him. It also gives her enough security to pursue independence.

This keeps the relationship from becoming simple mutual enclosure—at least at this stage.

---

# 27\. The Kousaka family has changed

The father’s development is quiet but consequential.

In Volume 1, he:

-   condemns Kirino’s hobbies;

-   distrusts her modeling;

-   and recognizes only the respectable version of his daughter.


By Volume 4, he attends her modeling work, tries to take photographs, and tells Kyousuke that she performed professionally and made him proud.

He still fears sending her overseas. His objections are serious:

-   she is fourteen;

-   the distance is enormous;

-   language barriers exist;

-   and the cost is high.


Kirino responds by presenting five million yen of her own earnings from modeling and royalties.

The father allows her to go not because he is indifferent but because he recognizes:

-   the depth of her preparation;

-   her financial responsibility;

-   and the impossibility of dismissing her commitment as a whim.


The family’s change is not that parental authority disappears. It is that Kirino’s work becomes legible enough to command respect.

---

# 28\. Separation reveals Kyousuke’s dependency

Kyousuke responds to the empty room with denial:

> He is relieved.
> Peace has returned.
> He no longer needs to hear noise through the wall.
> He will no longer be dragged into problems.

But this is the same denial structure that has characterized his narration from the beginning.

The difference is that he can no longer fully believe it.

He asks what his life will now become and realizes that returning to the old normal is impossible because Kirino has left behind:

-   knowledge;

-   memories;

-   friends;

-   habits;

-   obligations;

-   and changed values.


What was once extraordinary has become part of his ordinary life.

This is the first unequivocal confirmation that Kyousuke needs the world Kirino opened.

Not necessarily because he wants endless emergencies, but because that world has given him:

-   relationships;

-   purpose;

-   self-knowledge;

-   and a more expansive life.


Kirino may be physically absent, but the person Kyousuke has become cannot simply subtract her influence.

---

# 29\. The final consultation “continues forever”

Kyousuke eventually realizes why Kirino was satisfied.

He had promised:

-   to protect what she valued;

-   to continue being her brother;

-   and to preserve the life that had emerged through their consultations.


The last consultation therefore does not truly terminate.

It changes form.

No new problem has to be presented because the permanent answer is the relationship itself.

This is emotionally powerful, but it also reveals an asymmetry:

-   Kirino understands what she is asking.

-   Kyousuke gives the correct answer without understanding the question.

-   She leaves content.

-   He must reconstruct the meaning afterward.


Their intimacy has become deep, but their communication remains structurally unequal.

---

# 30\. Kuroneko enters the vacancy

The epilogue moves from Kirino’s absence directly to Kuroneko’s arrival at Kyousuke’s school.

Her first word establishes the new relation:

> **「おはようございます、先輩」**

This is not merely a teaser for Volume 5.

Structurally, it demonstrates that Kirino’s influence remains present through the relationships she created.

Kyousuke’s new everyday cannot return to its pre-Kirino state because Kuroneko has crossed into it.

The sequence is important:

1.  Kirino leaves.

2.  Kyousuke defines his altered normality.

3.  Kuroneko appears within that normality.


This creates a genuine alternative romantic route without making Kirino irrelevant. Kuroneko’s presence is one of the things Kirino has left behind.

The coming relationship can therefore be both:

-   independent of Kirino;

-   and historically produced through her.

# 31\. Japanese-language observations

## 「兄貴」

Kirino’s **兄貴** is rougher than **お兄ちゃん** and less formal than **兄さん**.

It fits her personality:

-   unsentimental;

-   combative;

-   colloquial;

-   yet unmistakably relational.


At the apology and final farewell, **兄貴** carries emotional weight because Kirino had once refused to recognize Kyousuke as a meaningful brother at all.

## 「じゃあね」

In isolation, **じゃあね** can be an ordinary casual goodbye. In the context of a nighttime conversation between cohabiting siblings, it is subtly wrong: **おやすみ** would be expected.

Kyousuke notices the nostalgic feeling but not the literal warning.

The phrase succeeds because it is both natural enough to miss and final enough to become devastating retrospectively.

## 「大丈夫」

Kirino’s **大丈夫** after the last consultation does not merely mean “this was sufficient.”

It means something closer to:

-   I can go now.

-   What matters is secure.

-   You will still be my brother.

-   My hidden life will not disappear.


Its full meaning is available only after the departure.

## 「普通」

By the epilogue, **普通** has changed from a classificatory barrier into an adaptive principle.

Kyousuke no longer defines normality by excluding:

-   otaku;

-   strange hobbies;

-   unusual friendships;

-   or personal change.


His new formulation allows people to remain grounded without remaining unchanged.

## 「最後」

The repeated **最後の人生相談** becomes ironic.

Kyousuke initially assumes “last” refers to the final discrete request. The ending reveals that the consultation has become a permanent relational obligation.

“Last” marks the end of the explicit ritual, not the end of what it created.

# 32\. Illustration analysis

The Volume 4 illustrations closely track its shift from ensemble comedy to separation.

## Cover

Kirino occupies the foreground while Kyousuke stands behind her burdened with shopping bags. The image summarizes the public comic relationship:

-   Kirino directs;

-   Kyousuke carries;

-   and her attractiveness converts his labor into franchise imagery.


## Cosplay material

Kanako’s performance is given large, spectacular treatment. Her visual dominance over Kyousuke and the audience reinforces the theme that performed character identity can overwhelm the performer’s private relationship to the work.

The publication itself treats Kanako exactly as the contest audience does: as a successful embodiment worth displaying.

## Manami’s discovery

The illustration emphasizes Manami’s embarrassment and Kyousuke’s panic, turning exposure of private sexuality into romantic-comedy spectacle.

Yet Manami’s expression is not one of disgust. The image visually supports her role as someone who can encounter Kyousuke’s humiliating private material without withdrawing affection.

## The maid ensemble

Kirino, Kuroneko, and Saori are visually packaged as a comic “harem,” even though the actual party is about gratitude, guilt, and friendship.

This is a particularly clear example of the series converting emotional labor into heroine fanservice.

## Kirino’s apology

The close-up illustration at **いつも、ありがとう** privileges one of her rare unguarded expressions. The scene is framed almost like a confession, matching Kyousuke’s own comparison.

That romantic visual grammar does not make the declaration romantic in content, but it deliberately charges sibling gratitude with heroine-confession imagery.

## The final consultation

Kirino’s soft smile near the end is radically different from her normal visual register. Because the reader soon learns she is leaving, the image becomes a farewell portrait.

## Kuroneko’s final image

The volume’s final character image belongs not to Kirino but to Kuroneko in her new school uniform.

The visual handoff is unmistakable: the absent central heroine gives way to the heroine entering Kyousuke’s daily environment.

# 33\. Character baselines after Volume 4

## Kyousuke

Kyousuke now consciously recognizes:

-   his envy of Kirino;

-   the value of effort;

-   the inadequacy of passive normality;

-   his emotional need for recognition;

-   and the permanence of the world Kirino introduced.


He has also developed a clearer boundary: care does not obligate him to accept unlimited mistreatment.

## Kirino

Kirino becomes more understandable without becoming simpler.

She is:

-   sincerely grateful;

-   aggressively possessive;

-   capable of apology;

-   willing to expose Kyousuke to humiliation;

-   intensely hardworking;

-   emotionally indirect;

-   and autonomous enough to leave for another country.


The report cards reveal that her identity is made, not simply given.

## Kuroneko

Kuroneko now has an independent path into Kyousuke’s life.

She shares:

-   creative ambition;

-   private gratitude;

-   route-coded intimacy;

-   and a future school environment with him.


Her willingness to help repair the harm done to Kyousuke also shows that her jealousy does not entirely govern her conduct.

## Manami

Manami remains the strongest representation of ordinary domestic continuity.

Her acceptance of Kyousuke’s embarrassing sexuality demonstrates the durability of their relationship. But Kyousuke’s maternal framing of her suggests that he takes her care for granted and has not yet perceived her as a fully reciprocal romantic subject.

## Ayase

Ayase puts Volume 2’s compromise into practice. She can serve Kirino’s interests without approving of them.

Her manipulation of Kanako shows that moral certainty and controlling behavior remain intertwined.

## Saori

Saori continues to function as social infrastructure. She detects relational damage, organizes repair, and pushes Kirino toward direct accountability.

The chat also reveals another performed Saori persona without yet exposing the private person beneath all of them.

## Kanako

Kanako emerges as a talented performer whose aptitude is independent of otaku sincerity. She understands attention, stage pleasure, and audience desire instinctively.

She is also crude, selfish, and self-destructive; the volume does not turn talent into moral purity.

# 34\. Hypothesis status after Volume 4

### Hypothesis 1

**Characters perform acceptable identities while using embarrassing relationships to disclose themselves.**

**Further strengthened.**

Kanako demonstrates that performance can produce something socially real even without private identification. Saori’s multiple registers also show that one person may use several deliberate personas for different functions.

### Hypothesis 2

**Otaku culture is the first confessional space.**

**Expanded again.**

Otaku media now functions as:

-   gift;

-   apology;

-   ritual;

-   public performance;

-   friendship marker;

-   substitute for direct affection;

-   and repository of memory.


### Hypothesis 3

**Kyousuke’s normality is defensive, and he needs to be needed.**

**Strongly confirmed.**

His devastation at feeling unnecessary during the party and his tears when Kirino recognizes his help demonstrate that usefulness has become central to his identity.

The epilogue also explicitly revises his idea of normality.

### Hypothesis 4

**Kirino wants one person who accepts every compartment of her identity.**

**Partly strengthened, still not exclusive.**

Kyousuke receives uniquely personal knowledge:

-   her collection;

-   her early failures;

-   her motivations;

-   and custody of what she leaves behind.


But Kirino also continues investing in Ayase, Saori, Kuroneko, family, modeling, and athletics. She leaves Kyousuke to pursue an independent goal.

### Hypothesis 5

**Shared secrecy grants the siblings access no outside relationship can reproduce.**

**Strongly strengthened.**

Kyousuke becomes caretaker not only of Kirino’s otaku collection but of her personal origin story. The final consultation creates an enduring private covenant.

### Hypothesis 6

**The heroines represent alternative forms of intimacy and futures.**

**Decisively strengthened.**

-   Manami offers unconditional continuity and domestic care.

-   Kuroneko offers reciprocal vulnerability, creativity, and a new school relationship.

-   Ayase offers moral conflict transformed into enduring attachment.

-   Kirino remains the relationship that changes Kyousuke’s definition of himself.


The afterword’s reader-vote branching project confirms that route competition is also part of the franchise’s explicit commercial design.

### Hypothesis 7

**The ending may grant impossible desire temporary reality while preserving social structure.**

Still not directly testable. Volume 4 does, however, continue applying confession and romantic-route imagery to sibling gratitude, while simultaneously emphasizing genuine family identity through **兄貴**.

# 35\. Critical assessment

Volume 4 is less unified than Volume 3. Its chapters initially resemble a collection of comic side stories, and the author explicitly says the volume increased the proportion of comedy.

Yet the comedy is not disposable.

Each episode tests a different form of acceptance:

-   Ayase accommodates a hobby she still rejects.

-   Kanako embodies a character she does not love.

-   Manami accepts embarrassing sexuality without understanding the subculture.

-   Kirino fails to respect Kyousuke’s privacy.

-   Saori and Kuroneko attempt to repair harm through performance.

-   Kyousuke accepts responsibility that will continue after separation.


The volume’s main weakness is that some harmful conduct is pushed very far for comedy:

-   Ayase’s coercion of Kanako;

-   Kirino’s deliberate sexual humiliation of Kyousuke;

-   Kanako’s underage smoking;

-   and repeated physical aggression.


The apology chapter partially acknowledges this, especially in Kirino’s case, but the comic register still cushions behavior that would be severe outside the genre.

The departure also depends on Kyousuke being implausibly uninformed about a major family decision. The text explains that Kirino intended to tell him herself, but the parents’ complete silence until after she leaves remains structurally convenient.

Nevertheless, the emotional construction is effective because Kyousuke’s failure to understand the final consultation is consistent with his established weakness:

> He can solve crises more easily than he can recognize an invitation to intimacy.

---

# Volume 4 verdict

Volume 4 is the conclusion of *Oreimo*’s first relational arc.

Over four volumes:

-   Kyousuke discovers Kirino’s secret.

-   He becomes her confidant.

-   He helps her find friends.

-   He defends the entirety of her identity.

-   He learns to recognize her work.

-   She finally acknowledges what he has done.

-   She reveals the ordinary, wounded origin beneath her excellence.

-   Then she leaves.


The departure is not a negation of their development. It is its first major test.

Kirino can leave because:

-   her identities are more integrated;

-   her friendships can survive knowledge;

-   her father respects her work more than before;

-   her collection is safe;

-   and she trusts Kyousuke to remain her brother.


Kyousuke, meanwhile, discovers that his preferred “ordinary life” has already been transformed beyond recovery.

The final consultation contains no problem because its real request is:

> Know me.
> Remember me.
> Protect what I leave behind.
> Keep being my brother when I am gone.

Kyousuke does not consciously understand the request, but he answers it correctly.

That is why Kirino can say **大丈夫**.

The most important conclusion after Volume 4 is therefore:

> **The siblings’ bond has outgrown the crisis mechanism that created it, but not the indirection that conceals it.**

Their intimacy is now structurally privileged and emotionally central, but it is still fundamentally legible as reconstructed sibling love rather than acknowledged romance. The romantic charge comes largely from the route language, visual framing, possessiveness, and commercial genre surrounding that bond.

Volume 5 begins under radically altered conditions: Kirino is absent, Kyousuke’s old normality cannot be recovered, and Kuroneko has entered his everyday life as his **後輩**. The next question is whether Kyousuke’s emotional world expands during Kirino’s absence—or whether her absence reveals that every new relationship remains organized around the place she left behind.
