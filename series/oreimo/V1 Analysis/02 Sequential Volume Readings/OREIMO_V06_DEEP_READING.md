Volume 6 functions less as a simple “Kirino returns” story than as a redefinition of nearly every relationship she left behind.

# *Oreimo* Volume 6 Deep Reading

## Performance, habit, and the right to remain multiple

Volume 6 opens one year after Kyousuke discovered Kirino’s secret. His retrospective formulation is the clearest summary of their relationship so far:

> He still hates his sister.
> She presumably still hates him.
> But she is now **大切な家族**—precious family.

This distinction matters. The first five volumes have not purified the siblings’ relationship into uncomplicated affection. They still irritate, envy, insult, and injure one another. What has changed is that hostility no longer permits emotional abandonment.

Volume 6 then expands that principle beyond the Kousaka siblings. Its characters repeatedly ask whether performed, contradictory, or socially compartmentalized selves can still be recognized as continuous persons:

-   Kanako is a crude delinquent and a disciplined professional performer.

-   Saori is a comic otaku caricature, a shy wealthy schoolgirl, an imitation of her older sister, and a genuine community-builder.

-   Kirino is an athlete, model, student, friend, otaku, creator, daughter, and sister.

-   Kyousuke is an ordinary high-school student who increasingly understands himself through extraordinary acts of care.


The volume’s central thesis is:

> **An identity does not become false merely because it is performed. What matters is whether the performance expresses, protects, or creates a relationship the person genuinely values.**

At the same time, Volume 6 begins moving the sibling bond into a more explicitly romanticized narrative structure. It strengthens Kyousuke’s familial explanation for loving Kirino even as it ends with Kirino asking him to become her boyfriend.

That tension is deliberate. The volume anchors the relationship in family history while intensifying its heroine-route framing.

---

# 1\. Narrative structure

Volume 6 consists of several episodes that initially appear episodic but share a common concern with performance and relational identity.

## 1\. Kirino returns to her social world

After returning from America, Kirino spends hours calling:

-   school friends;

-   modeling contacts;

-   Saori;

-   Ayase;

-   and other people she could not contact during her self-imposed isolation.


Kyousuke remains nearby, brings her a drink, pretends to read, and becomes vaguely irritated by the number of people eager to welcome her home.

He also obsesses over the unresolved meaning of Kuroneko’s cheek kiss.

## 2\. Ayase asks Kyousuke to manage Kanako again

Ayase wants Kyousuke to accompany Kanako to an official *Meruru* event and verify that she has stopped smoking.

Kanako is crude and abusive backstage but proves:

-   professionally prepared;

-   highly competent;

-   attentive to her nervous co-performer;

-   and capable of protecting someone despite her own fear.


Kyousuke’s perception of both Kanako and the fan audience changes.

## 3\. Kyousuke seeks interpretations of sibling affection

He speaks with Manami about Kirino’s apparently unchanged behavior and with Kōhei Akagi about what a little sister means to an older brother.

The two conversations produce different insights:

-   Manami reveals that Kyousuke expected greater intimacy after bringing Kirino home.

-   Akagi describes brotherly care not as abstract love but as lifelong **習慣**—habit.


## 4\. Saori’s performed identities are exposed

Saori misses Kirino’s return party and behaves with unusual emotional strain. The others trace her home and discover:

-   her wealthy family background;

-   an enormous inherited otaku space;

-   her shy uncostumed personality;

-   her older sister’s former social circle;

-   and the abandonment anxiety that motivated “Saori Bajeena.”


The group responds by accepting every version rather than identifying one as the definitive “real Saori.”

## 5\. Lia visits Japan

Kirino’s twelve-year-old American roommate and running rival arrives at the Kousaka home.

Kirino takes her through Akihabara and explains why she returned to Japan: athletics alone was not enough to let her remain herself.

They race again. Kirino loses, but the race clarifies both her motivation and Kyousuke’s identification with her.

## 6\. The consultation changes its name

Kirino asks Kyousuke for an **お願い** rather than a **人生相談**.

Kyousuke recognizes that the nominally final consultation did not end their relational ritual. It has merely changed terminology.

The volume ends with Kirino saying:

> **「あんた、あたしの彼氏になってよ」**
> “Become my boyfriend.”

---

# 2\. Kirino’s return does not restore the old normal

Kyousuke expected the America episode to produce an obvious alteration in their relationship.

He had:

-   crossed the Pacific for her;

-   admitted that he was lonely;

-   asked her to return for his own sake;

-   and presented himself as someone who would accept her even in failure.


Yet after returning, Kirino still:

-   insults him;

-   dismisses him;

-   restricts when he may call her;

-   and behaves as though little has changed.


Kyousuke becomes frustrated because he unconsciously expected emotional compensation.

He does not demand gratitude directly, but he wants evidence that the journey mattered to her.

This is important because it reveals that Kyousuke’s rescue was not entirely free of expectation. He wanted:

-   closeness;

-   recognition;

-   and perhaps a privileged place in Kirino’s restored life.


Instead, she immediately reconnects with everyone.

## Kyousuke’s irritation at Kirino’s many friends

Kirino calls friend after friend because she wants to hear their voices and announce her return directly.

The scene demonstrates that she has successfully integrated multiple social worlds:

-   school;

-   athletics;

-   modeling;

-   otaku friendship;

-   and family.


This should satisfy Kyousuke. He wanted her to return to a fuller life.

Yet he admits that he finds the sight vaguely unpleasant.

The emotion is not straightforward romantic jealousy. It includes:

-   disappointment that he is only one person among many;

-   resentment that she appears less visibly affected than he is;

-   fear that his unique importance during the crisis has already diminished;

-   and the ordinary possessiveness of someone who crossed the world to retrieve her.


Volume 5 allowed Kyousuke to say:

> I need you.

Volume 6 begins asking whether needing someone produces an unspoken demand to be needed equivalently.

---

# 3\. Kyousuke wants confirmation from both girls

Kyousuke is also preoccupied by Kuroneko.

She kissed his cheek before sending him to America, but after his return she behaves as though nothing happened.

He lies in bed imagining that the kiss may have been a confession. Then, when facing Kuroneko, he lacks the courage to ask directly.

This is a clear shift in his narrative role.

Kyousuke is no longer only a passive male recipient around whom heroines happen to generate ambiguity. He wants an answer.

He wants to know:

-   whether Kuroneko likes him;

-   what the kiss meant;

-   and whether he is permitted to interpret it romantically.


## Kirino becomes the measuring instrument

Kuroneko had previously said:

> **「好きよ……あなたの妹が、あなたのことを好きなくらいには」**
> “I like you about as much as your sister likes you.”

Kyousuke therefore calls Kirino and asks:

> **「おまえさ、俺のことどのくらい好き？」**

Kirino rejects the question as disgusting and hangs up.

The comedy conceals a revealing triangulation.

Kyousuke is attempting to measure Kuroneko’s feelings through Kirino’s.

This only works because he assumes that Kirino’s attachment to him is:

-   real;

-   emotionally significant;

-   and somehow quantifiable despite her hostility.


He no longer truly believes she is indifferent.

He is uncertain about the category and magnitude of the attachment—not its existence.

## His “hate” disclaimer is becoming ritualized

At the opening, Kyousuke insists again that he hates Kirino.

But the volume immediately shows him:

-   sitting near her without practical reason;

-   watching her reunions;

-   resenting others’ access to her;

-   worrying about whether their relationship changed;

-   asking how much she loves him;

-   and analyzing what she means to him.


“I hate her” is not wholly false. Kirino remains infuriating.

But it is no longer an adequate account. It increasingly functions as a verbal safety device that permits him to approach more dangerous truths without naming them directly.

---

# 4\. Manami and the blindness produced by familiarity

Kyousuke turns to Manami when he cannot interpret Kirino’s behavior.

Manami immediately recognizes the actual problem:

> Kyousuke hoped that bringing Kirino home would make the siblings visibly closer.

He admits that he was deeply lonely during Kirino’s absence and fears that he may have forced her to abandon something important without gaining even the relational change he wanted.

Manami reassures him that Kirino talks about him frequently. Kirino’s apparent normality does not mean the America episode was emotionally insignificant. It may mean she is:

-   embarrassed;

-   uncertain how to behave;

-   or deliberately restoring familiar interaction.


## Familiarity makes Kyousuke inattentive

Kyousuke also fails to notice that Manami has changed her hairstyle.

This is another small scene with longitudinal significance.

Manami’s constancy has become so central to his life that he often stops looking at her.

He assumes:

-   she will remain nearby;

-   she will understand;

-   she will offer perspective;

-   and she will continue being “the same Manami.”


The failure to notice her hair mirrors a larger danger:

> Kyousuke’s idea of Manami as unchanging ordinary continuity may prevent him from perceiving her as a person who also changes, wants, and evaluates him.

## “Because it is you, I cannot tell you”

Kyousuke considers asking Manami about Kuroneko’s kiss but stops himself.

He realizes:

> **おまえにだから、言えねえ。**
> Precisely because it is you, I cannot tell you.

Manami is normally the person to whom he can bring embarrassing relational confusion. But a possible romance with Kuroneko is different because Manami is not a neutral observer.

Even if Kyousuke has not consciously defined her as a romantic candidate, he recognizes that discussing another girl’s confession with her would carry emotional consequences.

This is an important sign that the heroine structure is becoming conscious inside Kyousuke’s relationships.

---

# 5\. Ayase increasingly trusts Kyousuke

Ayase’s reunion with Kirino is openly emotional.

She:

-   runs into Kirino’s arms;

-   cries;

-   asks whether she will disappear again;

-   and expresses unqualified happiness that she has returned.


The scene resembles Kuroneko’s airport reunion. It confirms again that Kirino’s return restores a network rather than only a sibling pair.

Afterward, Ayase approaches Kyousuke with another consultation.

Her willingness to use him has changed meaning across the series.

In Volume 2, she regarded him as:

-   suspicious;

-   potentially corrupting;

-   and possibly dangerous to Kirino.


By Volume 6, she trusts him to:

-   supervise Kanako;

-   act as an amateur manager;

-   recognize unperformed behavior;

-   monitor a serious health-related promise;

-   and intervene if something goes wrong.


Her insults and defensive alarm remain part of their comic rhythm, but beneath them lies substantial confidence in his competence.

Ayase now sees Kyousuke as someone who:

-   understands difficult girls;

-   does not abandon them;

-   and can function responsibly inside embarrassing situations.


---

# 6\. Kanako: professionalism beneath contempt

Kanako initially appears unchanged:

-   she uses an electronic cigarette;

-   bullies Bridget;

-   insults Kyousuke;

-   sprawls around backstage;

-   and treats fandom with contempt.


But the episode forces Kyousuke to revise his judgment.

## She distinguishes private behavior from professional obligation

Kanako has already memorized her script. When Kyousuke expresses surprise, she responds that she is being paid and no genuine idol would perform carelessly.

Her principle is crude but serious:

> **「金もらってんだからよお～、ちゃんとやるっつったらやる」**
> She is being paid, so she will do the job properly.

She may privately think:

-   the fans are ridiculous;

-   the anime is childish;

-   and the character is embarrassing.


None of that prevents her from understanding that an audience has paid, traveled, waited, and invested emotionally in the performance.

Performance is therefore not false merely because the performer’s private beliefs differ from the audience’s.

Kanako creates a convincing Meruru because she respects the obligation of the stage, not because she privately shares the fantasy.

## Her treatment of Bridget is abrasive care

At first Kanako appears to be tormenting Bridget.

She mocks:

-   Bridget’s nervousness;

-   her excessive reverence for *Meruru*;

-   and her fear of deviating from the script.


But Kyousuke eventually recognizes that Kanako is trying to provoke her out of paralysis.

She tells Bridget, in effect:

-   stop worshipping the audience;

-   stop treating the character as too sacred to embody;

-   trust that Kanako can cover mistakes;

-   and perform with the natural charm she showed before.


Kanako’s method is unpleasant, but the underlying judgment is perceptive.

She understands stage fear better than Kyousuke expects.

## Courage without purity

When Kanako mistakenly believes Bridget is threatened, she intervenes aggressively despite being visibly afraid.

The misunderstanding makes the scene comic, but her courage remains real. Her body trembles, yet she acts.

As with Kuroneko in Volume 3, the novel separates moral worth from emotional purity:

-   bravery does not require absence of fear;

-   professionalism does not require private sweetness;

-   and care does not always resemble gentleness.


Kanako’s virtues grow from the same personality traits as her faults:

-   pride;

-   aggression;

-   confidence;

-   appetite for attention;

-   and refusal to be intimidated.


---

# 7\. Kyousuke moves inside the production side of fandom

During the *Meruru* event, Kyousuke notices that the fan audience feels different from his position backstage.

Earlier, crowds of chanting adult fans appeared:

-   grotesque;

-   noisy;

-   alien;

-   and faintly threatening.


Now he is present as someone connected to the performers and production.

The audience’s enthusiasm produces:

-   gratitude;

-   reassurance;

-   and a sense of shared purpose.


He understands that the performers’ labor requires a receptive audience and that the fans’ intensity gives the event its energy.

This continues his cultural development:

-   Volume 1: fandom provides confessional friendship.

-   Volume 2: Comiket is collectively produced.

-   Volume 3: amateurs become creators and professionals.

-   Volume 4: character performance generates public fantasy.

-   Volume 5: clubs provide creative infrastructure.

-   Volume 6: professional performance and fan response form a reciprocal system.


Kyousuke remains an outsider in taste. But he is no longer socially external to the culture.

---

# 8\. What is a little sister? Akagi’s answer

Kyousuke asks Kōhei Akagi:

> **「おまえにとって、妹ってのは、なんだ？」**

Akagi answers first with comic excess:

> **「天使」**
> An angel.

But his actual explanation is more important.

He describes caring for Sena as **習慣**—habit.

When she was a baby and cried, he shook a rattle.

Now, when she becomes distressed, he may buy a BL game.

The object changes. The bodily pattern remains.

## Family love as pre-reflective practice

Akagi does not present brotherly care as a grand ethical doctrine.

It is something learned through repetition:

-   hear the younger sibling cry;

-   respond;

-   soothe;

-   protect;

-   repeat across years.


The affection precedes explicit reasoning.

This helps Kyousuke understand why his feelings resist simple classification as liking or disliking.

He may hate many things about Kirino.

But “Kirino is crying, therefore act” has been written into him from before either sibling could articulate what they meant to one another.

## Kyousuke remembers Kirino’s birth

The conversation evokes his earliest memory of seeing Kirino as a newborn.

He thinks of her as the small creature who has remained, in some part of his imagination, inside his arms and under his protection.

This gives his devotion a deeply familial foundation:

> Kirino is not a fantasy little sister. She is the particular child whose existence reorganized Kyousuke’s bodily and emotional habits.

The passage is one of the strongest arguments so far against treating his attachment as already straightforward romantic desire.

## The paternalistic danger

The insight also contains a limitation.

If Kyousuke continues seeing Kirino as:

-   the crying baby;

-   the vulnerable younger organism;

-   and the person he must protect until they separate,


he may struggle to recognize her as an autonomous near-adult whose choices can legitimately escape his supervision.

The protective habit is loving.

It can also freeze the younger sibling in permanent dependency.

This tension was already present when he overrode her request to destroy the collection. Volume 6 gives the instinct a family history without completely resolving its ethics.

---

# 9\. Saori’s absence exposes the group’s dependency on her

The group intends to hold a party celebrating Kirino’s return.

Saori is delayed by an unexplained obligation and behaves with unusual urgency about joining them later. Her anxiety reveals that she sees the gathering as more fragile than the others do.

For Kyousuke, Kirino, and Kuroneko, postponing a party might be inconvenient.

For Saori, a missed gathering can feel like the beginning of dissolution.

Her reaction makes sense once her history is revealed.

## Saori has been the group’s infrastructure

Since Volume 1, Saori has:

-   created the online community;

-   arranged the first offline meeting;

-   reduced a large gathering into a smaller viable group;

-   mediated Kirino and Kuroneko’s conflict;

-   organized Comiket;

-   arranged celebrations;

-   secured venues;

-   provided technology;

-   offered contacts;

-   and repaired social breakdowns.


Because she performs this work with comic confidence, the others have often treated it as effortless.

Volume 6 reveals the fear beneath the competence:

> Saori keeps groups functioning because she knows that groups disappear.

---

# 10\. The journey to Saori’s home

Using an old package address, Kyousuke, Kirino, and Kuroneko travel unannounced to Saori’s residence.

They discover an environment radically different from “Saori Bajeena”:

-   an affluent coastal home;

-   substantial family resources;

-   inherited collections;

-   large hobby spaces;

-   and access to networks that explain some of her extraordinary competence.


Her class privilege matters.

Saori’s ability to:

-   obtain rare goods;

-   know creators;

-   arrange spaces;

-   supply equipment;

-   and navigate the industry


does not emerge only from personal charisma. It is supported by family wealth and inherited social capital.

The novel does not condemn this. It contextualizes her role.

## Privilege does not prevent loneliness

Saori possesses:

-   objects;

-   space;

-   money;

-   and connections.


What she lacks is assurance that people will remain.

The huge collection is therefore both abundance and memorial.

It contains traces of an earlier social world that dissolved.

Material permanence contrasts with relational impermanence:

-   the figures remain;

-   the models remain;

-   the rooms remain;

-   but the people who animated them have left.


Saori’s fear is not that her hobbies will be confiscated, as Kirino feared.

It is that she will be left alone with them.

---

# 11\. Saori performs her older sister

When the group arrives, Saori initially claims to be her own older sister.

She adopts:

-   a different voice;

-   different posture;

-   military clothing;

-   sunglasses;

-   and a socially controlled demeanor.


The disguise is improvised, but not arbitrary.

Her older sister is one of the foundational figures in Saori’s understanding of otaku community.

By imitating her, Saori retreats into the model she used when constructing herself.

## The earthquake strips away control

An earthquake disrupts the performance.

Saori becomes preoccupied with protecting the collection. Her sunglasses come off, and the others see a shy, hesitant girl who has difficulty speaking without the practiced armor of “Saori Bajeena.”

The scene could easily have been framed as:

> At last, the real Saori is exposed.

The novel refuses that simplification.

Her friends do not say:

-   this shy girl is real;

-   the otaku caricature was fake;

-   discard the persona and become authentic.


Instead, Kyousuke’s response is:

> **「どんな〝キャラ〟を演じていようが、おまえはおまえだろ」**
> Whatever character you perform, you are still you.

This is the Volume 1 and 2 identity thesis applied to performance itself.

---

# 12\. “Saori Bajeena” is an authored self

Saori’s usual persona includes:

-   exaggerated male-coded otaku speech;

-   archaic endings;

-   conspicuous glasses;

-   deliberately unfashionable clothing;

-   and broad comic physicality.


It initially looked like concealment.

Volume 6 reveals that it is also creation.

Saori designed this self to be:

-   approachable;

-   entertaining;

-   memorable;

-   emotionally durable;

-   and capable of holding a group together.


The persona is not simply a wall between Saori and intimacy.

It is the tool through which she created intimacy.

## Kirino’s perceptive response

Kirino points out that Saori chose to be that person around them and consistently followed through.

That matters more to Kirino than deciding which face is ontologically “real.”

Saori Bajeena:

-   invited her;

-   gave her friends;

-   accompanied her to Comiket;

-   and maintained the group.


Those acts are real.

Therefore the persona through which they were performed cannot be dismissed as meaningless deception.

Kirino understands this because her own public fashionable self is also:

-   cultivated;

-   effortful;

-   socially responsive;

-   and authentic despite being performed.


---

# 13\. Saori’s uncostumed introduction

Eventually Saori appears in her ordinary school presentation and introduces herself as:

> **槇島沙織**

This is a moment of intimacy, but not the revelation of a final essence.

The group now gains access to another Saori:

-   affluent;

-   elegant;

-   shy;

-   conventionally beautiful;

-   and socially uncertain without deliberate comic distortion.


Her different selves can be understood as a relational repertoire:

No one version invalidates the others.

The question is not “Which Saori is real?”

It is “What fear, desire, and relationship does each Saori make manageable?”

---

# 14\. The older group and Saori’s abandonment wound

Saori explains that her older sister once belonged to a lively otaku circle.

The group created the kind of world Saori later attempted to reproduce:

-   gatherings;

-   shared collections;

-   arguments;

-   creative enthusiasm;

-   and a stable social center.


Then its members dispersed.

People:

-   entered relationships;

-   pursued education elsewhere;

-   changed priorities;

-   moved away;

-   or lost access to the shared venue.


Her older sister also left.

From Saori’s perspective, the group had been organized around someone else. Once that central person departed, the structure collapsed.

## Saori’s answer was to become the center

She created her own online community and the “Saori Bajeena” persona partly to prevent the same outcome.

Her implicit logic was:

-   if she organizes every meeting;

-   keeps everyone entertained;

-   never abandons the group;

-   and remains useful as its center,


then perhaps nobody will leave.

This gives her community-building a painful similarity to Kyousuke’s rescuer identity.

Both characters make themselves indispensable because indispensability feels safer than simply asking to be loved.

## Saori’s fear in Volume 5 becomes legible

When Kirino disappeared, Saori did not experience only the loss of one friend.

She saw the beginning of a familiar sequence:

1.  one member leaves;

2.  the group’s rhythm breaks;

3.  remaining members drift;

4.  the shared world becomes only a collection of memories.


Her increased visits to Kyousuke and insistence on future gatherings were attempts to prevent repetition.

---

# 15\. The group’s response

Kirino, Kuroneko, and Kyousuke do not promise that nothing will ever change.

Such a promise would be impossible.

They instead affirm that Saori’s different identities and complicated history do not disqualify her from the friendship she created.

Kirino’s statement is especially direct:

> She will not give up her hobbies, and she will not give up being Saori’s friend.

Kuroneko expresses the same through her usual language of curses.

Kyousuke says, in his own blunt manner, that he likes Saori and intends to remain nearby.

The effect is not to cure Saori’s abandonment anxiety instantly.

It gives her evidence that the group no longer exists only because she performs constant maintenance.

The others can choose it too.

---

# 16\. Cosplay as collaborative self-authorship

The group eventually uses Saori’s collection for cosplay.

The scene serves commercial fanservice, but it also extends the volume’s argument about performed identity.

Kyousuke discovers pleasure in:

-   changing appearance;

-   inhabiting another role;

-   being admired;

-   and participating in a shared fiction.


Kuroneko, normally the most theatrically dressed, becomes embarrassed when placed in a costume whose meaning she does not control.

Kirino wears a character from a work she habitually disparages.

Saori moves between her various identities with greater openness.

## Shared embarrassment becomes memory

Cosplay allows them to create an experience that is:

-   artificial;

-   knowingly theatrical;

-   visually exaggerated;

-   and emotionally genuine.


The costumes are not their everyday selves.

The pleasure and intimacy produced through them are real.

This echoes the volume’s treatment of Kanako:

> A performed identity can generate authentic emotion in performer, audience, and companions without requiring the performance to be a transparent autobiographical statement.

## Otaku creativity is identity experimentation

Earlier volumes treated fandom as a way to disclose hidden tastes.

Volume 6 shows it as a way to test possible selves.

Through costume and role, characters can temporarily become:

-   more confident;

-   more beautiful;

-   more ridiculous;

-   more masculine or feminine;

-   more heroic;

-   or more exposed.


The value lies not in discovering a single true self beneath the costume, but in expanding the self’s available forms.

---

# 17\. The album and the irreversibility of intimacy

Kyousuke asks Kirino to show him the album she attempted to reveal before leaving for America.

She refuses:

> **「あんたには、もう、絶対見せない」**

The contents remain undisclosed. We should not assume precisely what the album contains.

What matters is the relational consequence.

In Volume 4:

-   Kirino opened a vulnerable door.

-   Kyousuke misread it as another sexual or otaku provocation.

-   He declined.

-   She left shortly afterward.


Now he wants to reverse the decision, but the opportunity has passed.

## One save file

Kyousuke compares life to a game with only one save file.

There is no reset that permits him to select the better dialogue option.

This is one of the novels’ most meaningful uses of game logic because it identifies the limit of route fantasy.

Eroge encourages the belief that:

-   choices can be replayed;

-   affection flags can be optimized;

-   and every heroine’s hidden scene can eventually be unlocked.


Human intimacy does not guarantee renewed access.

Kirino’s refusal gives her control over what Kyousuke does not know.

Even after everything he has done, he is not entitled to every part of her.

---

# 18\. Lia as an alternate model of identity

Lia Hagry arrives as Kirino’s younger American rival.

She is:

-   extraordinarily talented;

-   physically gifted;

-   emotionally direct;

-   socially uninhibited;

-   and almost monomaniacally devoted to running.


Her presence allows the novel to compare two kinds of exceptional person.

## Lia’s concentrated identity

Lia’s life is organized around one central activity.

Running provides:

-   purpose;

-   identity;

-   pleasure;

-   competition;

-   and future direction.


She does not need the same quantity of social or subcultural attachments that Kirino does.

This gives her a kind of purity.

She can move through the world without constantly negotiating multiple selves.

## Kirino treats her as an actual younger sister

Kirino’s relationship with Lia differs sharply from her consumption of fictional little sisters.

With Lia, Kirino:

-   disciplines;

-   argues;

-   worries;

-   teaches;

-   competes;

-   and manages responsibility.


She does not simply adore Lia as cute.

The relationship demonstrates that Kirino’s imōto-moe and her real treatment of younger girls operate differently.

Her fictional tastes do not automatically determine her conduct.

This continues the novels’ distinction between fantasy and action.

---

# 19\. Kirino’s integrated-self declaration

Kirino takes Lia into Akihabara not merely to show her a tourist attraction.

She wants Lia to understand why she returned.

Her explanation is one of the most important statements in the first half of the series:

> In America, the person she became was not fully herself.
> She returned to the place where she could be her real self.
> Athletics, friends, family, school, and hobbies all have to coexist before she is wholly Kirino.

Her conclusion is:

> **「部活も、友達も、家族も、趣味も――全部があって、初めてあたしだから」**
> Only with athletics, friends, family, and hobbies together does she become fully herself.

This revises the meaning of her retreat from America.

She did not simply choose comfort over ambition.

She rejected an identity structure that demanded:

> Athlete first; everything else suspended until victory.

## The return is not surrender

Kirino tells Lia that she intends to defeat the international athletes as the person she actually is.

She will not win by amputating:

-   friendship;

-   fandom;

-   family;

-   creativity;

-   or ordinary adolescence.


Her challenge becomes harder:

> **「あたしはこのあたしのままで、あんたたちに勝ってみせる」**
> I will beat you while remaining exactly this version of myself.

The declaration connects Volume 6 directly to Volumes 1 and 2.

The original problem was whether Kirino could preserve both respectable success and embarrassing taste.

Now the question is whether the integrated self can compete against someone whose life is more narrowly optimized.

---

# 20\. The series does not declare Lia wrong

Lia’s concentrated life is not portrayed simply as unhealthy.

She:

-   loves running;

-   possesses extraordinary ability;

-   accepts the work required;

-   and does not appear internally divided by the choice.


Unlike Kirino in America, Lia is not suppressing a necessary half of herself.

The contrast is therefore not:

-   balanced life good;

-   specialized ambition bad.


It is:

-   Kirino requires multiplicity.

-   Lia may flourish through concentration.


A life that is liberating for one person can deform another.

This is a sophisticated extension of the novels’ anti-normalizing argument. There is no universal quantity of friendship, ambition, work, or hobby that produces an authentic life.

---

# 21\. The race denies Kirino a miracle

Kirino and Lia race again.

The event is narratively positioned for a triumphant home-ground victory:

-   Kirino has rediscovered herself.

-   Kyousuke is present.

-   her friends and hobbies have restored her.

-   the emotional stakes are high.


Kirino still loses.

This is important.

The volume refuses to claim that psychological integration magically defeats superior talent and more concentrated training.

Kirino’s restored identity gives her:

-   motivation;

-   speed;

-   emotional power;

-   and a reason to continue.


It does not guarantee victory.

Her response is characteristic:

-   fury;

-   humiliation;

-   refusal to accept final defeat;

-   and renewed commitment to revenge.


The loss therefore validates the integrated-self thesis without turning it into sentimental wish fulfillment.

---

# 22\. Kyousuke sees Kirino as an alternate self

Watching the race, Kyousuke thinks of Kirino as a version of himself produced by a different choice.

Both siblings encountered a younger person who surpassed them.

Kyousuke responded by:

-   withdrawing;

-   redefining himself as ordinary;

-   minimizing ambition;

-   and treating competition as beneath him.


Kirino responded by:

-   becoming angry;

-   training;

-   accumulating achievements;

-   and refusing to accept inferiority.


She represents the path he did not take.

This does not mean he should become Kirino. Her intensity can become self-destructive.

But the comparison confirms that his “ordinary” identity was partly a response to wounded pride, not only temperament.

## “I’m not a lolicon—I’m a siscon”

Kyousuke’s shouted encouragement converts this recognition into comedy:

> **「俺はロリコンじゃねえっ！　シスコンだぁぁ……！」**

The line publicly names the attachment he has repeatedly denied.

Within the immediate context, **シスコン** means:

-   he supports his actual sister rather than Lia;

-   he identifies with Kirino’s struggle;

-   and his loyalty overrides detached admiration for extraordinary talent.


But the franchise also uses the phrase to intensify the incest-adjacent comic frame.

As throughout Volume 6, sincere family attachment and provocative romantic coding occupy the same sentence.

---

# 23\. What made Kirino faster in America?

Lia reveals that her real purpose in Japan is not merely revenge.

She wants to understand why Kirino suddenly improved enough to defeat her once.

In America, Kirino had been:

-   isolated;

-   deprived of her ordinary supports;

-   ashamed of losing;

-   and increasingly trapped by her own rule.


After Kyousuke arrived, they played an eroge together and spoke honestly. Kirino then recovered enough to produce her breakthrough performance.

Lia concludes that Kyousuke caused the change because Kirino loves him.

Kyousuke attempts to broaden the explanation:

-   the game mattered;

-   friends mattered;

-   family mattered;

-   and returning to herself mattered.


His interpretation is more complete, but Lia’s is not simply wrong.

Kyousuke’s arrival acted as the catalyst that restored all the missing connections symbolically.

He represented:

-   home;

-   fandom;

-   family;

-   acceptance;

-   and permission to stop proving that she required nobody.


Thus the siblings’ relationship is privileged not because Kyousuke is literally Kirino’s only source of strength, but because he can stand in for the integrated world she left behind.

---

# 24\. Family explanation and romantic framing intensify together

Volume 6 makes two apparently opposing moves.

## It strengthens the familial explanation

The Akagi conversation roots Kyousuke’s care in:

-   Kirino’s birth;

-   lifelong habit;

-   embodied protection;

-   and the specific continuity of family.


His statement that Kirino is important family is explicit and sincere.

Kirino’s integrated-self speech also places **家族** alongside friends, hobbies, and athletics rather than naming Kyousuke as a romantic partner.

## It strengthens romantic ambiguity

At the same time:

-   Kyousuke asks how much Kirino loves him.

-   He compares that amount to Kuroneko’s feelings.

-   He is dissatisfied that Kirino appears unchanged.

-   He dislikes being only one among many people welcoming her home.

-   Lia interprets Kirino’s performance through love for Kyousuke.

-   Kyousuke publicly calls himself a siscon.

-   The volume closes with Kirino asking him to become her boyfriend.


The novel is not replacing sibling love with romance at this point.

It is deliberately constructing a relationship whose familial and romantic readings increasingly overlap in emotional structure while remaining distinct in explicit consciousness.

---

# 25\. The end of the consultation was only nominal

After Lia leaves, Kirino tells Kyousuke she has a request.

Kyousuke becomes pleased because the familiar **人生相談** ritual appears to have returned.

Kirino corrects him:

-   the life consultations ended;

-   this is only an **お願い**.


Kyousuke recognizes that the distinction is mostly nominal.

The original consultations allowed them to interact without admitting that they wanted to interact. Now the same relationship persists under a different label.

He thinks that their quarrelsome everyday life may continue indefinitely and finds himself wishing that it will.

This answers the question raised at the end of Volume 4:

> Can the siblings sustain a relationship after crisis stops being its official excuse?

Yes—but they still require indirect naming.

## The boyfriend request

Kirino then says:

> **「あんた、あたしの彼氏になってよ」**

At the cliffhanger, the exact practical context is withheld.

The line should not yet be treated as an unambiguous romantic confession. The novel clearly intends the next volume to explain the request.

But as a structural endpoint, it is decisive.

The vocabulary has progressed:

1.  **人生相談** — life consultation

2.  **お願い** — request/favor

3.  **彼氏** — boyfriend


The series is converting a sibling-help ritual into the language of romance.

Whether that language is:

-   performative;

-   instrumental;

-   deceptive;

-   exploratory;

-   or sincere


remains for the next volume.

The genre transition itself is already real.

# 26\. Japanese-language observations

## **大切な家族**

Kyousuke’s phrase does not mean that he has stopped disliking Kirino.

**大切** conveys that she is precious, important, and not disposable.

The construction allows:

-   dislike at the level of personality;

-   and unconditional commitment at the level of relationship.


This distinction is one of the series’ clearest formulations of family love.

## **習慣**

Akagi’s **習慣** demystifies sibling devotion.

It is not necessarily:

-   admiration;

-   agreement;

-   or constant warmth.


It is the repeated practice of responding to a specific person over time until care becomes embodied expectation.

## **キャラ**

In Saori’s arc, **キャラ** means more than fictional character.

It includes:

-   persona;

-   social role;

-   performed register;

-   visual identity;

-   and the version of oneself presented to a particular group.


The volume rejects the assumption that a constructed **キャラ** is automatically false.

## **素**

**素** refers to an unguarded or unperformed state, but the novel avoids treating it as the only authentic one.

Saori’s shy state reveals information concealed by her usual persona. It does not invalidate the friendships formed through that persona.

## **正体**

The group’s discovery of Saori’s identity resembles a revelation of **正体**, but the emotional resolution resists the idea that one hidden identity explains everything.

Her “true identity” is plural.

## **おかえり / ただいま**

These paired expressions continue to function as more than routine greetings.

They mark:

-   re-entry into community;

-   acknowledgment that one belongs somewhere;

-   and reciprocal acceptance between the returning person and those who waited.


Kirino receives several distinct homecomings because she belongs to several relational worlds.

## **人生相談 / お願い**

The difference is less functional than emotional.

**人生相談** carried the history of vulnerability and crisis.

**お願い** attempts to establish a less dramatic, more ordinary way of requesting Kyousuke’s involvement.

The renamed ritual indicates continuity with changed self-awareness.

## **全部があって、初めてあたし**

Kirino’s syntax accumulates the elements before reaching the conclusion:

> only when all are present does “I” become complete.

The phrase rejects both:

-   social compartmentalization;

-   and monomaniacal purification.


## Forms of sibling address

The volume continues to distinguish relationships through address:

-   Kirino’s **兄貴**: rough, intimate, resistant to sentimentality.

-   Lia’s **おにいちゃん**: cute, immediate, and easily romanticized.

-   Kuroneko’s **先輩**: socially direct and route-compatible.

-   Akagi and Sena’s sibling language: comparatively open and functional.


These terms do not merely identify age or family position. They define emotional scripts.

# 27\. Illustration analysis

The illustrations reinforce the volume’s concern with performance and identity.

## Kirino after return

Early images present Kirino as visually restored:

-   stylish;

-   confident;

-   socially central;

-   and apparently unchanged.


This supports Kyousuke’s frustration. The vulnerability of America is no longer visible on the surface.

## Ayase’s reunion

The embrace between Ayase and Kirino visually resembles romantic reunion imagery.

Its emotional content is friendship, but the publication packages intense female attachment through the same visual vocabulary used for heroine intimacy.

## Kanako and Bridget

The event illustrations emphasize the contrast between:

-   backstage vulgarity;

-   polished stage presentation;

-   and the visual appeal of costumed character performance.


The reader receives both the “real” crude Kanako and the idealized Meruru embodiment, reinforcing the volume’s argument that neither cancels the other.

## Saori’s transformations

The Saori material depends heavily on visual contrast:

-   exaggerated Bajeena appearance;

-   military disguise;

-   elegant uncostumed appearance;

-   school identity;

-   and cosplay.


The illustrations make identity multiplicity visible rather than merely described.

They also participate in the heroine-marketing mechanism: Saori’s newly revealed beauty turns psychological exposure into a franchise surprise.

## Cosplay group images

The costume material packages the cast as collectible alternative versions of themselves.

Thematically, this supports identity experimentation.

Commercially, it converts character development into variant design appeal.

## Lia and the race

Lia’s smaller body and youthful appearance make her extraordinary athletic threat visually paradoxical. Kirino’s defeat is not framed as humiliation by a physically imposing rival but as confrontation with concentrated natural talent.

## Final illustration

The image accompanying **「あんた、あたしの彼氏になってよ」** transforms the request into a heroine-confession cliffhanger before its practical meaning is known.

The visual framing deliberately outruns the available psychological evidence.

# 28\. Character baselines after Volume 6

## Kyousuke

Kyousuke now openly accepts that Kirino is precious family.

He also demonstrates:

-   desire for her attention;

-   resentment of being one relationship among many;

-   romantic curiosity about Kuroneko;

-   increased comfort inside otaku culture;

-   and an emerging awareness that his care for Kirino is rooted in lifelong bodily habit.


His need is shifting from “I must solve the crisis” toward:

> I must continue occupying a meaningful place in these people’s lives.

## Kirino

Kirino returns with the clearest account yet of her integrated identity.

She rejects the demand to become only:

-   athlete;

-   model;

-   otaku;

-   friend;

-   or sister.


Her strength comes from combination.

At the same time, she remains:

-   emotionally withholding toward Kyousuke;

-   possessive;

-   highly competitive;

-   and unwilling to reopen every vulnerable invitation after he misses it.


## Saori

Saori becomes one of the series’ richest characters.

Her community-building is revealed as:

-   genuine care;

-   creative social authorship;

-   use of class resources;

-   imitation of an admired older sister;

-   and defense against abandonment.


“Saori Bajeena” is not a lie. It is the self she made in order to create the relationships she wanted.

## Kuroneko

Kuroneko remains romantically and emotionally important even though Volume 6 delays direct resolution of the kiss.

She:

-   notices Saori’s vulnerability;

-   helps make disclosure safer;

-   retains her independent creative world;

-   and occupies Kyousuke’s thoughts as a possible romantic partner.


The unresolved kiss has become Kyousuke’s problem rather than merely Kuroneko’s action.

## Manami

Manami again interprets Kyousuke and Kirino accurately.

She recognizes Kyousuke’s disappointed hope for intimacy and reassures him without denying the complexity.

Yet Kyousuke’s refusal to discuss Kuroneko with her reveals that Manami is no longer outside the romantic structure.

## Ayase

Ayase is now both:

-   Kirino’s openly loving friend;

-   and a person who trusts Kyousuke with practical responsibility.


Her relationship with Kyousuke continues developing through hostile comedy, consultation, and reluctant reliance.

## Kanako

Kanako’s professionalism becomes undeniable.

She remains crude and coercive but demonstrates:

-   preparation;

-   courage;

-   stage intelligence;

-   audience awareness;

-   and abrasive care for Bridget.


## Lia

Lia represents a coherent alternative to Kirino’s multiplicity.

She is not merely a prodigy villain. She is a person whose concentrated life may genuinely suit her.

Her interest in Kirino reveals that even extraordinary talent seeks to understand the emotional source of another competitor’s breakthrough.

# 29\. Hypothesis status after Volume 6

### Hypothesis 1

**Characters perform acceptable, embarrassing, or theatrical identities in order to disclose desire.**

**Decisively strengthened.**

Saori’s entire arc demonstrates that a performed identity may be the most effective vehicle for sincere relational desire.

Kanako similarly shows that professional performance can be authentic as labor without being autobiographically sincere.

### Hypothesis 2

**Otaku culture is the first confessional space.**

**Further expanded.**

Otaku culture now provides:

-   persona construction;

-   inherited community memory;

-   cosplay experimentation;

-   professional obligation;

-   and the symbolic environment through which Kirino explains her integrated self.


### Hypothesis 3

**Kyousuke’s normality is defensive, and he needs to be needed.**

**Strengthened but refined.**

Volume 6 emphasizes not merely usefulness but positional security.

Kyousuke wants to know:

-   whether Kirino values him;

-   whether Kuroneko likes him;

-   and whether he retains a distinctive place after Kirino’s broader social restoration.


His deeper need may be less “I must be indispensable” than:

> I need evidence that I am not emotionally replaceable.

### Hypothesis 4

**Kirino wants one person who accepts every compartment of her identity.**

**Strongly supported but still nonexclusive.**

Kyousuke remains uniquely associated with:

-   acceptance in failure;

-   preservation of the collection;

-   family continuity;

-   and restoration of her integrated self in America.


But Kirino explicitly names friends, family, hobbies, and athletics together. Her ideal is a networked life, not a single exclusive relationship.

### Hypothesis 5

**Shared secrecy gives the siblings privileged access no outsider can reproduce.**

**Strengthened.**

Kyousuke understands the symbolic meaning of Kirino’s collection and America experience. Lia identifies him as the catalyst for her recovery.

Yet the unopened album demonstrates the limit of his access. Privilege is not total possession.

### Hypothesis 6

**The principal heroines represent meaningful alternative intimacies.**

**Strengthened.**

-   Kuroneko is now an explicit object of Kyousuke’s romantic curiosity.

-   Manami remains the trusted ordinary interpreter but is increasingly taken for granted.

-   Ayase continues developing a combative trust relationship.

-   Kirino’s bond remains family-rooted while becoming increasingly central and romantically framed.


### Hypothesis 7

**The ending may grant impossible desire temporary reality while preserving social structure.**

Still not directly testable, but Volume 6 sharply advances the mechanism.

The volume:

-   grounds Kyousuke’s attachment in family habit;

-   names him a siscon;

-   lets Lia describe Kirino’s love;

-   and ends with the word **彼氏**.


The series is now consciously translating sibling devotion into romantic-route vocabulary.

# 30\. Critical assessment

Volume 6 is less narratively concentrated than Volumes 3 or 5. Its multiple episodes can initially feel like post-return comedy and franchise ensemble maintenance.

Its deeper unity lies in the question of performed identity.

The strongest material is Saori’s arc. It substantially reinterprets a character who could easily have remained a functional comic mediator.

Her history explains:

-   why she speaks as she does;

-   why she organizes constantly;

-   why Kirino’s departure wounded her so deeply;

-   how class privilege supports her social role;

-   and why she fears the disappearance of groups more than exposure of taste.


The volume’s treatment of Kanako is also effective. It refuses the equation:

> unpleasant private personality = fraudulent public performance.

Kanako’s professionalism is real because labor and responsibility are real.

Kirino’s integrated-self declaration is the major longitudinal development. It clarifies that her return from America was not a rejection of ambition but a rejection of self-amputation.

The principal weakness is tonal.

The volume repeatedly turns:

-   underage sexuality;

-   smoking;

-   coercion;

-   accidental nudity;

-   and romanticized sibling situations


into broad comedy. The thematic arguments often survive, but the jokes can obscure the seriousness of the issues being raised.

The final cliffhanger is also openly commercial. It converts a volume concerned with plural identity, community, and athletic self-definition into a high-impact “sister asks brother to be boyfriend” hook.

That does not invalidate the preceding analysis. It demonstrates how aggressively the franchise places literary character development inside heroine-route provocation.

---

# Volume 6 verdict

Volume 6 confirms that Kirino’s return cannot reset the series.

During her absence:

-   Kyousuke developed relationships of his own.

-   Kuroneko became romantically important.

-   Saori’s community became something all its members had to choose.

-   Kyousuke learned that he could be happy when others grew without him.


After Kirino returns, he discovers that expansion has not reduced her emotional importance.

Instead, it produces a more difficult world in which:

-   he needs Kirino;

-   he desires clarity from Kuroneko;

-   Manami is no longer a neutral confidante;

-   Ayase trusts him;

-   and Saori’s group can survive only if everyone contributes to its continuity.


The volume’s most important character is arguably Saori. Her revelation provides the fullest articulation yet of a principle already present in Kirino and Kuroneko:

> **A constructed self can be authentic when it is the form through which a person creates something they sincerely need.**

Kirino applies the same principle to life as a whole.

She does not need to choose between:

-   ambition and friendship;

-   public success and private fandom;

-   athletic discipline and emotional support;

-   family and independence.


She intends to win without becoming less multiple.

Kyousuke, meanwhile, reaches a familial understanding of his own attachment:

> He protects Kirino partly because responding to her has become a lifelong habit written into his identity.

That explanation is deeply non-romantic.

Yet the volume ends by placing that habit inside the word **彼氏**.

The central contradiction after Volume 6 is therefore sharper than ever:

> **The more convincingly the novels explain Kyousuke and Kirino’s bond as particular, embodied sibling love, the more deliberately the franchise frames that same bond through romantic exclusivity and heroine-route language.**

Volume 7 must determine what Kirino means by “boyfriend,” but Volume 6 has already made the larger transition clear.

The life consultation has not ended.

It has entered the romance system.

Volume 6 also establishes Saori’s identity and abandonment anxiety as major longitudinal threads rather than supporting-character background.
