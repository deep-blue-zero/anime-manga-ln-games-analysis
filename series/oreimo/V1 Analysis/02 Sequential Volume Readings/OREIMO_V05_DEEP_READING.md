# *Oreimo* Volume 5 Deep Reading

## Substitution, collaboration, and the confession of selfish need

Volume 5 is the first sustained test of whether the world created by Kirino can continue without Kirino physically present.

Its answer is initially encouraging. Her departure does not dissolve the relationships formed through her secret:

-   Kuroneko enters Kyousuke’s school and everyday life.

-   Saori and Kuroneko continue visiting him.

-   Kyousuke joins an otaku creative community independently.

-   Kuroneko develops friendships and ambitions that do not require Kirino.

-   Kyousuke learns that helping someone does not require making himself indispensable.


But the volume then places a limit on this expansion. When Kirino sends a single message asking him to destroy her collection, Kyousuke abandons the new equilibrium and flies to America. There he admits that all his respectable explanations are secondary:

> **「おまえがいないと寂しいんだよ！」**
> “I’m lonely without you!”

Volume 5’s central thesis can therefore be stated as follows:

> **Care becomes healthier when it stops requiring indispensability—but Kyousuke’s relationship with Kirino is already too central to be replaced by healthier relationships elsewhere.**

This does not yet make their bond romantic. It does make it emotionally privileged in a way that the previous volumes had only implied.

---

## 1\. Narrative structure

Volume 5 has three major movements.

### 1\. Life after Kirino

A month after Kirino’s departure, Kyousuke insists that nothing has changed and that he enjoys the peace. Yet he remains preoccupied by her farewell and repeated failure to contact anyone.

Kuroneko enters his school as a first-year student. Saori and Kuroneko continue visiting Kyousuke’s room, explicitly declaring that their friendship does not depend on Kirino’s presence.

### 2\. Kuroneko, Sena, and the game research club

Kuroneko struggles to integrate socially at school. Kyousuke follows her into the game research club, where they meet Akagi Sena, a highly capable programmer who conceals an intense fujoshi identity.

Kuroneko and Sena clash over a game-development contest, separate, and then reconcile when Kuroneko publicly asks Sena to help complete the project. Their collaboration produces 『強欲の迷宮』, which does not win but attracts attention and establishes a real creative partnership.

### 3\. The message from America

Kirino sends Kyousuke one short message:

> **「アンタに預けたあたしのコレクション　ぜんぶ捨てて」**

Destroy the entire collection I entrusted to you.

Kuroneko forces Kyousuke to acknowledge the obvious crisis and models the direct emotional honesty he lacks. She kisses his cheek as a “curse” and sends him to Kirino.

Kyousuke travels to America, discovers that Kirino has isolated herself through a punishing self-imposed rule, and asks her to return—not merely for her friends or health, but because he personally cannot tolerate her absence.

# 2\. Kyousuke’s denial has become transparent

The volume opens with Kyousuke claiming that Kirino’s departure has had no meaningful effect on his life.

He lists practical advantages:

-   she no longer occupies the living room;

-   her friends no longer displace him;

-   he need not moderate the noise from his room;

-   and he is no longer pulled into absurd crises.


Yet his narration repeatedly returns to:

> **「じゃあね、兄貴」**

He is still angry that she left without telling him. His insistence that he is relieved is therefore not simply false, but incomplete.

He genuinely enjoys:

-   quiet;

-   autonomy;

-   fewer demands;

-   and reduced conflict.


He also feels:

-   abandoned;

-   excluded;

-   worried;

-   and deprived of the relational role the consultations gave him.


As in earlier volumes, Kyousuke is most misleading when he converts mixed feelings into categorical declarations.

He is not wrong that life is easier without Kirino.

He is wrong that easier means unchanged.

---

# 3\. Kirino’s world survives her absence

The opening provides one of the clearest confirmations of Volume 4’s revised concept of normality.

Kyousuke’s present everyday life includes:

-   an eroge in his room;

-   a computer supplied by Saori;

-   a gothic-lolita junior;

-   online friends;

-   dōjin culture;

-   and plans for collaborative game creation.


He observes that what once seemed extraordinary has become ordinary through repetition.

This is the maturation of his philosophy of **普通**.

Earlier, normality meant:

> a life protected from strange people and intense commitments.

Now it means:

> whatever relationships and practices have become part of one’s lived continuity.

The content has changed; the sense of everyday grounding remains.

Kirino altered Kyousuke’s life so thoroughly that her influence no longer depends on her immediate presence.

---

# 4\. Saori and Kuroneko refuse to be inherited accessories

Saori tells Kyousuke:

> **「たとえきりりん氏がいなくとも、我らの友情に変わりはござらん。」**

Their friendship remains even without Kirino.

This statement is structurally important. It prevents the ensemble from being reduced to “Kirino’s friends whom Kyousuke temporarily borrows.”

Saori and Kuroneko have become Kyousuke’s own friends.

Kyousuke responds internally that he loves them. His phrasing is casual and non-romantic, but emotionally direct. He has developed attachments he could not have imagined before discovering the Meruru case.

## Kirino’s disappearance hurts them independently

Kuroneko pretends that online friendships are disposable and that she had already grown tired of Kirino. Her language transparently reproduces Kirino’s own defensive hostility.

Saori responds very differently. She admits that she is angry, hurt, lonely, and unable to reconcile Kirino’s decision intellectually with her feelings.

She says, in effect:

-   she considered Kirino a friend;

-   she believed Kirino reciprocated;

-   being excluded from the decision feels like betrayal;

-   and she cannot accept that people keep leaving her.


This is the first substantial crack in Saori’s permanent comic cheerfulness.

Her remark that **everyone leaves on their own** suggests a prior abandonment wound without fully explaining it. The volume does not yet reveal the underlying history, but it establishes that her community-building is not merely generous extroversion.

Saori constructs groups because she fears their dissolution.

---

# 5\. Kuroneko becomes part of Kyousuke’s real-world identity

Kuroneko’s entrance into Kyousuke’s school changes the meaning of their relationship.

Previously, she existed primarily in:

-   Akihabara;

-   online spaces;

-   Kirino’s friendship group;

-   Kyousuke’s home;

-   and special creative or crisis situations.


At school, she becomes integrated into the most ordinary institutional structure of his life.

## From **兄さん** to **先輩**

Kuroneko changes her address from **兄さん** to **先輩**.

The shift matters.

**兄さん** linked her to Kyousuke through Kirino and through their earlier false-family performance. It also carried teasing pseudo-intimacy.

**先輩** creates a direct social relationship:

-   senior and junior;

-   same school;

-   visible everyday hierarchy;

-   and a plausible non-familial path toward romance.


Kyousuke is openly pleased that she attends his school. He tells her so without the usual layers of denial.

He even observes that he is unusually straightforward with Kuroneko because she is so indirect that he instinctively compensates.

This suggests a potentially reciprocal structure:

> Kuroneko’s guardedness invites Kyousuke’s honesty rather than allowing him to hide inside his usual irony.

---

# 6\. The real name: from “Kuroneko” to Gokō Ruri

Kyousuke’s acquisition of Kuroneko’s real name, **五更瑠璃**, is symbolically important.

Online relationships in the previous volumes depended on selective anonymity:

-   handles;

-   performed voices;

-   chosen costumes;

-   and deliberate separation from school and family identities.


Kuroneko’s entrance into Kyousuke’s school makes that division harder to maintain.

She explains that he may continue using “Kuroneko” outside school, but her human name now becomes available to him.

Her phrasing—

> **「それが人間としての私の名前」**

—preserves the theatrical persona while acknowledging the ordinary person beneath it.

The two names do not cancel one another.

As with Kirino’s identities, both are real:

-   Kuroneko is the authored aesthetic self.

-   Ruri is the school, household, and civil identity.

-   Intimacy means gaining access to both without insisting that one invalidate the other.


---

# 7\. Manami and Kuroneko meet as alternative worlds

Kyousuke introduces Kuroneko to Manami because he wants two important people in his life to coexist.

Kuroneko reacts to Manami as though she has met the demon **Belphegor**, a figure in her manga clearly modeled on Manami. This shows that Kirino has already narrated Manami to Kuroneko as a threatening figure.

Manami, by contrast:

-   remains warm despite Kuroneko’s hostility;

-   covers socially for her;

-   understands indirect gratitude;

-   and does not pressure her into immediate intimacy.


The meeting reveals that Manami’s apparent simplicity contains substantial social intelligence.

She understands:

-   when to intervene;

-   when not to insist;

-   how to soften another person’s refusal;

-   and how to protect Kuroneko’s reputation with classmates.


## Why Kuroneko distrusts Manami

Part of her hostility comes from loyalty to Kirino. But Manami also represents something structurally threatening:

-   a person who already occupies Kyousuke’s ordinary life;

-   knows his gestures without explanation;

-   communicates with him through habits developed over years;

-   and provides a socially legitimate intimacy Kuroneko lacks.


Kuroneko sees the ease between them before Kyousuke fully recognizes its significance.

The conflict is therefore not merely inherited from Kirino. Kuroneko has her own reason to feel displaced by Manami.

---

# 8\. Kuroneko’s social difficulty is genuine

At school, Kuroneko:

-   refuses invitations abruptly;

-   avoids classmates;

-   does not explain herself;

-   appears ungrateful when others help;

-   and retreats into an intimidating persona.


The volume does not frame her isolation solely as persecution by conventional people.

Her classmates initially make sincere efforts to include her. She repeatedly pushes them away until they begin treating her less generously.

This is an important qualification to the series’ defense of unconventional identity.

> Being authentic does not exempt a person from the interpersonal consequences of behaving contemptuously.

Kuroneko’s theatricality protects her from rejection by allowing her to reject first. But it also helps produce the isolation she fears.

Her problem is not that society refuses every unusual person. It is that she lacks the communicative tools required to let ordinary goodwill reach her.

# 9\. “I am not a substitute for your sister”

Kuroneko gives Kyousuke the sharpest psychological diagnosis anyone has offered him:

> **「私はあなたの妹の代用品ではないわ。莫迦にしないで頂戴」**
> “I am not a substitute for your sister. Don’t insult me.” ÄåüEæ}èGòt)(Æ╟ë┴ìZÉ│12-05-24)-8

She argues that Kyousuke is drawn to her because:

-   she is younger;

-   she is female;

-   she appears troubled;

-   she may depend on him;

-   and Kirino is no longer available as the recipient of his care.


Kuroneko does not deny that his concern is sincere. She challenges its origin.

This distinction is crucial:

> A feeling can be genuine while still being projected through an unresolved attachment.

Kyousuke initially resists but then recognizes that she is partly correct. Seeing Kuroneko alone has reactivated the emotional pattern established with Kirino:

-   isolated younger girl;

-   concealed vulnerability;

-   hostility;

-   and the opportunity to become necessary.


He is attracted to the role as much as to the specific problem.

## But substitution is not the whole relationship

Kyousuke’s insight does not lead him to withdraw.

He concludes that:

-   his motive may be unflattering;

-   Kirino’s absence may have intensified it;

-   but his concern for Kuroneko herself remains real.


The mature response is neither:

-   “Kuroneko is completely wrong,”

-   nor

-   “all my care is fraudulent.”


It is:

> My history shapes why I care, but that does not make the person before me interchangeable.

This becomes a central ethical task of Volume 5: Kyousuke must learn to care for Kuroneko without turning her into Kirino’s replacement.

# 10\. The game research club: fandom becomes institution

The game research club extends the series’ account of otaku culture.

Previous volumes explored:

-   hidden consumption;

-   offline friendship;

-   Comiket;

-   professional publishing;

-   and cosplay performance.


Volume 5 depicts a small creative institution.

The club contains:

-   equipment;

-   accumulated knowledge;

-   informal mentorship;

-   technical specialization;

-   older members funding younger creators;

-   internal rules;

-   collaboration;

-   rivalry;

-   and access to amateur competitions.


The clubroom is disorderly and comic, but it has real productive capacity.

Miura’s financial and technical support makes the club a miniature version of Saori’s social function:

> an eccentric older participant creates infrastructure in which younger people can experiment.

Otaku culture is not merely what characters like. It is the set of institutions through which they learn to make things.

---

# 11\. Sena and respectable otaku identity

Akagi Sena initially appears as Kuroneko’s opposite:

-   conventionally attractive;

-   academically capable;

-   orderly;

-   socially appropriate;

-   technologically skilled;

-   and committed to institutional rules.


She offers an important argument:

> Otaku are not necessarily disliked because of their tastes; they are often disliked because some behave without regard for place, audience, hygiene, or boundaries.

This position complicates the series’ earlier anti-stigma arguments.

Sena distinguishes between:

-   private preference;

-   and socially consequential conduct.


Her emphasis is **TPO**—time, place, and occasion.

A person may enjoy unusual fiction, but this does not grant permission to:

-   impose it on unwilling listeners;

-   ignore public norms;

-   expose others to sexual material;

-   or treat specialist knowledge as universally welcome.


This is one of the series’ clearest recognitions that acceptance is not identical to unlimited public expression.

---

# 12\. Sena’s fujoshi identity

Sena’s orderly persona collapses when Kuroneko deliberately insults her preferred pairing in *Maschera*.

She reveals herself as an intense fujoshi with:

-   fixed seme/uke interpretations;

-   extreme emotional investment;

-   specialist terminology;

-   male-character fantasies;

-   and little ability to remain restrained once provoked.


The scene parallels Kirino’s exposure in Volume 1, but with significant differences.

## Kirino

-   is exposed within the family;

-   fears total identity collapse;

-   and gradually chooses disclosure.


## Sena

-   is baited into exposing herself;

-   loses control publicly;

-   and turns the scene into a comic eruption.


The volume uses Sena for broad comedy, but her later explanation contains a serious subcultural ethic.

She describes fujoshi culture as relying upon:

-   coded language;

-   self-mockery;

-   concealment;

-   careful partitioning;

-   and awareness that one person’s excitement may be another’s revulsion.


Her principle is:

> **「あなたの萌えは他人の萎え」**
> Your *moe* may be someone else’s turnoff.

This is an ethic of coexistence rather than universal recognition.

Unlike Kirino, who often wants enthusiastic participation, Sena assumes that many desires cannot and should not be shared indiscriminately.

---

# 13\. The volume’s self-criticism of the fujoshi character

Sena herself criticizes shallow male-authored portrayals of fujoshi as exaggerated, sexually indiscriminate gag characters.

That criticism is conspicuously self-reflexive because Volume 5 frequently uses her in exactly that mode:

-   nosebleed-like enthusiasm;

-   pairing explosions;

-   graphic fantasies;

-   and comedic loss of composure.


The text therefore both:

-   acknowledges the stereotype’s inadequacy;

-   and benefits from its comic marketability.


This is characteristic of *Oreimo*’s relationship to otaku representation. The novels can identify the simplifications of character-consumption culture while continuing to participate in them.

Sena is more than the stereotype, but the book is willing to sell the stereotype before revealing the more thoughtful person.

---

# 14\. The Akagi siblings offer a revealing contrast

Sena’s older brother Kōhei is openly devoted to her.

He:

-   calls her the cutest girl in the world;

-   buys BL games for her;

-   protects her secrets;

-   comforts her after emotional collapse;

-   and accepts her contradictory identities.


Sena calls him disgusting and embarrassing, but she also:

-   relies on him;

-   calls him **お兄ちゃん**;

-   confesses distress to him;

-   and permits him to see her unperformed self.


Their relationship has the ease that Kyousuke and Kirino lack.

Kyousuke is repulsed by Kōhei’s overt sister worship while repeatedly behaving in ways that are at least as extreme.

The irony is deliberate:

-   Kōhei verbalizes affection and appears ridiculous.

-   Kyousuke suppresses affection and therefore experiences it as crisis.


The Akagi siblings show what a relatively functional version of eccentric sibling closeness looks like: embarrassing, excessive, but communicatively accessible.

Kyousuke feels the contrast.

# 15\. Kuroneko and Sena as competing creators

Miura forces Kuroneko and Sena to submit game proposals.

Their works embody two creative philosophies.

## Kuroneko

Her proposal is:

-   dark;

-   personal;

-   idiosyncratic;

-   aesthetically coherent;

-   narratively excessive;

-   and indifferent to broad commercial appeal.


She knows that her tastes may not match market demand.

Her description of the project as an extraordinarily elaborate act of self-indulgence is intentionally vulgar and artistically serious:

> Since she cannot guarantee success by chasing popular taste, she will create the most complete version of what she herself desires.

This continues Volume 3’s defense of amateur freedom.

## Sena

Her proposal initially appears:

-   polished;

-   accessible;

-   systematic;

-   market-conscious;

-   and technically sophisticated.


But the sample contains explicit male–male material modeled on the club’s own members.

Her private desire overwhelms the restrained, commercially sensible identity she claims to maintain.

The irony is that both girls ultimately produce deeply personal work.

Kuroneko admits it.

Sena disguises it as professionalism.

# 16\. Kuroneko’s responsibility: resisting Kyousuke’s rescue reflex

When Kuroneko’s game breaks, Kyousuke instinctively tries to absorb blame.

Kuroneko stops him:

> **「私の責任を――持っていかないで」**
> “Don’t take my responsibility away from me.” ÄåüEæ}èGòt)(Æ╟ë┴ìZÉ│12-05-24)-8

This is one of the most important lines in the volume.

Kyousuke often experiences taking responsibility as love:

-   he claimed Kirino’s eroge;

-   protected her manuscript;

-   mediated her friendships;

-   and solved problems without permission.


Kuroneko identifies the danger.

Taking another person’s blame can also mean taking:

-   authorship;

-   agency;

-   the right to fail;

-   and the right to learn from failure.


She does not want him to rescue her by converting her mistake into his.

This is a healthier form of intimacy than the pattern Kyousuke has developed with Kirino.

Kuroneko wants support without dispossession.

---

# 17\. Intimacy in Kyousuke’s room

As the project develops, Kuroneko spends increasing time alone with Kyousuke in his bedroom.

The scenes contain clear romantic coding:

-   private creative work;

-   physical proximity;

-   teasing over **兄さん** and **先輩**;

-   Kyousuke’s family interpreting her as a girl he has brought home;

-   and Kuroneko directly answering that she likes him before deflecting through Kirino.


When Kyousuke asks whether she likes him, she replies:

> **「好きよ!!　あなたの妹が、あなたのことを好きなくらいには」**

“I do! About as much as your sister likes you.”

The line performs several functions at once:

-   confession;

-   retreat;

-   comparison with Kirino;

-   and reminder that their relationship remains entangled with the absent sister.


Kuroneko can express desire by making Kirino the measuring instrument.

That does not cancel the desire. It shows that she cannot yet state it without triangulation.

---

# 18\. Kuroneko asks for honest criticism

Kuroneko wants Kyousuke’s reactions precisely because he is not an expert.

She values his ability to identify:

-   confusing transitions;

-   inaccessible explanations;

-   pacing problems;

-   and places where her private world fails to reach an outsider.


This develops her as an artist.

She does not abandon her idiosyncratic vision. But she learns that communicating the vision requires attention to the audience.

That is a more mature synthesis than either extreme:

-   creating only for market approval;

-   or treating incomprehensibility as proof of artistic integrity.


Kuroneko remains difficult, but she begins distinguishing:

-   compromise of core meaning;

-   from improvement of communication.


---

# 19\. Asking Sena for help

When the game becomes impossible to finish alone, Kuroneko enters Sena’s classroom and bows publicly:

> **「お願い、私と一緒にゲームを作って」**

For someone defined by pride, theatrical superiority, and fear of humiliation, this is enormous growth.

She does not ask Kyousuke to solve the problem.

She asks the person whose skill she needs and whose feelings she has injured.

## Why she refuses to quit

Kuroneko explains that she learned from the absent friend she claims to hate:

-   choose an ambitious goal;

-   finish what one begins;

-   exert oneself completely;

-   and refuse to let resentment become permanent defeat.


Kirino has become an internalized rival and moral standard.

Kuroneko wants to improve so that when Kirino returns she can face her proudly, insult her, and defeat her.

This is affection expressed through competitive futurity:

> I expect you to return, and I intend to have changed when you do.

## “Companions are remarkable”

Kuroneko admits that she has learned something new:

-   another person can make courage possible;

-   support can make effort bearable;

-   and one ordinary sentence can make unrewarded labor feel meaningful.


Her conclusion is:

> **「仲間がいれば、私はまだまだ頑張れるのよ。」**

With companions, she can keep going.

This is a direct reversal of the isolated creator seen in Volume 3.

---

# 20\. Sena’s answer: collaboration rather than assistance

Sena initially refuses:

> **「手伝いませんよ」**

Then corrects the premise:

> **「一緒に、作るんでしょ？」**

“I’m not helping you. We’re making it together.”

The distinction mirrors Kuroneko’s earlier refusal to let Kyousuke take her responsibility.

Sena will not enter as:

-   subordinate;

-   technical repair service;

-   or defeated rival.


She enters as co-creator.

This transforms the project from Kuroneko’s game repaired by Sena into a shared work whose final form neither could have produced alone.

Their arguments do not disappear. Collaboration means developing a structure capable of using disagreement productively.

---

# 21\. Kyousuke learns to be unnecessary

Watching Kuroneko and Sena reconcile, Kyousuke realizes that his plan to “make friends for Kuroneko” was arrogant.

They establish the relationship themselves.

He thinks:

> His help was unnecessary.

Crucially, he feels happy.

This qualifies the hypothesis that Kyousuke always needs to be indispensable.

His deeper desire can be separated into two components:

1.  He wants important people to be safe and connected.

2.  He enjoys being the person who makes that happen.


When the first is satisfied without the second, he experiences:

-   slight loneliness;

-   but genuine warmth and relief.


He later explains that he does not need credit as long as Kuroneko’s life improves.

That is significant growth.

> Kyousuke’s care is not reducible to narcissistic rescue, even though rescue remains an important source of identity for him.

---

# 22\. The game does not win—and that matters

『強欲の迷宮』 fails to receive a prize.

It does, however:

-   attract attention on the contest boards;

-   provoke strong reactions;

-   demonstrate Kuroneko’s distinctive vision;

-   and establish the team’s ability to finish a complex work.


The failure prevents the volume from repeating Kirino’s rapid-success pattern.

Kuroneko accepts responsibility:

> She made what she wanted, and users did not fully accept it.

But she does not conclude that the effort was meaningless.

The real achievement is relational and creative:

-   Kuroneko finishes.

-   Sena returns.

-   technical and aesthetic strengths combine.

-   classmates begin seeing Kuroneko differently.

-   and failure becomes survivable inside a group.


Volume 3 asked how creators respond when effort goes unrewarded.

Volume 5 supplies a healthier answer:

> Finish, assess, accept responsibility, and keep the collaborators.

# 23\. Fate/Setuna and the reintegration of the failed creator

The former plagiarist appears again, still struggling professionally.

Her old masculine **僕** register has begun returning after Kuroneko’s confrontation forced her to stop hiding entirely behind the polished author persona.

Kirino has also allowed her to contribute editorial ideas to the published version of 『妹空』.

This does not erase what she did.

It demonstrates that accountability need not require permanent exclusion.

Setsuna can:

-   lose the stolen authorship;

-   acknowledge Kirino’s originality;

-   contribute legitimate expertise;

-   and pursue a role better suited to her editorial ability.


The volume’s communities repeatedly work this way:

> People are not purified before they re-enter relationship; they are given a different way to participate.

---

# 24\. The collection as a relational archive

Kirino’s message is alarming because the collection no longer consists merely of consumer goods.

It contains:

-   the game that began the first consultation;

-   the Meruru material Kyousuke watched with her;

-   the figure her friends obtained for her;

-   the works that enabled her friendships;

-   personal records;

-   and the objects she explicitly entrusted to Kyousuke.


To destroy it would mean more than renouncing fandom.

It would symbolically erase:

-   the reconstructed sibling relationship;

-   Kirino’s integrated identity;

-   and the social world built during the first four volumes.


Kyousuke therefore cannot interpret the message literally as ordinary decluttering.

He understands that a person willing to destroy this archive must be in crisis.

The shared objects now communicate distress more clearly than Kirino’s words.

---

# 25\. Ayase recognizes Kyousuke’s privileged position

Kyousuke assumes Kirino would contact Ayase before contacting him.

Ayase assumes the opposite.

She tells him that if Kirino reaches out to anyone, it will probably be Kyousuke, and asks him to help her.

This exchange is a major marker in the sibling relationship.

Ayase now understands that Kyousuke occupies a position she cannot reproduce:

-   he knows the hidden identity;

-   has repeatedly received the consultations;

-   holds the collection;

-   and can be contacted without violating Kirino’s rule against speaking to “friends.”


The last point is paradoxical.

Kirino can contact Kyousuke because he is **not a friend**.

He is family.

The category that once signified emotional distance now gives him unique access.

---

# 26\. Kuroneko teaches Kyousuke directness

When Kyousuke hesitates, Kuroneko is furious.

She points out:

-   Kirino is not dead or unreachable;

-   Kyousuke knows where she is;

-   transportation exists;

-   he knows he is worried;

-   and no additional justification is required.


Her challenge is essentially:

> Why are you waiting for a more legitimate reason than love?

But before asking him to act, Kuroneko demonstrates how.

She directly thanks him for:

-   seeing her as herself rather than a substitute;

-   preferring **先輩** to **兄さん**;

-   joining the club;

-   worrying about her isolation;

-   supporting her presentation;

-   and choosing to spend time with her.


She concludes:

> **「私は、とても、嬉しかった。」**

“I was very happy.”

Then she asks:

> **「あなたはどうするの？」**

“What will you do?”

Kuroneko’s confession of gratitude is an emotional tutorial.

She shows Kyousuke that sincerity need not be disguised as:

-   accusation;

-   joke;

-   duty;

-   or practical problem-solving.


He follows her example when he reaches Kirino.

---

# 27\. The kiss as a “curse”

Kuroneko kisses Kyousuke on the cheek and calls it a curse that will kill him if he loses his nerve.

This is the clearest romantic action in the series so far.

Its ambiguity is real but limited.

Possible motives coexist:

-   she wants to help Kirino;

-   she wants to encourage Kyousuke;

-   she enjoys theatrical provocation;

-   and she has developing romantic feelings.


The act need not have only one motive.

What matters structurally is that Kuroneko uses romantic intimacy to send Kyousuke toward another girl—his sister.

That preserves her dignity as more than a route obstacle.

She does not exploit Kirino’s absence to keep Kyousuke nearby.

She recognizes that ignoring Kirino would make him a worse person and destroy the basis of the relationship Kuroneko herself values.

Her love, if that is what it is becoming, is not yet possessive enough to demand that he abandon his sibling bond.

---

# 28\. The father has prepared for Kyousuke’s decision

When Kyousuke tells his father, the father already has:

-   luggage;

-   maps;

-   money;

-   travel arrangements;

-   and instructions ready.


He says:

> **「すべておまえに任せる。頼むぞ」**

The father’s preparation implies that he has also recognized Kirino’s crisis and understands that Kyousuke may be the only person capable of reaching her emotionally.

This represents remarkable development from Volume 1.

The father once believed Kyousuke’s involvement with Kirino’s secret was corrupting.

Now he entrusts Kirino to precisely that involvement.

The family has tacitly accepted Kyousuke as mediator between:

-   Kirino’s pride;

-   private identity;

-   and emotional limits.


---

# 29\. Eroge becomes the language of reunion

Kyousuke arrives in America carrying 『妹×妹～しすこんラブすとーりぃ～』 and announces:

> **「俺はここに、エロゲーをやりにきたんだ」**

The absurdity is thematically exact.

He cannot begin with:

-   “I missed you”;

-   “I was afraid”;

-   or “Come home.”


He re-creates their established ritual:

-   sit together;

-   play a little-sister game;

-   talk while facing the screen rather than one another;

-   and let fiction provide emotional distance.


Otaku media again acts as the first confessional space.

Neither sibling initially looks directly at the other. The game absorbs the unbearable intensity and allows short truths to emerge between clicks.

---

# 30\. Kirino expected the game to substitute for her

Kirino becomes alarmed when Kyousuke chooses the Rinko route.

She reveals that the gift was supposed to be played under a specific condition:

> Kyousuke alone in his room, missing her.

This is one of the volume’s most emotionally and generically charged revelations.

Kirino had imagined:

-   her absence;

-   Kyousuke’s loneliness;

-   a fictional little sister standing in for her;

-   and the game preserving their relationship.


That does not by itself prove romantic desire. The substitution can still be understood as:

-   sibling narcissism;

-   possessiveness;

-   desire to remain present;

-   or a joke built from her favorite genre.


But it shows that Kirino did not give him the game merely as an impersonal hobby recommendation.

She designed it as a proxy for herself.

The series is therefore converting its media premise into relational structure:

> fictional little sisters become the medium through which an actual sister imagines being remembered by her brother.

---

# 31\. Kyousuke says he was lonely

Kirino asks whether he worried about her.

He says yes.

He then asks whether she missed him. She denies it.

Kyousuke responds:

> **「そっか、俺は寂しかったぞ」**

“I see. I was lonely.”

The simplicity is unprecedented.

There is:

-   no joke;

-   no fake confession;

-   no public crisis;

-   no claim that he is acting only for her friends;

-   and no attempt to disguise attachment as abstract brotherly duty.


Kuroneko’s lesson has reached him.

This is still compatible with intense familial love. But it marks a change in emotional form:

> Kyousuke can now identify his need for Kirino as his own feeling, rather than explaining it solely through what she needs from him.

---

# 32\. Kirino’s self-imposed rule

Kirino decided that she would not contact anyone in Japan until she defeated one of the international athletes in an official time trial.

She believed the goal was difficult but achievable.

Instead, she continued losing.

The rule transformed motivation into punishment:

-   no contact with friends;

-   no emotional relief;

-   limited access to her hobbies;

-   increasing shame;

-   declining health;

-   and escalating inability to ask for help.


Her message to Kyousuke becomes a further penalty. She wants the collection destroyed to eliminate what she calls **甘え**—dependence, softness, or a place of retreat.

## Determination becomes pathology

Kyousuke identifies Kirino’s true exceptional quality not as invulnerability but as:

> **断固たる決意**

Unyielding determination.

She is emotionally fragile in ordinary ways:

-   rejection hurts her;

-   failure makes her cry;

-   loneliness affects her;

-   and disapproval destabilizes her.


What is abnormal is her refusal to stop.

Her strength operates like an accelerator without a brake.

That quality produced:

-   academic success;

-   athletics;

-   modeling;

-   and creative achievement.


In America, it becomes self-destructive because the obstacle cannot be overcome simply by increasing force.

This is the series’ clearest criticism of Kirino’s success ideology so far.

---

# 33\. Kyousuke refuses the requested destruction

Kyousuke invokes the promise from Volume 4:

> He will protect the collection until she returns.

He therefore refuses even Kirino’s direct instruction to destroy it.

This is both respectful and paternalistic.

### Respectful

He understands that the request contradicts:

-   her enduring values;

-   her previous self;

-   and the relational meaning of the objects.


He refuses to assist a self-punitive act made under emotional collapse.

### Paternalistic

He decides which version of Kirino’s will is authentic.

He privileges:

-   the Kirino who entrusted the collection;

-   over

-   the Kirino currently asking him to destroy it.


The volume endorses his judgment, but the ethical structure remains important.

Kyousuke’s privileged knowledge gives him the power to say:

> I know your real desire better than your present words express it.

That can be care. It can also become control.

---

# 34\. “Come home with me”

Kyousuke initially offers practical arguments:

-   recover physically;

-   return later;

-   train more effectively;

-   stop imposing impossible conditions;

-   and do not confuse retreat with permanent defeat.


Kirino rejects all of them because they do not address the emotional structure of her pride.

She believes returning would mean:

-   humiliation;

-   betrayal of people she defeated in Japan;

-   proof that her ambitions were fraudulent;

-   and surrender before the youngest athlete in the program.


Kyousuke finally abandons rational persuasion and admits his own selfish motive:

> **「俺はおまえがいないと寂しくてイヤだから、連れ戻しに来た！」**

“I came to bring you back because I hate being lonely without you.”

He even says that Kuroneko and Ayase are, bluntly, secondary to this reason.

This is the strongest evidence yet that Kirino has become emotionally privileged over the wider network.

---

# 35\. Love and ego coexist in Kyousuke’s request

Kyousuke recognizes that he may be:

-   interfering with Kirino’s ambition;

-   encouraging retreat;

-   pulling her away from an independent future;

-   and asking her to return partly for his own comfort.


He thinks:

> **「俺の目の届くところで、幸せでいて欲しい」**

He wants her to be happy **where he can see her**.

The phrase is loving and possessive.

It contains:

-   concern;

-   fear;

-   need for proximity;

-   and desire to supervise the conditions of her happiness.


Volume 5 does not sanitize this ego. Kyousuke calls it ugly and self-centered.

But he presents it because polished selflessness has failed.

The series’ emotional logic is:

> An openly selfish attachment may be more truthful—and therefore more capable of reaching another person—than a supposedly altruistic argument that conceals personal need.

Psychological honesty does not automatically settle the ethics.

---

# 36\. Unconditional recognition

Kyousuke tells Kirino:

-   she need not continue proving herself;

-   she need not be extraordinary;

-   she may continue hating him;

-   and he will defend her from anyone who condemns her failure.


This answers the insecurity behind Kirino’s achievement structure.

Her public life is organized around being:

-   beautiful;

-   fast;

-   intelligent;

-   successful;

-   and superior.


Kyousuke offers recognition detached from performance.

He does not say:

> Return because you remain impressive.

He says:

> Return even if you are not impressive.

This may be why his recognition is uniquely valuable to her. He knows:

-   the hobbies;

-   the failures;

-   the cruelty;

-   the effort;

-   the pride;

-   and now the collapse.


He offers continuity across all of them.

---

# 37\. Did Kyousuke make Kirino give up?

The ending complicates any simple interpretation that Kyousuke merely rescues Kirino by ending her independent ambition.

Kirino later reveals that she defeated Lia once.

The exact timing is withheld. Kuroneko asks pointedly when the victory occurred, and Kirino becomes embarrassed and refuses to answer.

The strong implication is that the victory is connected to Kyousuke’s arrival and appeal, perhaps occurring in the final period before her return. The text does not state the precise sequence, so it should remain an inference rather than a fact.

What is clear is:

-   Kirino did not return having achieved nothing.

-   She fulfilled the literal condition she had imposed on herself at least once.

-   She still chose to leave the program.


This preserves more of her agency and pride than a simple collapse would have.

She does not entirely surrender to Kyousuke.

She proves she can win, then decides that continuing under the current conditions is not worth the cost.

---

# 38\. Kuroneko welcomes Kirino home

Kuroneko runs through the airport and arrives visibly exhausted.

Their reunion begins, predictably, with insults.

Then Kuroneko says:

> **「おかえり……なさい」**

Kirino replies:

> **「……うん……ただいま」**

This is one of the most emotionally direct exchanges either girl has produced.

It matters that the volume’s final reunion is not only between the siblings.

Kirino returns to:

-   Kyousuke;

-   Kuroneko;

-   Saori;

-   Ayase;

-   and the larger community.


Kyousuke’s request may be emotionally privileged, but the life he offers her is communal rather than exclusively dyadic.

He imagines:

-   Akihabara;

-   Comiket;

-   game creation;

-   and shared gatherings.


At this stage, bringing Kirino home means restoring a network, not isolating her inside the sibling relationship.

---

# 39\. Kuroneko’s place in the reunion

Kuroneko knows more than Kyousuke does at the end.

Her pointed question about when Kirino defeated Lia suggests that she recognizes a connection between:

-   Kyousuke’s arrival;

-   Kirino’s sudden performance;

-   and Kirino’s decision to return.


She then smiles knowingly while Kyousuke remains confused.

This reinforces a pattern:

> Kuroneko may understand the emotional structure of the sibling bond before Kyousuke does.

She can see:

-   how strongly Kirino responds to him;

-   how much he needs her;

-   and how the two convert attachment into competitive or hostile language.


Yet she does not use that insight to withdraw.

She remains present as:

-   Kirino’s friend;

-   Kyousuke’s junior;

-   creative partner;

-   and emerging romantic possibility.


That makes the coming route conflict psychologically credible rather than mechanically inserted.

# 40\. Japanese-language observations

## **代用品**

**代用品** means a substitute or replacement item.

Kuroneko’s use is deliberately dehumanizing:

> Do not treat me as a replacement object filling the empty “younger girl who needs me” function.

The word identifies Kyousuke’s tendency to convert people into roles within his rescuer identity.

## **構う**

Kyousuke repeatedly **構う** Kuroneko—pays attention, meddles, involves himself, refuses to leave her alone.

The term can imply:

-   care;

-   interference;

-   attention;

-   or unwanted involvement.


That ambiguity precisely describes his behavior.

## **持っていかないで**

Kuroneko’s **「私の責任を持っていかないで」** is unusual and forceful.

She does not merely say “It isn’t your fault.” She says:

> Do not carry my responsibility away from me.

The phrasing treats responsibility as something Kyousuke might seize under the guise of kindness.

## **手伝う / 一緒に作る**

Sena’s correction from **手伝う** to **一緒に作る** changes the relationship from hierarchy to co-authorship.

-   手伝う: assist someone else’s project.

-   一緒に作る: make the work together.


The distinction enacts the volume’s collaborative ethic.

## **寂しい**

Kyousuke’s direct use of **寂しい** is central.

He has previously expressed loneliness through:

-   irritation;

-   restlessness;

-   concern for other people;

-   or comic denial.


Here he names the feeling without qualification.

## **甘え**

Kirino uses **甘え** negatively to mean:

-   dependence;

-   emotional indulgence;

-   comfort she has not earned;

-   and the temptation to retreat into relationships and hobbies.


The volume challenges her interpretation. Human support is not necessarily weakness, and destroying every source of comfort does not produce mature independence.

## **友達ではない**

Kyousuke is the only person Kirino permits herself to contact because he is **not a friend**.

The phrase is superficially cold but structurally intimate.

Family exists outside the rule she imposed on friendship, allowing the sibling bond to penetrate a barrier no friendship can cross.

# 41\. Illustration analysis

The Volume 5 illustrations reinforce the novel’s dual-route structure.

## Kuroneko in uniform

The early images emphasize Kuroneko’s school uniform rather than her gothic dress. She is visually repositioned from subcultural friend to ordinary school heroine.

Her discomfort within the uniform also reflects the tension between:

-   chosen aesthetic identity;

-   and institutional integration.


## Kuroneko and Manami

Their meeting visually places two alternative futures in the same frame:

-   Manami’s soft conventional familiarity;

-   Kuroneko’s dark authored individuality.


Kyousuke stands between models of intimacy that are socially and aesthetically opposed.

## Sena’s exposure

The images of Sena’s fujoshi eruption turn concealed female desire into spectacle. As with Kirino, the publication markets the vulnerability it thematically asks readers to respect.

## Private work sessions

Kuroneko in Kyousuke’s room receives intimate domestic framing normally associated with heroine-route progression:

-   relaxed posture;

-   close physical distance;

-   creative companionship;

-   and reduced reliance on gothic costuming.


## The cheek kiss

The kiss is illustrated as an unmistakable romantic milestone. Calling it a curse preserves Kuroneko’s voice, but the image does not visually neutralize the action.

## America

The American scenes visually return Kirino to the center only after most of the volume has allowed Kuroneko to dominate.

Kirino appears:

-   physically diminished;

-   emotionally vulnerable;

-   and deprived of the polished control associated with her normal heroine imagery.


The final airport illustration restores the ensemble, preventing the return from becoming a purely private sibling ending.

# 42\. Character baselines after Volume 5

## Kyousuke

Kyousuke has made two apparently contradictory advances.

He learns that:

-   Kuroneko does not need him to create friendships;

-   another person’s independence can make him happy;

-   and care does not require credit.


But he also admits that:

-   Kirino’s absence is intolerable;

-   her friends are not replacements for her;

-   and he wants her happiness within his field of vision.


His care is becoming more honest and more possessive at the same time.

## Kirino

Kirino’s exceptionalism is revealed as precarious.

She possesses extraordinary determination but not unlimited resilience. Her coping method is self-denial:

-   isolate;

-   punish;

-   remove comfort;

-   and accelerate harder.


She returns not simply because she fails, but because Kyousuke offers a model of value independent of victory.

## Kuroneko

Kuroneko is the volume’s most developed character.

She becomes:

-   Kyousuke’s school junior;

-   a creator capable of criticism and failure;

-   Sena’s collaborator;

-   a person who can ask directly for help;

-   a friend who internalizes Kirino’s example;

-   and a genuine romantic possibility.


Most importantly, she refuses both roles the route structure could cheaply assign her:

-   substitute sister;

-   or rival who exploits Kirino’s absence.


## Sena

Sena combines:

-   social conventionality;

-   technical brilliance;

-   secret sexual subculture;

-   moral concern for boundaries;

-   and poor control when her own tastes are challenged.


Her collaboration with Kuroneko demonstrates that incompatible tastes can produce shared work without requiring complete aesthetic agreement.

## Saori

Saori’s anger reveals the emotional cost of her community-building. She fears abandonment and experiences Kirino’s departure as repetition of an earlier loss.

Her reduced presence later in the volume also allows Kuroneko and Kyousuke to form relationships without her constant mediation.

## Manami

Manami remains Kyousuke’s stable interpreter and emotional landing place.

She immediately understands that his apparent lack of concern for Kirino is false. She also manages Kuroneko socially without demanding recognition for doing so.

But Kyousuke again leaves her behind when Kuroneko or Kirino presents a stronger emotional crisis.

## Ayase

Ayase’s brief appearance confirms that she now views Kyousuke as Kirino’s most likely confidant.

Her hostility toward him coexists with deep trust in his capacity to help Kirino.

## Kōhei Akagi

Kōhei serves as both comic mirror and sibling countermodel. His open adoration looks ridiculous to Kyousuke, but his relationship with Sena is more communicatively functional than the Kousaka sibling bond.

# 43\. Hypothesis status after Volume 5

### Hypothesis 1

**People use embarrassing, theatrical, or prohibited relationships to disclose what they want.**

**Strongly strengthened.**

Kuroneko uses:

-   fictional personae;

-   creative work;

-   the language of curses;

-   and a kiss


to communicate desire.

Sena’s hidden identity similarly erupts through fictional pairing discourse.

### Hypothesis 2

**Otaku culture is the first confessional space.**

**Decisively strengthened.**

Eroge becomes the medium through which Kyousuke and Kirino can admit loneliness. Game creation becomes the medium through which Kuroneko and Sena create friendship.

### Hypothesis 3

**Kyousuke’s normality is defensive, and he needs to be needed.**

**Confirmed but significantly revised.**

Kyousuke does need the rescuer role, and Kuroneko explicitly identifies the pattern. Yet he demonstrates that he can feel genuine happiness when Kuroneko solves her own problem without him.

The stronger formulation is:

> Kyousuke needs meaningful connection and often constructs usefulness as the safest form of that connection.

### Hypothesis 4

**Kirino wants one person who accepts all compartments of her identity.**

**Strengthened.**

Kyousuke is the only person she contacts from America and the person entrusted with the archive of her identities.

But she also clearly longs for Kuroneko, Ayase, and Saori. Kyousuke is privileged, not yet exclusive.

### Hypothesis 5

**Shared secrecy progressively grants the siblings access no outside relationship can reproduce.**

**Strongly strengthened.**

The collection functions as a distress signal intelligible primarily to Kyousuke. His family status also permits Kirino to contact him without breaking her self-imposed rule against contacting friends.

### Hypothesis 6

**The heroines represent meaningful alternative futures.**

**Decisively strengthened.**

Kuroneko now offers:

-   mutual honesty;

-   creative partnership;

-   direct romantic escalation;

-   and healthier respect for responsibility.


This is not a decorative delay. It is a plausible and psychologically developed alternative intimacy.

### Hypothesis 7

**The ending may grant impossible desire temporary reality while preserving social structure.**

Still not directly testable. Volume 5 does, however, move the sibling relationship toward explicit emotional privileging:

-   Kyousuke crosses the world for Kirino.

-   He admits that her friends are secondary to his own need for her.

-   Kirino designed an imōto game to act as her proxy in his loneliness.


These developments remain compatible with intense sibling love, but the narrative is steadily giving that love the structure and imagery of romantic exclusivity.

# 44\. Critical assessment

Volume 5 is one of the strongest volumes so far because its two apparent plots are thematically connected.

### Kuroneko’s arc says:

-   Do not substitute one person for another.

-   Do not steal another person’s responsibility.

-   Ask directly for collaboration.

-   Accept being unnecessary.

-   Let friendship support agency rather than replace it.


### Kirino’s arc says:

-   No relationship can substitute for this particular sibling bond.

-   A person can punish herself in the name of independence.

-   Support may require overriding a destructive present request.

-   Direct selfish need can reach someone when abstract concern cannot.


The tension between these lessons is productive rather than accidental.

The principal weakness is that the American resolution is extremely compressed.

Kyousuke:

-   reaches Kirino quickly;

-   identifies the psychological problem accurately;

-   gives one major emotional appeal;

-   and returns with her within days.


The practical and ethical complexity of ending an international athletic program receives less attention than the emotional reunion.

The hidden timing of Kirino’s victory over Lia helps preserve her agency, but it is also a convenient mechanism for avoiding a harsher question:

> Did Kyousuke rescue Kirino from self-destruction, or did he derail an independent ambition because he could not tolerate separation?

The text wants the answer to be mostly the former, while admitting enough of the latter to keep the scene psychologically interesting.

Another weakness is the broadness of Sena’s comedy. Her thoughtful account of subcultural boundaries is stronger than the exaggerated behavior through which the novel initially presents her.

Even so, Volume 5 substantially deepens the work’s treatment of:

-   care;

-   autonomy;

-   creativity;

-   loneliness;

-   and route structure.


---

# Volume 5 verdict

Volume 5 proves that Kirino’s absence creates genuine new life rather than an empty pause.

Kyousuke:

-   becomes closer to Kuroneko;

-   enters a creative club;

-   participates in collaborative work;

-   and learns that people can grow without his intervention.


Kuroneko:

-   ceases to be Kirino’s satellite;

-   forms an independent relationship with Kyousuke;

-   confronts her own pride;

-   acquires a friend and collaborator;

-   and initiates the series’ clearest romantic gesture.


For much of the volume, it appears possible that Kyousuke’s world will expand beyond Kirino rather than orbit her.

Then the message arrives.

The collection functions as an emergency signal because it embodies everything the siblings created together. Kyousuke’s response reveals that his new relationships, however genuine, have not replaced Kirino’s position.

The decisive development is not simply that he rescues her. It is that he finally stops pretending the rescue is only for her benefit.

He says:

> I am lonely.
> I need you near me.
> I want you to be happy where I can see you.

That confession is:

-   loving;

-   familial;

-   selfish;

-   protective;

-   possessive;

-   and emotionally truthful.


The novel does not yet require us to call it romantic. But it is clearly no longer ordinary sibling concern in the weak sense of generic family obligation. Kirino has become one of the organizing conditions of Kyousuke’s emotional life.

At the same time, Kuroneko demonstrates a different model of intimacy:

> Support me, but do not replace me.
> Care about me, but do not take my responsibility.
> Stand beside me, but let me create my own relationships.

That contrast will matter enormously going forward.

Volume 5 therefore ends with two viable relational logics:

-   **Kuroneko:** reciprocal growth through collaboration and differentiated selves.

-   **Kirino:** privileged recognition through shared history, family identity, and an attachment neither sibling can comfortably name.


The question carried into Volume 6 is no longer whether Kirino can return.

It is:

> **What happens when the person Kyousuke crossed the world to recover comes home and discovers that, during her absence, Kuroneko has become emotionally and romantically important to him?**
