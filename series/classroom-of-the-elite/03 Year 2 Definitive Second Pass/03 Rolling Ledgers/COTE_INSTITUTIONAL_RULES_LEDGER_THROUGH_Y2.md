# Institutional Rules Ledger
## Canonical current-state governance through Year 2 / *Second List*

# 0. Ledger responsibility

This ledger does **not** attempt to reproduce every special-exam rule.

Its primary responsibility is the **institutional constitution** of Advanced Nurturing High School and the surrounding systems that can alter student opportunity, risk, membership, mobility, evidence, and official reality.

It tracks:

- who can author rules;
- who can interpret rules;
- who can enforce rules;
- who controls the record;
- what protections and exit mechanisms exist;
- which rights are conditional on points, office, or information;
- when private actors can convert formal rules into patronage;
- when administrators can create a covert rule layer;
- how students can create independent provenance against hostile authority;
- how the student council approaches rule authorship;
- how external political actors penetrate the school;
- how the White Room retrospectively changes the institutional genealogy;
- and how official measurement becomes useful without becoming total.

Detailed exam scoring, turn structure, pair composition, category tables, and event-by-event mechanical adjudication belong in `COTE_EXAM_LEDGER_THROUGH_Y2.md`.

Detailed OAA interpretation and ability metrics belong in `COTE_ABILITY_MEASUREMENT_LEDGER_THROUGH_Y2.md`.

Detailed normative judgment belongs in `COTE_ETHICS_LEDGER_THROUGH_Y2.md`.

The governing institutional question is narrower:

> **What makes a rule binding, contestable, trustworthy, and legitimate when the institution can measure students more precisely than students can observe the people who author their environment?**

# 1. Executive thesis — Year 2 increases legibility downward faster than transparency upward

Year 1 ended with three scales of institutional authorship:

1. **exam architecture** decides what capacities become visible;
2. **student and teacher political actors** manipulate information, incentives, and implementation;
3. **administrative sovereignty** can alter the official record itself.

Year 2 does not replace that model.

It adds a fourth problem:

4. **the rule-authoring environment may itself be politically authored from outside the visible school system.**

The strongest Year-2 institutional thesis is therefore:

> **ANHS becomes more legible without becoming proportionately more transparent. Students are measured, ranked, tracked, priced, protected, exposed, and transferred through increasingly explicit systems, while the actors capable of altering, exploiting, or politically repurposing those systems often remain less visible than the students governed by them.**

This asymmetry appears immediately in Y2V01.

The school introduces OAA as a public infrastructure for comparing students. At nearly the same moment, a small subset of first-years operates under a hidden twenty-million-point incentive directed at Ayanokōji.

The governed become more visible.

The hidden rule layer does not.

That pattern recurs throughout Year 2:

- secret ballots can be counted perfectly while still producing morally opaque unanimity;
- GPS logs can be accurate while causation remains uncertain;
- a formal examination can be fairly described while an administrator creates a private extraction zone inside it;
- a representative can possess formal authority without possessing ethical jurisdiction over irreversible sacrifice;
- a student council can open mobility while centralizing access to mobility in a patron;
- a class-transfer right can create formal freedom while making twenty million points the price of exercising it;
- an official guidebook can make the student file richer while repeatedly demonstrating that the file still does not contain the person.

The Year-2 institutional problem is thus not simply "corrupt rules."

It is the difference among:

> **rule text → operational environment → enforcement → record → political authorship of the system itself.**

A rule can be fair at one layer and compromised at another.

# 2. Five institutional layers

Year 2 is easiest to read if "the institution" is disaggregated.

## 2.1 Layer 1 — formal public rule

This is the rule students are told:

- OAA fields;
- exam conditions;
- penalties;
- private-point prices;
- class-point consequences;
- transfer rights;
- Protection Point effects;
- voting procedures;
- student-council offices;
- rescue mechanisms.

This layer matters.

The series does not support the view that formal rules are meaningless.

Students repeatedly reason from them successfully.

## 2.2 Layer 2 — operational implementation

A rule becomes real only through implementation:

- how pairings are negotiated;
- who possesses information;
- who can afford a rescue;
- which leader gets scarce protection slots;
- whether a target reports abuse;
- whether an anonymous ballot becomes a collective deadline;
- whether a transfer right is actually fundable;
- whether a student can make a high OAA capacity politically available.

Two students may formally possess the same right while facing radically different practical access.

## 2.3 Layer 3 — enforcement and record

Institutional reality depends on:

- surveillance;
- tablets;
- GPS;
- witness testimony;
- teachers;
- administrative review;
- official result recording;
- whether evidence is admissible;
- and whether the person controlling the record is trustworthy.

Year 1's Tsukishiro chess intervention established the hardest case:

> **displayed result != recorded result.**

Year 2 develops both the danger and its countermeasure.

## 2.4 Layer 4 — discretionary political authority

Student-council presidents, teachers, acting administrators, chairmen, and politically connected adults can affect the environment without necessarily rewriting the public rule text.

Their authority may include:

- agenda control;
- exam concept influence;
- mobility resources;
- discipline;
- access to information;
- appointment;
- interpretation;
- intervention;
- surveillance;
- emergency response.

The crucial point is that ANHS is **not a unitary actor**.

Competing adults can constrain one another.

## 2.5 Layer 5 — external political authorship

Volume 0 and V12.5 make the outer layer explicit.

ANHS exists inside:

- factional national politics;
- Kijima's political project;
- Atsuomi's interests;
- the White Room's counter-project;
- elite family power;
- administrative appointments;
- external observation.

At Year-2 end, a special examination itself can be altered because a major political observer is present.

The school is therefore not a sealed laboratory.

# 3. Frozen Year-1 institutional baseline

`COTE_INSTITUTION_EXAM_LEDGER_THROUGH_Y1.md` established several rules that remain controlling through Year 2.

## 3.1 Institutional scarcity is often manufactured

The school does not merely observe who fails.

It can deliberately create mechanisms that force:

- expulsion risk;
- scarce protection;
- unequal rescue costs;
- leadership liability;
- class-point loss;
- irreversible membership decisions.

The Year-1 Class Vote is the canonical example.

The exam existed because no student had yet been expelled.

This means expulsion is sometimes not an accidental consequence of inability.

It is a designed output.

## 3.2 Private points are political infrastructure

Private points are not merely spending money.

They can become:

- insurance;
- rescue capital;
- bargaining capital;
- patronage;
- collateral;
- transfer funding;
- and a way to purchase institutional options unavailable to students without sufficient capital.

## 3.3 Protection is priced

Year 1 established the `プロテクトポイント / Protection Point` as a non-transferable institutional survival asset.

It converts reputation and competition into conditional immunity.

Protection is therefore not a universal right.

It is a scarce institutional resource.

## 3.4 Student politics can approach rule authorship

Manabu's Year-1 explanation made clear that the student council can influence school culture and special-exam concepts.

Nagumo turns that opening into a political project.

The institutional importance is not that students become administrators.

It is that student government can shape the incentive environment experienced by other students.

## 3.5 Administrative sovereignty can capture the record

Tsukishiro's Year-1 chess intervention proves that good exam design is insufficient if a hostile administrator controls the communication or record layer.

A fair rule requires trustworthy infrastructure.

## 3.6 The school contains internal counterweights

V11.5 already prevented the project from treating "ANHS" as one will.

Chairman Sakayanagi, Mashima, and Chabashira can become alternative adult nodes.

Year 2 makes this pluralism operational.

# 4. Y2V01 — public OAA, asymmetric risk, and the hidden bounty

Y2V01 is the decisive opening institutional volume because it places **public measurement** beside **covert rule asymmetry**.

# 4.1 OAA becomes live infrastructure

Unlike *First File*'s hypothetical retrospective Year-1 OAA, Year 2 gives students an actual application.

The system records school-observed performance across:

- academics;
- physical ability;
- quick-thinking/adaptive-social capacity;
- social contribution;
- composite overall score.

It preserves separate yearly records.

This matters institutionally because the school acknowledges development.

A person's earlier visible state does not have to become an eternal score.

Chabashira explicitly frames the school as fallible rather than omniscient.

The correct institutional object is:

> **recorded performance under an observation regime.**

Not:

> the total person.

# 4.2 Public legibility creates a market

Once OAA becomes searchable, it immediately alters bargaining.

Students can be treated as:

- desirable partners;
- liabilities;
- scarce resources;
- expensive recruits;
- information targets.

The institution reduces one form of uncertainty while producing new strategic behavior around the information.

This is a recurring Year-2 rule:

> **Greater transparency about the governed can increase the power of whoever can act on the data.**

# 4.3 Formal exam risk is asymmetric

The first-year/second-year pairing structure places different consequences on the two cohorts.

The second-year student's risk can include expulsion.

The first-year student ordinarily faces a much lighter private-point penalty.

The formal rule therefore creates bargaining power.

Ability is not merely measured.

It becomes priced by the consequence structure.

# 4.4 A hidden twenty-million-point bounty creates a parallel rule layer

A small set of first-years is exposed to a covert incentive aimed at producing Ayanokōji's expulsion.

This is institutionally more important than its detective function.

The public exam says one thing.

The covert incentive creates another practical objective inside the same environment.

The Year-2 institutional ledger therefore introduces:

> **parallel rule sets inside one nominal examination.**

Students can appear to participate in the same school mechanism while actually responding to different incentive systems.

# 4.5 Institutional conclusion

Y2V01's deepest political irony is:

> **The school begins Year 2 by making ordinary student ability more visible than ever while administrative power leaves the most important hidden rule almost completely invisible.**

That is the Year-2 baseline for institutional asymmetry.

# 5. Y2V02–Y2V04 — large-scale surveillance, inter-year sovereignty, and the counter-provenance solution

The island examination expands the institution spatially and politically.

Exact scoring mechanics belong in the exam ledger.

The institutional consequences are broader.

# 5.1 Surveillance is information, not omniscience

GPS, tablets, designated areas, watch signals, and recorded movement create a dense data environment.

But the Year-2 reading repeatedly distinguishes:

- location;
- identity;
- presence;
- causation;
- and official attribution.

A record can accurately show where a device was without proving who authored the event the record seems to imply.

This is an institutional limit:

> **more telemetry does not eliminate interpretation.**

# 5.2 Nagumo creates a shadow polity through control of mobility

Nagumo's third-year network demonstrates how institutional opportunity becomes patronage.

Students cooperate because ordinary class mobility is constrained while Nagumo can offer exceptional routes.

This produces a structure in which:

- the formal school permits mobility;
- the patron controls practical access;
- dependence migrates from class to individual gatekeeper.

The political lesson is not that mobility is false.

It is:

> **a right becomes patronage when practical access depends on one discretionary intermediary.**

# 5.3 Centralized network power creates a single institutional failure point

Nagumo's command system is powerful precisely because it is centralized.

When he is removed from communication, the network stops functioning as intended.

This mirrors the later class-succession problem.

Institutional robustness is not identical to leader capacity.

# 5.4 Tsukishiro turns administrative authority into a private environment

At I2, Tsukishiro and Shiba possess:

- administrative standing;
- altered conditions;
- physical coercive capacity;
- access to extraction support;
- and the possibility of suppressing or neutralizing the record.

The island exam therefore contains a privately authored retrieval operation.

This is the strongest Year-2 example of **administrative capture**.

# 5.5 The countermeasure is not superior violence but independent provenance

Ayanokōji's most important institutional response is procedural.

Using Sakayanagi's transceiver and an existing rescue route, he arranges a report that directs Mashima and Chabashira to the relevant location.

The crucial property is that the report can be independently grounded.

The adults arrive for institutionally intelligible reasons rather than merely because Ayanokōji privately claims misconduct.

This produces one of the strongest constructive institutional principles in the corpus:

> **When a hostile authority can control the immediate record, resistance becomes more durable when a fact can be routed through a procedure that creates an independent witness or provenance chain.**

This is stronger than secrecy.

It is **counter-authored institutional reality**.

# 5.6 Adult counterweights can stop action without guaranteeing truthful adjudication

Tsukishiro can still declare that nothing happened.

The immediate extraction fails.

The public record does not become fully transparent.

Thus:

> **intervention capacity > truth-forcing capacity.**

ANHS contains counterweights, but it does not yet possess a demonstrated mechanism that guarantees full accountability for abuse at the administrative level.

# 6. Y2V05 — perfect vote counting, manufactured unanimity, and the limit of procedural fairness

The Unanimous Vote Special Examination is one of the strongest institutional case studies because the mechanism is technically clean.

There is no need to allege record corruption.

# 6.1 Individual intent is strongly protected

The examination uses:

- secret ballots;
- repeated emphasis on anonymity;
- prohibition of binding vote contracts;
- prohibition of outside coercion;
- individual ballot casting;
- physical arrangements that reduce direct monitoring.

The school explicitly prioritizes `生徒個人の意思`.

At the micro-level, this is unusually strong procedural protection.

# 6.2 Collective outcome is coercive

The class must reach unanimity.

Failure within the time limit creates a massive class-point penalty.

The final question can offer class-point gain in exchange for expelling one member.

Once the YES route becomes unanimous, some expulsion must actually occur.

Protection Points are disabled.

This produces the defining institutional paradox:

> **maximum privacy of individual choice + maximum coercion of collective closure.**

The school protects the ballot from peers while making the cost of unresolved disagreement enormous.

# 6.3 Procedure can be accurate without containing the meaning of the decision

A final 38–0 vote can be recorded perfectly.

That record does not tell us:

- who wanted the path initially;
- who yielded under time pressure;
- who accepted only after information changed;
- who was preserving another person;
- who was responding to prior promises;
- who authored the criterion of sacrifice;
- or whose relational influence made consent psychologically possible.

Thus:

> **accurate record != complete political meaning.**

Year 1 showed that the record can be falsified.

Year 2 adds the harder case:

> **the record can be completely accurate and still under-describe the act.**

# 6.4 Institutional scarcity converts legibility into disposability

Ayanokōji uses OAA and social information after the class has entered the expulsion route.

The school does not itself declare Airi the least valuable person.

But the rule structure creates a scarcity in which student leaders need a decision procedure.

Institutional legibility becomes available for culling.

The institutional lesson is:

> **measurement systems acquire ethical meaning from the decisions into which they are inserted.**

OAA can support development in one context and disposability in another.

# 6.5 Chabashira demonstrates institutional afterlife

Her own historical unanimous-vote trauma shows that special-exam design can become an adult identity.

An institution does not cease causing consequences when the score is entered.

The social and psychological afterlife remains outside most official metrics.

# 7. Y2V06–Y2V08 — aftermath, ordinary governance, and the importance of what the school does not fully regulate

These volumes are institutionally quieter than V01, V04, or V05.

That quiet is itself informative.

# 7.1 Formal rules do not resolve social legitimacy

Kushida remains enrolled after public exposure.

The school can regulate:

- enrollment;
- exam penalties;
- disciplinary events;
- formal office.

It does not mechanically solve:

- whether classmates trust her;
- whether public/private identity can coexist;
- whether she becomes socially legitimate;
- whether cooperation is morally acceptable.

Institutional membership and social membership remain different.

# 7.2 Expulsion is not ontological deletion

Airi's outside-school life becomes increasingly relevant to surviving students.

The institution can terminate ANHS membership.

It does not determine whether the expelled person has:

- a future;
- dignity;
- relationships;
- success;
- or meaning.

This matters because ANHS's internal language repeatedly risks turning departure into total failure.

The corpus rejects that ontology.

# 7.3 Ordinary life is a governance blind spot

Friendship, leisure, school trips, dating, meals, shopping, and private conversation generate forms of development the school does not fully score.

This is not a defect to be "fixed" by adding more measurement.

It is evidence that the educational institution's most important developmental outputs may exceed its formal evaluation system.

The ordinary-life ledger will own the full argument.

Institutionally, the rule is:

> **unmeasured does not mean institutionally irrelevant.**

# 8. Volume 0 — ANHS and the White Room become politically entangled institutions rather than simple opposites

Volume 0 must be handled retrospectively.

It changes the reader's institutional model without granting the ordinary cast new knowledge.

# 8.1 White Room origin is political before it is educational

Volume 0 reveals the White Room as a political project using education as its medium.

Naoe commissions the human-development project inside a political struggle against Kijima.

The project combines:

- concealed child procurement;
- elite financing;
- research;
- ideological conditioning;
- political ambition;
- controlled developmental environments.

This revises any model in which the White Room began as neutral education and was later politicized.

Politics is constitutive.

# 8.2 ANHS is also politically situated

Kijima's political capital includes the government-backed ANHS.

Atsuomi and Tsukishiro have interests in:

- Kijima;
- ANHS;
- Kiyotaka's future environment;
- and how that environment may affect his development.

Thus ANHS is no longer readable as a pure outside world accidentally encountered by Kiyotaka.

# 8.3 Authored opportunity does not imply authored outcome

Atsuomi and Tsukishiro anticipate or partly arrange an opportunity structure connected to Kiyotaka's eventual ANHS entry.

That revelation changes the institution's genealogy.

It does **not** prove:

- that every later school relationship is Atsuomi-authored;
- that Kiyotaka's personal purposes are paternal compliance;
- that Horikita, Kei, Hiyori, Ryūen, Ichinose, or others are controlled outputs.

The correct rule is:

> **authorship of access != ownership of what emerges after access.**

# 8.4 White Room versus ANHS is now a normative, not geographical, contrast

The two institutions are politically entangled.

Their strongest difference is not simply "inside versus outside."

It is the degree of:

- plurality;
- exit;
- unscripted relationship;
- dissent;
- conflicting authorities;
- counter-authorship;
- ordinary life;
- capacity for purposes not assigned by curriculum.

ANHS remains coercive and unequal.

It nevertheless produces surplus the White Room cannot fully pre-author.

# 9. Y2V09 — student-council succession and the constitutionalization of counterforce

Y2V09 returns the institutional question to student government.

# 9.1 Student-council office is not one fixed constitution

Manabu, Nagumo, and Horikita do not simply occupy the same chair with different personalities.

Their administrations embody different institutional styles.

### Manabu
- competence;
- restraint;
- strong legitimacy;
- distance.

### Nagumo
- expanded mobility;
- cross-class patronage;
- personal network;
- discretionary access;
- centralized authority.

### Horikita
- begins with less prestige;
- inherits an office whose recent history demonstrates both capacity and capture risk;
- must make rival claims auditable.

The office is therefore a platform whose political meaning changes with institutional practice.

# 9.2 Nagumo's meritocracy is freedom through custody

Nagumo does open routes beyond class fate.

That is not fake.

But access to those routes becomes tied to his:

- money;
- status;
- recognition;
- discretionary favor.

The Year-1 question about "true meritocracy" therefore matures:

> **mobility may liberate students from class dependence while making them dependent on the patron who controls mobility.**

# 9.3 Kiriyama converts granted privilege into a constraint on the grantor

One of V09's strongest institutional developments is that Kiriyama uses authority and transfer privilege produced inside Nagumo's system to constrain Nagumo's future use of that system.

This is a counter-authorship test.

A privilege becomes more constitutionally meaningful when the holder can use it **against the preference of the person who distributed it**.

That is the opposite of patronage loyalty.

# 9.4 Horikita's first council test is answerability

The Kiryūin/Kiriyama/Nagumo conflict requires reconstruction, not charismatic command.

Horikita's growing authority becomes more legitimate because the relevant actors can:

- contradict;
- expose information;
- offer competing interpretations;
- and receive a settlement that is not simply the president's private preference.

This establishes:

> **institutional authority becomes more trustworthy when counterforce is part of the procedure rather than an enemy of the procedure.**

# 9.5 Kushida's recruitment separates office from coerced gratitude

Horikita wants Kushida in the council because Kushida possesses genuinely useful social capacity.

But participation only becomes developmentally meaningful when Kushida has a reason she can own.

The office does not become legitimate merely because a superior leader knows it would be good for her.

This is an institutional version of the corpus's broader authorship rule:

> **assignment without internal reason can produce compliance; legitimate participation requires a meaningful path of refusal and appropriation.**

# 10. Y2V10 — finite protection makes institutional valuation explicit

The survival/elimination exam belongs in the exam ledger mechanically.

Institutionally, it exposes the politics of scarce protection.

# 10.1 Leaders receive finite protective capacity

A leader cannot protect everyone.

A protected slot becomes simultaneously:

- probability management;
- public valuation;
- political insulation;
- possible survival protection.

The institution forces leaders to reveal priority under scarcity.

# 10.2 Institutional, strategic, contractual, and relational value diverge

V10 separates four categories:

1. **institutional value** — measurable performance;
2. **strategic value** — contribution to winning;
3. **contractual value** — what is owed under explicit agreement;
4. **relational value** — what a particular person has become to another.

The institution can formalize the first three more easily than the fourth.

Kamuro's expulsion becomes diagnostic because Sakayanagi discovers relational irreplaceability after a decision model treated several vulnerable members as effectively interchangeable.

# 10.3 Formal protection is not equivalent to care

Kei wants Ayanokōji's personal guarantee.

Horikita has class-political reasons to protect Kei.

Those are not the same fact.

The rule system therefore prevents a common analytical collapse:

> **protected != loved; unprotected != worthless.**

# 10.4 The school can make plural knowledge a survival variable

The exam uses knowledge categories broader than academics.

This reflects a richer theory of school-legible capacity.

But the institutional rule remains:

> **plural measurement is still measurement.**

A richer metric can remain reductive if used as a total personhood claim.

# 11. Y2V11 — recognition without jurisdiction

V11's central institutional contribution is less about a new school system than about a limit on intervention.

Ayanokōji arrives at the stopping rule:

`ここから先は、オレの立ち入る領域じゃない`

The principle is institutionally important because competent actors repeatedly operate near authority boundaries.

A person may possess:

- information;
- predictive advantage;
- social leverage;
- office;
- technical capacity.

None of these automatically gives jurisdiction over another person's answer.

The ledger therefore adds:

> **capacity to intervene != right to intervene.**

This will become critical in V12.

# 12. Y2V12 — representative authority, adult conflict of interest, and formally free choices inside authored environments

# 12.1 The year-end exam deliberately separates representative and participant rule sets

Representatives observe.

Participants act under incomplete knowledge.

The representative cannot simply command participants.

The design makes:

- partial information;
- hidden roles;
- delegated action;
- social knowledge;
- inference

part of the institution.

This is an explicit Year-2 trend:

> **the school increasingly measures action under uncertainty rather than merely static ability.**

# 12.2 Formal authority can still exceed legitimate jurisdiction

Ayanokōji can operate within the exam's representative structure and still produce outcomes whose ethical authorization is contested.

Maezono's expulsion is the clearest example.

The rule permits the consequence.

That does not establish that the representative is ethically entitled to engineer the terminal condition.

V12.5 later sharpens the point when Hirata describes the action as approaching `越権行為`.

The institutional distinction is:

> **rule-enabled power != unlimited representative sovereignty.**

# 12.3 Hoshinomiya demonstrates teacher conflict of interest

Hoshinomiya's adult grievance toward Chabashira becomes active intervention in current students' futures.

The teacher role therefore cannot be assumed to neutralize private history.

This reinforces the Year-1/V04 rule:

> **office is an authority position, not proof of neutral motivation.**

# 12.4 Formal freedom can be an authored control surface

V12's hardest institutional insight extends beyond relationships.

A system can preserve formal choice while arranging:

- information;
- timing;
- incentives;
- trust;
- dependency;
- scarcity.

The resulting choice remains a choice.

But its environment may be heavily authored.

This matters institutionally because many ANHS mechanisms are designed precisely to make students choose under manufactured constraints.

Thus:

> **formal voluntariness is necessary evidence of agency, not sufficient evidence of uncoerced or fully informed choice.**

# 13. Y2V12.5 — external political permeability, witness architecture, and class transfer as institutional reconstitution

V12.5 changes the institutional scale one final time.

# 13.1 ANHS is politically permeable

The opening reveals that the originally planned year-end examination was changed when an external political observer arrived.

Prime Minister Kijima's presence is not background color.

The school alters its examination architecture under elite political observation.

This establishes:

> **ANHS is responsive to national political context.**

Its exam environment is not produced solely by educators or students.

# 13.2 Expulsion has an afterlife the institution can be asked to account for

Kijima asks about post-expulsion follow-up.

This matters because the series repeatedly treats expulsion as terminal **inside the competition**.

The question from outside the system reopens the person:

- What happens afterward?
- Who remains responsible?
- Does institutional membership loss end institutional concern?

The ledger's answer is not supplied completely.

But the question becomes institutionally legitimate.

# 13.3 Hoshinomiya containment uses witness architecture rather than private domination

Later V12.5 material addresses Hoshinomiya through:

- truth;
- proof;
- record;
- witnesses;
- deterrence.

This is a constructive institutional pattern.

The strongest response to an adult actor with private grievance is not merely a stronger private threat.

It is to create a record environment in which future abuse becomes harder to deny or isolate.

This repeats V04 at a different scale:

> **independent witness structure is one of the corpus's best counters to private sovereign power.**

# 13.4 The twenty-million-point class-transfer right becomes real rather than theoretical

Earlier volumes treat class transfer as expensive possibility and patronage resource.

V12.5 completes an actual transfer.

Ayanokōji uses the class-transfer right to enter the former Sakayanagi class.

The transfer is funded through collective resources and accepted by the receiving class.

`Second List` records the transfer as institutional data (`Y2SL-E080`).

This establishes several distinctions.

### Formal mobility
The institution genuinely permits movement between classes.

### Economic gate
The right is extraordinarily expensive.

### Political aggregation
Collective financing can turn individual mobility into a class-level constitutional act.

### Succession ambiguity
Receiving Ayanokōji can restore competitive capacity without proving the class internally generated a successor.

Transfer is therefore both:

- personal mobility;
- and institutional reconstitution.

# 13.5 The school can move people more easily than it can guarantee what the move means

The transfer rule can specify:

- price;
- destination;

# 16. Institutional invariants established through Year 2

# 16.1 Rule text is only one layer of a rule

A complete institutional reading requires:

`formal text + information distribution + implementation + enforcement + record + review`

A rule may be formally neutral and operationally asymmetric.

# 16.2 A right with an impossible access cost is not practically equal

Twenty-million-point mechanisms are real rights.

They are also capital-gated.

The relevant distinction is:

> **formal availability != practical accessibility.**

# 16.3 Protection is not universal

The school repeatedly prices protection:

- points;
- leader slots;
- reputational reward;
- contracts;
- rescue capital.

This makes protection an institutional allocation problem.

# 16.4 Secrecy can protect agency and obscure responsibility simultaneously

Anonymous voting protects the dissenter from direct retaliation.

It also prevents the class from knowing who is imposing the veto.

The same institutional feature can therefore protect liberty and create political opacity.

# 16.5 Reporting thresholds shape what becomes actionable

Year 1 rumor governance and Year 2 witness architecture produce the same rule.

A harm may remain institutionally invisible until it is:

- reported;
- witnessed;
- recorded;
- or expensive for the school to ignore.

This creates incentives to manufacture provenance.

# 16.6 The institution is not a single mind

Tsukishiro, Chairman Sakayanagi, Mashima, Chabashira, Hoshinomiya, Nagumo, Horikita, and external political actors occupy different roles and interests.

"ANHS wants X" should be used only when the source genuinely establishes a school-wide rule.

Otherwise identify the actor and jurisdiction.

# 16.7 Accurate measurement can still be incomplete

OAA and point systems may truthfully record their defined objects.

The error begins when the defined object is silently expanded into total personhood or total class worth.

# 16.8 Formal choice can coexist with environmental coercion

A secret ballot, contract, or transfer decision may still occur inside:

- scarcity;
- informational asymmetry;
- dependency;
- time pressure;
- manufactured alternatives.

Agency survives.

So does environmental responsibility.

# 16.9 External political authorship does not erase internal counter-authorship

Volume 0 proves that ANHS is politically situated.

It does not reduce every school development to elite political intent.

Institutions can produce outcomes their sponsors did not fully design.

# 16.10 A trustworthy institution requires counterforce

The strongest constructive evidence in Year 2 involves:

- independent witnesses;
- rival authorities;
- auditable procedure;
- dissent;
- transferable privileges that can constrain the patron;
- offices whose occupants can reinterpret inherited practice.

Counterforce is not evidence the institution has failed.

It may be evidence the institution can correct itself.

# 17. Administrative sovereignty versus procedural sovereignty

The corpus now requires two different senses of sovereignty.

## Administrative sovereignty

The power to:

- access systems;
- change conditions;
- suppress information;
- direct staff;
- alter implementation;
- control institutional infrastructure.

Tsukishiro is the strongest example.

## Procedural sovereignty

The power of a rule or process to remain valid **even against the preferences of a powerful actor**.

Examples include:

- rescue procedure generating adult witnesses at I2;
- secret ballots resisting direct peer surveillance;
- transfer privilege used against patron preference;
- formal transfer completing despite enormous political consequences.

The strongest institutions are not those with the strongest administrator.

They are those in which procedure can survive the administrator.

# 27. Canonical Year-2 institutional synthesis

Year 2 makes Advanced Nurturing High School easier to see and harder to simplify.

It is neither:

- a neutral meritocratic machine;
- nor a completely corrupt sham;
- nor a simple opposite of the White Room;
- nor one unified sovereign actor.

It is a layered institution containing:

- real rules;
- real measurement;
- real rights;
- real protections;
- manufactured scarcity;
- expensive mobility;
- social life that exceeds measurement;
- student-level rule influence;
- adult conflict of interest;
- administrative capture risk;
- internal counterweights;
- national political permeability;
- and procedures capable of generating unplanned human outcomes.

Its deepest contradiction is asymmetrical legibility.

The school increasingly asks:

> **How well can we know the student?**

OAA, points, surveillance, voting, protection allocations, profiles, and official guidebooks all improve the answer.

But the more important constitutional question is often the reverse:

> **How well can the student know, challenge, and constrain the institution that is knowing them?**

Year 2 repeatedly shows that legitimacy increases when the answer is not "trust the strongest authority."

The strongest institutional successes involve:

- independent witnesses;
- rival authorities;
- protected disagreement;
- auditable provenance;
- transferable rights that can be used against the patron;
- leaders who accept limits;
- and procedures capable of surviving the preferences of the people who administer them.

The terminal Year-2 institutional principle is therefore:

> **A trustworthy rule system is not one that perfectly models the people inside it. It is one in which the people inside it retain enough information, counterforce, provenance, and practical access to make the rule-author answerable too.**

And the unresolved boundary question is:

> **Can ANHS make institutional power as legible and corrigible to students as it has made students legible to institutional power?**
