# Ethics Ledger
## Canonical normative state through Year 2 / *Second List*

# 0. Ledger function

Year 1 established the project's governing ethical warning:

> **Beneficial outcomes do not settle moral authority.**

A more capable actor may diagnose another person correctly, choose an outcome that later proves beneficial, and execute the intervention with extraordinary skill. None of those facts alone establishes the right to:

- select the other person's goal;
- conceal the relevant terms;
- manufacture danger;
- monopolize protection;
- distribute risk to uninformed third parties;
- use violence beyond necessity;
- or treat later gratitude as retroactive consent.

Year 2 preserves that framework and makes the problem substantially harder.

The dangerous actor no longer always needs to issue a command.

Instead, a sufficiently informed actor can manipulate:

- information;
- timing;
- incentives;
- social dependency;
- trust;
- love;
- institutional rules;
- reputation;
- scarcity;
- and another person's own expectations

so that the target freely performs the act the designer prefers.

This produces the central Year-2 ethical question:

> **When another person's agency remains formally intact but the choice environment has been authored around a superior model of that person, what makes the resulting choice genuinely answerable rather than merely predictable?**

The ledger therefore owns six linked ethical fields:

1. **autonomy and self-authorship**;
2. **consent, information, refusal, and exit**;
3. **protection, dependency, and relational jurisdiction**;
4. **developmental intervention, paternalism, and counter-authorship**;
5. **expulsion, disposability, scarcity, and sacrifice**;
6. **force, violence, institutional coercion, and accountability**.

The governing rule remains non-scalar.

An action can be:

- effective but illegitimate;
- caring but controlling;
- consensual at one surface level but uninformed at another;
- violent but proportionately defensive;
- rule-compliant but unjust;
- strategically necessary but politically unauthorized;
- or autonomy-enhancing in outcome while autonomy-compromising in method.

No single adjective such as “good,” “bad,” “healthy,” “toxic,” “necessary,” or “manipulative” is sufficient.

# 1. Executive thesis — Year 2 moves from the right to intervene to the ethics of authored choice

The strongest Year-1 terminal standard was the **answer-back standard**:

> **Power is most legitimate when the person affected can understand it, contest it, refuse where possible, demand reasons, survive disagreement, and become someone the powerful actor did not design.**

Year 2 strengthens this rather than replacing it.

The year begins with public measurement and hidden bounties. It moves through coercive grouping, information architecture, administrative record capture, romantic curriculum, manufactured sacrificial scarcity, distributed agency, staged agency, relational legibility, retrospective preference engineering, desire, protection under scarcity, bounded intervention, relational jurisdiction, and finally separation.

The ethical progression can be summarized as:

> **intervention → environmental authorship → staged agency → relational jurisdiction → separation → counter-authorship**

The decisive Year-2 discovery is that **formal choice is too weak a standard**.

A person may technically choose while:

- lacking decisive information;
- acting under a timetable selected by someone else;
- facing alternatives that have been deliberately discredited;
- fearing catastrophic loss if they refuse;
- attempting to perform the identity the designer expects;
- or responding to a relational preference the designer knows will function like a command.

Conversely, the presence of influence does not make agency unreal.

Kei's rooftop refusal is real despite a coercively authored relationship.

Kushida's decision to continue contributing in V06 is real despite Horikita's earlier nonconsensual preservation.

Sakayanagi's decision to lose in V12 is real despite Ayanokōji's designed clue environment.

Ichinose's third answer is real precisely because it exceeds the binary Ayanokōji prepared.

The correct ethical object is therefore **distributed authorship**.

Ask separately:

- Who authored the rule?
- Who authored the danger?
- Who authored the available options?
- Who authored the information state?
- Who authored the motive?
- Who authored the final act?
- Who bears the cost?
- Who can later challenge the decision?
- Who controls the meaning of what happened?

Year 2's strongest positive ethical result is **bounded intervention**:

> recognize a condition, open an option, preserve meaningful refusal, disclose enough for the person to own the choice, then stop before superior understanding becomes permanent jurisdiction.

V11 gives the cleanest local articulation:

> `ここから先は、オレの立ち入る領域じゃない。`

The strongest negative result is **relational jurisdiction**:

> knowing another person so well that their trust, fear, loyalty, love, or self-conception becomes part of the control surface.

V12 demonstrates that direct coercion can decrease while ethical responsibility remains high.

The Year-2 terminal standard is therefore:

> **Power becomes most answerable not when it can accurately predict another person's choice, but when it leaves the other person enough information, refusal, independent purpose, corrective standing, and future freedom to produce an answer the powerful actor does not already own.**

# 2. Inherited Year-1 ethical controls — preserved

The Year-1 specialist remains immutable authority for the following controls.

## 2.1 Explanation is not justification

Trauma, conditioning, institutional pressure, grief, dependency, and strategic necessity can explain conduct.

They do not automatically excuse it.

Volume 0 makes this distinction even more important. Atsuomi's political environment explains his preference-engineering worldview. Kiyotaka's White Room history explains the ease with which he treats environments as adjustable systems. Neither fact grants moral permission.

## 2.2 Effectiveness is not legitimacy

A plan working does not establish the right to use the method.

Year 2 repeatedly provides highly effective interventions whose legitimacy remains contested:

- preserving Kushida;
- expelling Airi;
- exposing Yagami;
- destabilizing Ichinose;
- making Sakayanagi infer the desired loss;
- separating from Kei on a predetermined curriculum;
- transferring classes without informing Horikita in time to participate in the meaning of the separation.

## 2.3 Sincere choice is not necessarily fully autonomous choice

Sincerity and autonomy are separate variables.

A choice can be emotionally real while the alternatives, information, or incentives have been authored by someone else.

## 2.4 Autonomy is not isolation

The mature corpus strongly rejects atomistic freedom.

Autonomous people can:

- rely on friends;
- accept protection;
- learn from teachers;
- enter contracts;
- belong to a class;
- inherit norms;
- love someone;
- and remain constrained by institutions.

The question is whether dependence preserves the ability to:

- know relevant terms;
- refuse;
- contradict;
- revise;
- leave where possible;
- form purposes not supplied by the protector;
- and remain a person if usefulness changes.

## 2.5 Protection is not ownership

Protection is strongest when it expands future options.

Protection becomes proprietary when safety is made conditional on obedience, secrecy, dependency, or continued access.

## 2.6 Developmental improvement is not consent to the method

Later growth is evidence of consequence.

It is not retroactive authorization.

This rule becomes especially important for:

- Airi;
- Kushida;
- Kei;
- Ichinose;
- Horikita.

## 2.7 Rule compliance is not justice

Formal permission does not create complete political or moral authorization.

V12.5's `越権行為` language is the mature Year-2 expression of this distinction.

## 2.8 Survival value is not human worth

The school may rationally model contribution under scarcity.

That does not authorize:

`low utility -> lesser person -> deserved disappearance`.

## 2.9 Violence must remain disaggregated

Defensive force, coercive assault, torture, intimidation, disciplinary violence, retaliatory violence, sexualized coercion, procedural expulsion, informational manipulation, and institutional exposure to harm are not interchangeable.

## 2.10 Later affection does not cleanse coercive origin

The Year-2 relationship ledger strengthens this into a general non-retroactivity rule:

> **Later affection, recovery, usefulness, independence, or reconciliation does not retroactively legitimate the earlier method.**

# 3. The through-Year-2 intervention audit

The Year-1 framework used twelve dimensions. Year 2 preserves them and adds three dimensions required by authored-choice problems.

Every major intervention should be audited independently across all fifteen.

# 3.1 Goal authorship

Who selected the end?

- the affected person;
- a friend;
- a protector;
- a class leader;
- the institution;
- a hidden strategist;
- an adult political actor?

Supporting a goal the subject already owns is ethically stronger than selecting a developmental goal on the subject's behalf.

# 3.2 Epistemic warrant and model humility

How reliable is the diagnosis?

Superior knowledge matters.

It does not create unlimited jurisdiction.

Year 2 repeatedly shows accurate models with incomplete ontology:

- Ayanokōji accurately predicts much of Kei while failing to generate romantic feeling on schedule;
- Sakayanagi accurately ranks strategic contribution while failing to model Kamuro's irreplaceability;
- Ayanokōji prepares a binary for Ichinose and receives a third answer;
- OAA is useful and incomplete.

A morally serious expert must account for the possibility that the model is good and the person is still larger than it.

# 3.3 Imminence and necessity

Is serious harm imminent enough to justify overriding ordinary consent?

Immediate defensive force has a stronger exception than developmental pressure.

Someone being immature, strategically weak, lonely, or emotionally avoidant does not create emergency jurisdiction.

# 3.4 Proportionality

Does the severity of the method fit the threatened harm?

The burden rises with:

- physical injury;
- humiliation;
- retraumatization;
- public exposure;
- irreversible membership loss;
- destruction of trust;
- third-party harm.

# 3.5 Less-coercive alternatives

Could teaching, honest confrontation, delay, invitation, contract, witness, or refusal-preserving assistance plausibly achieve the goal?

The Year-2 corpus contains enough low-coercion successes that severe methods cannot be excused as the only form of competence available to Ayanokōji.

# 3.6 Information and informed consent

What does the affected person know about:

- the goal;
- the real purpose;
- the risks;
- the alternatives;
- the likely consequence;
- the future obligations;
- the timing?

Consent to the surface event does not imply consent to a hidden curriculum.

# 3.7 Refusal and exit capacity

Can the person say no without catastrophic loss of:

- safety;
- status;
- belonging;
- protection;
- educational future;
- or the relationship itself?

Formal options that cannot be meaningfully exercised are weak autonomy protections.

# 3.8 Legitimate authority / jurisdiction

Why does the actor have standing to intervene?

Possible grounds include:

- immediate defense;
- office;
- explicit delegation;
- contract;
- reciprocal relationship;
- fiduciary role;
- emergency necessity.

Mere superior ability is the weakest ground.

# 3.9 Risk distribution and third-party externalities

Who pays if the plan fails?

Hidden authorship is ethically worse when the planner remains insulated while:

- classmates;
- romantic partners;
- rumor targets;
- weaker students;
- or an entire class

bear the downside.

# 3.10 Dependency created

Does assistance make the subject:

- more capable of independent relation;
- more plurally supported;
- or more dependent upon the intervener?

The strongest protection reduces monopoly.

# 3.11 Transparency, provenance, and answerability

Can the affected person or community:

- reconstruct what happened;
- identify who decided;
- demand reasons;
- challenge the account;
- preserve evidence;
- correct the actor?

Year 2 strengthens provenance into an ethical good because hidden administrative or strategic authors can otherwise deny responsibility.

# 3.12 Reversibility and repair

Can the harm be repaired?

A lost point is not equivalent to:

- public trauma exposure;
- expulsion;
- destroyed trust;
- physical injury;
- permanent political withdrawal.

The less reversible the consequence, the higher the justification threshold.

# 3.13 Temporal authorship — NEW

Who controls **when** the subject receives the information or choice?

Year 2 shows that timing can itself create jurisdiction.

Examples:

- Ayanokōji reserves the timing of Ichinose's collapse;
- Kei learns the breakup timetable only when the termination is executed;
- Horikita learns the class transfer only after it is effectively decided;
- Sakayanagi receives the clue only at the point where the loss decision becomes immediately actionable.

A choice offered too late can be formally real and substantively pre-authored.

# 3.14 Separation and succession — NEW

Does development survive the intervener's absence?

This is ethically relevant because protection or teaching that makes the protector permanently indispensable is weaker than development that supports plural future agency.

But survival after separation is not sufficient.

A subject may continue by performing the exact answer the developer expected.

# 3.15 Counter-authorship / corrective standing — NEW

Can the affected person generate an answer the intervener did not supply and make the intervener respond to it?

This is the strongest Year-2 extension of the answer-back standard.

Counter-authorship does not erase earlier wrongs.

It demonstrates that the subject has not been reduced to the intervention's output.

# 4. Consent taxonomy at the Year-2 boundary

“Consent” is too coarse unless its object is specified.

# 4.1 Surface consent

The person agrees to the immediate act.

Example form:

> “Yes, I will participate in this meeting/exam/relationship.”

This says little about hidden purposes.

# 4.2 Informed consent

The person knows the material purpose, risk, alternatives, and likely consequences well enough to own the decision.

COTE rarely provides perfect informed consent because information asymmetry is a central dramatic mechanism.

# 4.3 Constrained consent

The person chooses among options whose costs are heavily unequal but not secretly manufactured.

Many exam decisions fall here.

# 4.4 Authored-choice consent

The person chooses genuinely, but another actor designed information, timing, dependency, or incentives to make one choice predictably attractive.

V12 is the canonical Year-2 field.

# 4.5 Retrospective appropriation

A person later adopts a path that was originally imposed.

Kushida is the cleanest example.

V05 preservation is nonconsensual.

V06 continued participation becomes self-authored:

> `私は私のためだけに１年半戦ってクラスに貢献する。`

The later appropriation makes the **ongoing project** more legitimate.

It does not retroactively legitimate the imposition.

# 4.6 Counter-authored consent

The person changes the offered terms and produces a new agreement the stronger actor did not supply.

Ichinose's V12.5 third answer is the strongest boundary case.

# 5. Protection and ownership — Year-2 refinement

The Year-1 distinction becomes sharper.

Protection can mean:

- preventing immediate injury;
- preserving institutional membership;
- providing information;
- maintaining reputation;
- absorbing political cost;
- preserving another person's future options.

Protection becomes ownership when the protector converts superior access into an ongoing claim over the protected person's choices.

The strongest Year-2 protection test asks:

> **After I make you safer, are you more able to disagree with me?**

Positive cases include:

- Satō remaining beside Kei while disagreeing with Ayanokōji;
- Horikita accepting Kushida's self-interested reason rather than demanding gratitude;
- bounded witness/provenance architectures that allow communities to challenge stronger actors;
- Ayanokōji's V11 message to Amasawa opening a future without specifying the future's content.

Negative or mixed cases include:

- Ayanokōji becoming Kei's psychological pillar while withholding decisive information;
- Ayanokōji controlling Ichinose's developmental timing;
- Nagumo making mobility depend upon centralized patronage;
- Atsuomi designing opportunity as a means of testing and personality formation.

The Year-2 rule is:

> **Protection is ethically strongest when it pluralizes support and weakens the protector's monopoly.**

# 6. Developmental intervention — from teaching to jurisdiction

Ayanokōji is the central test because he possesses exceptional epistemic power.

He can often identify:

- what a person fears;
- what motivates them;
- which environmental pressure will expose a trait;
- which relationship can move them;
- when intervention will have maximal effect.

The ethical temptation is obvious:

> if the diagnosis is accurate and the outcome beneficial, why not intervene?

Year 2 answers:

because accuracy does not settle **jurisdiction**.

The same environmental-authorship capacity can produce:

- teaching;
- protection;
- distributed agency;
- staging;
- culling;
- emotional dependency;
- cognitive sabotage;
- or separation.

The moral question must therefore follow the **method**, not merely the result.

# 7. Ayanokōji — the ethics of environmental authorship

Ayanokōji's Year-2 ethical state cannot be summarized as “becoming nicer” or “becoming more manipulative.”

Both humanization and control capacity increase.

## 7.1 Positive development: he becomes capable of lower-jurisdiction relations

The strongest evidence includes:

- allowing Horikita decisions he considers irrational to remain consequential;
- reducing first-line intervention so others must solve problems;
- explicitly refusing credit when Sudō reaches an answer himself;
- participant-mode ordinary relation with Hiyori;
- recognizing a stopping point in V11;
- desiring unmodeled answers and unexpected defeat;
- acknowledging that other people can generate discoveries beyond his analysis.

These are genuine ethical resources.

## 7.2 Negative continuity: information asymmetry remains a power technology

He repeatedly controls:

- what another person knows;
- when they know it;
- what alternatives appear viable;
- how much of his motive is visible;
- and whether he remains accountable for the environment he built.

## 7.3 V03 — information architecture as development

Ayanokōji withholds a high-probability rescue plan from Ike because hope might reduce determination.

Ike's eventual words remain his own.

Ayanokōji nevertheless authors part of the motivational environment.

This is a relatively mild but important form of paternalism.

## 7.4 V05 — developmental authorship has a culling branch

Once the class locks itself into the expulsion branch, Ayanokōji converts OAA, social network, self-assertion, and expected future contribution into a disposability model.

Airi's final request is sincere.

The environment is not neutral.

Ayanokōji knows:

- her guilt;
- attachment to Haruka;
- weak self-assertion;
- and the special authority his judgment carries for her.

The strongest ethical formulation remains:

> **Airi authors the final act, but Ayanokōji authors much of the choice environment in which that act becomes psychologically unavoidable.**

## 7.5 V07 — stage-setting can preserve agency and diffuse accountability

The Yagami operation is not ordinary coercion.

Ayanokōji designs a test field in which multiple actors act from their own motives.

Yagami's guilt is real.

The evidence produced by victims, Horikita, Ryūen, Nagumo, teachers, and others is not fabricated.

Yet the hidden stage-setter deliberately creates the convergence.

The moral danger of staged agency is that the designer can say:

> “I did not make them choose.”

while omitting:

> “I made this the environment in which their characteristic choice would expose them.”

## 7.6 V08 — protective comfort plus timing jurisdiction

Ayanokōji's support for Ichinose is genuine.

So is the internal claim that the time and place of her student “life or death” are not entirely hers.

The problem is not fake care.

It is **jurisdiction over when another person is permitted to break**.

## 7.7 V09.5 — pleasure in manipulation raises the internal-restraint problem

Ayanokōji recognizes that manipulating through speech can be fun.

The ethical significance is not that pleasure automatically makes every intervention sadistic.

It is that the actor's own appetite can now reward the exercise of control.

This increases the importance of external answerability and self-imposed stopping rules.

## 7.8 V11 — bounded intervention is the strongest positive local model

With Yamamura/Sakayanagi and Amasawa, he identifies a problem, opens a route, and states a boundary beyond which the answer is no longer his.

This is the clearest Year-2 alternative to total curriculum.

## 7.9 V12 — relational jurisdiction is the strongest negative model

Ayanokōji demonstrates that formal freedom can be used as a control surface.

Maezono lies.

Ichinose agrees to the bargain she understands.

Sakayanagi chooses to lose.

Agency remains real.

But Ayanokōji controls enough of the relevant:

- information;
- incentives;
- timing;
- trust;
- and inferred preference

that the final acts become highly predictable.

The ethical burden therefore shifts from:

> “Did he command them?”

to:

> **“How much of the choice architecture did he author, and could the affected person meaningfully answer the hidden author before the consequence became irreversible?”**

## 7.10 V12.5 — separation does not settle the ethics of the curriculum

Ayanokōji hopes Kei will become more independent.

She performs independence partly because she believes that is what he expects.

He wants Horikita's class to function without him.

She is not informed in time to participate in the transfer's meaning.

He wants Ichinose to recover.

Her strongest recovery is the answer he did not supply.

The ethical hierarchy becomes:

`beneficial outcome < functional independence < informed self-authorship < counter-authorship`

# 8. Horikita — public accountability, paternalism, and increasing answerability

Horikita is the strongest Year-2 countermodel to hidden sovereignty, but not a morally clean one.

## 8.1 Strength: reasons become public and contestable

Her leadership increasingly operates through:

- visible authority;
- explicit reasons;
- negotiation;
- delegated expertise;
- bounded contracts;
- acceptance of correction.

This makes error more corrigible.

## 8.2 V05 Kushida preservation — independent judgment with serious jurisdiction problem

Horikita independently rejects Ayanokōji's intended Kushida expulsion.

That is strong self-authorship.

It is not automatically legitimate toward Kushida or Airi.

Kushida does not consent to preservation.

The class is already locked into an expulsion requirement.

Saving Kushida necessarily transfers the terminal burden elsewhere.

Horikita therefore authors a morally serious gamble with third-party consequences.

## 8.3 V06 — appropriation improves the ongoing arrangement

The ethical structure changes when Kushida chooses her own reason to continue.

Horikita no longer requires:

- gratitude;
- affection;
- ideological reconciliation.

She accepts self-interested cooperation.

This is one of Year 2's strongest examples of **adversarial reciprocity**.

## 8.4 Developmental humility

Horikita becomes better at distinguishing:

- her judgment;
- another person's domain;
- the possibility she is wrong.

This does not make her non-paternalistic.

It makes paternalism more answerable because the person affected can increasingly contest the leader.

# 9. Kei — agency inside dependency

Kei remains the canonical warning against binary autonomy language.

## 9.1 Genuine growth

Year 2 strengthens:

- study persistence;
- public functioning under romantic stress;
- horizontal friendship with Satō;
- willingness to confront Ayanokōji;
- post-breakup behavioral self-control.

## 9.2 Dependency remains real

Ayanokōji becomes her `完全に精神的な柱` in V08.

The relationship remains vertically informationally asymmetric.

## 9.3 V12.5 — behaving independently is not the same as independently authoring the norm

Kei wants to cling and bargain.

She restrains herself partly because she believes Ayanokōji expects her to become able to stand alone.

That is real agency.

It is also a case where the developer's expected answer has been internalized.

The correct Year-2 judgment is:

> **behavioral independence can coexist with emotional dependency and norm-level authorship by the person being left.**

# 10. Kushida — nonconsensual preservation becomes self-authored cooperation

Kushida provides one of the clearest distinctions between **imposition** and **appropriation**.

V05:

- Horikita preserves her against her preference;
- the cost is displaced onto Airi;
- public/private identity collapses.

V06:

- Kushida chooses continued participation;
- states a self-interested motive;
- accepts bounded secrecy/cooperation;
- does not promise affection.

Ethically, the second act improves the continuing arrangement without cleansing the first.

This yields a general rule:

> **A person can make an imposed future partly their own without thereby consenting to having been imposed upon.**

# 11. Hirata — non-disposability, dirty hands, and the right to name overreach

Hirata's Year-1 ethics risked paralysis because universal preservation made discriminating judgment nearly impossible under forced scarcity.

Year 2 does not “correct” him by proving disposability good.

It makes his ethic more politically mature.

He can:

- remain functional through painful outcomes;
- mediate without demanding total control;
- trust another person's domain;
- and later identify jurisdictional overreach.

His V12.5 `越権行為` diagnosis is especially important.

It distinguishes:

- exam permission;
- representative authority;
- moral authorization.

Hirata becomes one of the corpus's strongest voices for the principle:

> **someone may need to decide under scarcity, but necessity does not erase the need to justify who had the standing to decide.**

# 12. Ryūen — legitimacy without protected dissent remains ethically incomplete

Ryūen develops substantially.

He gains:

- recruitment;
- delegation;
- contracts;
- model updating;
- voluntary follower loyalty;
- a more demanding concept of victory.

These changes matter ethically.

They do not abolish coercive jurisdiction.

Tokitō remains the strongest negative control.

Dissent can exist.

It is not institutionally protected from bodily coercion.

The mature judgment is:

> **voluntary loyalty can make authoritarian rule more sociologically legitimate without making it ethically answerable enough.**

The missing component is not follower affection.

It is protected corrective standing.

# 13. Ichinose — solidarity, vulnerability, and the strongest counter-authorship result

Ichinose's Year-2 ethical importance lies in refusing two false binaries.

## 13.1 Solidarity versus competence

Her class can become more strategically opaque without abandoning its anti-disposability identity.

Humane commitment is not disproven by earlier vulnerability.

## 13.2 Attachment versus autonomy

Ichinose can be intensely attached to Ayanokōji and still become the strongest person to answer his developmental model back.

V12 is ethically severe because Ayanokōji uses trust as a cognitive vulnerability.

V12.5 does not retroactively legitimate that attack.

Her third answer instead demonstrates that the target of manipulation can regain standing by changing the terms of relation.

The terminal result is:

> **counter-authorship is stronger autonomy evidence than emotional detachment.**

# 14. Sakayanagi — superior perception, fiduciary duty, and relational veto

Sakayanagi's Year-2 ethics center on the danger of converting accurate judgment into sovereign jurisdiction.

## 14.1 Kamuro — optimization under-describes irreplaceability

Sakayanagi's V10 decision is not irrational in a narrow strategic sense.

The error is that her model fails to represent relational value until the loss is irreversible.

The ethical lesson is not “never optimize.”

It is:

> **a contribution model must remain corrigible by forms of value it does not currently encode.**

## 14.2 V12 — love becomes relational veto

Sakayanagi has formal freedom to win.

Ayanokōji does not command her.

She infers his preference and chooses to satisfy it.

Her agency is strong.

The relation is nevertheless ethically distorted because his unspoken preference overrides fiduciary responsibility to an entire class.

## 14.3 V12.5 — personal self-authorship can coexist with political externality

Sakayanagi can turn school defeat into a self-authored future outside ANHS.

# 15. Kōenji — autonomy without universal obligation

Kōenji is indispensable because he demonstrates that exceptional capacity does not automatically become collective property.

The ethical questions are:

- what obligations does membership create?
- when is a gift a genuine benefit versus a purchase of future compliance?
- can refusal be legitimate even when cooperation would help others?

His Year-2 bargain converts undefined conflict into bounded obligation.

V10 adds an important distinction between:

- unsolicited benevolence;
- strategic assistance offered to purchase future compliance.

Kōenji's autonomy is not presented as complete moral virtue.

He can be indifferent to weaker others.

But he remains a standing critique of the proposition:

> **“You are able, therefore we own your ability.”**

# 16. Nagumo — mobility versus patronage

Nagumo's meritocratic project contains a genuine emancipatory component.

It can create mobility unavailable under strict class fate.

But the mechanism depends upon:

- centralized custody of points;
- ranking;
- personal allocation;
- obedience;
- revocability.

The ethical distinction is therefore:

> **opportunity can be real while the institution granting it remains patronal.**

V11's self-financed wager is a constructive contrast.

A personal desire for validation is ethically cleaner when the actor bears the cost rather than externalizing it to dependent third parties.

# 17. Expulsion and disposability — Year-2 escalation

> **institution creates scarcity -> class selects a criterion -> strategist models disposability -> target consents under pressure -> collective vote records removal**

# 17.3 V10 — protection scarcity is not a personhood ranking

It does not establish total human value.

# 17.4 V12 / V12.5 — Maezono and manufactured terminality

Airi's Shizuku future is the strongest evidence.

> **removal from a competitive system does not erase the person's future as a moral object.**

# 18. Violence and force — through-Year-2 taxonomy

The ethics ledger distinguishes seven categories.

# 18.1 Immediate defensive force

Used to stop imminent physical harm.

This receives the strongest presumptive justification when proportionate.

# 18.2 Coercive violence

Used to compel obedience or submission.

Primary Year-2 examples include Hōsen and Ryūen's treatment of dissent/opponents.

# 18.3 Punitive / retaliatory violence

Used to impose suffering for a prior wrong or assertion of status.

Requires separate justification from defense.

# 18.4 Disciplinary violence

Violence used to regulate an internal subordinate population.

Ryūen/Tokitō is a key late-Year2 negative case.

# 18.5 Threatened violence / intimidation

The threatened harm may achieve control without actual injury.

The ethical burden remains because refusal is shaped by credible coercive consequence.

# 18.6 Institutional exposure to foreseeable harm

An institution or administrator creates/permits a setting in which serious harm becomes a predictable means of producing an outcome.

Tsukishiro/Shiba are especially important because administrative authority can become an instrument of physical danger.

# 18.7 Nonphysical coercion and relational attack

Do not casually call every manipulative act “violence.”

Year 2 nevertheless contains severe nonphysical harms:

- cognitive destabilization;
- reputational manipulation;
- engineered dependency;
- information withholding;
- relational betrayal used instrumentally;
- procedural expulsion.

These require ethical analysis without collapsing them into bodily assault.

# 25. Ethics of measurement and merit

The ability ledger owns measurement ontology.

The ethical conclusion is narrower.

A measurement becomes morally dangerous when it silently migrates from:

V05 demonstrates the danger most severely.

> **A merit model should inform coordination without becoming a theory of deserved personhood.**

# 26. Privacy, truth, and informational harm

Year 2 strengthens privacy from a personal preference into a political ethical dimension.

Information can:

- protect;
- expose fraud;
- enable consent;
- prevent manipulation.

It can also:

- create leverage;
- price vulnerability;
- destroy trust;
- make refusal impossible;
- convert intimacy into control.

The mature distinction is not “secrecy bad / transparency good.”

It is:

> **Who needs which information to make which decision answerably?**

Ayanokōji's central relational failure is not that he keeps any secret.

It is that he withholds information **material to other people's decisions about their own relationship or future**.

`Y1H-004` therefore remains `STILL_OPEN` at the Year-2 boundary.

# 27. Responsibility under authored environments

Year 2 requires a layered responsibility model.

Use four levels.

## 27.1 Act responsibility

Who performed the terminal act?

## 27.2 Choice-environment responsibility

Who made the alternatives, incentives, or timing take their present form?

## 27.3 Rule responsibility

Who authored the institutional structure within which the act became valuable or mandatory?

## 27.4 Record / narrative responsibility

Who controls what later observers will believe happened?

This prevents two opposite errors:

### Error A — total puppet theory

> “The hidden strategist caused everything, therefore everyone else had no agency.”

False.

### Error B — formal-choice absolution

> “The target chose, therefore the hidden strategist has no responsibility.”

Also false.

The correct model is:

> **agency and authored conditions can coexist; responsibility must be distributed accordingly.**

# 31. Canonical through-Year-2 synthesis

Year 1 asked whether superior understanding gives a stronger person the right to intervene.

Its answer was already restrictive:

> **No. Accuracy and benefit matter, but authority still requires necessity, proportionality, information, refusal, accountability, and respect for the subject's ability to answer back.**

Year 2 makes the problem harder because superior power becomes less visibly coercive.

Ayanokōji increasingly learns that direct orders are crude.

If he understands someone well enough, he can alter:

- the information they possess;
- the time at which they receive it;
- the social cost of refusal;
- the alternatives they believe exist;
- the meaning of a relationship;
- and the institutional rule under which their choice will matter.

Then the other person can choose.

That choice is real.

The hidden authorship is real too.

The completed Year-2 corpus therefore rejects two moral shortcuts.

The first shortcut says:

> **“They chose, so the designer is innocent.”**

The second says:

> **“The environment was designed, so the chooser had no agency.”**

Both fail.

Maezono lies.

Sakayanagi chooses.

Kei refuses to cling.

Kushida chooses to continue.

Ichinose creates a third answer.

The ethical task is to distribute responsibility without erasing authorship at either level.

Year 2 also demonstrates that asymmetry can become more ethical without becoming equal.

Horikita can remain leader while accepting correction.

Kushida can hate Horikita while cooperating on self-owned terms.

Kei can remain attached while building horizontal support.

Hirata can care without claiming universal jurisdiction.

Kōenji can owe bounded obligations without surrendering ownership of his capacity.

Hiyori can matter to Ryūen without possessing office.

Ichinose can love someone while forcing that person to answer a solution he did not design.

These relationships suggest that the alternative to domination is not independence from all influence.

It is **answerable interdependence**.

The strongest form of answerable interdependence contains:

- material information before the decision;
- meaningful refusal;
- more than one source of support;
- bounded authority;
- public or reconstructable reasons;
- proportionate methods;
- risk borne by decision-makers as well as subjects;
- room for dissent;
- repair where possible;
- and a future in which the subject can become something the helper did not plan.

That last condition is the Year-2 addition that matters most.

A developer can create a competent successor and still remain the hidden author of the successor's norm.

A protector can leave and still organize the protected person's behavior through an internalized expectation.

A leader can permit choice and still control the field so tightly that the choice becomes a predictable instrument.

The strongest evidence of autonomy is therefore not simple persistence after separation.

It is **counter-authorship**:

> the ability to produce an answer the stronger actor did not supply, to make that answer consequential, and to require the stronger actor to respond to it as another person's claim rather than absorb it as merely the successful completion of the original plan.

The terminal ethical principle through Year 2 is therefore:

> **Power is most legitimate when superior knowledge becomes a reason for greater restraint rather than greater jurisdiction: when the powerful actor can help without manufacturing necessity, disclose before timing becomes coercive, preserve refusal, accept correction, share risk, weaken dependency monopoly, and finally stop at the point where the other person's answer belongs to the other person.**

And the terminal warning is its mirror:

> **The most sophisticated domination in Year 2 does not eliminate choice. It learns how to use choice.**

That is why the Year-1 answer-back standard survives the entire second year.

It simply becomes harder to satisfy.
