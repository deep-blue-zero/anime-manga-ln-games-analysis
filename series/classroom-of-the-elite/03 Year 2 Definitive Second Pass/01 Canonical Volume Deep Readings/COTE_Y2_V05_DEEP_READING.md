# 『ようこそ実力至上主義の教室へ ２年生編5』
## Year 2 Volume 5 — Second-Pass Deep Reading
### Responsibility, unanimity, and the constitutional politics of sacrifice

> **Local analytical boundary:** this document uses the complete original Japanese Year 2 Volume 5, the frozen Year 1 V2 corpus, and canonical Y2V01–Y2V04.5 as inherited state. It deliberately does **not** use Y2V06 or later material, Volume 0, *Second List*, Year 3, adaptations, wikis, or remembered later outcomes to settle local claims. Later consequences of the Unanimous Vote Examination are therefore inadmissible here. Historical first-pass Year-2 analysis appears only in the explicit revision-audit section and is never treated as evidence.

# 1. Scope, provenance, and inherited analytical state

`Y2V05` is the sixth canonical sequential artifact in the Year 2 definitive second pass. The Japanese EPUB was read completely in source order through the afterword and publication matter before targeted retrieval. The source exactly matches the Phase-0 lock: **2,155,499 bytes**, SHA-256 `829bf63db3496f654d8d9c871d0fbbacfd699d169eb0cc4d17235faadf24c719`. The deterministic text layer contains **3,825 paragraph records** in the namespace `COTE:Y2V05:spineXX:paraYYYY`. Narrative illustration evidence is kept separate from prose evidence; small gaiji glyph images are not promoted as visual evidence.

The inherited Year-2 conceptual progression is:

> **V01 — legibility → V02 — jurisdiction → V03 — attribution → V04 — sovereignty / counter-authorship → V04.5 — embeddedness.**

V04.5 ended with Ayanokōji becoming increasingly embedded in friendships, romance, class purpose, rival networks, teacher history, and public reputation. It simultaneously showed that embeddedness does not automatically humanize power: Kei is genuinely loved and privately made part of a `恋愛のカリキュラム`; Nagumo turns mobility into patronage; Ichinose-class trust risks becoming conformity; and the White Room language of designed development remains active inside ordinary life.

V05 asks what follows once an embedded person must participate in **collective injury**.

The volume opens and closes on two radically different meanings of responsibility. Chabashira Sae has spent roughly eleven years frozen by a choice she could not complete. At the climax, Horikita, Ayanokōji, Kushida, Airi, Haruka, Hirata, and the rest of the class are each forced to occupy a different position within a decision that can no longer leave everyone unharmed. No one can simply remain an observer.

The governing question is therefore:

> **When every meaningful collective choice imposes a cost on someone, who becomes answerable for choosing, persuading, vetoing, exposing, sacrificing, or refusing—and what makes that burden legitimate?**

This is a transition from **embeddedness** to **responsibility**.

# 2. Executive thesis — embeddedness becomes responsibility

Year 2 Volume 5 is a constitutional tragedy disguised as a school special examination.

The `満場一致特別試験` — the Unanimous Vote Special Examination — is formally built to maximize individual independence. Votes are secret. The school repeatedly emphasizes anonymity. Binding vote contracts are forbidden. Outside coercion can itself trigger punishment. Each student must personally cast a ballot, and the room is configured so classmates cannot simply inspect one another’s decisions. Chabashira explicitly states that the system prioritizes `生徒個人の意思`, the individual student’s will. `COTE:Y2V05:spine12:para0135–0208`; `spine13:para0109–0120`.

Yet that protected individual will is placed inside an institution that demands **unanimity**. Five questions must be resolved within five hours. Failure costs 300 class points. The final question creates a particularly vicious structure: the class may gain 100 class points if one member is expelled; if YES becomes unanimous, some expulsion must actually occur. Protect Points are disabled. Once the class locks the YES route, it cannot preserve everyone. `spine13:para0465–0490`; `spine17:para0210–0235`.

The examination therefore produces a paradox:

> **maximum private freedom at the level of the ballot, maximum collective coercion at the level of the required outcome.**

A leader may persuade but cannot legitimately compel. A dissenter may veto but cannot avoid imposing the cost of that veto on everyone. A majority cannot claim that sheer numbers make it morally entitled to victory. A minority cannot claim that anonymity makes its obstruction politically consequence-free. The school protects the person from direct social surveillance while forcing the class to confront what individual freedom means when its exercise can cost hundreds of class points or another person’s membership.

The four classes become four constitutional models.

**Ichinose’s class** treats solidarity as a substantive constitutional principle. Kanzaki alone votes YES because he believes the class’s refusal to contemplate sacrifice reveals dangerous `正常性バイアス`. His diagnosis of strategic weakness has force, but he lacks the political legitimacy to transform the class’s norm. Ichinose’s answer is not merely sentimental: Class A reached by sacrificing a friend would be a victory emptied of the class’s reason for pursuing it. They choose NO unanimously. `spine14:para0001–0186`.

**Ryūen’s class** contains real dissent, fear, manipulation, and increasingly sophisticated preservation of opposition. Ryūen first allows internal preferences to surface, then uses false YES ballots—coordinated through Hiyori and allied actors—to identify the true dissenter. Tokitō openly demands that Ryūen step down. Ryūen threatens and humiliates, but he does not simply purge Tokitō when he could. Katsuragi reads the maneuver as leaving Tokitō a path back into the polity. The result is authoritarian **managed dissent**, not democratic pluralism—but it is a real development from a class in which disagreement once meant domination or disposal. `spine15:para0272–0419`; `para0461–0473`.

**Sakayanagi’s class** reaches unanimity almost immediately because elite agenda control pre-solves the decision. Sakayanagi calculates that 100 class points are not worth the loss of a useful member, income, morale, distrust, and future optionality. She says she will not discard companions who work for her; the narration immediately qualifies that statement as strategic rather than categorical. Under genuine danger, she would sacrifice. This is stable consensus produced by aristocratic optimization, not proof of egalitarian deliberation. `spine16:para0001–0033`.

**Horikita’s class** becomes the tragedy because it combines all of the contradictions at once:

- a legitimate visible leader;
- a hidden Ayanokōji support architecture;
- a prior promise to Kōenji that Horikita refuses to break;
- a dissenter using anonymity to force the expulsion route;
- Hirata’s old universal-preservation ethic under extreme pressure;
- Kushida’s secret/public identity collapse;
- Horikita’s independent refusal to accept Ayanokōji’s intended solution;
- and Ayanokōji’s conversion of OAA and social ties into a ranking of who is most disposable.

The volume’s decisive transition occurs when Horikita chooses to **preserve Kushida**.

Ayanokōji had judged Kushida impossible to reconcile and planned to expel her. Horikita does not defer. She reasons from her own view of Kushida’s rare social capabilities, declares that expelling her is not the correct answer, and wagers her own leadership on making Kushida’s strengths useful. Ayanokōji recognizes that Horikita has reached a stage beyond his expectation and accepts the divergence. `spine17:para0727–0830`.

This is one of Year 2’s strongest positive developmental events.

It is also what makes the volume ethically devastating.

Horikita’s rescue of Kushida does **not** rescue the class from the sacrifice structure. The class has already unanimously chosen YES. Someone still has to leave. Ayanokōji immediately prevents Horikita from becoming the visible executioner and substitutes a criterion he can present as fair: current measurable value, with OAA as the most legible baseline and current social relationships as additional evidence. Sakura Airi is lowest. `spine17:para0831–0910`.

The result completes an arc begun in V01.

> **V01: OAA makes a person institutionally legible.**
>
> **V05: institutional legibility becomes a disposability technology under scarcity.**

Ayanokōji does not literally claim that the file *is* the person. He supplements OAA with social network, self-assertion, growth probability, and the likelihood that others will protect a candidate. But that makes the sorting system more comprehensive, not necessarily more humane. He converts a complex person into a decision-relevant model precise enough to identify whom the class can most easily survive losing.

Airi’s final consent therefore cannot be treated as simple free choice.

She genuinely chooses to ask Haruka to vote for her expulsion. She genuinely takes responsibility for the class. Her final transformed appearance—glasses removed, hair deliberately styled, smiling as the self she had only begun to imagine becoming—is an act of dignity. `spine17:para1079–1103`.

But Ayanokōji has also explicitly modeled the psychology that will make that choice possible. He knows Airi’s weak self-assertion, class guilt, attachment to Haruka, and the special weight his judgment carries for her. He refuses Haruka’s substitution precisely because changing the criterion from ability to voluntary martyrdom would destroy the rule he has imposed. He then uses his knowledge to make Airi herself break Haruka’s resistance. `spine17:para0930–1045`.

The correct formulation is therefore:

> **Airi authors the final act, but Ayanokōji authors much of the choice environment in which that act becomes psychologically unavoidable.**

This is the terminal-stakes version of a problem already visible in V03 with Ike: authentic agency can exist inside a hidden developmental environment. V05 demonstrates that environmental authorship is not only capable of cultivating people. It can also be used to organize **elimination**.

The final White Room comparison makes the warning explicit. Watching Airi leave, Ayanokōji recalls defeated children in the White Room and concludes that losers regret only after it is too late; in that respect, the White Room and this school are no different. `spine17:para1095–1103`.

ANHS remains profoundly different from the White Room in pluralism, friendship, legal procedure, exit, adult counterweights, and the possibility of self-authored relationships. But V05 destroys any easy thesis that ordinary school life is ethically innocent merely because it is socially rich.

The stronger Year-2 proposition is:

> **Plurality creates the possibility of answerable development. It does not abolish sacrifice, hierarchy, or the temptation to rank human beings by institutional usefulness.**

# 3. Position in the Year 2 architecture — from embeddedness to responsibility

The six-volume progression can now be stated as a sequence of political questions:

| Work | Governing concept | Governing question |
|---|---|---|
| Y2V01 | **Legibility** | Who can be seen and measured? |
| Y2V02 | **Jurisdiction** | Who may bind another person’s future? |
| Y2V03 | **Attribution** | Who actually authored the visible outcome? |
| Y2V04 | **Sovereignty / counter-authorship** | Whose authored environment becomes binding when actors collide? |
| Y2V04.5 | **Embeddedness** | What changes when an isolated subject becomes socially situated and vulnerable to ties? |
| **Y2V05** | **Responsibility** | Who must answer for a collective choice once every available outcome harms someone? |

V05 contracts the spatial scale after V03–V04 expanded it.

The island arc distributed causality across geography, years, administrators, hidden agents, GPS data, and overlapping command systems. V05 removes almost all of that complexity. Students sit in one room. The ballots are counted correctly. The rule is visible. The teacher is present. There is no uncertainty about whether the institutional mechanism works.

The uncertainty is moral and constitutional.

This is an important formal reversal. V03 asked whether the visible record could reveal who caused an event. V05 makes the record nearly perfect—secret votes are accurately counted—while making responsibility **harder**, not easier, to assign.

A secret ballot can tell the school that one person dissents without telling the class who. A final unanimous vote can tell the institution that everyone selected an outcome without proving that everyone desired it equally. Airi’s 38–0 expulsion vote records formal unanimity after an environment of asymmetric information, grief, pressure, prior commitments, and authored psychology.

The novel therefore distinguishes:

> **accurate procedure from complete moral truth.**

This is a direct extension of `TRUTH_PROOF_RECORD` beyond document authenticity. The record may be correct and still fail to explain the political meaning of the act it records.

# 4. Narrative architecture — nine movements toward an authored wound

The volume is unusually concentrated. Its chapter structure reads almost like a constitutional casebook.

## 4.1 Chabashira Sae’s monologue — responsibility frozen in time

The opening is not merely foreshadowing. It establishes the adult endpoint of unresolved collective authorship.

Chabashira repeatedly dreams of the unanimous-vote exam her own class took roughly eleven years earlier. Her class was close to Class A. Six classmates had already left across the first two years, but the third year had remained intact. The final unanimous-vote problem broke that cohesion. The person whose sacrifice became decisive was not an abstract classmate: he was a close friend and newly formed romantic partner. Their relationship began and effectively ended inside the choice. `COTE:Y2V05:spine10:para0001–0036`.

The prologue establishes V05’s temporal structure:

> **an unresolved school decision can become an adult identity.**

Chabashira has not merely remembered a bad exam. She has organized eleven years of ambition, guilt, resentment, and pedagogy around a choice she never accepted as completed.

## 4.2 `波乱の足音` — ordinary generativity before coercion

The early culture-festival material matters because it shows the class producing value before the examination forces it to rank value.

Sudō studies through his own curriculum and explicitly wants to raise his OAA academics into the B range. His reduced lateness and sleeping are no longer Ayanokōji-authored tactical adjustments; development is increasingly self-owned. `spine11:para0003–0009`.

The maid-café proposal rises from Satō, Matsushita, Mii-chan, Maezono, and others with concrete thinking about staffing, costume, revenue, and customer demand. Horikita creates incentives for initiative and delegates management rather than insisting that all useful ideas originate from the leader. Ayanokōji is then assigned oversight precisely because his strengths differ from the enthusiasts’ domain knowledge. `spine11:para0081–0269`.

This is **generative ability** in class form: value emerges from heterogeneous members.

The contrast with the final exam is deliberate. A class that can create more because different people possess different kinds of usefulness is later forced to answer which person it can most afford to lose.

## 4.3 `２人の教師、運命の特別試験` — constitutional design

The exam rules establish protected private will and collective coercion simultaneously. `spine12:para0135–0208`.

The class then grants Horikita emergency leadership. This does not eliminate individual refusal because anonymity is institutionally protected. Ayanokōji explicitly warns that a 38–1 majority has no automatic entitlement to prevail: if the one persuades the 38, political legitimacy changes direction. `spine12:para0210–0285`.

He also creates a hidden succession layer around Horikita. Hirata and Kei are privately asked to infer her intent, support deliberation, and coordinate through covert cough/desk signals if necessary. The rationale is explicitly future-facing: Ayanokōji could be unavailable through illness, accident, or unexpected expulsion, so the class must develop flexible operational capacity that does not collapse with his absence. `spine12:para0452–0510`.

This is major `SUCCESSION_SEPARATION` evidence.

It is also another ethical contradiction: the class is being trained toward independence through a structure whose authorship is concealed from most of the people being made independent.

## 4.4 `暗雲` — protected will meets hidden pressure

Teacher-space reproduces student-space. Chabashira’s body relives the old examination. Hoshinomiya carries her own interpretation of that history and treats Chabashira’s failure as an unresolved moral claim on the present. The adults who administer the students’ “independent” choices are themselves historically embedded actors. `spine13:para0001–0135`.

The class clears four questions with increasingly sophisticated procedures—strategic opponent choice, fair lottery-like resolution of travel preference, explicit material investment in Horikita through a Protect Point, and acceptance of higher-risk exam conditions. These sections show that unanimity can be legitimate when the stakes are divisible, alternatives are contestable, and fair procedure supplies a loss everyone can accept. `spine13:para0136–0464`.

The fifth question changes the ontology of the decision because the object is no longer a policy. It is a person.

## 4.5 Ichinose Honami’s choice — solidarity as constitutional substance

Kanzaki supplies the lone YES. He wants the class to confront the possibility that unconditional preservation has become strategic self-deception. His language of `正常性バイアス` is not frivolous; the class has indeed lost points over time, and its assumption that there will always be a no-sacrifice route can become dangerous. `spine14:para0001–0065`.

But the political failure is equally real.

Kanzaki has spent too long acting as quiet adviser to possess the legitimacy required to force a constitutional transformation in one afternoon. His classmates do not experience his proposal as loyal opposition with a mandate. They experience it as a request to betray a constitutive norm.

Ichinose’s answer is that if a future exists in which the only route to Class A is abandoning a friend, the proper project is to become strong enough that the situation never arises. `spine14:para0066–0145`.

That answer may be strategically insufficient. It is nonetheless politically coherent. Class A is not an external prize whose value is independent of the way it is reached. For this class, solidarity partly defines what “winning” is supposed to mean.

Kanzaki eventually yields and judges himself unable to change the class. Himeno understands his concern but also recognizes that the current polity will not convert to YES. The final vote is 0 YES / 40 NO. `spine14:para0146–0186`.

The tragedy is not that Ichinose’s class has no dissent. It is that dissent lacks a legitimate institutional language.

## 4.6 Ryūen Kakeru’s choice — managed dissent

Ryūen’s class begins with far more strategic heterogeneity: 14 YES / 26 NO. He initially wants members’ `真意`, their actual intentions, to become visible through the anonymous result. `spine15:para0001–0100`.

When he later announces he will respect the majority’s NO preference, that choice matters. It is not democratization: he continues to intimidate people and identify targets through fear. But the leader publicly acknowledges that his own repeated YES vote does not by itself authorize the class outcome. `spine15:para0101–0220`.

Tokitō eventually declares himself the dissenter and demands that Ryūen resign. The confrontation looks like an authoritarian purge waiting to happen. Katsuragi changes the frame by asking a succession question: if Ryūen goes, who can actually govern? `spine15:para0292–0366`.

The later reveal is crucial. Several persistent YES votes were deliberately false, coordinated so that the real dissenter could be isolated. Hiyori, Albert, Katsuragi, and Ryūen are part of a structure that turns anonymous ballots into diagnostic pressure. Once Tokitō is exposed, Ryūen still leaves him a route back to NO rather than making expulsion inevitable. Katsuragi explicitly interprets this as a chance to survive. `spine15:para0382–0419`.

This is morally ambiguous institutional growth:

> **Ryūen is learning that opposition can be politically useful enough to preserve.**

Fear remains. Manipulation remains. But disagreement is no longer automatically synonymous with disloyalty deserving deletion.

## 4.7 Sakayanagi Arisu’s choice — elite agenda control

Sakayanagi’s class completes the problem with almost no drama. The initial split is partly prearranged to avoid accidental closure before discussion. Sakayanagi calculates the expected value of sacrifice and concludes that current conditions do not justify it. `spine16:para0001–0033`.

Her language of protecting companions is immediately qualified by narration: if class survival genuinely required expulsion, she would do it without hesitation.

The class’s unanimity is therefore not evidence that nobody is disposable. It is evidence that **the leader can currently define disposal as inefficient**.

Sakayanagi’s polity remains stable because most members accept her competence to pre-solve the relevant calculation.

## 4.8 Horikita Suzune’s choice — consequential independence

Horikita’s class becomes trapped at 1 YES / 38 NO after Kōenji is bought out of his economically rational YES vote. Horikita refuses Sudō’s suggestion that they break the prior Kōenji contract. This is a significant constitutional act: promise-keeping remains binding even when opportunistic breach could solve an immediate crisis. Kōenji explicitly reads that reliability as grounds for trust. `spine13:para0520–0615`.

The hidden dissenter’s preference eventually becomes legible through persistence. Ayanokōji realizes that the ranking is not ordinary `NO ≥ YES > timeout`, but something closer to `YES > timeout > NO`. The dissenter is willing to accept catastrophic class damage rather than surrender the right to force the expulsion route. `spine17:para0001–0095`.

Ayanokōji himself then experiences hesitation. He describes an irrational emotion, mentally invokes Horikita Manabu as a comparative standard, and gives one additional NO attempt before shifting into the YES coalition. This hesitation should not be romanticized into moral conversion; he still executes the strategy. But it is real evidence that embeddedness now produces friction inside what had once been near-automatic optimization. `spine17:para0086–0092`.

Hirata also develops. He recognizes that his own wish to preserve everyone cannot become a reason to impose catastrophic loss on the entire class. He remains distressed, but he stays functional and accepts the need to move forward. `spine17:para0178–0189`.

Once YES becomes unanimous, Kushida’s strategy collapses into public exposure. Ayanokōji converts years of hidden evidence—past middle-school history, non-aggression contract, recordings, prior betrayal, Ryūen collaboration—into a prosecutorial narrative whose cumulative effect destroys her public persona. `spine17:para0400–0726`.

Then Horikita refuses the expected ending.

Her interior monologue explicitly rejects simply entrusting the decision to Ayanokōji. She values his ability and respects him, but asks what **she** wants and what only she can do. `spine17:para0727–0755`.

She reaches the opposite conclusion from his planned strategy.

Kushida’s ability to listen, form ties, collect trust, and sustain social labor is rare. Horikita calls those abilities genuine strengths and says she will stake herself on making them useful. She will `絶対に生かしてみせる`. `spine17:para0760–0819`.

This is not Kushida-authored reconciliation. Kushida actively rejects it. It is not ethically clean. It is, however, **Horikita-authored policy**.

Ayanokōji had judged Kushida impossible to win over and planned elimination. Horikita contradicts him, sustains the contradiction, and changes the final outcome. He does not secretly restore his preferred policy. He accepts the divergence and recognizes her development. `spine17:para0820–0835`.

`Y1H-013` therefore reaches its first locally sufficient answer:

> **Horikita can consequentially disagree with Ayanokōji and sustain the disagreement.**

The answer is by **development**, not retrospective revelation.

## 4.9 `過去との決別` — the adult replay is finally interpreted

After the exam, Chabashira discloses the exact structure of her old failure. Her class had been 73 points behind Class A. They chose the YES route because 100 points looked like the final chance to overtake. Her newly formed romantic partner became the sacrifice candidate. She could not vote to expel him; he tried to protect her; the class timed out and lost 300. Class A took the opposite route, producing an effective swing of 450 and ending realistic competition. `spine18:para0001–0075`.

Ayanokōji’s most important response is procedural rather than accusatory. He argues that there was no perfect moral answer, but there was a strategic mistake: once insufficient time remained to complete a post-YES expulsion process, YES voters would have been forced back toward NO. The class might have preserved everyone and still collected the completion reward. `spine18:para0076–0097`.

Chabashira’s trauma therefore contains two layers:

1. grief over a person and relationship;
2. fixation on a false binary she never re-evaluated.

She asks whether Airi’s loss hurt Ayanokōji. He explains his selection with disturbing precision: current OAA, relationships, personality, class impact, and Airi’s likely willingness to accept sacrifice. He frames organizational leadership as sometimes requiring the burden of becoming `鬼` or `外道`. Chabashira asks how many people he has already “cut” to become capable of thinking this way. His narration returns to `人為淘汰`—human/artificial selection—in his past. `spine18:para0098–0122`.

The conversation ends with real Chabashira development. She decides to stop living as the student who failed eleven years earlier and to work, within a teacher’s proper bounds, for the students’ present goal. Ayanokōji describes her frozen heart as finally beginning to catch up with chronological time. `spine18:para0123–0131`.

Responsibility does not erase the past. It changes what one does with it.

# 5. The Unanimous Vote Examination as constitutional design

## 5.1 Anonymity is not a convenience; it is the protected right of dissent

The exam repeatedly insists on `完全匿名` / complete anonymity and on `生徒個人の意思`. That language establishes the student as a political unit prior to the class.

This matters because ANHS usually makes class identity materially coercive. Class points, monthly private income, advancement, and exam survival are shared. Here the institution deliberately creates a protected zone in which a student may oppose the collective without immediate social identification.

That is a real freedom.

The paradox is that the same institution then demands unanimity.

## 5.2 Unanimity converts every veto into collective externality

A 1–39 ballot gives one person enormous negative power. The dissenter cannot simply abstain from the collective. Their refusal consumes time shared by everyone and may eventually cost 300 class points.

The majority therefore experiences minority freedom as imposed harm.

Conversely, the minority experiences majority persuasion as pressure backed by shared institutional loss.

The exam does not resolve this tension. It makes the class **author a constitution under deadline**.

## 5.3 The anti-contract rule prevents the easiest commodification of conscience

Students cannot simply buy votes with private points or make enforceable agreements about how to cast them. This distinguishes the exam from V02’s explicit card/point markets.

The school intentionally removes ordinary exchange as a legitimate route to consensus.

Horikita’s arrangement with Kōenji is possible only because it concerns the *economic reason* for his preference and does not function as a school-enforceable ballot contract. She compensates him for the expected material loss of choosing NO. The legitimacy comes from voluntary performance and, more importantly, from her prior demonstrated reliability in honoring their island agreement.

## 5.4 The exam distinguishes procedure from moral absolution

A unanimous result is procedurally valid.

It does not mean all participants bear equal moral responsibility.

Airi’s final vote passes 38–0 because Airi has transformed the choice environment by asking Haruka to release her. Hirata votes within a class already committed to expulsion. Horikita has vetoed Kushida but does not select Airi. Ayanokōji supplies the criterion and psychological routing. Kushida created the deadlock that drove the class into YES but is then preserved against her will. Chabashira administers the procedure while her own trauma shapes the emotional field.

A single institutional record—`38–0`—therefore contains multiple forms of authorship.

This is V03’s attribution problem returning inside a perfectly functioning formal system.

# 6. Ayanokōji Kiyotaka — from developmental author to elimination architect

V05 requires keeping all four analytical layers active because the volume makes them contradict one another in unusually compressed form.

## 6.1 Ordinary adolescent layer — hesitation, friendship, and the cost of being embedded

The early volume continues ordinary embeddedness. Ayanokōji tells Sudō that Kei is his first girlfriend. Haruka confronts him over Airi’s feelings. He accepts ordinary social obligations around the cultural festival and works within peer-generated proposals. `spine11:para0017–0075`; `spine12:para0285–0365`.

The decisive evidence, however, is the hesitation before he commits to the YES route.

He knows the strategic logic. Time is expiring. The hidden dissenter will accept timeout. Yet he gives one more NO attempt and describes an emotion that does not fit clean rationality. `spine17:para0086–0092`.

The importance is modest but real:

> **relationships now create internal decision cost.**

That is different from merely knowing that other people will feel pain.

## 6.2 Relational layer — Airi is not emotionally “nothing”

The later selection of Airi should not be misread as proof that Ayanokōji never valued her.

He knows her growth, her affection, her friendships, her desire to change, and the specific meaning his judgment carries for her. The very effectiveness of his strategy depends on intimate relational knowledge.

Horikita later suggests he need not force himself to act cold. Ayanokōji starts to deny this and then chooses to leave the possibility open, saying perhaps he is suppressing painful feeling. The narration does not settle whether this is genuine self-recognition, strategic compliance, or unresolved introspection. `spine17:para1065–1079`.

This ambiguity should be preserved.

The stronger claim is:

> **V05 separates personal value from political protection.**

Ayanokōji may value someone and still decide that their continued membership is the least defensible under a forced utility comparison.

That is more disturbing than simple indifference.

## 6.3 Strategic layer — evidence conversion and coalition manufacture

The hidden YES voter forces Ayanokōji to infer preference ranking from repeated ballots. He distinguishes someone who merely likes the +100 reward from someone willing to accept timeout in order to retain the right to expel. `spine13:para0616–0867`; `spine17:para0001–0095`.

He then engineers a strategic sequence:

1. gives one last NO attempt;
2. privately shifts YES;
3. expands the YES coalition;
4. later publicly identifies himself as a YES voter;
5. offers a safety rule: only the original dissenter will be targeted;
6. converts Kushida’s hidden history into public proof;
7. after Horikita vetoes Kushida, installs a measurable ranking criterion;
8. anticipates Haruka’s resistance;
9. refuses voluntary-substitution logic;
10. uses Airi’s own moral psychology to break the final blocking vote.

This is strategic excellence.

It is also **constitutional authorship**: he does not merely exploit the rules; he creates the class’s operative decision procedure inside them.

## 6.4 Environmental-author layer — succession and independence

Before the crisis, Ayanokōji trains Kei and Hirata as an invisible support network around Horikita because he may someday be absent. `spine12:para0452–0510`.

This is one of the clearest positive versions of his authorship so far. The explicit goal is not to keep himself indispensable. It is to create a class capable of functioning without him.

That moves the `SUCCESSION_SEPARATION` thread forward even though `Y1H-006` remains open because no mature legacy or successor identity exists yet.

## 6.5 Environmental-author layer — elimination threshold

The Airi sequence marks a qualitative escalation.

Earlier Year-2 authorship had been predominantly:

- defensive;
- developmental;
- motivational;
- informational;
- or procedural.

V05 demonstrates that the same cognitive toolkit can produce **culling**.

Ayanokōji defines the admissible criterion, ranks candidates, predicts reactions, prevents criterion drift, and induces the chosen target to produce the consent necessary to complete the expulsion.

This should not be euphemized as “making a hard decision.”

The distinctive danger is that he can make a hard decision **legible as a system**.

## 6.6 The `鬼 / 外道` burden is self-description and institutional theory

In the conversation with Chabashira, Ayanokōji treats leadership as sometimes requiring someone to become the demon or outsider who accepts moral contamination on behalf of the organization.

This is not identical to endorsement of cruelty.

It is a theory of responsibility in which the leader’s role includes doing what distributed ordinary morality may be unable to coordinate.

The problem is that such a theory easily becomes self-authorizing. The person willing to bear guilt may also be the person who decides that others must bear the material loss.

V05 therefore sharpens the project’s standing ethical test:

> **Is asymmetrical competence answerable to those whose lives it reorganizes?**

Airi’s expulsion gives the most troubling answer so far.

# 7. Horikita Suzune — legitimate leadership, independent judgment, transferred danger

## 7.1 The class publicly recognizes her as a political asset

Question 3 offers one of the cleanest indicators of Horikita’s changed status. Keisei argues that the Protect Point should go to Horikita because losing the leader would impose unusually high collective cost. Kōenji agrees. The class unanimously invests the protection in her. `spine13:para0350–0435`.

This is not intimacy.

Later, when Horikita herself becomes an expulsion candidate, **16 classmates vote YES to expel her**. `spine17:para0300–0360`.

The two ballots together are analytically valuable:

> **institutional leadership legitimacy ≠ personal irreplaceability.**

Horikita can be widely trusted as the class’s best governor without being universally loved or protected for her own sake.

## 7.2 Promise-keeping with Kōenji is constitutional rather than tactical

Sudō proposes breaking the earlier Kōenji bargain when Kōenji votes YES for the 100-point reward. Horikita refuses.

She does not claim class necessity retroactively voids the agreement.

Instead she bargains with the actual economic reason for his vote and pays for the change. Kōenji explicitly recognizes her reliability as the reason he can trust the new arrangement. `spine13:para0520–0615`.

This is a strong answer to the recurring question of what membership legitimately requires from Kōenji: obligation becomes more legitimate when promises remain binding even when breach would become politically convenient.

## 7.3 Her Kushida choice is genuine independent authorship

The crucial interior sequence is not “Horikita realizes Kushida is useful.”

It is Horikita refusing to outsource judgment.

She acknowledges Ayanokōji’s competence and then explicitly asks whether leaving everything to him can be right. Her answer is no. `spine17:para0727–0755`.

That interior move matters more than whether later volumes vindicate the strategy.

The V05-local claim is:

> **Horikita makes a consequential policy choice whose content Ayanokōji opposed.**

This is development of sovereignty.

## 7.4 The positive case for preserving Kushida

Horikita’s argument is unusually perceptive because it identifies labor that the class has taken for granted.

Kushida listens to people others find boring. She remembers private details. She closes social distance quickly. She sustains a friendly presentation through enormous emotional effort. Those capacities made her dangerous because they accumulated secrets, but they are not therefore unreal capacities.

Horikita distinguishes moral condemnation from ability evaluation. `spine17:para0760–0819`.

This is consistent with the project’s broader insistence that a person cannot be exhausted by their worst act or by the institutional file written about them.

## 7.5 The negative case — preservation without consent

Kushida does not authorize Horikita’s project.

She tells Horikita to stop. She cries from rage and humiliation. She wants expulsion rather than being made into a salvaged class asset. Horikita promises to make her strengths useful anyway.

That keeps `Y1H-019` open.

The project is still leader-authored.

## 7.6 The deeper negative case — preserving Kushida transfers danger to Airi

Horikita’s choice happens after the class has locked in YES.

Therefore “save Kushida” is not a choice between expelling Kushida and expelling nobody. It is a choice between expelling Kushida and making someone else bear the already-mandatory institutional death.

This is why `Y1H-014` can only move negatively and partially.

Horikita proves she can preserve Kushida.

She does **not** prove she can preserve Kushida without imposing unchosen danger on another person.

## 7.7 Ayanokōji shields her from becoming the executioner

Once Horikita commits to Kushida, Ayanokōji interrupts before she can personally select the substitute.

His rationale is partly temporal and partly political: the burden is too heavy, time is short, and Horikita’s leadership would be damaged if she became visibly responsible for naming the sacrificial classmate.

This preserves her political capital by transferring moral contamination to him.

The consequence is central to `Y1H-012`:

> Horikita’s visible leadership becomes more independent, but the class still survives the crisis through hidden Ayanokōji security and execution capacity.

Her sovereignty is real and incomplete.

# 8. Kushida Kikyō — exposure changes the integration problem

## 8.1 Yagami authors pressure while staying near the rule boundary

Before the classroom collapse, Yagami predicts that the rule set may contain a sacrifice question and steers Kushida toward seeing the exam as an opportunity to eliminate Horikita and Ayanokōji. He openly acknowledges prior knowledge of her past and the fact that he tested her adaptability. `spine12:para0584–0657`.

The manipulation is especially notable because he articulates the forbidden version of the threat without formally issuing it. He knows that “expel them or I expose your past” would violate the exam’s anti-coercion rule, and he carefully remains adjacent to that language while making the consequence obvious.

This is **coercive insinuation**: environmental authorship designed to remain just outside enforceable rule violation.

## 8.2 Public exposure ends the old dual-self equilibrium

Ayanokōji’s disclosure destroys the arrangement in which Kushida’s public friendliness and private hostility could coexist under secret compartmentalization.

She ultimately admits the middle-school history, hostility, prior attempts to expel Ayanokōji and Horikita, and the logic behind her self-protective behavior. She then begins exposing classmates’ secrets in retaliation. `spine17:para0400–0726`.

The old handoff question—“Can Kushida’s public/private selves coexist?”—therefore changes form.

The relevant future problem is no longer how two concealed selves can occupy the same class.

It is:

> **Can a person whose contradictory selves are now publicly known become socially and politically integrated without recreating coercive secrecy?**

`Y1H-017` should therefore become `REFRAMED`.

## 8.3 Her destructiveness does not invalidate the social labor Horikita identifies

The exposure scene is designed to make Kushida maximally condemnable. She weaponizes intimate knowledge, humiliates classmates, and tries to destroy trust before leaving.

Horikita’s intervention works because it refuses the easy inference that “used badly” means “not an ability.”

The same social intelligence can support or corrode a group depending upon incentive structure and chosen purpose.

That makes Kushida a concentrated example of **generative ability** versus **extractive ability**.

## 8.4 Hatred and legitimate cooperation survive only narrowly

V04.5 established that Ayanokōji and Kushida could honor a bounded contract despite mutual distrust.

V05 shows how fragile that equilibrium is. Under Yagami’s pressure and a new institutional opportunity, Kushida again pursues expulsion.

`Y1H-018` therefore remains only `PARTIALLY_ANSWERED`: hatred can coexist with legitimate cooperation, but the legitimacy is narrow, incentive-sensitive, and not self-sustaining.

# 9. Sakura Airi and the Ayanokōji Group — friendship meets disposability

## 9.1 The volume establishes Airi as developing before it expels her

The early cultural-festival thread returns to Airi’s desire to change her public presentation. Haruka acts as protector and advocate. Ayanokōji considers her potentially powerful in the maid-café context if she can overcome social inhibition. `spine12:para0285–0365`.

This matters because the later expulsion cannot be ethically sanitized as removal of someone static.

Airi is precisely a student whose **developmental potential exceeds current measured output**.

## 9.2 Ayanokōji explicitly excludes speculative future growth from the sacrifice criterion

After Horikita preserves Kushida, Ayanokōji needs a rule that can be presented as fair under deadline.

He rejects projecting uncertain future development and instead privileges current value. OAA becomes the obvious institutional baseline because it makes multiple dimensions comparable. `spine17:para0850–0910`.

This is rational under one conception of fairness:

- everyone is evaluated by the same present-facing rule;
- friendship does not create exemption;
- voluntary martyrdom cannot distort the criterion;
- future promises cannot be selectively credited.

It is unjust under another:

- current institutional output is partly path-dependent;
- slow developers are systematically disadvantaged;
- social anxiety becomes political liability;
- the class benefits from people’s latent potential while refusing to price it at the moment of survival.

V05 does not tell us to choose one account without remainder.

It shows why scarcity makes measurement morally dangerous.

## 9.3 OAA is not used alone—and that makes the procedure more, not less, revealing

Ayanokōji notes that Ike, despite low measurable ability, has more people likely to defend him. Social network itself becomes an ability relevant to survival. `spine17:para0884–0895`.

This is important because it prevents a simplistic critique that he mistakes OAA for omniscient personhood.

He does not.

He builds a broader model.

The disturbing conclusion is therefore stronger:

> **even a sophisticated model of the person can become a disposability machine if its purpose is to rank whose loss the institution can absorb.**

## 9.4 Haruka’s substitution tests whether self-sacrifice can become an institution

Haruka offers herself in Airi’s place.

Ayanokōji refuses, not because he doubts her sincerity, but because accepting the substitution would change the rule from “least current class value” to “whoever volunteers.” He argues that this would teach the class that future hard choices can be resolved by waiting for a martyr. `spine17:para0960–0990`.

This is a serious constitutional argument.

It is also emotionally brutal because it denies Haruka control over whom her loyalty may save.

## 9.5 Airi’s consent is real and environmentally authored

Ayanokōji predicts that Airi’s weak self-assertion, guilt, attachment to Haruka, and trust in him make her likely to accept the class’s judgment. He understands that Haruka is the final veto and that Airi herself is the person best positioned to break it.

Airi then asks Haruka to let her go.

The act is self-authored in the ordinary sense: nobody physically forces the words from her mouth, and her desire to protect the class and Haruka is genuine.

But the choice environment is deliberately structured by someone who knows exactly which psychological levers matter.

This should be classified as:

> `AUTHENTIC_AGENCY_WITHIN_AUTHORED_CONSTRAINT`.

That category is not part of the formal transition vocabulary, but it is useful as an ethical sub-description of `ENVIRONMENTAL_AUTHORSHIP`.

## 9.6 The Ayanokōji Group’s earlier “survival” finding is no longer sufficient

At V02, Keisei experienced the math-score concealment as betrayal and the group survived. That justified a partial positive answer to `Y1H-033`.

V05 demonstrates that hidden competence was never the deepest concealed person.

The rupture now concerns **moral calculus**.

Ayanokōji is willing to rank and sacrifice a member of his own low-pressure friendship group when class survival demands it. Haruka cannot assimilate that discovery. Keisei accepts the strategic necessity and is disgusted with himself. Akito cannot resolve the interpersonal conflict. Airi leaves. The group’s prior form is badly damaged. `spine17:para0900–1065`.

`Y1H-033` therefore becomes `REFRAMED`:

> The question is no longer whether friendship can survive revelation of hidden *ability*. It is whether intimacy can survive revelation of Ayanokōji’s hidden **disposability logic**.

## 9.7 Airi’s final image refuses reduction to the sacrificed file

Before leaving, Airi appears without glasses and with deliberately styled hair—the version of herself she had been trying to find the courage to show. She asks Ayanokōji to look after Haruka, says goodbye, and gives him her biggest smile before crying once out of sight. `spine17:para1079–1103`.

The scene matters because it restores a layer the exam’s utility calculus cannot represent.

Airi leaves as an authored subject, not merely as the bottom-ranked object.

The tragedy is that subjecthood does not save her institutional membership.

# 10. Hirata, Kei, Sudō, and the class middle — ordinary people under constitutional pressure

## 10.1 Hirata no longer makes universal preservation everyone else’s burden

Year 1 Hirata could respond to exclusion pressure by collapsing into a universal demand that nobody be sacrificed, even when that demand itself disabled collective action.

V05 shows development.

He remains emotionally opposed to expulsion, but recognizes that his private ideal cannot justify dragging the class into a 300-point failure. He continues to mediate, accepts the YES route once the final NO route becomes impossible, and even volunteers himself as an initial candidate. `spine17:para0178–0245`.

The key development is not willingness to die for everyone.

It is the ability to **remain politically functional while an ideal is violated**.

That moves `Y1H-034` to `PARTIALLY_ANSWERED`, though the exact future question about choosing particular intimate attachment over universal obligation remains incompletely tested.

## 10.2 Kei’s defense reveals reciprocal intimacy and priority distortion

When Kushida tries to redirect responsibility toward Ayanokōji, Kei openly defends him and says, in effect, that she does not care whether the class falls if Kiyotaka is safe. `spine17:para0300–0330`.

This is powerful evidence of genuine attachment.

It is also strong evidence that romance does not yet produce politically neutral autonomy. Her hierarchy of value can place Ayanokōji above the collective polity.

`Y1H-008` therefore remains “both.”

## 10.3 Sudō demonstrates constitutional anger without seizing authority

Sudō wants Horikita to break the Kōenji bargain and later reacts strongly to the crisis. His loyalty to Horikita remains intense.

Yet the leader can refuse his preferred route, and Sudō does not convert loyalty into a right to command her.

That is a quieter sign of his continued development from reactive force toward membership in a governed collective.

## 10.4 Keisei’s strategic agreement does not erase moral injury

Keisei understands the arithmetic of the 300-point failure and ultimately supports the Airi choice.

Afterward he still asks whether what they did was right.

The point is important: strategic necessity does not automatically produce retrospective moral certainty.

The class can complete the exam and remain injured by the very reasoning that saved its competitive position.

# 11. Ichinose Honami and Kanzaki Ryūji — solidarity, loyal opposition, and the legitimacy deficit

## 11.1 Kanzaki’s diagnosis is not wrong

Kanzaki’s argument deserves stronger treatment than “he becomes ruthless.”

He has watched his class lose relative position while preserving an ethic of universal inclusion. He fears that the class has become unable even to imagine a case in which moral comfort and strategic survival diverge. His use of `正常性バイアス` identifies a genuine cognitive failure mode: when a group’s identity depends upon believing that nobody will need to be sacrificed, evidence of existential danger can be reinterpreted as impossible rather than confronted. `COTE:Y2V05:spine14:para0019–0065`.

The problem is political legitimacy.

Kanzaki has not created an internal coalition, public vocabulary, or prior record of open opposition capable of making the class experience his challenge as a loyal constitutional intervention. He goes from quiet adviser to proposing that someone might need to be expelled. Even if his strategic diagnosis is correct, the class has no institutional reason to accept his claim to redefine its moral boundary in one meeting.

This is a crucial refinement of `Y1H-021`.

> **Loyal opposition requires more than loyalty plus correct criticism. It requires a recognized political relationship in which criticism can be heard as belonging to the polity rather than attacking its identity.**

V05 shows Kanzaki’s intent but also his legitimacy deficit.

## 11.2 Ichinose’s answer is constitutive, not merely emotional

Ichinose’s refusal to trade a friend for 100 points is often easy to characterize as naïve kindness.

The local text supports something stronger.

She treats the means of reaching Class A as partly constitutive of the value of Class A. A victory purchased by abandoning a classmate would no longer represent the community she wants to advance. The school’s reward therefore cannot be evaluated independently of the type of class that receives it.

This is a political-philosophical position:

> **goods can be internally constituted by the practices through which they are obtained.**

Class A is not simply 1,000+ points on a ledger. For Ichinose, it is supposed to be the success of *this* group under *these* bonds.

## 11.3 Himeno is the missing constitutional bridge

Himeno understands Kanzaki’s concern but recognizes that the class will not convert. Her earlier V04.5 alienation from compulsory sociability now becomes analytically important.

She represents the possibility of internal pluralism that is neither Kanzaki’s abrupt strategic rupture nor the class’s total harmony.

V05 does not yet build that structure.

It proves why one is needed.

## 11.4 `Y1H-020` and `Y1H-021` remain partial, not reversed

Ichinose’s class has demonstrated adversarial competence in earlier volumes. V05 does not erase that evidence.

It reveals a deeper limit:

- the class can read an external rival;
- it is much worse at legitimizing internal contradiction.

Kanzaki’s loyal opposition also remains real in motive even though it fails politically.

The appropriate delta is therefore **strengthen and complicate**, not downgrade.

# 12. Ryūen Kakeru, Katsuragi Kōhei, and Shiina Hiyori — authoritarian pluralization

## 12.1 Ryūen now seeks information about dissent rather than only obedience

The initial 14–26 split gives Ryūen something he wants: information about the class’s actual preferences.

His later conduct remains coercive. He threatens Yajima. He uses fear to make people reveal themselves. Nobody should confuse that with liberal pluralism.

But the target of power has changed.

Earlier Ryūen often treated dissent as a challenge to sovereignty that had to be crushed. V05 increasingly treats dissent as **diagnostic data**.

That is a real leadership development even though the method remains predatory.

## 12.2 Hiyori’s role is structurally important even without a direct moral confrontation

The false-YES architecture is routed through Hiyori. The volume does not frame her as overpowering Ryūen through ethical appeal. Instead, she participates in a design that allows the polity to distinguish genuine opposition from noise.

This is not enough to move `Y1H-026`.

It does, however, strengthen the broader claim that Ryūen’s regime now contains actors whose judgment matters independently of brute force.

## 12.3 Katsuragi institutionalizes the succession question

When Tokitō demands Ryūen’s removal, Katsuragi does not answer with “Ryūen is strongest.”

He asks the harder question:

> who governs after him?

That converts personal opposition into institutional succession analysis. `spine15:para0319–0366`.

Katsuragi’s presence therefore changes the class’s political grammar. Leadership can now be assessed in terms of:

- continuity;
- replacement capacity;
- collective competitive strategy;
- and the cost of removing the incumbent.

This is a more mature polity even though its sovereign remains coercive.

## 12.4 Preserving Tokitō is the important result

Ryūen could have converted exposure into elimination.

He does not.

Katsuragi later interprets the route left open to Tokitō as deliberate preservation. `spine15:para0382–0419`; `para0461–0473`.

This strongly reinforces the partial answers to `Y1H-025` and `Y1H-027`:

- voluntary allied competence can constrain outcomes;
- a dissenter can survive confrontation with the leader;
- and the class can become more strategically plural without becoming democratic.

The unresolved question remains whether this pluralization eventually constrains Ryūen **morally**, rather than merely improving his regime’s performance.

# 13. Sakayanagi Arisu — consensus by agenda control

Sakayanagi’s section is short enough to function almost as a control group.

# 14. Chabashira Sae and Hoshinomiya Chie — when an old student becomes a present institution

## 14.1 The teachers are not outside the exam’s moral field

The unanimous-vote exam is administered by adults whose own identities were shaped by the same mechanism.

This matters for institutional analysis because it breaks a simple model of neutral adult supervision. Hoshinomiya and Chabashira carry competing memories, resentments, and counterfactuals from their student years into present professional relationships. `spine13:para0001–0135`.

The institution has procedural continuity.

It does not therefore have psychologically neutral administrators.

## 14.2 Chabashira’s trauma is frozen responsibility, not merely romance regret

The romantic loss is central, but the deeper wound is that she never found a stable account of what she owed:

- the person she loved;
- her class;
- her own future;
- and the collective promise of reaching A.

Because no account was integrated, the choice became a permanent temporal loop.

She continued to live as though reaching Class A in the present could retroactively answer the past.

That is precisely the kind of **adult-authored curriculum** Ayanokōji criticized in V04.5.

## 14.3 Ayanokōji’s retrospective solution exposes the danger of false binaries

His argument about the old class is not that Chabashira should have voted YES.

He notices that the class’s late-stage strategic space had changed. Once too little time remained to complete the expulsion process, the apparent binary between “sacrifice him for 100” and “lose 300” was no longer fixed. The rational coalition should have returned to NO, preserved everyone, and secured completion. `spine18:para0076–0097`.

The importance is epistemic:

> **trauma can preserve the decision as it felt rather than as the full rule structure actually allowed.**

Chabashira’s eleven-year self-judgment is partly organized around a decision model she never re-opened.

## 14.4 Her development is real and present-tense

After the conversation, Chabashira resolves to support the class because **the students** want Class A, not because she needs them to repair her past.

This is a clean developmental transition in the project’s pedagogy framework:

> teacher-owned goal → learner-owned goal.

Ayanokōji’s description of her heart catching up to her actual age makes the change explicit.

The volume thereby supplies one of its few hopeful models of responsibility: not undoing the harm, but ceasing to make new people live inside the old answer.

# 15. OAA: from institutional legibility to disposability

V05 is the most important OAA volume since Y2V01.

## 15.1 V01 established the file/person distinction

OAA made ability visible and politically actionable. The score could be accurate about displayed output and still incomplete about possessed capacity, development, private history, or context.

The governing warning was:

> **The file is evidence about the person. The file is not the person.**

## 15.2 V05 shows what happens when a good file is used for the wrong normative purpose

Ayanokōji’s use of OAA is not factually absurd.

Airi really does have low current institutional metrics. Those metrics matter to class competition. Future growth is uncertain. A common metric appears fairer than “the leader dislikes you.”

The problem is not primarily bad measurement.

It is the transition:

> **measurement → comparison → survival ranking → legitimate-disposal claim.**

OAA was designed to make ability legible.

The class turns legibility into a decision about who retains membership.

## 15.3 The danger survives even after supplementation

Ayanokōji supplements OAA with social relations and behavioral traits.

That does not solve the normative problem.

A person can be comprehensively modeled and still possess moral claims not reducible to the institution’s ability to absorb their loss.

The better Year-2 rule is therefore:

> **More complete information improves prediction. It does not automatically create moral jurisdiction.**

## 15.4 Airi is a direct challenge to static evaluation

Her final transformed appearance shows the visual gap between current record and possible self.

This is not proof that she would have become an elite class asset. The local boundary cannot know that.

It is proof that the sacrificed student is already in motion.

Institutional fairness that ignores future growth may be procedurally consistent while systematically selecting against late developers.

# 16. Expulsion, sacrifice, and consent

## 16.1 `退学` is not death, but the novel deliberately gives it social-death structure

The consequences of expulsion in ANHS are not equivalent to physical killing and should not be rhetorically inflated into literal death.

Within the school polity, however, expulsion functions as terminal membership removal:

- the class cannot reverse it;
- ordinary daily relationships are severed;
- the expelled person loses access to the institution’s future rewards;
- and the remaining class receives political/economic benefit.

That is why sacrifice language is analytically appropriate while literal-death language is not.

## 16.2 Voluntary sacrifice is not automatically non-coercive

Airi eventually asks to be chosen.

Haruka earlier offers herself.

The two acts show why “volunteered” is insufficient as an ethical category.

Haruka’s offer emerges from attachment and desire to save Airi. Airi’s offer emerges from class guilt, affection, weak self-assertion, trust in Ayanokōji, and recognition that Haruka’s refusal is blocking everyone else.

Both are voluntary in one sense.

Both are produced inside extreme institutional pressure.

The correct ethical questions are therefore:

- What alternatives did the person experience as live?
- Who structured those alternatives?
- What information did they possess?
- What relationships made one choice psychologically privileged?
- Could refusal occur without catastrophic cost to others?

## 16.3 The class’s 38–0 vote creates distributed responsibility without equal authorship

After Airi breaks Haruka’s veto, everyone votes YES to expel her.

That makes the class collectively responsible in a formal sense.

It does not make every member equally causal.

The decision is layered:

- the school authors the rule;
- Kushida authors the original obstruction;
- Yagami helps author Kushida’s threat environment;
- Ayanokōji authors the move to YES and the substitute criterion;
- Horikita authors Kushida’s preservation;
- Haruka authors resistance;
- Airi authors the final release of that resistance;
- the class authors the terminal ballot.

V05 is thus a textbook case of **distributed but asymmetric responsibility**.

## 16.4 “Someone had to do it” is an explanation, not absolution

Ayanokōji’s leadership theory emphasizes the necessity of a person willing to become morally contaminated.

That may be institutionally true.

But necessity must be demonstrated, not assumed.

The old Chabashira exam is an important warning: a group can believe the binary is fixed when another procedural route exists.

A legitimate “hard decision” therefore requires continuous re-audit of whether the hard binary remains real.

# 17. Truth, proof, anonymity, and responsibility

## 17.1 The ballot is accurate but incomplete evidence

V03 taught that official records can be falsified.

V05 offers the opposite problem: a record can be completely accurate and still underdetermine meaning.

`1 YES / 38 NO` proves a dissenter exists.

It does not prove who, why, or whether the voter’s motive is points, revenge, ideology, fear, or manipulation.

The class must infer motive from persistence and behavior.

## 17.2 Anonymous truth forces inference without permitting accusation to become proof

Horikita suspects Kushida before the exposure but does not name her without sufficient evidence. Hirata explicitly resists unsupported accusation.

This restraint matters.

The exam structurally invites witch-hunt logic because the class experiences real harm from an unknown voter. The novel distinguishes justified suspicion from justified public accusation.

## 17.3 Ayanokōji’s case against Kushida is a proof-conversion event

Once he acts, he does not rely on “everyone knows she is suspicious.”

He converts multiple independent pieces of historical evidence into a narrative that the class can inspect:

- shared middle-school provenance;
- recorded statements;
- contract history;
- past voting manipulation;
- Ryūen collaboration;
- behavioral consistency.

The power of the scene comes from the shift:

> private knowledge → public proof → political reclassification.

Kushida’s social identity collapses because the evidence becomes collectively usable.

## 17.4 Proof can destroy a false identity without determining what should happen next

Ayanokōji proves Kushida’s hidden behavior.

That does not logically entail “therefore expel Kushida.”

Horikita’s intervention demonstrates the distinction between:

1. **factual adjudication** — what did Kushida do?
2. **normative adjudication** — what should the class do with the person who did it?

This is one of V05’s strongest procedural contributions.

Proof establishes responsibility for acts.

It does not by itself establish the correct sanction.

# 18. Developmental authorship: cultivation, succession, and culling

V05 expands the method’s `ENVIRONMENTAL_AUTHORSHIP` ledger enough that three distinct forms must now be separated.

## 18.1 Cultivation

Examples:

- Horikita trying to redirect Kushida’s social ability;
- Ayanokōji training Hirata and Kei to support Horikita;
- Sudō using self-authored study routines;
- Chabashira reorienting toward student-owned goals.

The ethical question is whether development remains answerable to the developing person.

## 18.2 Succession

Ayanokōji’s hidden support layer explicitly anticipates his absence.

This is positive insofar as success means the developer becomes less necessary.

The project should continue to test:

> **Does the capacity survive when the architect is gone—and can the successors understand, revise, or reject the architecture they inherited?**

## 18.3 Culling

The Airi sequence is different.

The same skills used to cultivate become tools for selecting and inducing elimination:

- model the person;
- model the group;
- define the criterion;
- predict resistance;
- design the choice environment;
- cause the target’s own agency to complete the desired outcome.

This is the first Year-2 volume where “environmental authorship” must explicitly include a **destructive** subcategory.

The mature year synthesis should not allow “Ayanokōji makes people grow” to obscure that his authorship can also decide who no longer remains inside the developmental environment.

# 19. Class constitutions under the same rule

The exam works as controlled comparative political theory because all four classes receive the same institutional problem.

| Class | Primary norm | Dissent mechanism | Leader relation | Final Q5 result | Constitutional strength | Constitutional risk |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Ichinose | solidarity / no abandonment | Kanzaki openly dissents but lacks mandate | trust-centered, weak internal opposition | NO | substantive moral identity | conformity / inability to legitimize hard dissent |
| Ryūen | survival + strategic sovereignty | manipulated anonymity exposes real opposition | authoritarian but increasingly pluralized | NO | adversarial realism + preserved opposition | fear, coercion, leader dependence |
| Sakayanagi | elite optimization | dissent largely pre-solved by trusted agenda control | aristocratic fiduciary calculation | NO | speed, coherence, strategic confidence | overdependence on leader’s private priorities |
| Horikita | rule-bound collective advancement | anonymous veto, visible deliberation, hidden Ayanokōji support | increasingly legitimate visible leader + covert second layer | YES, then Airi expelled | promise-keeping, independent judgment, adaptability | transferred sacrifice, hidden security, unequal moral burdens |

No constitution is simply declared “best.”

The test reveals different failure modes.

## 19.1 Ichinose: virtue without normalized contradiction

The polity has a strong answer to “what will we not become?”

It lacks a robust answer to “how can internal critics change policy without appearing disloyal?”

## 19.2 Ryūen: pluralism without freedom from coercion

The polity can now preserve opposition.

It still uses fear to make opposition legible.

## 19.3 Sakayanagi: competence without distributed checking

The polity is stable while leader and class interest align.

The local volume does not show what happens when they do not.

## 19.4 Horikita: developmental independence without distributive innocence

The polity produces the most dramatic growth in leader independence.

It also commits the most visible sacrifice.

That pairing is the volume’s refusal to equate development with goodness.

# 20. Japanese conceptual vocabulary

## 20.1 `満場一致` — unanimity as both legitimacy and coercion

`満場一致` usually carries a positive implication of complete agreement.

The examination deliberately disassembles that intuitive value.

Because unanimity is mandatory, the appearance of universal agreement may be the endpoint of prolonged coercive pressure rather than the origin of consensus.

The phrase therefore asks:

> **When is unanimity evidence of shared judgment, and when is it simply the only legal way to stop the clock?**

## 20.2 `生徒個人の意思` — individual will as an institutional design object

The school explicitly prioritizes the individual student’s will through anonymity.

This is striking because the school simultaneously subordinates students to class consequences.

The institution is not simply collectivist. It engineers tension between individual sovereignty and collective fate.

## 20.3 `正常性バイアス` — Kanzaki’s critique of harmony

The term is used to argue that Ichinose’s class interprets severe strategic danger through the assumption that their familiar moral order can continue indefinitely.

Whether Kanzaki’s remedy is correct is separate from whether the diagnosis has force.

## 20.4 `責任` — responsibility as role, blame, and burden

V05 uses responsibility in multiple registers:

- leader responsibility for exam outcome;
- Tokitō assigning responsibility to Ryūen;
- Kushida being told to take responsibility for the situation she created;
- Horikita accepting responsibility for preserving Kushida;
- Ayanokōji taking responsibility for naming Airi;
- Chabashira carrying responsibility for an old class failure.

The word is therefore not reducible to culpability.

It also marks who is expected to **own consequences**.

## 20.5 `犠牲` — sacrifice and the concealment of agency

Calling Airi a sacrifice can make the event sound like impersonal necessity.

V05 repeatedly forces us to ask who made the sacrifice necessary, who selected the target, and who benefited.

The concept should therefore be used with causal attribution attached.

## 20.6 `仲間` — classmate, companion, and political membership

Kōenji explicitly rejects the assumption that classmates are automatically `仲間` in the emotionally binding sense.

Ichinose’s class effectively makes `仲間` constitutive of the polity.

Sakayanagi uses companion-language strategically.

The same school therefore contains sharply different theories of what membership means.

## 20.7 `鬼` / `外道` — moral contamination as leadership function

Ayanokōji’s language frames the hard-choice leader as someone who may need to occupy the socially condemned role.

`鬼` evokes inhuman severity; `外道` marks departure from accepted moral path.

The rhetoric is important because it acknowledges contamination rather than pretending the action is morally clean.

It is also dangerous because self-designating as the person willing to be `鬼` can become a license for unilateral power.

## 20.8 `人為淘汰` — artificial selection and the White Room echo

Ayanokōji’s retrospective language links present sacrifice to a history in which people disappeared through designed selection.

This is one of V05’s strongest anti-triumphalist signals.

The school may contain freedom the White Room lacks, but under institutional scarcity it can reproduce a recognizable selection grammar.

## 20.9 `公平` — fairness by common criterion

Ayanokōji’s criterion seeks fairness through consistency rather than compassion.

The volume demonstrates that procedural fairness and substantive justice can diverge:

> applying the same metric to everyone does not answer whether the metric should govern the right to remain.

# 22. Formal and literary strengths

## 22.1 One rule produces four political systems

The strongest formal device is controlled comparison.

The exam rule does not change across classes, so differences in outcome reveal differences in class constitution rather than arbitrary rule variation.

This lets the novel perform political theory through dramatic repetition:

- substantive solidarity;
- authoritarian managed dissent;
- aristocratic agenda control;
- and hybrid visible/hidden leadership.

## 22.2 The volume moves from abstract vote to named body

The first four questions concern policy choices.

The fifth initially appears as another vote.

Then it becomes Kushida.

Then Horikita.

Then Airi.

The gradual narrowing from abstract class points to a specific face is the formal mechanism by which the novel converts political economy into moral injury.

## 22.3 The Chabashira frame converts a one-day exam into an eleven-year problem

Without the adult frame, V05 could be read as “a brutal but successful exam.”

Chabashira shows that the choice may outlive the institution that demanded it.

Political decisions create biographies.

## 22.4 The Horikita perspective shift is architecturally essential

The movement into Horikita’s interiority prevents her Kushida decision from being reduced to an external twist that surprises Ayanokōji.

The reader sees the judgment become hers.

This is necessary evidence for `HORIKITA_INDEPENDENCE`.

## 22.5 Airi’s final visual reveal is intentionally too late

The narrative had already established her desire to change.

The final image makes that change visible only after the class has selected her on present-facing evidence.

The formal sequencing is the critique.

# 23. Reservations, counterreadings, and claims that must remain bounded

## 23.1 Do not claim Horikita is “proven right” about Kushida

V05 proves that the decision is independently hers and that her ability analysis is serious.

It does **not** provide later evidence about whether the integration project succeeds.

The correct V05-local conclusion is developmental independence plus unresolved strategic/ethical outcome.

## 23.2 Do not claim Ayanokōji feels nothing about Airi

The text gives relational knowledge, hesitation earlier in the exam, and an ambiguous exchange in which he leaves open the possibility that he is suppressing pain.

It does not provide clean access to a settled emotional state.

## 23.3 Do not claim Airi’s consent makes the expulsion ethically uncoerced

Her agency is genuine.

The environment is also coercive and deliberately authored.

Both must be preserved.

## 23.4 Do not claim OAA “caused” Airi’s expulsion by itself

OAA supplies a central common metric.

Ayanokōji also evaluates social network, personality, resistance probability, present usefulness, and uncertain future growth.

The stronger critique is about **normative use of legibility**, not simplistic metric error.

## 23.5 Do not call Ryūen democratic

He preserves dissent and sometimes respects majority preference.

He also uses fear, deception, and coercive revelation.

The correct concept is **authoritarian pluralization / managed dissent**.

## 23.6 Do not reduce Ichinose’s refusal to naïveté

Kanzaki’s strategic critique is real.

Ichinose’s refusal is also a coherent claim that the end-state “Class A” loses value if achieved by violating the class’s constitutive solidarity.

The tension remains open.

## 23.7 Do not claim the unanimous ballot proves equal moral consent

The 38–0 result is procedurally exact.

It is produced by unequal causal and informational positions.

## 23.8 Do not retroactively use later Kushida, Haruka, Airi, Chabashira, or class outcomes

The proper V05 boundary ends with:

- Kushida preserved but unwilling;
- Airi expelled;
- Haruka wounded and the Ayanokōji Group fractured;
- Chabashira newly committed to present students;
- the class competitively strengthened but morally unstable.

Anything later belongs to V06+.

# 33. Endpoint open questions created or sharpened by V05

## 33.1 Can Horikita’s Kushida policy become answerable to Kushida herself?

V05 establishes Horikita’s independent right to choose policy, not Kushida’s consent to being made its object.

The next question is whether preservation becomes integration rather than ownership.

## 33.2 Can the Ayanokōji Group exist after moral rather than ability disclosure?

The group has survived surprises before.

V05 exposes something qualitatively different: a friend’s willingness to rank one of them as the necessary sacrifice.

The future question is not simple forgiveness. It is whether a low-pressure friendship can survive political knowledge it was partly designed not to need.

## 33.3 Does Ayanokōji’s hesitation become a real constraint on future culling?

The hesitation is locally real.

He still completes the operation.

The project must track whether ordinary attachment ever changes the outcome rather than merely increasing subjective cost.

## 33.4 Can a class institutionalize dissent before crisis?

Ichinose’s class most clearly needs this, but the problem is general.

Anonymous veto is a terrible substitute for normalized opposition because it makes dissent legible only as obstruction.

## 33.5 Will Ryūen’s managed dissent become genuine constraint?

Tokitō survives.

That does not yet mean opposition can reliably bind Ryūen’s behavior outside situations where he finds dissent useful.

## 33.6 Can OAA remain useful without becoming a survival score?

V05 shows the endpoint risk of institutional legibility.

Future analysis should distinguish using OAA for:

- diagnosis;
- development;
- opportunity allocation;
- risk management;
- and membership disposal.

Those are not ethically equivalent uses of the same data.

## 33.7 What does Chabashira’s adult development change about teacher legitimacy?

She now articulates a present-oriented, student-owned purpose.

That needs later behavioral verification before becoming a mature conclusion.

## 33.8 Is ANHS a White Room alternative or a plural environment capable of reproducing White Room logic under pressure?

V05 makes the second formulation increasingly necessary.

The school’s plurality is real.

So is the selection grammar Ayanokōji recognizes.

The relevant comparison can no longer be categorical “school good / White Room bad.” It must ask what institutions do with measurement, scarcity, exit, dissent, and the right to refuse.

# 34. Final synthesis — the class learns that choice does not distribute guilt evenly

Year 2 Volume 5 is the point at which the project’s abstract institutional vocabulary becomes personal tragedy.

OAA was once a public score.

Now it helps decide who leaves.

Horikita’s independence was once a developmental hope.

Now it means she can reject Ayanokōji’s judgment in a way that saves one person and exposes another to sacrifice.

Hirata’s recovery from universal care was once a positive sign.

Now it means he can remain functional while a classmate is expelled.

Kushida’s hidden self was once a secret-information problem.

Now proof destroys the secret but cannot decide what justice requires.

Ayanokōji’s developmental authorship was once most visible in cultivating people.

Now the same model of environment, prediction, and individual psychology can make a target participate in her own removal.

The volume therefore refuses the comforting assumption that **development, competence, and legitimacy naturally converge**.

Horikita develops and makes an ethically contestable decision.

Ayanokōji acts competently and authors severe harm.

Ichinose preserves a coherent moral constitution and exposes strategic rigidity.

Ryūen becomes a better leader while remaining coercive.

Sakayanagi produces efficient consensus without proving distributed accountability.

Airi becomes more visibly self-authored at exactly the moment current-value logic judges her least necessary.

That is the mature V05 thesis:

> **Responsibility begins where choice can no longer keep every good at once. The measure of a political order is therefore not whether it avoids all sacrifice, but how it distributes the right to choose, the ability to refuse, the burden of explanation, and the power to decide whose loss counts as acceptable.**

The `満場一致特別試験` is cruel because it makes unanimity mandatory.

It is philosophically effective because it reveals that “everyone voted for it” is not the same as “everyone authored it equally.”

The class receives a formally unanimous result and emerges morally divided.

Airi’s expulsion is institutionally valid.

It is strategically intelligible.

It is partly self-authored.

It is also the product of asymmetric power, measurement, hidden curricula, friendship, coercive deadlines, and leader choices.

None of those truths cancels the others.

The strongest longitudinal transition is therefore:

> **V04.5 showed that Ayanokōji had become embedded enough for other people to matter. V05 asks what happens when the embedded person becomes responsible for harming someone who matters.**

His answer is not yet humane enough to dissolve White Room grammar.

But his hesitation, Horikita’s successful disagreement, Airi’s final dignity, Hirata’s functional grief, and Chabashira’s adult reorientation all demonstrate something the White Room model struggles to contain:

> **human beings can remain authors even inside environments designed to rank, use, and remove them.**

That is why V05 is not simply a story about the class sacrificing Airi.

It is a story about the fight over **who gets to author the meaning of that sacrifice**.
