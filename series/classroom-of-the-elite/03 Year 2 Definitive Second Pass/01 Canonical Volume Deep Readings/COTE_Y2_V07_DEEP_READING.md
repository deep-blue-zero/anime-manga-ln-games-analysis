# 『ようこそ実力至上主義の教室へ ２年生編7』
## Year 2 Volume 7 — Second-Pass Deep Reading
### Staged agency, self-authored consequence, and the hidden author who lets the cast complete the scene

> **Local analytical boundary:** this document uses the complete original Japanese Year 2 Volume 7, the frozen Year 1 V2 corpus, and canonical Y2V01–Y2V06 as inherited analytical state. It deliberately does **not** use Y2V08 or later material, Volume 0, *Second List*, Year 3, adaptations, wikis, or remembered later outcomes to settle V07-local claims. The historical full-Year-2 first pass is consulted only as an explicit revision target. The unnamed male actor connected to `綾小路先生` remains unnamed here because V07 itself does not identify him by name.

# 1. Scope, provenance, and inherited analytical state

`Y2V07` is the eighth canonical sequential artifact in the Year 2 definitive second pass. The Japanese EPUB was read completely in audited spine order through the afterword before targeted retrieval. The source exactly matches the Phase-0 lock: **14,888,219 bytes**, SHA-256 `db45b6e29206b048d9311d05214550ad4f2d0e5907034b27092103d848cbde0c`. The deterministic text layer contains **4,425 paragraph records** in the namespace `COTE:Y2V07:spineXX:paraYYYY`. Narrative illustrations are tracked separately from text; the four 128×128 gaiji assets are excluded from the visual evidence layer.

The inherited Year-2 progression is:

> **V01 — legibility → V02 — jurisdiction → V03 — attribution → V04 — sovereignty / counter-authorship → V04.5 — embeddedness → V05 — responsibility → V06 — distributed agency.**

V06 ended with a remarkable proposition. Ayanokōji no longer merely wanted Horikita’s class to become useful while he remained its indispensable hidden security layer. He explicitly imagined himself as `補助輪`—training wheels—whose successful endpoint is removal. He privately intended to leave Horikita’s class, stand against it, and discover whether a collective he currently believed he could defeat could make that expectation wrong.

That endpoint created several live tests entering V07:

- whether the class’s distributed agency survives a public economic contest rather than a controlled sports field;
- whether Haruka’s unresolved revenge becomes destruction, bargaining, grief work, or something else;
- whether Kushida’s self-authored cooperation remains stable outside the private compact with Horikita;
- whether Kei’s growing ordinary social life can acquire depth independent of Ayanokōji;
- whether Yagami’s covert authorship remains hidden once independent traces converge;
- whether the unknown external visitor belongs to the same threat architecture as Tsukishiro, Yagami, Amasawa, or something not yet locally classifiable;
- whether Ayanokōji’s succession project actually reduces his authorship, or merely changes authorship from direct command into environmental design.

V07 answers the last question most clearly.

Ayanokōji’s power changes form.

He increasingly does not tell other people exactly what to do. Instead, he places:

- a letter;
- a rumor;
- a resource;
- a rival;
- an observer;
- an incentive;
- a timing window;
- an incomplete clue;
- or a protected opportunity

into a field, and then watches actors complete the result through motives that are genuinely their own.

This is why the strongest V07 concept is **staged agency**.

> **Staged agency** is action that remains meaningfully authored by the immediate actor while occurring inside a field deliberately arranged by another person to make particular choices, collisions, or revelations more likely.

It differs from direct coercion because the actor still chooses. It differs from neutral nonintervention because the stage has been designed. It is therefore both more plural and more ethically evasive than overt command.

The governing question becomes:

> **When people freely complete a scene whose conditions were secretly arranged for them, how should responsibility be distributed between the actors and the hidden stage-setter?**

# 2. Executive thesis — distributed agency becomes staged agency

V07 is formally organized around **performance**.

The obvious performance is the cultural festival. Classes create cafés, attractions, costumes, menus, price systems, queues, spectacle, rivalry, and an image of themselves for adult visitors. Students who have spent most of the series inside a closed institutional market suddenly perform the school before an external audience.

The less obvious performance is covert.

Ayanokōji writes a false love letter containing an anagram. Kei performs irritation so Horikita will not immediately deliver it. Horikita passes the letter to Yagami for reasons unrelated to Ayanokōji’s true aim. Yagami opens it because his suspicion and fixation make the bait irresistible. Nagumo enters the scene with his own grievance. Ryūen arrives pursuing the island attacker. Komiya and Kinoshita recover recognition. Horikita contributes a handwriting discrepancy she discovered independently. Ibuki acts from her own crude protective logic. Teachers enter under institutional jurisdiction. Amasawa arrives because of her own attachment to Yagami. White Room-linked adults remove him. The person at the center of the convergence—Ayanokōji—is not even in the room. `COTE:Y2V07:spine17:para0300–0475`; `spine18:para0201–0223`.

The scene therefore does not resemble a puppet master pulling strings attached to passive bodies.

It resembles a stage on which every performer has a different script.

Horikita’s later realization is exact: even if she and Ibuki had never entered the student-council room, the process would still have converged because the letter was designed to draw Yagami toward Nagumo. Her independent handwriting suspicion becomes additional evidence rather than the sole mechanism. `spine17:para0398–0410`.

Ayanokōji himself later minimizes the design:

> `オレがしたことは本当に些細なことばかりだ。`

He claims that what he did consisted only of small acts. `spine18:para0221`.

Later he makes the stronger moral claim:

> `白か黒かを決めたのはオレじゃない。`

and:

> `試すための舞台をセッティングしたに過ぎない。`

He did not choose whether Yagami was white or black; he merely set the stage for the test. `spine18:para0271–0283`.

There is real truth in this self-description. Yagami is not innocent. His own actions leave traces: manipulating Kushida, helping produce another first-year’s expulsion, injuring uninvolved students on the island, concealing his position, opening another person’s letter, and repeatedly organizing his identity around Ayanokōji. If Yagami had done none of those things, Ayanokōji’s letter would not by itself have produced the same proof field.

But `舞台をセッティング` is not ethically neutral.

Ayanokōji writes the bait specifically because he expects White Room training, elegant handwriting, suspicion, and fixation to make Yagami respond. He asks Kei to perform a mood that will alter Horikita’s delivery behavior. He gives Ryūen enough information to place independent victims and teachers near the target. He accepts that the scene may fail, but designs it so that Yagami’s characteristic choices become evidence against him. `spine18:para0208–0219`.

The result is therefore best understood as **shared causal authorship with asymmetric epistemic responsibility**:

- Yagami authors his prior misconduct and immediate decisions;
- Horikita authors her investigation;
- Ibuki authors her intervention;
- Nagumo authors his suspicion;
- Ryūen authors his pursuit of the attacker;
- the victims author their recognition;
- Amasawa authors her attempt to stop Yagami;
- the adults author the institutional removal;
- Ayanokōji secretly authors the **conditions of convergence**.

V07 applies the same structure outside the Yagami plot.

The cultural-festival “war” between Horikita and Ryūen is publicly presented as betrayal and genuine rivalry. In reality the broad conflict is jointly staged to create visitor attention, while the public’s enthusiasm and spending are still real. `spine14:para0200–0406`; `spine16:para0480–0492`; `spine18:para0084–0125`.

The Ichinose-class reform begins because Ayanokōji has already invited Himeno into a conversation before Kanzaki unexpectedly asks him for help. He then refuses to “fix” Ichinose directly, gives Kanzaki and Himeno a method for finding other dissenters, tells them to grow strong enough to defeat Horikita’s class, and withdraws with the statement:

> `ここから先は第三者であるオレが立ち入る領域じゃない。`

From this point on, it is not territory for a third party like him. `spine12:para0340–0384`.

That is more answerable than direct long-term control. It is still covert intervention into an enemy class whose members have not collectively consented to Ayanokōji defining their constitutional problem.

Haruka provides the volume’s most important counterexample to hidden authorship. She begins V07 believing Airi has been sacrificed and destroyed. Haruka therefore plans to destroy the class’s route to A and then leave school with Akito. What changes her is not Ayanokōji successfully teaching the intended lesson. It is evidence that **Airi has authored a future outside his model**. Airi resumes the Shizuku identity, passes an audition, and publicly declares that she will become someone her best friend can meet without shame. `spine16:para0310–0345`.

Haruka later recognizes her own error:

> `退学した人は全員不幸になると決めつけてた。`

She had decided in advance that everyone expelled would become unhappy. `spine18:para0021–0041`.

This is also an implicit correction to Ayanokōji’s V05 culling ontology. The school can decide that Airi is presently the least useful person inside a competitive class. It cannot decide what Airi can become outside the school.

The compact progression is therefore:

> **V06 asked whether agency could be distributed. V07 asks what happens when a hidden author stops commanding distributed agents and instead stages the conditions under which their independent motives collide.**

The gain is real. Other people contribute more of the answer.

The danger is also real. The stage-setter can increasingly deny ownership of outcomes he deliberately made more probable.

# 3. Position in the Year 2 architecture — a public festival conceals a private purge

V07 begins the next structural movement after the V05–V06 class-fracture dyad.

The cultural festival moves the series out of an examination whose rules are primarily internal and into an event where the school is **looked at** by outsiders. Visitors include people connected to school governance and their families, along with people who normally work in the school’s closed commercial environment. `spine11:para0048–0060`.

This produces three simultaneous economies.

## 3.1 Economic performance

Classes receive budget, rent space, purchase materials, set prices, manage queues, attract customers, and compete for private-point revenue. Visitor spending becomes a limited shared market.

## 3.2 Institutional performance

Student-council or extracurricular contribution is converted into additional festival budget. Previous public service becomes present investment capital. Institutional legibility therefore becomes economically fungible. `spine11:para0061–0095`.

## 3.3 Identity performance

Classes perform themselves:

- Horikita’s class sells a maid café and coordinated spectacle;
- Ryūen’s class sells a Japanese-themed concept café and public antagonism;
- third-years use their final festival partly as memory-making rather than optimization;
- individual students perform hospitality, sexuality, embarrassment, authority, or social fluency before outsiders.

The festival therefore literalizes a recurring COTE proposition:

> **institutional value is not merely possessed; it must be made visible, persuasive, and exchangeable.**

But the volume repeatedly reveals invisible backstage structures beneath visible performance.

The maid-café versus Japanese-café rivalry is partially staged. Chabashira’s humiliating late appearance is produced by a rule-based market in teacher labor. Yagami’s public student-council excellence conceals covert violence. Satō’s ordinary friendship trouble is an access route into a first-year operation. A “love letter” is an attack surface. A festival guest carries a White Room-linked extraction message. Ayanokōji’s most consequential intervention occurs while almost everybody else believes he is simply helping with a school festival.

This makes V07 a formal study of **front stage and back stage**.

The front stage asks what the school can display.

The back stage asks who arranged the display, who understands the arrangement, and which actions remain genuinely owned by the performers.

# 4. Narrative architecture — nine stages of public and hidden authorship

## 4.1 `長谷部波瑠加の独白` — grief authors a revenge script

Haruka’s opening monologue refuses the class’s emerging recovery narrative. She names Airi a sacrificial victim of Horikita and Ayanokōji’s selfish decision and refuses to accept class success as compensation. Her revenge is not an accidental mood; it is a declared project.

This matters because the volume begins by granting the injured party **authorship of a hostile interpretation**. The class does not get to define V05 as settled merely because Kushida has become useful and the sports festival went well.

## 4.2 `文化祭に向けて` — visible development becomes economic capital

Festival rules transform school contribution into budget and visitor attention into revenue. Sudō’s academic ascent becomes publicly visible. Ayanokōji deliberately suppresses his own non-mathematics performance because classmates need to see Sudō-like development rather than a hidden genius who can always carry them. `spine11:para0088–0125`.

The chapter also reopens Nagumo. Ayanokōji admits he previously ignored him because he had no interest. Now he initiates the proposed contest himself and experiences genuine curiosity. `spine11:para0897–0925`; `para0972–1007`.

The conversation then exposes a severe relational invariant: Ayanokōji says even a lover or family member does not override self-preservation. `spine11:para1028–1046`.

## 4.3 `反逆の狼煙` — reform begins below the sovereign

Kanzaki approaches Ayanokōji because he believes Ichinose’s class is failing despite adequate individual ability. Ayanokōji refuses the obvious request—change Ichinose—and instead reframes the problem as class consciousness.

Himeno names `同調圧力`: if she believes white and the majority says black, black becomes the socially permissible answer. `spine12:para0260–0288`.

Kanzaki and Himeno then expose traits they had concealed from one another. Ayanokōji interprets this as evidence that the class’s pleasant surface contains people who have never become comrades in the full sense. His prescription is not “become cruel.” It is **become capable of open difference**.

The rebellion begins as a network rather than a coup: two find a third, then a fourth. `spine12:para0340–0384`.

## 4.4 `一通のラブレター` — ordinary empathy becomes covert infrastructure

Horikita accepts an anonymous request to deliver a love letter to Nagumo partly because she can now remember the vulnerability of her own unsent letter to Manabu. Ordinary adolescent experience changes how she treats somebody else’s embarrassment. `spine13:para0100–0165`.

That mundane act leads her to Yagami. She sees his unusually immaculate handwritten student-council minutes and notices similarity to the anonymous island note. Her suspicion is local, incomplete, and independently generated. `spine13:para0220–0302`.

## 4.5 `文化祭前日の打ち合わせ` — public rehearsal, private exits

The festival rehearsal tests performance before outside observers. The class discovers that a plan stable in an empty room behaves differently under actual social load. This is the benign form of staged agency: the environment reveals capacities that rehearsal alone cannot certify.

Kushida also seeks to terminate her prior relation with Yagami. She no longer wants the pre-V05 project and recognizes she cannot stop him alone. `spine14:para0420–0515`.

## 4.6 `文化祭本番` — spectacle becomes market power

The festival day converts staged rivalry into real revenue. Visitor attention is not fake merely because some of the rivalry is co-produced. The distinction is analogous to theater: an authored scene can produce genuine audience emotion.

The same chapter introduces the unknown visitor’s note:

> `迎えに来た。どうするかは自分自身で決めろ。正門で待つ`

“I came to pick you up. Decide for yourself what you will do. I’ll wait at the main gate.” `spine15:para0218–0235`.

The note offers choice while demonstrating access. It is therefore a miniature version of the Year-2 freedom problem: an actor can be told to “decide for yourself” inside a situation somebody else has already created.

## 4.7 `愛里の残したもの` — the expelled person refuses ontological disappearance

Haruka and Akito are prepared to destroy the class’s route to A and leave school. Kushida’s independent investigation reaches them. The decisive evidence is Airi herself: outside school she has resumed public activity, passed an audition, and is building a future. `spine16:para0280–0380`.

Haruka returns in Airi’s unused maid outfit. The choice does not forgive the class. It changes what loyalty to Airi requires.

## 4.8 `見えざる登場人物` — evidence converges around an absent author

Horikita, Ibuki, Nagumo, Ryūen, injured victims, teachers, and Amasawa converge around Yagami. The scene progressively converts private suspicion into public evidence. Yagami’s own reaction supplies the final catastrophic disclosure.

Horikita calls attention to the invisible actor because the event is too coordinated to be explained by the people visibly present. `spine17:para0380–0420`.

## 4.9 `裏で暗躍する者たち` — every puppet master discovers another stage

The final chapter reveals successive layers:

1. Ayanokōji staged Yagami’s convergence.
2. Tsubaki staged Satō’s pressure so Ayanokōji would act.
3. An unnamed male actor staged Tsubaki/Utomiya’s push because he wanted Yagami removed.
4. That actor references `綾小路先生` and then withdraws to enjoy school life.

The volume therefore refuses a single-master cosmology.

> **Every apparent author may itself be acting inside somebody else’s authored field.**

# 5. The cultural festival as political economy — contribution becomes capital, attention becomes revenue

The festival rules deserve treatment as political philosophy rather than logistical decoration.

The school allocates additional class budget based partly upon accumulated contribution. Horikita’s class receives the largest additional Year-2 allocation—39,400 points—while Sakayanagi, Ryūen, and Ichinose receive different additions. `COTE:Y2V07:spine11:para0060–0099`.

This implements a familiar institutional sequence:

> **past legible contribution → present budget → better capacity to attract future value.**

The process resembles OAA but at class scale. Institutional recognition does not merely describe performance; it becomes productive capital.

Visitors are also numerically bounded. The festival brings 283 adults with 10,000 private points each and 202 minors with 5,000 each, producing a finite spending pool. `spine11:para0048–0060`.

Classes therefore compete not for abstract applause but for a **scarce audience wallet**.

The ranking structure rewards the top four with +100 class points, ranks five through eight with +50, and gives the lower ranks no class-point gain. Crucially, there is no direct negative class-point penalty for a low festival rank. `spine11:para0070–0085`.

This changes the risk structure compared with exams built around punishment. The festival encourages entrepreneurship because failure is opportunity cost rather than immediate destruction.

That relative safety helps explain the volume’s creativity.

Ryūen and Horikita can stage public rivalry. Students can experiment with concepts. Visitors can choose. Classes can compete through hospitality rather than expulsion. Even Nagumo’s class can choose memory-making over strict optimization because the event’s downside is bounded.

The festival therefore provides a small but significant positive model for ANHS:

> **competition becomes more generative when losing does not automatically mean disappearance.**

This principle later matters for the contrast between Airi and Yagami. Airi’s expulsion proves that institutional loss need not terminate life; Yagami’s removal threatens a return to an institution in which defeat may indeed erase ordinary self-authorship.

## 5.1 Space itself is priced

Classrooms nearer the entrance cost more. Higher floors are cheaper but sacrifice traffic. A class must decide how much scarce budget to invest in location versus food, décor, staffing, and marketing. `spine11:para0160–0188`.

This makes the campus a market in **visibility**.

The same logic governs the characters. Ayanokōji spent Year 1 minimizing visibility; Ryūen weaponizes it; Kushida specializes in social visibility; Yagami performs impeccable visibility while concealing causal authorship; Haruka temporarily refuses the visible class; Horikita increasingly accepts the burdens of public leadership.

## 5.2 Teacher labor is purchasable

One of the volume’s funniest scenes is also an unusually blunt expression of institutional jurisdiction. Festival rules permit student classes to purchase teacher assistance at 100,000 private points per hour. Ayanokōji invokes the rule against Chabashira’s intense reluctance and uses her rarity, embarrassment, and adult attractiveness as a late “limited event.” `spine16:para0539–0629`.

She takes 63 photo requests and becomes a high-value attraction.

The scene is comic. It also contains a genuine consent problem.

Ayanokōji’s argument is effectively:

> the institution has made this service purchasable, therefore the employee cannot simply refuse because she dislikes the task.

The rule creates legitimate jurisdiction at the institutional level. It does not erase the interpersonal fact that Chabashira is embarrassed and does not want the role.

V07 therefore supplies a low-stakes mirror of a recurring COTE distinction:

> **procedural authorization is not identical to relational consent.**

# 6. Ayanokōji — from distributed agency to stage-setting sovereignty

V06 showed Ayanokōji deliberately reducing himself as first-line solver.

V07 reveals what replaces direct intervention.

The answer is not absence.

It is **stage-setting sovereignty**.

## 6.1 Controlled self-suppression continues

Ayanokōji’s midterm scores remain deliberately uneven. Sudō reaches eleventh overall with strong scores across subjects, while Ayanokōji places fourteenth despite capacity far beyond that result. `spine11:para0088–0124`.

He explicitly recognizes the developmental utility of classmates seeing Sudō rise through work. A hidden genius who always rescues the class would undermine the lesson.

This is a strange form of pedagogical restraint:

- he does not display maximum ability;
- he does so for the sake of other people’s motivation;
- he still privately decides what level of truth they should be allowed to see.

The action is therefore **non-dominating in output but paternalistic in information control**.

## 6.2 Nagumo becomes the first actively desired contest

Ayanokōji tells Nagumo that until recently he had no reason to engage him. He now initiates the contest himself. `spine11:para0897–0925`.

The internal narration goes further. He feels genuine interest and admits that wanting to fight Nagumo is itself part of why he came. `spine11:para0972–1007`.

This matters for `AYANOKOJI_FREEDOM`.

The contest is not assigned by his father, Tsukishiro, White Room curriculum, class necessity, or immediate self-defense. It is a **positive desire**.

Yet the desire is immediately entangled with another strategic operation. Even a self-chosen interest enters his life as a multi-purpose instrument.

V07 therefore strengthens the proposition that Ayanokōji is becoming capable of wanting, while refusing the simpler proposition that desire has displaced instrumental cognition.

## 6.3 Self-preservation remains the deepest declared invariant

The Nagumo conversation contains some of the strongest negative relational evidence in Year 2.

When Nagumo asks whether threatening Kei would force him to act, Ayanokōji first says he would resist loss when possible. Nagumo then presses on the difference between losing a class member and losing a supposedly special lover. Ayanokōji answers:

> `消えたら消えたでそれまでの存在。`

If she disappears, then that is all she was. `spine11:para1028–1038`.

The narration removes the possibility that this is purely bluff:

> `相手が他人であれ恋人であれ、家族であれ同じこと。`
>
> `失敗し脱落する時が来たらそれで終わりだ。`
>
> `最優先すべきは自分を守ること。`

Whether the person is stranger, lover, or family, failure and removal are the end; protecting himself has highest priority. `spine11:para1041–1046`.

This does **not** mean Kei is emotionally meaningless.

It means V07 supplies strong evidence that no current relationship has displaced the White-Room-shaped self-preservation hierarchy at the level of declared final priority.

The question of Kei’s irreplaceability therefore becomes dimensional:

- **operational irreplaceability:** strong negative evidence;
- **emotional irreplaceability:** still unresolved;
- **developmental significance:** clearly positive;
- **ordinary-life significance:** clearly positive.

Reducing all four to “does he love her?” would erase the actual structure.

## 6.4 The Yagami operation is a mature environmental intervention

The love-letter trap is a high-level example of Ayanokōji’s preferred late-Year-2 method.

He designs for **probabilistic convergence** rather than deterministic obedience.

The operation includes:

- generic stationery available to anyone;
- a complex anagram likely to attract a suspicious high-level reader;
- his own handwriting as controlled information;
- Kei’s performed irritation to delay direct delivery;
- Horikita as an unwitting transfer node;
- Yagami as the person given opportunity to violate privacy;
- Nagumo as the institutional interrogator;
- Ryūen as an independently motivated avenger;
- victims as independent memory witnesses;
- teachers as jurisdictional closure;
- Amasawa as an emotional constraint on Yagami.

`spine18:para0208–0223`.

This is not White Room curriculum in the simple sense. White Room pedagogy attempts to tightly constrain environment and desired output. The Yagami stage **uses plurality**. It works because actors bring motives Ayanokōji does not fully own.

But the ethical asymmetry remains severe. He knows the architecture while the participants do not.

## 6.5 “He chose black” is both true and self-exculpating

Ayanokōji’s later defense is important enough to preserve without either accepting or dismissing it.

He argues that Yagami’s removal depends on Yagami’s own accumulated choices. `spine18:para0271–0283`.

That is factually supported.

Ayanokōji did not invent:

- the first-year expulsion;
- Kushida manipulation;
- island injuries;
- covert hostility;
- Yagami’s decision to open a private letter;
- Yagami’s inability to resist a clue pointing toward Ayanokōji;
- Yagami’s violent breakdown.

Yet the phrase `舞台をセッティング` exposes the unresolved responsibility gap.

If one designs a test because one knows how a target’s disposition will likely interact with it, the target’s free action does not make the designer causally absent.

V07 therefore suggests a principle for later ethical audit:

> **The more accurately an environmental author predicts another person’s “free” response, the less persuasive it is to treat the resulting outcome as wholly external to the author’s responsibility.**

## 6.6 The contradiction with his criticism of Yagami

Ayanokōji condemns Yagami for using uninvolved people and creating unnecessary collateral damage. That judgment has force: Yagami harms students to provoke and signal.

But Ayanokōji himself routinely routes uninformed actors through designed environments.

The difference is not that one manipulates and the other does not.

The stronger local distinction is:

- Yagami increasingly treats collateral harm as acceptable expression of fixation;
- Ayanokōji usually prefers lower-noise, lower-cost staging and claims to minimize unnecessary harm;
- both retain asymmetric knowledge over people they use as variables.

That makes V07’s contrast **degree and purpose**, not a clean moral binary.

# 7. Haruka and Airi — expulsion is not ontological annihilation

Haruka’s arc is the ethical center of V07 because it revises the meaning of V05 without erasing V05’s harm.

## 7.1 Haruka begins from a morally intelligible false totalization

Haruka knows something real:

- Airi did not betray the class;
- Airi had only begun to become more socially visible;
- Airi loved Ayanokōji;
- Ayanokōji used that attachment when driving the unanimous decision;
- Horikita’s independent choice to preserve Kushida transferred the cost onto Airi.

Her hatred is therefore not a misunderstanding created by missing one convenient fact.

Her mistake is a different one.

She turns the wrong done to Airi into a theory that **Airi’s future has been destroyed**.

That theory gives revenge its moral force.

If expulsion equals permanent ruin, then destroying the class and leaving with Akito can feel like loyalty to the destroyed person.

## 7.2 Airi invalidates the victim script

The crucial information comes from Airi’s post-school life.

Her account announces that she has resumed idol activity. She decides to do what she can, wants to become someone her best friend can meet without shame, and passes an audition. `COTE:Y2V07:spine16:para0318–0345`.

This is powerful precisely because Airi is absent.

No one at ANHS gets to author the meaning for her.

Ayanokōji cannot claim:

> I expelled her so that she would become an idol.

Horikita cannot claim:

> keeping Kushida was justified because Airi improved outside.

Haruka cannot claim:

> Airi’s life has been permanently destroyed, therefore I must destroy mine too.

Airi becomes a **counter-author from outside the institution**.

## 7.3 Haruka’s return is not forgiveness

After changing into Airi’s unused maid outfit and helping with the festival, Haruka later tells the class:

> `私は、ここにいる全員を許したわけじゃない`

She has not forgiven everyone. `spine18:para0015–0023`.

That refusal is essential.

V07 does not convert emotional recovery into retrospective moral acquittal.

Haruka instead identifies a mistake in her own framing:

> `退学した人は全員不幸になると決めつけてた。`

She had decided that expelled people must all become unhappy. `spine18:para0020–0041`.

The correction is epistemic before it is emotional:

> **ANHS controls membership in ANHS. It does not possess jurisdiction over the total future of the expelled person.**

## 7.4 Horikita answers dissent without demanding absolution

Horikita does not require Haruka to say that V05 was right. When Haruka offers to accept punishment, Horikita says the real “punishment” is continuing as her classmate and asks whether she can face that reality. `spine18:para0042–0055`.

This is a much healthier leadership move than compelled reconciliation.

The polity does not need unanimous moral memory in order to continue functioning.

It needs participants capable of remaining in relation despite incompatible judgments of the past.

## 7.5 The Ayanokōji Group survives only by ceasing to be the Ayanokōji Group

Keisei, Akito, and Haruka begin reconnecting after the festival. They look toward Ayanokōji.

He consciously refuses re-entry.

> `5人だったグループは3人になってしまったが、以前よりも強い絆で結ばれていくことを願う。あの場所にオレの存在は必要ない。`

The five-person group has become three, and he hopes their bond becomes stronger; his presence is no longer necessary. `spine18:para0057–0066`.

This is one of V07’s clearest succession acts.

Ayanokōji does **not** repair the friendship space by restoring himself as center.

The original group does not survive.

A successor does.

That distinction is strong enough to answer the inherited question prospectively:

> **The Ayanokōji Group cannot survive the concealed person in its original form. A successor friendship can survive by reorganizing around the people who still choose one another after the concealed person exits.**

# 8. Horikita — leadership learns empathy, staging, and incomplete sovereignty

V07 advances Horikita on three different axes.

## 8.1 Ordinary vulnerability becomes political empathy

The anonymous love-letter request initially looks trivial. Horikita remembers the difficulty she herself had in imagining a letter to Manabu and recognizes that what looks inefficient from outside can be difficult for the person inside the situation. `spine13:para0100–0165`.

This is ordinary-life counter-curriculum at work.

Year-1 Horikita often treated social difficulty as noise around rational procedure. V07’s Horikita increasingly understands that embarrassment, affection, memory, and pride are themselves facts a leader must interpret.

## 8.2 She acquires independent evidence against Yagami

Yagami’s minutes remind Horikita of the anonymous island note. She does not immediately collapse resemblance into certainty. She considers coincidence and reads his behavior cautiously. `spine13:para0268–0302`.

This is exactly the evidentiary discipline the V2 method requires.

Horikita’s later role in the student-council-room scene therefore matters because she is not merely executing Ayanokōji’s secret instruction. Her evidence has independent provenance.

## 8.3 She accepts deception as a tool without becoming a hidden sovereign

The cultural-festival rivalry with Ryūen is partially staged. Afterward, Horikita tells the maid-café core that the “betrayal” was planned and apologizes for concealing it. `spine18:para0084–0125`.

Ayanokōji’s own narration marks a developmental difference: earlier he might have excluded Horikita from planning. Now he recognizes that the visible class leader must possess real veto authority and that if she had rejected the plan, he would have accepted the rejection.

This is a significant movement toward **answerable environmental authorship**.

The scheme remains secret from most classmates while it operates. But the person publicly responsible for the class is no longer merely a mask through which Ayanokōji exercises policy.

## 8.4 Her sovereignty remains incomplete

Horikita still does not know the full Yagami/White Room architecture. She asks Ayanokōji about the hidden incident, but the conversation is interrupted. `spine18:para0120–0145`.

The class leader is therefore gaining veto power over class strategy while remaining excluded from the deepest security facts affecting her classmate.

That asymmetry preserves `Y1H-012` as only partially answered.

# 9. Kei and Satō — horizontal intimacy grows while Ayanokōji’s disclosure deficit hardens

V07 sharply separates **Kei’s development** from **Ayanokōji’s relational hierarchy**.

## 9.1 Satō becomes a genuine independent attachment

Satō is pressured by Tsubaki through knowledge about Kei, Ayanokōji, and Kei’s past. Ayanokōji eventually realizes the purpose is not actually to recruit Satō but to make the pressure reach him. `spine18:para0233–0244`.

He then uses the crisis for a private purpose: he brings Kei into the conversation and has Kei disclose almost all of her bullying history.

The manipulation is his.

The friendship’s response is Satō’s.

Satō does not abandon Kei after receiving the socially dangerous truth. Ayanokōji concludes that the relationship has moved:

> `友人から親友の関係へと本当の意味で昇華させた。`

from friend to true best friend. `spine18:para0244`.

This provides locally sufficient evidence that Kei’s social identity can remain organized through horizontal ties not reducible to her boyfriend.

## 9.2 Ayanokōji’s intervention is both enabling and appropriative

It would be too simple to praise him for “helping Kei open up.”

He decides the disclosure moment because it serves his reading of the threat. Kei’s past becomes material inside an intervention he controls.

But the result exceeds him.

Once Satō knows, the shared knowledge belongs to the two girls’ relationship. Ayanokōji cannot privately own the friendship created by Satō’s acceptance.

This is precisely why staged agency matters as a concept: the stage-setter may create the opening, while the actors produce a relationship whose future is not his property.

## 9.3 Kei is not operationally irreplaceable

The Nagumo scene simultaneously supplies the strongest negative evidence so far for a different question.

Ayanokōji’s internal statement that stranger, lover, and family all remain subordinate to self-preservation means Kei has not become an absolute operational veto on his behavior. `spine11:para1028–1046`.

This is not equivalent to proving no love exists.

It does mean the romance has not displaced the deep answer:

> `最優先すべきは自分を守ること。`

The result is therefore not `Y1H-011 = no` in an exhaustive emotional sense. It is a strong **partial negative answer**.

## 9.4 Disclosure sufficiency remains poor

Kei learns more of Ayanokōji than most classmates. She still lacks the decisive external/White-Room architecture and his class-separation project inherited from V06.

V07 deepens her peer-world autonomy while doing little to equalize knowledge inside the romance.

The two developments move in opposite directions:

> **Kei becomes more independent socially while remaining structurally subordinate informationally.**

# 10. Ichinose, Kanzaki, and Himeno — from solidaristic harmony to loyal opposition

The chapter title `反逆の狼煙`—roughly “signal fire of rebellion”—is deliberately provocative. The actual reform is less a coup than an attempt to create **legitimate internal contradiction**.

## 10.1 Kanzaki’s problem is constitutional, not personal hatred

Kanzaki tells Ayanokōji that Ichinose’s class cannot reach A despite having students with adequate individual ability. His desperation leads him to ask Ayanokōji to influence Ichinose because she has a special attachment to him. `spine12:para0120–0200`.

Ayanokōji refuses that route.

He argues that forcibly changing Ichinose could destroy the quality that makes the class distinctive. The target should instead be the class’s consciousness and response to dissent. `spine12:para0200–0250`.

This is an important refinement of the earlier “Ichinose is too nice” diagnosis.

The problem is not kindness itself.

It is a constitution in which kindness can become **socially compulsory agreement**.

## 10.2 Himeno names `同調圧力`

Himeno gives the volume’s clearest description of the class’s hidden cost:

> `私が白だと思っても多数が黒だと言えば黒になる。`

Even if she believes white, if the majority says black, black becomes the practical answer. `spine12:para0260–0288`.

She describes minority speech as exhausting because dissenters are surrounded and persuaded until they accept the group’s conclusion.

This transforms the class’s harmony from a simple moral strength into an epistemic problem.

> **A group can be sincerely kind and still produce conformity pressure severe enough that members stop revealing what they believe.**

## 10.3 Kanzaki becomes loyal opposition

Kanzaki wants the class to win. He is not attempting to destroy Ichinose or defect for convenience. He is willing, however, to leave rather than remain inside a collective that refuses all serious reform.

That threat forces Himeno to name a desire she had protected herself from expressing:

> she wants to win too.

The resulting alliance is not founded on affection. It begins in shared refusal of the class’s current equilibrium.

This is locally sufficient for `Y1H-021`:

> **Kanzaki becomes loyal opposition when his opposition is organized around preserving the class’s future rather than merely asserting that the leader is wrong.**

## 10.4 The reform is seeded, not owned

Ayanokōji had already contacted Himeno before Kanzaki unexpectedly asks him for help. This prevents a flattering reading in which he merely responds neutrally to others’ initiative.

He was already preparing an intervention.

But he also imposes a limit:

> `ここから先は第三者であるオレが立ち入る領域じゃない。`

`spine12:para0360–0384`.

He tells the pair to find people capable of speaking minority positions, expand the network, and become strong enough to defeat Horikita’s class. Then he withdraws.

This is one of the best positive examples so far of **seeded counter-institution**.

The ethical question is not whether he controls every later action. He does not.

It is whether he had the right to secretly define an enemy class’s constitutional problem and begin changing the field before the affected people knew he was involved.

# 11. Ryūen and Horikita — staged antagonism can produce real value

The cultural-festival rivalry is a particularly useful demonstration of how fiction, theater, politics, and markets overlap.

Horikita and Ryūen publicly appear to be escalating competition. Ryūen’s class launches a Japanese-style concept café. A one-million-private-point wager is discussed. The rivalry is marketed as a direct confrontation.

The public antagonism attracts visitors precisely because conflict is legible and narratively satisfying.

Later the core class members learn that the broad “betrayal” structure was coordinated between the leadership sides rather than being a spontaneous collapse of cooperation. `spine18:para0084–0125`.

This does not make the revenue fake.

The visitors really choose.

Students really work.

The cafés really compete for finite spending.

The staged narrative changes the environment in which those genuine choices occur.

That makes the festival a benign analogue of the Yagami trap.

The moral difference is important:

- the festival stage aims primarily at attention and economic competition under bounded risk;
- the Yagami stage aims at exposing a hostile covert actor and leads to expulsion/removal.

The shared mechanism does not make the two interventions ethically equivalent.

It shows that **staging is a neutral form whose legitimacy depends on stakes, disclosure, vulnerability, and available exit**.

Ryūen’s V06 development also persists. He can cooperate under bounded secret terms without losing the public combative identity that helps mobilize his class. The public predator and private contract partner coexist.

`Y1H-027` therefore remains correctly `REFRAMED`: better leadership and more sophisticated predation are compatible descriptions.

# 12. Kushida — exposed self-interest becomes usable independence

V06 established the hostile compact between Horikita and Kushida. V07 tests whether that compact survives outside the scene that created it.

It does.

Kushida contributes to the festival despite abandoning the “purely kind classmate” fiction. She remains socially skilled enough that other people continue to depend on her abilities. Her irritation and strategic self-interest do not prevent useful behavior.

More importantly, Kushida independently investigates Haruka and Akito.

She does not act because she suddenly loves Haruka. She acts because the class’s future is now aligned with her own chosen future.

That is exactly what the V06 compact promised.

Her encounter with Yagami is equally important. Kushida tells him that their relationship is finished and threatens reciprocal disclosure if he exposes her. `spine14:para0430–0505`.

This is no longer the Kushida who must destroy every witness to restore a perfect mask.

Her secret has already ceased to be universally secret. She can therefore bargain from a more integrated self.

V07 strengthens, rather than reopens:

- `Y1H-017` public/private coexistence;
- `Y1H-018` hostile legitimate cooperation;
- `Y1H-019` self-authored ongoing participation.

The volume also shows a new limit: Kushida recognizes she cannot stop Yagami alone. Self-authorship is not self-sufficiency.

That distinction is healthy.

# 13. Yagami — exceptional ability without an independent self

Yagami is the volume’s most dramatic negative model of elite formation.

## 13.1 His public surface is excellent

Before the collapse, Yagami appears:

- socially fluent;
- academically capable;
- politically useful;
- physically controlled;
- student-council compatible;
- and unusually polished.

His beautiful handwriting becomes part of the evidentiary path because the very training that makes him institutionally impressive leaves a recognizable trace. `spine13:para0268–0302`; `spine18:para0208–0215`.

This is another example of legibility turning political.

A skill is not merely a capability. Under the right comparison, it becomes provenance evidence.

## 13.2 His problem is not inability to hide one clue

The downfall is produced by accumulation.

Yagami has repeatedly acted because he assumes superiority will protect him from the consequences of small traces. Ayanokōji later calls this `火遊び`—playing with fire. `spine18:para0271–0283`.

The decisive weakness is therefore not simply “he opened a letter.”

It is a theory of self in which every action remains subordinate to defeating Ayanokōji.

## 13.3 He cannot tolerate the absence of the rival

When Yagami realizes that Ayanokōji is not even present in the room, the humiliation becomes existential.

He has not received the direct contest through which he expected to validate himself.

His language collapses toward:

- “we have not even fought”;
- “I will kill him”;
- the teachers waiting;
- the need to become number one.

`spine17:para0420–0475`.

Amasawa’s arrival is especially revealing. She calls him `拓也`, not merely “the White Room student” or “enemy.” Their shared institutional origin has produced personal attachment despite the institution’s attempt to subordinate ordinary relational life.

## 13.4 Removal exposes White Room defeat logic

Five adult men identify themselves as connected to Yagami and Amasawa, say they were called to collect Yagami, and state that Chairman Sakayanagi has been informed. `spine17:para0460–0485`.

Yagami’s reaction changes from violent superiority to childlike fear and compliance.

The local text does not tell us every detail of what awaits him.

What it does establish is enough:

> **for an extraordinary student produced by a high-control institution, defeat does not automatically open a plural outside future in the way Airi’s expulsion does.**

The contrast is one of V07’s strongest arguments for ordinary social plurality.

Airi loses the school and gains another future.

Yagami loses the school and appears to be returned toward the institution whose comparative hierarchy has organized his self.

## 13.5 The anti-climax is thematic and narratively costly

Yagami’s resolution is intentionally asymmetric. A long-running covert threat is removed without a direct climactic duel with Ayanokōji.

That is thematically coherent because direct combat would reward Yagami’s worldview. His entire identity expects value to be determined by direct comparison to the White Room masterpiece. Being defeated by a distributed convergence in Ayanokōji’s absence is a much harsher answer.

The reservation remains.

The series has invested significant mystery and threat capital into Yagami. The abrupt removal risks underusing him as a character even if the form of defeat is conceptually precise.

The strongest judgment is therefore:

> **the anti-climax is thematically stronger than a simple duel and dramatically less satisfying than the buildup may have promised.**

# 14. Amasawa — origin, attachment, and refusal of comparative destiny

Amasawa’s V07 role is brief but analytically dense.

She remains White Room-confirmed from V04 and openly relationally attached to Yagami. That attachment does not make her politically aligned with him.

During the festival, Amasawa draws Kushida away and becomes part of Ayanokōji’s reconstruction of the earlier White Room pressure network. When she threatens Kushida as leverage, Ayanokōji crosses roughly five meters and restrains her almost immediately. `COTE:Y2V07:spine15:para0400–0515`.

The physical disparity is less important than the dialogue.

Ayanokōji says he has not hunted the other White Room student because he did not regard the situation as a contest. He criticizes the use of unrelated people as collateral and tells Amasawa that if the other student wanted to challenge him, the challenge should have been direct.

He then gives Amasawa a role rather than treating her as an enemy to eliminate: secretly watch the student-council room after 3 p.m.

This preserves a pattern already visible in V04.5:

> **White Room origin is a provenance field, not a political party.**

Amasawa can:

- originate in the institution;
- admire Ayanokōji;
- care about Yagami;
- resist Tsukishiro’s preferred alignment;
- threaten Kushida;
- and still help contain Yagami’s collapse.

Her final appearance in the student-council room is especially important because she addresses Yagami as a person whose behavior can still be stopped, not merely as an enemy asset.

The relationship exposes the White Room’s persistent inability to monopolize meaning.

It may shape cohort structure and comparative ranking.

It cannot prevent cohort members from becoming emotionally significant to one another.

# 15. Tsubaki, Utomiya, and the unnamed male actor — counter-authorship behind counter-authorship

The final chapter prevents the Yagami operation from becoming a simple celebration of Ayanokōji’s omniscience.

Tsubaki tells Ayanokōji she is satisfied that Yagami has been expelled. He reconstructs that her repeated pressure on Satō was never a serious attempt to recruit Satō. The escalating pressure was designed to make Satō seek help, eventually routing information toward Ayanokōji. `COTE:Y2V07:spine18:para0226–0255`.

Tsubaki had also falsely attributed the Satō pressure to Yagami.

Ayanokōji correctly concludes that Yagami was not involved in that specific incident.

The important turn comes when he realizes Tsubaki herself is not the final author.

Utomiya hands him a phone. The male voice is the same voice associated with an earlier telephone/door encounter. V07 gives several locally admissible facts:

- Yagami caused one of Tsubaki and Utomiya’s classmates to be expelled;
- the pair therefore had an independent motive to help remove him;
- the man believed Ayanokōji could eliminate Yagami but was choosing not to;
- he wanted to create enough pressure that Ayanokōji would act before further casualties occurred;
- a prior warning about continued danger was partly a bluff intended to accelerate Ayanokōji’s response;
- the man says `綾小路先生の言う通り、この学校を選んで正解だった`;
- he says he intends to enjoy school life and, unless first- and second-years directly collide, considers his relationship with Ayanokōji ended for now.

`spine18:para0261–0292`.

The correct local classification is therefore:

> **unnamed male actor with direct knowledge of / connection to `綾小路先生`, prior access to Ayanokōji, and sufficient knowledge of Yagami’s White Room fixation to design indirect pressure through Tsubaki and Utomiya.**

Do **not** attach a later name at this boundary.

This layered authorship is one of the strongest formal achievements of V07.

Ayanokōji thinks he is solving a field created by Yagami.

Tsubaki and Utomiya are simultaneously using Ayanokōji to solve a field created by Yagami.

The unnamed man is using Tsubaki and Utomiya to motivate Ayanokōji.

Atsuomi’s name enters the scene through the man’s statement, implying still another possible authorial layer without locally establishing the exact purpose.

The sequence therefore resists the fantasy of a final puppet master.

> **Power is recursive: authors operate inside authored environments.**

This matters for Ayanokōji himself. His emerging four-class project may feel self-authored. V07 reminds the reader that even a person capable of designing environments cannot assume he has escaped every environment designed around him.

# 16. Nagumo — desire for a worthy opponent and the pathology of untested dominance

Nagumo’s V07 material changes Ayanokōji more than it changes Nagumo.

Ayanokōji’s diagnosis is that Nagumo has spent too long defeating people who ceased to press him seriously. His problem is not lack of ability but lack of formative defeat. `COTE:Y2V07:spine11:para0690–0752`.

He calls Nagumo a man who does not know defeat in the full sense.

That description links Nagumo to Yagami despite their different institutions.

Both are exceptional.

Both have identities organized around superiority.

Both become distorted when the field fails to provide an equal rival.

The difference is developmental opportunity.

Nagumo has spent years in a plural school environment where classmates, seniors, Kiryūin, Manabu, and Ayanokōji can still frustrate him.

Yagami’s comparative self is tied to a much narrower institutional hierarchy.

Ayanokōji’s new interest in Nagumo is therefore not arbitrary. He has begun wanting contests that may create experiences neither side can obtain in a field of uncontested superiority.

At the same time, V07 preserves the political critique of Nagumo’s system.

His reform creates real opportunities for lower-class third-years to reach A. But those opportunities remain tied to the resources and authority he has centralized. Mobility exists through personal sovereignty rather than independent right.

The cultural festival briefly reveals another side of his class: with ranking no longer his main concern, classmates appear able to pursue things they simply want to do for their final school festival. `spine11:para0555–0575`.

This does not dissolve patronage.

It shows that even Nagumo’s polity contains ordinary desires that exceed its political economy.

# 17. Truth, proof, and the authored record — V07’s distributed-evidence model

V03 made attribution the central problem.

V07 shows one way attribution can become robust:

> **not through one perfect piece of evidence, but through independent partial records that converge.**

The Yagami scene contains several evidentiary channels:

- Horikita’s handwriting comparison;
- Nagumo’s information about leaked special-examination rumors;
- the love-letter anagram and Yagami’s decision to open it;
- Ryūen’s pursuit of the island attacker;
- Komiya and Kinoshita’s recovered recognition;
- GPS/watch irregularity narrowing the possible attacker;
- Yagami’s own statements;
- his sudden display of hidden physical ability;
- Amasawa’s relationship-specific intervention;
- White Room-linked adults recognizing him as someone they have come to collect.

No single source is treated as omniscient.

This is a major improvement over record systems in which one institutional file can define the person.

Yet the model remains vulnerable to a hidden coordinator.

Ayanokōji deliberately brings evidence channels into proximity. The resulting proof is stronger because the channels are independent in origin, but the **moment of convergence** is authored.

This produces an important distinction for `TRUTH_PROOF_RECORD`:

> **independent evidence can be epistemically strong even when the opportunity for the evidence to meet was strategically staged.**

The task is therefore not to reject the proof because Ayanokōji designed the scene.

It is to preserve two claims simultaneously:

1. Yagami’s actions are genuinely evidenced by multiple independent sources.
2. Ayanokōji is causally responsible for creating the high-pressure context in which those sources become mutually reinforcing at one time.

V07 thus advances the series beyond the question “is the record true?”

The stronger question is:

> **who controlled the conditions under which truth became actionable?**

# 18. Expulsion, defeat, and the right to continue becoming

V05 treated expulsion as institutional culling.

V07 juxtaposes two removed students to show why “expulsion” cannot be a single developmental category.

## 18.1 Airi — removal from one institution, continuation elsewhere

Airi loses her place in ANHS.

She does not lose:

- identity;
- friendship;
- future labor;
- public visibility;
- the ability to choose a new environment;
- the ability to generate meaning for people still inside the school.

Her post-expulsion agency becomes strong enough to reverse Haruka’s own plan from outside the institution.

Airi therefore demonstrates **generative ability after institutional rejection**.

The school’s ranking may have been locally useful for class competition. It was never a complete description of her possible life.

## 18.2 Yagami — removal as possible return to prior ownership

Yagami’s removal is structurally different.

The adults who collect him are connected to the same high-control world that produced his comparative identity. His terror suggests that leaving ANHS does not mean access to a plural ordinary outside in the same way it does for Airi.

This contrast sharpens the Year-2 freedom question.

> **Expulsion is most ethically dangerous when the person does not possess a genuine outside from which a different life can be authored.**

## 18.3 Ayanokōji’s culling model receives a correction

V05 showed Ayanokōji capable of deciding who was least valuable under current institutional constraints.

V07 demonstrates a limit to that knowledge.

He may know who is least useful **here**.

He does not therefore know who is least capable of flourishing **elsewhere**.

This is not a small qualification.

It attacks the epistemic premise that makes culling feel comprehensive.

The correct revision is:

> **institutional disposability is local, not ontological.**

# 19. Ordinary life as counter-curriculum — the festival produces what strategy cannot fully own

V07 occurs under active White Room pressure.

An unknown visitor delivers a retrieval message. Amasawa and Yagami remain connected to the White Room. Ayanokōji’s father is explicitly referenced by the unnamed caller. Yagami is removed by people connected to that institutional field.

Yet the same volume is saturated with ordinary life:

- a school festival;
- costumes;
- queues;
- cookies;
- awkward love letters;
- friendship disclosure;
- embarrassing teacher photos;
- café rivalry;
- reading during work;
- classmates choosing to stay;
- people caring about graduation;
- first-years discovering that school can be fun.

The juxtaposition matters.

Tsubaki says she initially had little attachment to school but has found it unexpectedly enjoyable. The unnamed man likewise says he intends to enjoy school life. `spine18:para0287–0302`.

These are not proof that ANHS is unambiguously free.

They show why it remains a meaningful alternative developmental ecology.

The White Room attempts to make the environment legible through planned curriculum.

The cultural festival generates value through **excess**:

- somebody becomes a best friend;
- a teacher’s embarrassment becomes shared memory;
- Haruka finds another meaning for Airi’s maid outfit;
- a first-year discovers attachment to school;
- Horikita learns empathy from remembering an unsent letter;
- Hiyori reads quietly while participating in a café;
- Ayanokōji becomes interested in a rival he once considered irrelevant.

These are not efficient outputs of a single curriculum.

They are emergent products of social density.

V07 therefore strongly strengthens `ORDINARY_LIFE_COUNTER_CURRICULUM`, though it still does not close the question because Ayanokōji repeatedly converts ordinary relationships into strategic infrastructure.

The counter-curriculum survives.

It has not yet escaped appropriation.

# 20. Japanese conceptual vocabulary and register

V07’s thematic architecture is especially visible in its Japanese language.

## 20.1 `舞台` / `舞台をセッティングする`

Literally “stage” / “to set the stage.” Ayanokōji’s self-description after Yagami’s removal is the volume’s central conceptual phrase. It shifts authorship from direct command to environmental arrangement.

The metaphor is unusually apt in a cultural-festival volume because the school is literally staging performances while Ayanokōji stages a covert evidentiary collision.

## 20.2 `火遊び`

Literally “playing with fire,” but in context it names Yagami’s accumulation of risky covert actions. The phrase rejects a model in which one fatal mistake explains his removal. Many locally small transgressions create enough traces that a later stage can connect them.

## 20.3 `同調圧力`

“Conformity pressure” / peer pressure toward agreement. Himeno’s use is politically specific. The problem is not merely that classmates are friendly. It is that friendliness can become a process through which minority judgment is surrounded and normalized away.

## 20.4 `本当の意味で仲間になる`

“To become comrades/companions in the true sense.” Ayanokōji uses the phrase for the Ichinose-class reform. The implication is that harmony without exposed disagreement can be socially pleasant while still shallow as political solidarity.

## 20.5 `第三者`

“Third party.” Ayanokōji’s declaration that the next stage is not territory for a third party is a self-limitation claim. Its credibility matters because he has already seeded the reform environment.

## 20.6 `最優先`

“Highest priority.” The word makes his self-preservation hierarchy unusually explicit. The issue is not whether he cares about others; it is what remains first when values collide.

## 20.7 `親友`

“Best friend / close friend.” V07 uses the word for both loss and reconstruction:

- Haruka’s lost best friend Airi;
- Kei and Satō’s friendship becoming `親友`;
- Himeno refusing to become anyone’s `親友` as a defense against class conformity.

The repeated term creates a three-way contrast between attachment, durable disclosure, and avoidance of dependence.

## 20.8 `正解` and `解`

Ayanokōji often treats life in answer-language. V07’s `揺るぎなき『解』`—unshakable “solution”—for self-preservation makes the mathematical register of White-Room cognition explicit. The volume then surrounds that answer with people who generate futures he did not calculate: Airi, Haruka, Satō, Himeno, Tsubaki, and the unnamed caller.

# 21. Visual-form analysis

V07’s illustrations strongly reinforce its front-stage/back-stage architecture.

## 21.1 Festival color art — performance before interpretation

The opening color illustrations foreground festival spectacle: games, maid costuming, and emotionally exposed students. They establish the volume as one in which bodies are deliberately made visible to an audience.

The maid image of Chabashira is especially important. The prose treats her participation as strategically purchased labor; the illustration converts that reluctant labor into exactly the commodity the plan depends upon—a visually memorable adult performance.

## 21.2 Asahina’s fall — spectacle fails into ordinary vulnerability

`embed0009_HD.jpg` is inserted immediately after Asahina, dressed as a haunted-house ghost, trips instead of successfully frightening Ayanokōji. The performer becomes the person requiring assistance.

# 22. Formal strengths

## 22.1 The cultural festival is not filler

V07 uses a familiar school-life event as a formal laboratory for nearly every major Year-2 theme:

- visibility;
- market value;
- public identity;
- hidden authorship;
- role performance;
- institutional access;
- ordinary memory;
- and covert threat.

The “light” school-festival form makes the Yagami purge more destabilizing because the same stage supports hospitality and expulsion.

## 22.2 Public spectacle and covert staging mirror one another

The best formal achievement is the parallel between:

- the Horikita–Ryūen staged rivalry;
- the maid café;
- Chabashira’s purchased performance;
- the fake love letter;
- Tsubaki’s false attribution;
- the unnamed actor’s pressure campaign.

The volume repeatedly asks whether an authored performance produces “fake” effects.

The answer is no.

The scene may be staged while the spending, friendship, suspicion, grief, or expulsion it produces remains real.

## 22.3 Yagami’s removal denies him the genre of conflict he wants

A direct duel would center Yagami’s comparative worldview. Instead, he is destroyed by distributed evidence and his own inability to tolerate an Ayanokōji-coded invitation.

The form of defeat is therefore an argument about his psychology.

## 22.4 Airi acts from outside the narrative space that selected her

The strongest correction to V05 arrives from outside ANHS. This prevents the school from narratively monopolizing development. Airi is absent yet causally powerful.

## 22.5 The invisible actor motif remains epistemically disciplined

The unnamed male actor is left unnamed. His Atsuomi connection is direct enough to matter; his exact identity and project are not. The volume preserves a productive layer of attribution uncertainty after Yagami’s removal.

# 23. Reservations and counterreadings

## 23.1 Ayanokōji’s “stage only” rhetoric risks laundering responsibility

The text gives Ayanokōji a compelling defense: Yagami’s own acts produce his downfall.

A counterreading must insist that accurate prediction and deliberate staging still create responsibility. The fact that the target chooses does not erase the stage-setter’s purpose.

## 23.2 Haruka’s turnaround is emotionally compressed

Airi’s outside-school agency is thematically excellent. The transition from revenge serious enough to destroy the class to festival participation occurs quickly once the new evidence appears.

The text partially protects against excessive neatness by preserving Haruka’s refusal to forgive. Still, the emotional pace may feel faster than the severity of the prior rupture warrants.

## 23.3 Yagami’s anti-climax underuses accumulated threat

As noted above, the method of defeat is thematically precise. The character’s removal is abrupt relative to the long White Room mystery buildup. Both judgments can be true.

## 23.4 The Ichinose reform risks protagonist annexation

The scene is one of Ayanokōji’s healthier interventions because he withdraws and asks lower-level actors to own the continuation.

But it also risks making another class’s political maturation an Ayanokōji-generated subplot. Later analysis must watch whether Kanzaki, Himeno, Ichinose, and their classmates produce developments irreducible to his seed.

## 23.5 The Chabashira maid sequence aestheticizes coerced labor for comedy

The institutional rule makes the scheme technically legitimate. The scene is deliberately comic. The comedy should not prevent recognition that formal purchasability and subjective willingness are different categories.

## 23.6 Female relationships remain too often routed through Ayanokōji

Kei and Satō’s friendship grows through a crisis Ayanokōji chooses to structure. Ichinose reform is seeded by him. Tsubaki pressures Satō to reach him. Amasawa’s actions orbit him and Yagami.

V07 also contains counterevidence—Airi and Haruka, Horikita and Ibuki, Kanzaki and Himeno, Kei and Satō themselves after disclosure—but the protagonist’s gravitational pull remains a formal risk.

# 33. Final synthesis — the hidden author discovers that a good stage requires actors who can surprise him

V07 begins with a class trying to put on a festival.

That sounds smaller than an island survival exam, an expulsion bounty, or a direct confrontation with adults.

It is not.

The festival asks the series to convert its deepest questions into theater.

Who gets the good room?

Who has enough budget to become visible?

Who can sell an image?

Who is embarrassed by the role assigned to them?

Who performs willingly?

Who performs because the rules make refusal costly?

Who understands that a rivalry is partly staged?

Who thinks the performance is real?

What happens when the audience’s reaction becomes real economic value anyway?

The same questions govern the covert plot.

Yagami believes he is the hidden actor moving behind the school’s visible surface. He has manipulated Kushida, left traces around the island, hidden physical ability, and pursued Ayanokōji through indirect pressure. His confidence depends upon an assumption common to many elite figures in the series:

> if I understand more than the people around me, I am the author of the field.

V07 dismantles that assumption.

Horikita has information Yagami does not know she has.

Ryūen has a grievance Yagami underestimates.

Komiya and Kinoshita possess damaged but recoverable memory.

Nagumo has his own political suspicions.

Ibuki acts for reasons too crude to fit a high-level strategic model and therefore still affects it.

Amasawa cares about Yagami in a way White Room provenance does not erase.

Teachers possess jurisdiction Yagami cannot simply ignore once his violence becomes public.

Tsubaki and Utomiya have an independent revenge motive.

An unnamed male actor is using them.

Ayanokōji is using the entire field.

And Ayanokōji himself may still exist inside a larger field connected to his father.

The result is not a world of puppets.

It is a world of **nested stages**.

This is why `staged agency` is more accurate than “manipulation.” Manipulation implies that one person’s will simply replaces another’s. V07 repeatedly shows something more difficult.

The actors’ motives are real.

Yagami really chooses to open the letter.

Horikita really notices the handwriting.

Ryūen really wants the attacker.

Satō really chooses Kei.

Kanzaki really wants his class to change.

Himeno really hates conformity pressure.

Haruka really loves Airi.

Airi really chooses a new life.

Tsubaki really wants Yagami gone.

The hidden author’s power lies in understanding which conditions will let those motives encounter one another.

That is a more advanced form of environmental authorship than the earlier hidden-solver model.

It is also ethically harder to judge.

Direct coercion makes responsibility visible. If a person is forced, the coercer cannot plausibly deny authorship.

Staged agency distributes causation.

Ayanokōji can say Yagami chose black.

He can say Satō chose to remain Kei’s friend.

He can say Kanzaki and Himeno must continue the reform themselves.

He can say the old Ayanokōji Group no longer needs him.

All of those statements contain truth.

But the V2 method has to preserve the missing half:

> **who designed the conditions in which the choice became salient, urgent, or consequential?**

The Yagami case is the harshest answer.

Ayanokōji does not fabricate the misconduct. He does not force the confession. He designs an environment that is likely to make Yagami’s own history collide with independent evidence.

That is epistemically sophisticated.

It may even be more procedurally defensible than simply assaulting or blackmailing him.

Yet the sophistication creates a temptation toward moral laundering. If the environmental author is accurate enough, another person’s agency can become the very instrument through which the author avoids admitting authorship.

V07 therefore adds a necessary refinement to the Year-2 question.

The problem is no longer only:

> can Ayanokōji create independent people?

It is also:

> **can he accept responsibility for the stages he builds after the people on those stages begin acting independently?**

Airi supplies the volume’s most humane counterexample.

Nobody inside ANHS successfully authors her post-expulsion future.

She does.

The school selects her as expendable.

Ayanokōji treats her as the rational sacrifice.

Haruka imagines her as permanently broken.

Airi invalidates all three narratives by acting somewhere else.

Her idol restart is therefore not a retroactive defense of V05. It is a rebuke to institutional totality.

> **The school can remove a student from its competition. It cannot convert that local verdict into ownership of the student’s possible future.**

That insight reaches backward into Ayanokōji’s White Room-shaped model of defeat.

White Room logic tends to treat losing the curriculum as the end of the relevant developmental story. Airi demonstrates that a person can leave the institution that measured them and become legible differently elsewhere.

Yagami’s removal then supplies the tragic inverse.

Airi has an outside.

Yagami may not.

His breakdown is not only that he loses to Ayanokōji. He does not even receive the direct contest around which he has organized meaning. The people who collect him belong to the institutional world from which his comparative identity emerged.

This makes ordinary school life more than decorative freedom.

Satō accepting Kei matters.

Haruka wearing Airi’s maid outfit matters.

Himeno being able to say “I want to win” matters.

Tsubaki discovering school is unexpectedly fun matters.

Horikita remembering an unsent letter matters.

Chabashira being embarrassed in front of colleagues matters.

These experiences are not optimized White Room output. They are the social excess through which people acquire reasons that are difficult to pre-author.

Ayanokōji is increasingly fascinated by exactly that excess.

He wants Nagumo because Nagumo has become interesting.

He wants Horikita’s class to surprise him.

He creates a reform network and then steps away.

He refuses to reenter the friendship group that no longer needs him.

Those are meaningful developments.

But his deepest declared answer remains:

> `最優先すべきは自分を守ること。`

Protect himself first.

The volume therefore ends in productive contradiction.

Ayanokōji is building environments that require increasingly independent actors.

He is learning that people can continue after the institutions that judged them.

He is accepting that succession sometimes means his own disappearance from the structure.

And he is perfecting a form of authorship in which the actors can be genuinely free enough to choose while he still knows far more than they do about why the stage exists.

The Year-2 progression after V07 is consequently:

> **legibility made people measurable; jurisdiction made them bindable; attribution made hidden causation contestable; sovereignty made control plural; embeddedness made relationships meaningful; responsibility made collective choice morally costly; distributed agency made the developer less necessary; staged agency lets the developer hide inside the conditions of other people’s freedom.**

Whether that final movement is a step toward ethical developmental authorship or a more sophisticated form of control remains open.
