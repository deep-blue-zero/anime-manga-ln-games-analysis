# 『ようこそ実力至上主義の教室へ』
## Year 2 — Ethics, Autonomy, Protection, Expulsion, and Violence
### From the right to intervene to the right to author choice: jurisdiction, refusal, externalized cost, and counter-authorship under asymmetrical power

> **Canonical specialist synthesis at the complete Year-2 / Second List boundary.**
>
> This document is the primary Year-2 home for intervention ethics, autonomy, consent, refusal, paternalism, protection, dependency, expulsion, disposability, violence, coercion, informational harm, jurisdiction, repair, responsibility under authored environments, and counter-authorship. It synthesizes the frozen Year-1 ethics specialist, the canonical through-Year-2 ethics ledger, immutable source-local readings, the Y1→Y2 handoff tracker, and the completed `Y2_01–Y2_07` specialist layer. It does not replace the source-local readings or compact ledgers and imports no Year-3 narrative evidence.

# 0. Scope, primary home, and interpretive controls

Year 1 established the governing warning:

> **Beneficial outcomes do not settle moral authority.**

A more capable actor may diagnose another person correctly, choose an outcome that later proves beneficial, and execute an intervention with exceptional skill. None of those facts alone establishes the right to select the other person's goal, conceal material terms, manufacture danger, monopolize protection, distribute risk to uninformed third parties, use violence beyond necessity, or treat later gratitude as retroactive consent.

Year 2 does not weaken that conclusion. It makes the problem harder because coercion becomes less visible.

A sufficiently informed actor does not need to command. The actor can instead alter:

- what the target knows;
- when the target knows it;
- which alternatives appear available;
- how expensive refusal becomes;
- which relationship is trusted;
- which vulnerability is activated;
- which institutional rule converts a decision into consequence;
- which audience receives which version of the truth;
- and which future is framed as the natural completion of development.

The target can then choose sincerely.

That sincerity does not erase the authored environment.

The ethical problem of Year 2 is therefore not whether agency or manipulation is “really” present. They can coexist. The problem is how responsibility should be distributed when one person authors conditions and another person authors the terminal act.

This specialist keeps five controls permanently active.

## 0.1 Explanation is not justification

White Room conditioning, trauma, institutional incentives, grief, attachment, political pressure, and developmental history explain conduct. They do not automatically authorize it.

Volume 0 is especially dangerous if used carelessly here. It reveals a genealogy for preference engineering and developmental sovereignty. It does not convert that genealogy into an excuse for later behavior.

## 0.2 Effectiveness is not legitimacy

A plan's success is evidence of competence and causal efficacy.

It is not by itself evidence of necessity, proportionality, legitimate authority, informed consent, or fair distribution of risk.

## 0.3 Agency and authored conditions are not mutually exclusive

The corpus rejects both shortcuts:

> “The hidden strategist caused the environment, therefore everyone else was a puppet.”

and

> “The target chose, therefore the environment designer is morally irrelevant.”

Maezono lies. Sakayanagi chooses. Kei refuses. Kushida chooses to continue. Ichinose constructs a third answer.

Those acts remain theirs.

The conditions around those acts can still be heavily authored by someone else.

## 0.4 Later improvement is not retroactive consent

A later good life, useful contribution, repaired relationship, successful separation, or self-authored appropriation changes the consequence record.

It does not rewrite who possessed legitimate standing at the earlier moment of imposition.

## 0.5 Year 3 remains locked

Every terminal judgment is frozen at `Y2SL`. Questions whose decisive missing condition lies beyond that boundary remain open even where later evidence may exist elsewhere.

# 1. Executive thesis — Year 2 turns intervention ethics into authored-choice ethics

The strongest Year-1 positive standard was the **answer-back standard**:

> **Power is most legitimate when the person affected can understand it, contest it, refuse where possible, demand reasons, survive disagreement, and become someone the powerful actor did not design.**

Year 2 strengthens that standard into a harder test.

Direct domination is ethically visible. A threat, blow, command, blackmail demand, or explicit institutional sanction announces the fact that another person's options are being constrained.

Year 2 increasingly studies something subtler: **formal freedom used as a control surface**.

Ayanokōji can leave the final choice formally open while arranging information, timing, trust, incentives, dependency, and relational cues so that a preferred answer becomes highly predictable. A class leader can act inside an exam rule while still exceeding legitimate jurisdiction over a classmate. An institution can preserve secret ballots while designing a unanimity structure that converts continued refusal into collective punishment. A protector can genuinely care while withholding material information required for the protected person to decide what the relationship means.

The central Year-2 ethical question becomes:

> **When another person's agency remains real but the choice environment has been authored around a superior model of that person, what makes the result answerable rather than merely predictable?**

The answer is not perfect independence.

COTE repeatedly rejects autonomy-as-isolation. People become stronger through friendship, teaching, trust, institutional support, contracts, and even unequal relationships. The morally important question is whether dependence becomes **answerable interdependence**.

Answerable interdependence requires, in increasing strength:

1. a goal the subject can recognize as relevant to their own life;
2. a diagnosis strong enough to justify intervention but humble enough to admit error;
3. material information before timing makes the decision effectively irreversible;
4. meaningful refusal or exit where circumstances permit;
5. a legitimate basis of jurisdiction rather than mere superior ability;
6. proportionate methods and serious consideration of less-coercive alternatives;
7. risk borne by decision-makers rather than exported only to subjects and third parties;
8. reconstructable reasons and evidence sufficient for later challenge;
9. repair when intervention causes avoidable harm;
10. plural support that reduces dependency monopoly;
11. survival after separation;
12. and, strongest of all, **counter-authorship**—the ability to produce an answer the stronger model did not supply and make that answer consequential for the stronger actor.

This creates the Year-2 ethical hierarchy:

> **beneficial outcome < functional independence < informed self-authorship < counter-authorship**

The lower levels matter. They are simply not sufficient substitutes for the higher ones.

The year's strongest positive local rule arrives in V11:

> `ここから先は、オレの立ち入る領域じゃない。`
>
> **From here on, this is not an area for me to enter.**

The line does not make prior staging disappear. Its importance is that Ayanokōji identifies a stopping point: recognition may justify attention, invitation, warning, or bounded assistance without creating permanent jurisdiction over the recognized person's answer.

The strongest negative movement occurs in V12. Ayanokōji demonstrates that the final act can remain formally free while earlier design makes another person's trust, love, fear, guilt, or loyalty function like an instrument.

The strongest terminal corrective then comes from Ichinose in V12.5:

> `綾小路くんが私を利用するように、私もあなたを利用する。その権利はあるよね?`

The point is not symmetry. Their information and causal power remain unequal.

The point is **corrective standing**. She names the method, refuses its one-sided legitimacy, creates terms he did not supply, and makes him answer to her causal claim.

Year 2 therefore ends with a principle stricter than “respect choice”:

> **Superior knowledge should increase the burden of restraint, not expand the presumption of jurisdiction.**

And with a warning stricter than “manipulation is bad”:

> **The most sophisticated domination in Year 2 does not eliminate choice. It learns how to use choice.**

# 2. The complete Year-2 intervention audit

The Year-1 specialist established a multidimensional intervention audit. The through-Year-2 ethics ledger preserves it and adds temporal authorship, succession, and counter-authorship. This specialist adopts the full fifteen-dimension form as the canonical Year-2 test.

No one dimension decides every case. Severity and irreversibility determine how much failure the intervention can tolerate.

## 2.1 Goal authorship

Who selected the end?

Possible authors include:

- the affected person;
- a friend or romantic partner;
- a class leader;
- a protector;
- the school;
- a hidden strategist;
- a teacher;
- an administrator;
- or an external political actor.

Supporting a goal the person already owns is presumptively stronger than imposing a developmental goal because the intervener believes the future self will eventually approve.

## 2.2 Epistemic warrant and model humility

How reliable is the diagnosis?

Ayanokōji often possesses exceptional epistemic warrant. That matters. An ignorant intervener and an accurate expert are not ethically identical.

But Year 2 repeatedly demonstrates **good models with incomplete ontology**:

- Ayanokōji can predict Kei's behavior without producing the romantic feeling he expected in himself;
- Sakayanagi can rank contribution while failing to understand Kamuro's irreplaceability;
- Ayanokōji can prepare a binary for Ichinose and still receive a third answer;
- OAA can measure real observed performance without exhausting latent capacity or total personhood.

The ethical requirement is not ignorance. It is the capacity to treat the model as corrigible.

## 2.3 Imminence and necessity

Is serious harm imminent enough to justify overriding ordinary refusal?

Immediate physical defense receives a much stronger exception than developmental pressure.

Someone being immature, strategically weak, lonely, emotionally dependent, socially awkward, or politically inconvenient does not create emergency jurisdiction.

## 2.4 Proportionality

How severe is the method relative to the threatened harm?

The justification burden rises sharply with:

- bodily injury;
- sexual or bodily violation;
- retraumatization;
- public humiliation;
- destruction of trust;
- irreversible expulsion;
- severe reputational damage;
- and third-party harm.

## 2.5 Less-coercive alternatives

Could teaching, warning, invitation, contract, delay, witness, honest confrontation, or refusal-preserving assistance plausibly achieve the relevant goal?

Year 2 contains enough successful low-jurisdiction interventions that severe methods cannot be defended simply as the only form of competence Ayanokōji possesses.

## 2.6 Information and informed consent

What does the affected person know about:

- the goal;
- the real purpose;
- the material risk;
- the alternatives;
- the likely consequences;
- hidden future obligations;
- and timing?

Consent to the surface event is not consent to a hidden curriculum.

## 2.7 Refusal and exit capacity

Can the person say no without catastrophic engineered loss of:

- safety;
- status;
- belonging;
- protection;
- educational future;
- mobility;
- or the relationship itself?

Formal options that are too costly to exercise may preserve legal or procedural choice while providing weak substantive autonomy.

## 2.8 Legitimate authority / jurisdiction

Why does this actor have standing to decide?

Possible grounds include:

- immediate defense;
- fiduciary office;
- explicit delegation;
- transparent contract;
- reciprocal relationship;
- institutional role;
- or genuine emergency necessity.

Mere superior ability is the weakest general ground.

Year 2 sharpens the distinction with Hirata's V12.5 diagnosis of `越権行為`: the fact that a representative can execute a permitted decision does not mean the representative possesses sovereign moral ownership over the classmate sacrificed by that decision.

## 2.9 Risk distribution and third-party externalities

Who pays if the plan fails—or succeeds?

Hidden authorship is ethically worse when the planner remains comparatively insulated while classmates, romantic partners, rumor targets, weak students, or entire class polities absorb the downside.

The V05 Kushida/Airi decision is especially important because preserving one person shifts terminal cost onto another.

## 2.10 Dependency created

Does assistance leave the person:

- more capable of acting without the helper;
- supported by multiple relationships;
- able to refuse the helper;
- or more dependent on the helper's protection, interpretation, and approval?

The strongest protection weakens monopoly.

## 2.11 Transparency, provenance, and answerability

Can affected people later reconstruct:

- what happened;
- who designed it;
- who authorized it;
- what evidence existed;
- and why the chosen method was used?

Can they demand reasons, contest the account, preserve contrary evidence, or correct the decision-maker?

Year 2 makes provenance an ethical good because hidden strategists and administrators can otherwise control not only action but the later narrative of action.

## 2.12 Reversibility and repair

Can a mistaken intervention be undone?

A lost point is not equivalent to a destroyed relationship, serious injury, public trauma exposure, or expulsion.

The more irreversible the outcome, the higher the threshold for intervention and the stronger the duty of repair.

## 2.13 Temporal authorship

Who controls **when** the subject receives material information or the chance to answer?

Timing becomes jurisdiction when truth is withheld until the target's meaningful alternatives have narrowed.

Year 2's major cases include:

- reserving the timing of Ichinose's developmental crisis;
- keeping Kei outside the breakup timetable until termination;
- informing Horikita of the class-transfer reality only after the choice is effectively settled;
- and delivering Sakayanagi's clue at the moment it can function as an actionable relational veto.

A truth delivered too late may be accurate while still failing the autonomy test.

## 2.14 Separation and succession

Does the developed person continue after the developer leaves?

This matters because protection that makes the protector permanently indispensable is ethically weaker than development that survives separation.

But separation is not enough.

The separated person may merely perform the norm the developer installed.

## 2.15 Counter-authorship / corrective standing

Can the affected person produce an answer the intervener did not pre-author and require the intervener to respond to it as another person's claim?

This is the strongest Year-2 extension of the answer-back standard.

Counter-authorship does not erase earlier wrongdoing.

It proves that developmental causation has not become ownership.

# 3. Consent taxonomy — what exactly was agreed to?

“Consent” is too coarse unless its object and informational boundary are specified.

## 3.1 Surface consent

The person agrees to the immediate visible act.

This says little about hidden purposes, future use, or concealed risks.

## 3.2 Informed consent

The person knows the material purpose, risks, alternatives, likely consequences, and relevant future obligations well enough to own the decision.

COTE rarely provides ideal informed consent because asymmetric information is central to its drama. That does not make the category dispensable. It makes deviations analytically important.

## 3.3 Constrained consent

The person knowingly chooses among options with heavily unequal costs not secretly manufactured by the immediate intervener.

Many special-exam decisions belong here.

## 3.4 Authored-choice consent

The terminal choice is real, but another actor has deliberately designed information, timing, dependency, or incentives to make one answer especially likely.

V12 is the canonical field.

## 3.5 Retrospective appropriation

A person later adopts a path that was originally imposed.

Kushida is the cleanest Year-2 case.

Her V05 preservation is not self-authored. Her V06 continuation is:

> `私は私のためだけに１年半戦ってクラスに貢献する。`

The later choice makes the **ongoing arrangement** more legitimate.

It does not retroactively legitimate the earlier imposition or the cost borne by Airi and Haruka.

## 3.6 Counter-authored consent

The person alters the offered terms, creates a new agreement, or claims reciprocal standing the stronger actor did not provide.

Ichinose's V12.5 answer is the strongest boundary case.

## 3.7 Forgiveness is not retroactive consent

Kei's forgiveness and later romance are ethically real because they are hers.

They do not establish that no wrong occurred earlier.

Forgiveness has meaning precisely because an actual moral debt can remain part of the relationship's history.

# 4. Autonomy — not isolation, but authorship inside relation

The completed Year-2 corpus strongly rejects the idea that autonomy means needing nobody.

A more mature model separates at least four forms.

## 4.1 Negative autonomy

Protection against being forced, threatened, blackmailed, surveilled without limit, injured, expelled without sufficient process, or made dependent through manufactured danger.

## 4.2 Positive autonomy

The ability to choose what one's development is for rather than merely becoming better at the goal another actor selected.

## 4.3 Relational autonomy

The ability to remain attached, dependent in some domains, and socially embedded while still preserving information, refusal, independent purpose, corrective standing, and survivable disagreement.

## 4.4 Political autonomy

The ability to possess standing inside a collective without being reduced to current utility, majority preference, or the leader's model of what is best for the group.

Kōenji is important here. Membership creates bounded obligations and enforceable bargains. It does not grant the class ownership of his total private capacity.

## 4.5 Counter-authorship as strongest autonomy evidence

Functional independence asks whether the person can continue without the intervener.

Counter-authorship asks whether the person can generate a purpose, refusal, judgment, or future the intervener did not already own.

That second test is stricter.

Sudō's development after romantic rejection, Kushida's hostile self-authored cooperation, Ichinose's third answer, and Horikita's independent political judgments all show forms of autonomy that remain relational rather than atomistic.

# 5. Protection — safety without title over the protected person

Year 1 established that protection is not ownership. Year 2 makes the positive standard more precise.

Protection can include:

- preventing bodily harm;
- preserving membership;
- preserving reputation;
- providing information;
- absorbing political cost;
- opening mobility;
- offering emotional refuge;
- creating evidence routes;
- or keeping future options available.

Protection becomes proprietary when superior access is converted into an ongoing claim to decide what risk another person may take, what truth they may hear, when they may break, whom they may become, or when the relationship should end.

The strongest Year-2 protection test is therefore:

> **After I make you safer, are you more able to disagree with me?**

Positive cases include:

- Satō becoming a horizontal support route for Kei rather than another branch of Ayanokōji's monopoly;
- Horikita accepting Kushida's self-interested reason instead of demanding gratitude or affection;
- independent witness and provenance systems that make stronger actors answerable;
- bounded intervention that opens a future without specifying its content.

Mixed or negative cases include:

- Ayanokōji serving as Kei's central psychological pillar while withholding material relationship information;
- Ayanokōji reserving Ichinose's developmental timetable;
- Nagumo concentrating mobility through patronage;
- White Room protection that treats development as ownership.

The canonical rule is:

> **Protection is ethically strongest when it pluralizes support, preserves exit, and weakens the protector's monopoly.**

# 6. Developmental intervention — teaching, paternalism, and jurisdiction

COTE does not support a blanket ethic of nonintervention.

Refusing to intervene can abandon people to violence, false records, isolation, institutional coercion, or predictable harm.

The question is which intervention mode is being used.

## 6.1 Autonomy-supporting assistance

The subject substantially owns the goal; the helper provides information, skill, opportunity, or limited scaffolding; refusal remains meaningful; and the helper does not claim the result.

## 6.2 Bounded paternalism

The helper temporarily withholds or overrides some preference under a plausible protective or developmental rationale, but the method is proportionate, limited, and oriented toward restoring greater agency.

V03 Ike is a relatively low-severity example: rescue information is withheld to preserve determination, while Ike still authors the decisive social act.

## 6.3 Coercive developmental authorship

The intervener selects the developmental goal, engineers pressure, narrows options, withholds material information, or creates a crisis in order to force growth.

Year 1 supplied several severe cases. Year 2 demonstrates that the same structure can operate through timing and relationship rather than obvious crisis.

## 6.4 Manufactured dependency / abusive domination

The intervener creates or exploits danger and then becomes the privileged route to safety, status, or belonging.

The Year-1 Kei origin remains the canonical warning.

## 6.5 Counter-authorable development

The Year-2 positive extension is development that deliberately leaves enough independent space for the subject to revise, reject, or exceed the model.

V11 supplies the cleanest local stopping rule.

The ethical objective is not to eliminate influence.

It is to keep influence from becoming title.

# 7. Year-2 ethical progression I — V01 to V06

The first half of Year 2 moves from public legibility and hidden violence toward the most consequential sacrificial decision yet made inside Horikita's class.

## 7.1 Y2V01 — public measurement, hidden bounty, coercive bargaining, and bodily self-instrumentalization

V01 begins by making ordinary students more legible through OAA while leaving the rule that matters most to Ayanokōji hidden: the bounty created around his expulsion.

This establishes an ethical asymmetry that persists through the year:

> **the governed may be highly visible while the authorship of the governing pressure remains opaque.**

The cross-year partner market also demonstrates that a formally educational rule can create coercive bargaining power. First-years gain leverage because second-years face far more severe consequences from failed pairings.

Horikita's response is ethically stronger than pure market extraction. She attempts equal class-to-class cooperation rather than pricing weak students out of access.

Hōsen provides the contrary model. His political authority relies on bodily intimidation, threat credibility, and willingness to treat violence as bargaining infrastructure.

Ayanokōji's knife solution is a different problem. He allows his own hand to become evidence. The act is strategically effective and directed toward defeating a planted-weapon narrative, but it also demonstrates an inherited ethical distortion: his body can be treated as an expendable instrument with remarkably little ordinary hesitation.

The case therefore belongs partly to autonomy: a person can exercise self-authorship while still carrying a conditioning-derived conception of bodily cost that deserves scrutiny.

## 7.2 Y2V02 — partner choice and the jurisdiction problem

V02 deepens the distinction between measuring capacity and possessing jurisdiction over another person's future.

The school can publish scores and define pair incentives. Classes can negotiate. Individuals still retain interests that do not collapse into aggregate optimization.

The volume's ethical importance lies less in one spectacular violation than in the normalization of **future-binding decisions through partial models**.

A public score can make another student strategically attractive or dangerous. That does not make the score a moral entitlement to recruit, exclude, or own the student's contribution.

## 7.3 Y2V03 — paternalistic information withholding and rescue

Ayanokōji withholds a high-probability rescue plan from Ike because he believes hope may reduce Ike's determination.

Ike's eventual words remain his own.

The intervention is therefore neither pure autonomy nor severe domination.

It is a low-to-moderate paternalistic case: the developer controls information for motivational effect, but the target remains the author of the decisive relational act and the cost is comparatively bounded.

Nanase provides a second mixed case. Ayanokōji chooses preservation rather than disposal, which is a real protective gain. Yet he continues to frame the interaction through strategic utility and superior informational position.

The lesson is not that all opacity is equally wrongful. It is that **motivational opacity is still an exercise of jurisdiction and should be justified proportionately to the stakes**.

## 7.4 Y2V04 — immediate danger, administrative capture, and counterforce

The island confrontation demonstrates one of the strongest cases for forceful intervention in the corpus because physical danger is immediate and administrative power itself is compromised.

Tsukishiro and Shiba show why formal office cannot settle legitimate authority. Administrative position becomes an instrument of private purpose and physical danger.

Ayanokōji's resistance is therefore not illegitimate merely because it resists an officeholder or relies on force. Immediate defense and preservation of independent evidence can justify conduct that ordinary school procedure would not.

But the deeper positive structure is counterforce.

The local environment can be controlled. The later truth cannot be fully monopolized once independent rescue and witness routes activate.

This yields a crucial distinction:

> **stopping power is not truth-forcing power.**

An ethical institution needs both the capacity to stop abuse and the capacity to reconstruct what occurred after an interested actor attempts to control the record.

## 7.5 Y2V04.5 — embeddedness makes care and coercion more powerful

V04.5 makes relationships denser and therefore ethically more consequential.

Nagumo's third-year network shows how mobility and opportunity can coexist with patronage dependency. Kushida correctly identifies that possession of a secret can create latent coercive capacity before any explicit threat is spoken. Kei and Ayanokōji's romance becomes more genuinely reciprocal in ordinary behavior while the hidden `恋愛のカリキュラム` remains intact.

The volume's key ethical result is double:

> **social embeddedness can humanize power and also give power more intimate control surfaces.**

Ayanokōji's statement to Chabashira provides a positive role principle: a teacher should not make students pursue Class A because the teacher wants it; professional authority should respond to the students' own strongly held goal.

## 7.6 Y2V05 — unanimity, Kushida, Airi, and manufactured disposability

V05 is the first indispensable Year-2 dirty-hands case.

The school creates an exam in which a class can enter a branch that makes some expulsion mandatory. The class then has to convert a person into a terminal cost.

The ethical sequence is:

> **institution authors scarcity → class locks the branch → leader selects preservation priority → strategist identifies substitute victim → target consents under pressure → collective vote records removal**

Every stage matters because responsibility is distributed.

Horikita's decision to preserve Kushida is genuinely independent judgment. It cannot be dismissed as Ayanokōji's order. Her anti-static-disposability principle is also morally serious: she refuses to reduce a difficult person to current hostility when she sees rare future capacity.

But that principle does not generate unlimited jurisdiction.

Kushida did not ask to be preserved. Horikita's choice protects one person's future against that person's immediate preference while moving irreversible cost onto Airi and Haruka.

Ayanokōji's substitute selection compounds the problem. He uses a transparent strategic criterion relative to the crisis—low current contribution—but also possesses unusually precise knowledge of Airi's guilt, attachments, weak self-assertion, and valuation of his judgment.

The mature conclusion is:

> **Airi authors the final act, but Ayanokōji authors much of the choice environment in which that act becomes psychologically difficult to refuse.**

This is not mind control. It is not moral innocence either.

Airi's later successful Shizuku life changes the consequence story. Kushida's later usefulness changes the consequence story. Neither fact retroactively creates legitimate authority over the original sacrifice.

## 7.7 Y2V06 — responsibility after sacrifice and self-authored appropriation

V06 refuses moral closure.

Ayanokōji explicitly regards Horikita's V05 choice as irrational by his own model but allows the result to remain consequential because he wants to see where her independent path leads. This is both genuine respect for another person's judgment and continued conversion of that independence into experimental value.

Horikita/Kushida then produce the clearest example of **retrospective appropriation without retrospective cleansing**.

Horikita stops demanding that Kushida cooperate from gratitude, class affection, or moral rehabilitation. Kushida selects her own motive:

> `私は私のためだけに１年半戦ってクラスに貢献する。`

Their hostile compact matters because it does not falsify the history. The ongoing project becomes more legitimate precisely because hatred, self-interest, and refusal of sentimental reconciliation are permitted to remain.

The principle is:

> **The rescue was Horikita-authored. Continued participation becomes Kushida-authored.**

That does not erase Airi's cost.

V06 also strengthens distributed agency as an ethical good. Sudō can reach answers without Ayanokōji owning them. Hirata, Kei, Horikita, Sudō, Onodera, and others begin to solve more problems without a single hidden help desk.

The relevant moral gain is not merely efficiency.

It is reduction of epistemic and protective monopoly.

# 8. Year-2 ethical progression II — V07 to Y2SL

The second half of Year 2 moves from distributed stage-setting and relational legibility to a terminal confrontation with authored choice.

## 8.1 Y2V07 — stage-setting, Yagami, and the accountability problem

The Yagami operation is ethically mixed because it preserves a surprising amount of other people's independent agency while still depending on hidden stage-setting.

Ayanokōji does not simply fabricate a charge and force an outcome. He constructs an environment in which prior actions, handwriting evidence, other students' suspicions, institutional actors, and Yagami's own decisions become causally relevant.

That is ethically stronger than manufacturing the entire case.

It also creates an accountability problem.

A sufficiently skilled stage-setter can arrange conditions, allow others to perform the visible acts, and later minimize his own contribution without uttering a simple lie.

Year 2 therefore requires responsibility for **choice-environment authorship**, not only responsibility for the terminal act.

The same volume also returns Airi to the analysis from outside ANHS. Her life does not end where the school's membership record ends. This becomes the strongest early evidence for a post-expulsion personhood principle:

> **institutional removal is not ontological deletion.**

## 8.2 Y2V08 — care can be real while timing remains proprietary

Ayanokōji's treatment of Ichinose is ethically difficult precisely because the care is not fake.

He recognizes distress, provides comfort, and helps preserve functioning.

He also retains the belief that a later collapse, confrontation, or decisive answer should occur on a timetable he controls.

The lesson is stronger than “care can be manipulation.”

It is:

> **accurate care can increase control capacity because intimacy improves the model.**

The more one person knows about another person's vulnerabilities, loyalties, hopes, and self-conception, the more important jurisdiction and restraint become.

## 8.3 Volume 0 — preference engineering as ethical genealogy, not excuse

Volume 0 is inserted here because the architecture mandates its retrospective position after Y2V08.

Its ethical importance is not that it reveals a single hidden motive for every later act.

It reveals a prior political grammar.

Atsuomi's preference-engineering logic asks how to shape conditions so that another person comes to prefer the outcome the designer wants. The White Room extends that logic into development itself.

Children become educational material, measured outputs, political investments, and future resources. Consent is structurally absent. Curriculum, relationship availability, emotional expression, acceptable failure, and future use all become objects of centralized authorship.

This is why the White Room's central wrong cannot be summarized as “harsh education.”

Its central wrong is **total developmental sovereignty converted into proprietorship**.

The system assumes that because it caused excellence, it acquires title over the excellent person's future.

That inference is rejected.

Volume 0 simultaneously proves the limits of total authorship. Yuki seeks relation. Shirō wants ordinary freedom. Kiyotaka develops curiosity about the outside.

The ethical principle is:

> **authorship of an environment creates responsibility for the environment; it does not create ownership of every purpose produced inside it.**

## 8.4 Y2V09 — measurement can become developmental rather than terminal

V09 is ethically important because it supplies a constructive correction to V05's disposability logic.

A static low academic profile can become high marginal value when the rule rewards improvement and development. The student who once looked like a liability under one conversion system can become disproportionately useful under another.

The ethical significance is not simply “weak students can improve.”

It is that institutional measurement should remain smaller than personhood and temporally revisable.

A record should inform coordination without becoming a warrant for terminal judgment.

## 8.5 Y2V09.5 — ordinary participant mode and the internal-restraint problem

The Hiyori outing supplies one of the cleanest low-jurisdiction relations in the corpus.

A desire is stated. An invitation is made. Refusal remains available. The relation does not need crisis, rescue, debt, or developmental ownership to justify itself.

The ordinary-life countermodel is simple:

> **I want this; you may refuse; the relationship can survive the answer.**

At the same time, Ayanokōji recognizes pleasure in manipulating through speech.

That observation must remain narrow. It does not prove sadism and cannot be generalized to every intervention.

It does create a serious ethics-of-power problem.

If control has intrinsic experiential reward, then internal benevolence is a weaker safeguard. External answerability, stopping rules, independent relationships, and people capable of correcting him become more important.

## 8.6 Y2V10 — protection scarcity, Kamuro, and personhood beyond optimization

V10 forces leaders to allocate finite protection.

Scarcity can make prioritization necessary without turning the resulting ranking into a theory of human worth.

Horikita's decision-making improves because she can be corrected from below. Ike's intervention changes the decision environment. Ibuki's possible loss creates real personal friction rather than automatic rescue. Kōenji's contract remains bounded instead of becoming a claim on all his capacity.

Sakayanagi/Kamuro provides the negative optimization case.

Sakayanagi can accurately model contribution and still fail to understand irreplaceability until loss makes it visible.

The case demonstrates that a strategist can be correct about replaceable function while wrong about the person-shaped remainder that function does not measure.

Later regret is meaningful.

It does not reverse the irreversible choice.

## 8.7 Y2V11 — recognition without ownership and the bounded-intervention rule

V11 is the strongest positive ethical local model in Year 2.

Ayanokōji accurately diagnoses Yamamura and Sakayanagi's relational problem. He stages access to an encounter. That staging remains hidden and therefore is not ethically pure.

But he then names a limit:

> `ここから先は、オレの立ち入る領域じゃない。`

The future may become closer, more distant, or end entirely. Recognition does not entitle him to own that answer.

A similar structure appears with Amasawa. The message reopens a future without specifying what the future must contain.

This is the closest the completed Year-2 corpus comes to a mature principle of developmental restraint:

> **recognize; open an option; preserve alternatives; stop before the answer becomes yours.**

Ryūen/Tokitō provides the negative control. Violence against internal dissent may preserve leader control and coexist with voluntary follower loyalty, but sociological legitimacy does not substitute for protected corrective standing.

A polity in which disagreement can still be physically disciplined remains ethically incomplete.

## 8.8 Y2V12 — relational jurisdiction and authorship without command

V12 is the most severe stress test of the Year-2 ethical architecture.

Its governing problem is not direct coercion.

It is whether a person can preserve formal freedom while making the other person's own trust, fear, guilt, loyalty, or love perform the work of command.

### Maezono — procedure becomes culling technology

Ayanokōji knows that Maezono's information leak does not ordinarily require expulsion and that correction is plausible. He nevertheless designs the traitor bargain so Ichinose can identify Maezono, reassures Maezono that he will not identify her, induces a predictable denial, and allows the exam rule to convert that denial into terminal membership loss (`Y2V12-E111–E127`; `COTE:Y2V12:spine18`).

The formal procedure remains real.

The terminal act remains Maezono's lie.

The environment is deliberately designed around that expected act.

This is therefore a paradigmatic case of **disproportionate relational/procedural jurisdiction**.

### Ichinose — polity protection and personal violation coexist

The bargain has real class-level benefits. Ichinose's classmates are protected from traitor-expulsion risk and her class gains points.

The same arrangement makes her unknowingly complicit in Maezono's expulsion and gives Ayanokōji the position from which he can attack the non-exam basis of her reasoning: her trust in him.

The intervention deliberately degrades cognition in order to defeat a particular exam strength.

The ethical offense is therefore not cancelled by the strategic benefit.

It is **relational instrumentalization**: accumulated trust is converted from a good of the relationship into an attack surface.

### Sakayanagi — inferred preference becomes relational veto

Sakayanagi is not ordered to lose.

She can win.

She reconstructs Ayanokōji's preference from a deliberately designed clue environment and chooses to satisfy it.

Her agency is unusually strong. The ethical problem remains because Ayanokōji knows her attachment well enough to make an inferred preference function like a command without issuing one.

This is the year's clearest demonstration that:

> **less direct command can coexist with more sophisticated control.**

Sakayanagi's later decision remains hers. Ayanokōji's responsibility for constructing the cue field remains his.

### V12's governing rule

> **Leaving the last act free is not sufficient when earlier design has made one answer predictably serve the hidden author's preference.**

## 8.9 Y2V12.5 — aftermath, dirty hands, separation, and counter-authorship

V12.5 prevents victory from becoming moral closure.

Horikita's class reaches provisional Class A, yet Maezono is still being processed out of the institution. Achievement and distributive cost remain visible together.

Sudō asks whether victory without expulsion was possible. Ayanokōji's own assessment is that it was **not impossible**, only less probable (`COTE:Y2V12.5:spine12`).

That admission matters enormously.

Maezono's expulsion cannot be defended as forced by physical impossibility. It was selected because it increased reliability for a larger plan.

Hirata identifies the constitutional problem with `越権行為`: representative authority does not automatically include sovereign moral title to sacrifice a classmate merely because a rule permits that route.

### Kei — functional independence without informed authorship

Kei can behave with strength during separation. Her horizontal support is stronger than before. She can refuse further explanation and remain socially functional.

Those are genuine gains.

The hidden curriculum and timetable remain ethical failures. She did not receive material information early enough to co-author the meaning, duration, or developmental purpose of the relationship.

Separation is therefore evidence of capability, not retroactive proof of legitimate curriculum.

### Horikita — succession without timetable reciprocity

Ayanokōji's goal of a class capable of functioning without him is legitimate as a developmental aspiration.

Horikita is not given timely information sufficient to participate in the meaning or timetable of the transfer.

The year therefore ends with a difference between **judgment reciprocity** and **timetable reciprocity**.

She can correct him and oppose him on substantive judgments. She cannot yet co-author the separation that will reorganize her political environment.

### Ichinose — strongest counter-authorship result

Ichinose does not simply recover into the role Ayanokōji prepared.

She identifies the method, criticizes its one-sidedness, predicts his arrival, and claims reciprocal causal standing:

> `綾小路くんが私を利用するように、私もあなたを利用する。その権利はあるよね?`

Ayanokōji cannot legitimately deny the proposition.

The relation remains asymmetric. Important information remains unavailable to her. Attachment remains intense.

But the causal structure has changed.

The target of developmental authorship has become a modeler who can force the developer to answer.

That is why the Year-2 terminal autonomy standard is **counter-authorship**, not separation alone.

## 8.10 Second List — boundary calibration without moral retroactivity

*Second List* can confirm boundary state, public profiles, relationship framing, OAA snapshots, and class-level retrospective commentary.

It cannot be used to rewrite the ethical status of earlier acts merely because later outcomes look favorable.

The guidebook boundary therefore strengthens several conclusions—development is real, class trajectories are real, people changed—without altering the anti-retroactivity rule.

# 9. Ayanokōji — the ethics of environmental authorship

Ayanokōji remains the central Year-2 ethical problem because his epistemic power is unusually high and because his methods become progressively less dependent on direct command.

His Year-2 development is genuinely double.

He becomes more capable of ordinary participation, affection, restraint, and low-jurisdiction relation.

He also becomes more capable of designing environments in which other people choose what he wants without being ordered.

Neither trend cancels the other.

## 9.1 Principal ethical virtue — developmental possibility

Ayanokōji repeatedly sees future capacity where static institutions, current scores, or other students see defects.

That vision can protect people from premature disposability.

It can also create better teaching, distributed agency, and opportunities for people to become more capable.

## 9.2 Principal ethical failure — insight mistaken for jurisdiction

The recurrent failure is not simply “manipulation.”

It is the inference:

> **I understand what would help you; therefore I am entitled to author the conditions that will make you become it.**

Year 2 supplies repeated negative evidence against that inference.

## 9.3 Hidden authorship and accountability deficit

Ayanokōji often retains the advantages of invisible authorship:

- others perform the visible act;
- he controls only one part of the information chain;
- the full purpose remains private;
- and later observers cannot easily reconstruct the degree of causal design.

This creates an accountability deficit even when the outcome is good.

## 9.4 Information asymmetry as power technology

His most ethically serious relational secrets are not merely private biography.

The problem arises when withheld information is **material to another person's decision about their own future or relationship**.

This is why `Y1H-004` remains `STILL_OPEN`.

Kei and Horikita provide strong negative evidence. Ichinose receives selected truths at a strategically chosen moment rather than as a basis for prior informed choice.

## 9.5 The stopping-rule capacity is real

V11 proves that Ayanokōji can identify a point where his role should stop.

This matters because it means coercive authorship is not his only available repertoire.

The ethical question is therefore not whether he possesses the concept of restraint.

It is whether he can generalize restraint when the stakes are high and the strategic objective matters deeply to him.

## 9.6 Desire for unmodeled answers

His desire to see outcomes he cannot fully predict is potentially important moral development.

Yet curiosity about model-breaking answers can itself become another experimental motive.

The positive transition would require more than enjoying surprise.

It would require accepting another person's counter-authored answer even when it defeats a major objective he strongly values.

That condition remains open at Y2SL.

## 9.7 Vulnerability remains asymmetrical

Ayanokōji increasingly reveals preference, ignorance, ordinary desire, and emotional novelty.

That is not yet the same as making himself meaningfully protectable.

`Y1H-035` remains `STILL_OPEN` because the missing condition is voluntary weakness that grants another person real protective or corrective authority over him.

He often knows enough to protect others.

The corpus has not yet shown him surrendering enough control that another person can protect him against his own preferred course.

# 10. Horikita — public reasons, dirty hands, and corrective leadership

Horikita's ethical development is not a movement from harshness to kindness.

It is a movement from scalar judgment and personal certainty toward more public, contextual, bounded, and corrigible authority.

## 10.1 Public accountability is a genuine ethical strength

Horikita increasingly states reasons, accepts visible responsibility, delegates, permits dissent, and allows others to correct her.

Public power can still be wrong.

It is ethically stronger than hidden sovereignty because affected people can identify who decided and contest the reason.

## 10.2 Kushida preservation remains a dirty-hands case

The decision is not ethically purified by later success.

## 10.3 V06 is ethical improvement through authorship transfer

Horikita's strongest corrective act is not apologizing away the original judgment.

It is changing the ongoing relationship so Kushida's own motive can govern continued participation.

Hostility is allowed. Gratitude is not required. Cooperation becomes bounded and self-interested.

## 10.4 Error does not destroy legitimacy

V12's genuine 0–10 defeat matters because ethical leadership cannot depend on omniscience.

A leader who can fail, admit limits, seek expertise, and remain answerable may be normatively stronger than a more capable hidden strategist who cannot be audited.

## 10.5 Corrective leadership versus self-exoneration

A leader who has imposed cost does not discharge responsibility merely by saying the decision was necessary.

Corrective leadership asks whether future practice changes:

- better consent where possible;
- more distributed expertise;
- clearer reasons;
- more space for dissent;
- less willingness to equate current utility with total worth.

Horikita's Year-2 trajectory is strongest where those changes become visible.

## 11.4 Breakup behavior is not informed-consent retroactivity

> **functional independence increased inside an inadequately disclosed relational architecture.**

# 12. Kushida — from imposed rescue to hostile self-authorship

Kushida provides Year 2's clearest demonstration that later appropriation and original legitimacy must be separated.

# 13. Hirata — non-disposability, tragic choice, and the right to name overreach

- hard choices can be necessary;

# 14. Ichinose — solidarity, vulnerability, violation, and counter-authorship

## 14.2 Intimacy increases both care and exploitability

# 15. Sakayanagi — recognition, fiduciary duty, relational veto, and sovereign withdrawal

## 15.3 Love can become political veto without explicit command

## 15.4 Self-authorship can externalize collective cost

Her later withdrawal can be personally self-authored while still imposing class-point loss and a leadership vacuum on the polity she governed.

The ability to choose for oneself does not erase fiduciary responsibilities to others whose interests the office was supposed to represent.

This is why autonomy and political legitimacy must remain separate dimensions.

# 16. Ryūen — voluntary legitimacy without protected dissent

> **voluntary loyalty can legitimate continued leadership without legitimating every available means of maintaining it.**

# 17. Kōenji — autonomy without universal obligation

Kōenji is useful because he exposes an ethical error that collective narratives often make automatically:

> **membership creates claims, therefore the collective owns the member's best capacity.**

Year 2 rejects that inference.

That does not create a permanent claim over every reserve of his ability.

The distinction is:

Kōenji therefore supplies a limit case for class solidarity. A morally serious polity must be able to demand some contribution without treating extraordinary private ability as automatically expropriable.

# 19. Expulsion and disposability — terminal membership loss without personhood erasure

Expulsion is not literal death.

It is nevertheless one of ANHS's most severe irreversible sanctions because it removes the student from the educational polity, destroys the current class trajectory, and can radically alter future opportunity.

Year 2 therefore requires a high threshold for treating expulsion as legitimate.

## 19.1 Expulsion can be defensible in principle

The ethics framework does not prohibit all removal.

Deliberate serious sabotage, persistent coercive harm, or conditions under which no less severe remedy can protect others may justify terminal membership loss.

The required standard should include:

- established conduct;
- relevant notice;
- proportionate response;
- reviewability where possible;
- meaningful attention to alternatives;
- and refusal to treat low current utility as sufficient personhood judgment.

## 19.2 Manufactured disposability is ethically distinct from sanction

The Class Vote and V05 unanimity structures demonstrate how an institution can first create a condition in which someone must become expendable and then ask students to identify the expendable person.

That is not morally equivalent to responding to independently serious misconduct.

The institution authors scarcity and therefore shares responsibility for the cruelty the rule makes rational.

## 19.3 Airi — the canonical Year-2 sacrifice case

Airi's expulsion is not justified merely because she had low current contribution.

Her later flourishing as Shizuku demonstrates precisely why a competitive file is not a complete account of future personhood.

The correct conclusion is not that the class was therefore strategically irrational.

It is that **strategic value and moral entitlement to a future are different objects**.

A class may sometimes be unable to preserve both.

That tragic fact does not authorize pretending they were always the same.

## 19.4 Kushida's later utility does not settle Airi's original claim

One of the strongest temptations in retrospective reading is:

> Kushida later contributed heavily; therefore preserving her and expelling Airi was correct.

This is rejected.

Later contribution improves the consequence side of Horikita's gamble.

It does not prove that Horikita possessed legitimate authority to impose that gamble on every person who bore its risk.

## 19.5 V10 — scarce protection does not rank personhood

Finite protection forces prioritization.

That may reveal which people leaders judge most strategically necessary under the current rule.

It does not establish who is more deserving of belonging in a total moral sense.

## 19.6 Maezono — correctable conduct converted into terminality

V12 intensifies the problem because Ayanokōji later acknowledges a no-expulsion win was not impossible.

Maezono's removal is therefore best understood as a reliability-enhancing chosen route rather than an unavoidable terminal necessity.

The existence of misconduct does not automatically justify the maximum available sanction.

## 19.7 Post-expulsion responsibility

Airi's post-ANHS future and Kijima's V12.5 interest in what happens after expulsion produce an important institutional question:

> **When a school ends membership, does its responsibility for harms produced by its own competitive design also end?**

Year 2 does not provide a complete doctrine.

It establishes that the question cannot be dismissed simply because the student has left the scoreboard.

## 19.8 Canonical expulsion principle

> **A legitimate expulsion regime should respond to serious established conduct through proportionate, reviewable judgment. It should not manufacture a victim merely to teach scarcity, confuse current utility with personhood, or treat departure as the end of institutional moral concern.**

# 20. Violence and force — seven categories that must not be collapsed

COTE contains extensive force, but “violence” is not one ethical category.

The same physical capability can protect, dominate, punish, discipline, intimidate, or respond to institutional danger.

## 20.1 Immediate defensive force

Force used to stop imminent bodily harm has the strongest presumptive justification when proportionate.

Key questions:

- Was serious harm actually imminent?
- Was force directed at stopping rather than humiliating?
- Did it end when the threat ended?
- Did the defender create the danger for developmental or strategic purposes?

## 20.2 Coercive violence

Force used to compel obedience, silence, disclosure, or submission.

Hōsen's political style and several Ryūen interactions fall here.

The ethical wrong is not merely injury. It is the conversion of another person's body into the mechanism by which refusal becomes too costly.

## 20.3 Punitive or retaliatory violence

Force imposed because another person deserves suffering for an earlier act or status challenge.

This requires a separate justification from defense.

Neutralizing a threat does not create unlimited permission to inflict additional pain.

## 20.4 Disciplinary violence

Force used by a leader against members of the leader's own political population in order to maintain hierarchy.

Ryūen/Tokitō is the clearest late-Year2 case.

The presence of voluntary follower loyalty does not legitimize bodily punishment of dissent.

## 20.5 Threatened violence and intimidation

Control can be achieved without actual injury when the threatened consequence is credible.

A target's eventual compliance remains a real act but occurs under constrained refusal capacity.

## 20.6 Institutional exposure to foreseeable harm

An administrator or institution can become responsible for violence without personally delivering every blow if it creates or preserves a setting in which serious harm is a predictable means of achieving the objective.

Tsukishiro/Shiba are crucial because official authority itself can become the mechanism that isolates a target from ordinary safeguards.

## 20.7 Severe nonphysical coercion

The specialist deliberately does **not** call every manipulation “violence.”

That would blur rather than sharpen ethical judgment.

Year 2 nevertheless contains severe nonphysical harms:

- engineered dependency;
- cognitive destabilization;
- reputational manipulation;
- strategic information withholding;
- relational betrayal used as an attack;
- procedural expulsion;
- and authored scarcity.

These can be deeply wrongful without being redescribed as bodily assault.

## 20.8 Self-directed bodily instrumentalization

Ayanokōji's willingness to use his own body as an instrument deserves a separate category.

Self-directed risk is not equivalent to violence against another person because ordinary jurisdiction over one's own body is stronger.

But extreme ease with self-injury can still reveal inherited conditioning and a distorted relationship to personal bodily value.

## 20.9 Violence and competence

The corpus rejects the idea that overwhelming capability itself decides legitimacy.

The stronger actor often has **more** options and therefore a stronger burden to choose the least coercive sufficient method.

Superior force can reduce necessity for cruelty rather than justify it.

# 21. Privacy, truth, secrecy, and informational harm

Year 2 makes information a moral resource and a weapon.

The mature distinction is not “secrecy bad, transparency good.”

Some privacy is necessary for personhood, strategy, safety, and ordinary relation.

The relevant question is:

> **Who needs which information, at what time, to make which decision answerably?**

## 21.1 Material nondisclosure

Ayanokōji's central relational problem is not that Kei, Horikita, or Ichinose lack access to every detail of his biography.

It is that they lack information material to decisions about their own relationships, futures, or political environment.

That is why `Y1H-004` remains open.

## 21.2 Truth can be weaponized by timing

A fact can be true and still be delivered in a way designed to reduce another person's capacity to use it.

V12's Ichinose confrontation is the strongest example.

Information ethics therefore concerns both content and temporal authorship.

## 21.3 Secrets can create jurisdiction before threats are spoken

Kushida's V04.5 concern is structurally important.

Possessing another person's damaging secret creates a latent power relation even if the holder sincerely promises restraint.

The ethical safeguard is not simply “trust the holder.”

It is minimizing monopoly, clarifying purpose, preserving boundaries, and creating conditions under which disclosure cannot casually become coercive leverage.

## 21.4 Provenance is an ethical good

The ability to reconstruct what happened matters morally because accountability depends on it.

A hidden author who controls the record can avoid correction, deny responsibility, or convert an event into a misleading official narrative.

Independent evidence is therefore not merely epistemic infrastructure.

It is part of ethical answerability.

# 22. Institutional ethics — authored scarcity, rules, and responsibility for incentives

ANHS is not a neutral container within which students independently create moral problems.

The school authors environments.

It publishes scores, structures pair markets, creates expulsion branches, rations protection, defines point values, controls mobility, records performance, and converts relational knowledge into formal examination resources.

The ethical question is therefore not only:

> Did the student obey the rule?

It is also:

> **What conduct did the rule author make rational, valuable, or unavoidable?**

## 22.1 Rule compliance is not justice

A procedure can be formally valid while morally deficient.

Conversely, resistance to a captured rule can be justified when official authority has been converted into private purpose.

## 22.2 Secret ballot does not eliminate structural coercion

The V05 unanimity structure preserves individual vote secrecy.

That is a real procedural protection.

But the rule can still make continued dissent expensive enough that autonomy is substantively constrained.

Accurate vote counting is not the same thing as a noncoercive choice environment.

## 22.3 Administrative capture increases the duty of counterforce

Y2V04 and the broader Tsukishiro arc demonstrate why a plural institution needs rival authority centers, independent witness, record provenance, and routes of appeal.

An institution is not ethically legitimate merely because abuse can eventually be defeated by exceptional individuals.

The unresolved test is whether the counterforce becomes durable infrastructure.

## 22.4 Rule authors share responsibility for manufactured scarcity

If an exam requires sacrifice, makes low-rated students easier to discard, or turns protection into a scarce commodity, the institution cannot describe resulting cruelty as pure revelation of student character.

It created part of the incentive environment.

## 22.5 Institutional permission and personal jurisdiction are different

An exam may permit a representative to take an action.

That does not settle whether the representative has sufficient moral standing to choose the most severe option, especially where less destructive alternatives remain possible.

# 23. Measurement, merit, and personhood

`Y2_06` owns the full measurement theory. The ethics specialist needs one controlling conclusion.

A metric becomes morally dangerous when it silently migrates from:

> **observed performance in a defined domain**

into:

> **entitlement to protection, belonging, future opportunity, credibility, or voice.**

V05 supplies the strongest negative form. V09 supplies the corrective form: a previously low rating can become high marginal value after development and under a different rule.

The ethical principle is:

> **A merit model may inform coordination. It must not become a theory of deserved personhood.**

This principle also limits retrospective moral judgment.

Being strategically weak at one moment does not prove that the person was the morally correct sacrifice.

# 24. Class-polity ethics — four constitutions, four characteristic risks

`Y2_05` owns comparative class politics. The ethics layer asks what kind of moral failure each political constitution is most vulnerable to.

Risks:

# 25. White Room and ANHS — developmental sovereignty versus contestable authorship

Volume 0 and `Y2_07` prevent an ethically simplistic comparison.

ANHS is not innocent.

It is coercive, politically situated, highly competitive, and capable of administrative capture, surveillance, patronage, manufactured scarcity, and severe sanctions.

Its moral advantage over the White Room lies elsewhere.

The White Room aims toward **concentrated developmental sovereignty**.

ANHS permits enough plural authorship that students can:

- form unscripted relationships;
- produce surplus purposes;
- create independent evidence;
- contest authority;
- move between affiliations;
- reject imposed identities;
- and surprise the systems modeling them.

The ethical distinction is therefore not:

> harsh institution versus free institution.

It is:

> **proprietary development versus development that remains structurally contestable.**

The White Room's effectiveness intensifies its moral problem because the institution treats causal authorship as title over the developed person.

ANHS remains a genuine alternative only to the extent that plurality, exit, ordinary life, provenance, dissent, and counter-authorship are durable enough to prevent the same inference from taking hold in softer form.

# 26. Ordinary life as ethical counterfield

The ordinary-life ledger owns the topic in full, but its ethical contribution is indispensable.

Low-stakes interaction often supplies cleaner relation because:

- goals are openly personal rather than imposed;
- refusal costs less;
- failure is reversible;
- information need not be monopolized;
- and pleasure does not require justification through institutional utility.

The yogurt-maker sequence, Hiyori outings, casual friendship, shopping, food, and leisure are ethically important not because ordinary consumption is intrinsically virtuous but because they show relations in which one person can say:

> **I want this; you may refuse; neither your refusal nor my desire must become a developmental crisis.**

This is the corpus's most understated alternative to proprietary care.

Ordinary life also matters because it gives people purposes not generated by the competitive institution.

A person with hobbies, friendships, embarrassment, affection, curiosity, and purposeless conversation is harder to reduce to exam value or developmental output.

# 27. Responsibility under authored environments — four layers

Year 2 requires a responsibility model capable of preserving agency while tracking hidden causation.

## 27.1 Act responsibility

Who performed the terminal act?

Examples:

- Maezono lies;
- Sakayanagi chooses to lose;
- Kushida chooses to continue;
- Ichinose proposes reciprocal use.

## 27.2 Choice-environment responsibility

Who shaped the alternatives, incentives, information, dependency, or timing within which the terminal act occurred?

Ayanokōji's V12 responsibility often lies most strongly here.

## 27.3 Rule responsibility

Who authored the institutional structure that made the act valuable, terminal, or coercively expensive?

ANHS bears this layer in V05 and other expulsion/scarcity structures.

## 27.4 Record / narrative responsibility

Who controls what later participants believe happened and why?

This becomes especially important where hidden authorship, administrative power, or selective disclosure can convert an incomplete account into institutional reality.

## 27.5 Why the four-layer model matters

The model prevents two opposite distortions.

A total-puppet account erases morally meaningful agency.

A formal-choice account erases morally meaningful environmental authorship.

The canonical rule is:

> **Responsibility can be distributed without being diluted into nothing.**

Multiple people and institutions can each bear responsibility for different causal layers of the same outcome.

# 28. A positive ethics of development — what the corpus supports instead of nonintervention

The critique of coercive authorship should not end in moral paralysis.

COTE repeatedly shows that refusing to help can abandon people to violence, false judgment, isolation, institutional machinery, or predictable exploitation.

Year 2 supplies a constructive developmental ethic.

## 28.1 See capacity beyond current metrics

Static weakness should not become destiny.

A dated, domain-specific evaluation can inform action without closing the person's future.

## 28.2 Translate rather than humiliate

Teaching is ethically stronger when the instructor changes explanation, structure, or opportunity rather than using shame as the primary engine of growth.

## 28.3 Scaffold without stealing discovery

Provide enough support that the subject can reach an answer while preserving the fact that the answer is theirs.

Sudō's increasing peer-level problem solving is stronger evidence than a hidden strategist delivering the correct solution from above.

## 28.4 State reasons

Public reasons permit challenge, learning, and attribution of responsibility.

A visible leader can be wrong and still create a more answerable political environment than a hidden sovereign who is usually right.

## 28.5 Preserve meaningful refusal

The ability to say no is not inefficiency.

It is evidence that the person remains a participant rather than raw material.

## 28.6 Use the least coercive sufficient method

Emergency force may be necessary.

Developmental pain should not be manufactured merely because it is efficient, dramatic, or reliably produces the desired trait.

## 28.7 Share risk

Decision-makers should not remain perfectly insulated while subjects bear all downside.

The more a planner authors the environment, the stronger the reason that the planner should also accept responsibility for failure, repair, and third-party cost.

## 28.8 Repair what intervention damages

Successful intervention does not cancel the duty to address:

- injury;
- privacy loss;
- damaged trust;
- reputation;
- material loss;
- or reduced future options.

## 28.9 Build plural support

Protection is safer when no one person monopolizes security, belonging, interpretation, and future access.

Horizontal friendship is therefore an autonomy resource, not a distraction from the primary relationship.

## 28.10 Keep authority bounded

Role, contract, emergency, and expertise can justify some decisions.

They do not create unlimited title over the other person's future.

## 28.11 Stop before the answer becomes yours

V11's stopping rule is the strongest positive formulation.

An intervener can recognize a problem, open a route, preserve alternatives, and then accept that the final relation, purpose, or identity belongs to the people living it.

## 28.12 Accept counter-authorship

The most demanding ethical achievement is to permit a developed person to produce a future that genuinely changes the developer's plan.

A person who can only choose among answers the developer would celebrate remains less autonomous than one whose legitimate answer can force the developer to revise.

# 30. Severity matrix — what raises the justification threshold?

The intervention audit should be sensitive to severity rather than mechanically applied.

| Factor | Lower-severity form | Higher-severity form |
|---|---|---|
| goal | assists target-owned aim | replaces target's aim with developer-owned curriculum |
| information | temporary bounded opacity | material deception about target's own future |
| refusal | socially awkward but viable | refusal risks safety, belonging, education, or relationship |
| method | advice / invitation / transparent bargain | manufactured danger / humiliation / cognitive attack |
| bodily stakes | ordinary inconvenience | serious injury or coercive bodily threat |
| reversibility | easy retry or correction | expulsion, destroyed trust, durable trauma, political withdrawal |
| third-party cost | planner bears most cost | uninformed weaker parties bear terminal cost |
| dependency | plural support grows | intervener becomes sole route to safety/status |
| accountability | visible reasons and witnesses | hidden authorship plus record control |
| temporal authorship | truth arrives before choice | truth arrives only after alternatives narrow |
| succession | subject functions independently | subject merely reproduces installed norm |
| corrective standing | advice can be rejected | modeler can absorb every answer as expected output |

The more columns shift toward the right, the less ethical failure the intervention can tolerate.

# 31. Strong counterarguments and their Year-2 answers

The specialist should confront the strongest defenses of coercive strategy rather than treating them as unserious.

## 31.1 “The outcomes justify the methods.”

Ayanokōji's harsh interventions often work. Kushida contributes. Kei becomes more independent. Ichinose becomes stronger. Horikita develops. The class reaches A.

### Response

Outcome is morally relevant.

It is not sufficient.

If success created retrospective permission, accurate powerful actors would acquire a standing license to impose severe costs while failed cases disappeared from the victory narrative.

The correct audit still asks about necessity, alternatives, jurisdiction, information, refusal, risk distribution, and repair.

## 31.2 “Superior knowledge creates paternalistic authority.”

Ayanokōji often sees what the target cannot.

### Response

Superior knowledge strengthens the reason to advise, warn, teach, or intervene in genuine emergency.

It does not create general title over another person's developmental process.

The model can be wrong, the person may value another end, and a valuable future remains the person's rather than the diagnostician's property.

## 31.3 “Teenagers cannot provide perfect informed consent.”

Schools and adults routinely impose developmental structure.

### Response

The framework does not demand impossible adult independence.

It demands proportionality and increasing participation: disclose as much as the stakes allow, preserve as much refusal as circumstances permit, and do not hide a private purpose merely because the subject is younger or less informed.

## 31.4 “Students accepted harsh competition by enrolling.”

### Response

Consent to a demanding institution is not blanket consent to hidden bounties, captured administration, falsified records, manufactured victim requirements, preventable serious injury, or every future rule regardless of notice and proportionality.

## 31.5 “Kushida's later usefulness proves Horikita was right.”

### Response

It strengthens the consequential case for the gamble.

It does not create retroactive consent from Kushida or Airi, erase Haruka's cost, or prove that preserving one person's potential granted unlimited jurisdiction over another person's future.

## 31.6 “Airi succeeded outside school, so no lasting wrong occurred.”

### Response

A good later life is morally important and should be recognized.

It demonstrates that expulsion did not erase her future.

It does not show that the original intervention was rightful.

A person can build meaning after a wrongful loss.

## 31.7 “Sakayanagi freely chose to lose, so Ayanokōji bears no responsibility.”

### Response

Her choice is real and should not be erased.

The designer's responsibility lies in constructing the clue field while knowing that his preference had unusual relational authority for her.

Agency and authored conditions coexist.

## 31.8 “Ichinose became stronger, so the V12 shock was necessary.”

### Response

The completed corpus does not establish necessity.

V11 already demonstrates less-coercive intervention modes. The burden therefore falls on the severe method to show why bounded alternatives were insufficient.

V12.5 recovery cannot supply retroactive permission.

## 31.9 “Kei's forgiveness and love settle the moral question.”

### Response

Her judgment about the relationship must be respected.

Respecting it does not require pretending the coercive origin and later disclosure asymmetry never existed.

Forgiveness is compatible with a real prior wrong.

## 31.10 “Ryūen's followers choose him, therefore his methods are legitimate.”

### Response

Voluntary loyalty is meaningful evidence that his rule offers something beyond fear.

It does not legitimate disciplinary violence or guarantee protected dissent.

## 31.11 “ANHS is better than the White Room, therefore its coercion is justified.”

### Response

Comparative superiority is not complete legitimacy.

ANHS's stronger moral architecture lies in plural authorship, contestability, ordinary life, exit, and counterforce. Those advantages create standards the school itself can still fail.

## 31.12 “Manipulation makes agency unreal.”

### Response

This overcorrects.

Sakayanagi's choice, Maezono's lie, Kushida's continuing compact, and Ichinose's answer remain their acts.

The correct response is layered responsibility, not deletion of agency.

   → Consent must be indexed to information, timing, refusal cost, hidden purpose, and choice-environment authorship.

   → Survival may reproduce the developer's expected answer. Counter-authorship is stronger evidence.

   → Loyalty strengthens sociological legitimacy but does not replace protected dissent and correction.

6. **“Later embrace of an imposed path proves the imposition was right.”**  
   → Later appropriation changes the continuing relation, not the original authority judgment.

7. **“Less command means less control.”**  
   → Stage-setting and relational jurisdiction can preserve formal choice while retaining substantial causal authorship.

8. **“Autonomy requires detachment.”**  
   → Strong autonomy can coexist with love, dependence, friendship, obligation, and institutional membership.

9. **“Expulsion reflects who mattered least.”**  
   → Expulsion outcomes reflect rule design, current utility, coalition, information, leadership, and scarcity; they do not reveal total worth.

10. **“Violence is either justified or unjustified by the target's moral status.”**  
    → Necessity, purpose, proportionality, danger authorship, and post-neutralization restraint must be audited separately.

# 38. Canonical Year-2 synthesis

Year 1 asked whether superior understanding gives a stronger person the right to intervene.

Its answer was already restrictive:

> **No. Accuracy and benefit matter, but legitimate intervention still requires attention to necessity, proportionality, information, refusal, authority, risk, accountability, and the subject's capacity to answer back.**

Year 2 makes the problem harder because the most capable actors learn that direct coercion is often unnecessary.

If a strategist knows another person well enough, the strategist can alter the information they possess, the moment at which truth arrives, the social cost of refusal, the alternatives that seem plausible, the institutional rule that converts choice into consequence, and the relational preference through which the target interprets the situation.

Then the target can choose.

That choice remains real.

So does the design.

This is why the completed Year-2 corpus cannot be adequately described through a binary between manipulation and agency.

The stronger model is **distributed authorship with layered responsibility**.

Airi can sincerely choose to leave while the class and Ayanokōji remain responsible for the environment that makes her sacrifice rational and psychologically difficult to refuse.

Kushida can later choose self-interested cooperation while Horikita's original nonconsensual rescue and Airi's cost remain ethically active.

Sakayanagi can autonomously infer and satisfy Ayanokōji's preference while his deliberate clue architecture remains causally and morally relevant.

Kei can become more independent while the romantic curriculum remains inadequately disclosed.

Ichinose can become stronger while the V12 attack remains wrongful.

These are not contradictions to eliminate.

They are the ethical structure of Year 2.

The year also rejects the idea that autonomy means isolation.

People become more self-authored through relations when those relations provide:

- material information before the choice closes;
- meaningful refusal;
- more than one source of support;
- bounded authority;
- reasons that can be contested;
- risk shared by decision-makers;
- repair after avoidable harm;
- room for disagreement;
- and futures not completely specified by the helper.

That is **answerable interdependence**.

Protection is strongest when the protected person becomes more capable of disagreeing with the protector.

Leadership is strongest when a subordinate can correct the leader without ceasing to belong.

Teaching is strongest when the learner can generate purposes the teacher did not install.

Institutional authority is strongest when rules, records, and administrators can themselves be challenged.

The decisive Year-2 addition is counter-authorship.

A developer can create a competent successor and still remain the hidden author of the successor's norm.

A protector can leave and still govern behavior through an internalized expectation.

A leader can permit formal choice while shaping the field so tightly that one answer becomes the reliable instrument of the leader's hidden purpose.

Counter-authorship asks for more:

> **Can the affected person produce an answer the stronger actor did not supply, make that answer consequential, and require the stronger actor to respond to it as another person's claim rather than absorb it as the successful completion of the original design?**

Ichinose's V12.5 answer is the strongest positive boundary evidence.

Ayanokōji's V11 stopping rule is the strongest positive evidence of self-restraint.

V12 is the strongest negative evidence that he can still choose relational jurisdiction when the strategic objective matters enough.

The completed year therefore does not resolve his ethical arc into improvement or deterioration.

It identifies the exact constitutional problem of his power:

> **Can a person trained to treat superior understanding as permission learn to treat superior understanding as a reason for greater restraint?**

At Y2SL, the answer is partially yes in local practice and decisively not yet as a generalized rule.

The terminal ethical principle through Year 2 is therefore:

> **Power is most legitimate when superior knowledge becomes a reason for greater restraint rather than greater jurisdiction: when the powerful actor can help without manufacturing necessity, disclose before timing becomes coercive, preserve refusal, accept correction, share risk, weaken dependency monopoly, repair avoidable harm, and finally stop at the point where the other person's answer belongs to the other person.**

The terminal warning is its mirror:

> **The most sophisticated domination in Year 2 does not eliminate choice. It learns how to use choice.**

And the terminal autonomy standard is stronger than either independence or survival:

> **The clearest evidence that development has not become ownership is the emergence of a counter-author whose legitimate answer can make the developer change.**
