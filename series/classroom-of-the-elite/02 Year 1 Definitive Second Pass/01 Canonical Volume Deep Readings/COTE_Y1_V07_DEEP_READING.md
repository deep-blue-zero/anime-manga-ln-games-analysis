## Narrative structure

The volume is organized into seven movements:

1. `龍園翔の独白` — Ryūen Kakeru’s monologue: fear, violence, victory, and boredom.
2. `真冬の足音` — ordinary winter life, friendship, and the tightening search for X.
3. `再会と別れの知らせ` — Ayanokōji’s father, the White Room, Chairman Sakayanagi, Chabashira’s lie, and the declared withdrawal from class politics.
4. `非常識` — Kōenji as an actor outside ordinary assumptions of fear, obligation, and usefulness.
5. `決着の刻` — Ryūen’s rooftop experiment and Kei’s confrontation with trauma and betrayal.
6. `交錯する思い` — Ayanokōji’s delayed intervention, institutional insurance, strategic revelation, and procedural violence.
7. `龍園が得るもの、失うもの` — defeat, follower loyalty, the 800-million-point calculation, and the beginning of political reconstruction.

The architecture moves from Ryūen’s theory of fear to three practical tests of it. Kei is terrified yet refuses betrayal. Ayanokōji accepts pain without becoming governable through it. Ryūen finally experiences the fear he defined himself against. The volume therefore does more than reveal Ayanokōji’s physical strength. It stages a comparative philosophy of fear, loyalty, and autonomy.

# 1. Executive thesis

Volume 7 asks who gains authority over another person by controlling that person’s future.

Ayanokōji’s father claims the clearest version:

> `おまえは俺の所有物だ。`

Kiyotaka is property. The owner therefore decides whether the property lives, dies, remains at school, returns to the White Room, or fulfills a political destiny chosen before the child could consent.

Chabashira uses a narrower version. She cannot literally own Ayanokōji, so she makes his continued enrollment appear conditional upon obedience. Her fabricated paternal-expulsion threat turns the school—the place meant to protect his autonomy—into leverage for her unfinished Class A ambition.

Ryūen governs through fear of future retaliation. A person may defeat him today, but tomorrow’s vulnerability remains. He wins by making resistance appear permanently unsafe.

Ayanokōji rejects all three claims when they are directed at him. He refuses paternal ownership, exposes Chabashira’s lie, and closes every route through which Ryūen could turn one physical loss into endless future pressure.

Yet his relationship with Kei reproduces the same structural proposition:

> **The person who can determine another person’s future may convert protection into authority over the protected person’s present.**

Volume 4 gave Ayanokōji leverage over Kei through an assault he orchestrated and evidence he controlled. Volume 7 makes the resulting dependency explicit. He severs contact while predicting that abandonment will intensify her anxiety and loneliness. He knows Ryūen is moving toward her. He receives her emergency message. He waits while cold water is repeatedly carried upstairs because a later intervention better serves his objectives. After the rescue, he calls himself her `宿木 / host`, concludes she can no longer remain at school without him, and treats both possible responses to torture as useful: refusal proves loyalty; betrayal would create guilt leverage.

The rescue is real. So is the authored need for rescue.

That contradiction is the moral center of Volume 7.

The most important act on the rooftop is not Ayanokōji defeating four students. It occurs before he opens the door.

Ryūen gives Kei substantially accurate information. Ayanokōji arranged the earlier Manabe confrontation, allowed the assault, secured evidence, and used her vulnerability to obtain cooperation. Kei concludes that she was entrapped rather than rescued and that the entire operation was another form of bullying. She has reason to believe Ayanokōji has discarded her. Naming him offers the only visible route to immediate relief.

She refuses.

Crucially, the object of belief changes. Kei says what she must believe is neither Ryūen’s account nor Kiyotaka’s presence. The final authority becomes her own choice. She wants at least one act through which she can regard herself as admirable:

> `あたしにだって、最後まで格好つけたいことはある。`

This is not simple obedience to a protector. It is an action that contradicts immediate survival even when the protector may not come.

Ayanokōji authored the test’s conditions. He did not fully author the answer.

The distinction should not be romanticized. Her refusal is shaped by gratitude, stimulation, attachment, pride, and the dependency he created. But the text makes the choice exceed the parasite model. A person who understands herself chiefly as someone who survives by attaching to a stronger host selects dignity over the safest available path.

Ayanokōji later translates this moral act back into strategic language. Kei has become a highly reliable piece. She should remain in his hand whether immediately necessary or not. His interpretation does not exhaust what she did.

Ryūen’s theory fails for the same reason. He believes sufficient fear reveals the lever that controls every person. Kei remains afraid and refuses. Ayanokōji understands fear and makes it strategically unavailable through conditioning, preparation, and emotional compartmentalization. Ryūen finally experiences fear only when every route of recovery—physical, psychological, institutional, and future retaliation—has been closed.

The three fear models are therefore:

- **Kei:** fear remains present, but does not determine the final choice.
- **Ayanokōji:** fear is cognitively known and operationally neutralized.
- **Ryūen:** fear is denied as identity until comprehensive defeat makes denial impossible.

Volume 7 also gives Ayanokōji the clearest positive account yet of why he wants school life. He does not deny the White Room’s efficacy. He credits it with forming his abilities and even much of his personality. His rebellion arises because it cut away the `俗世間 / ordinary social world` and could not teach `自由とは何か、縛られないことの意味 / what freedom is, and what it means not to be bound`.

The volume then makes that ordinary world concrete:

- first snow;
- mystery novels;
- lunch with Hiyori;
- a voluntary friend group;
- shopping, karaoke, and dinner;
- Christmas talk;
- romantic curiosity;
- and people whose company has no assigned educational objective.

These scenes are not relief from the real plot. They are the content of Ayanokōji’s rebellion.

His father sees the school as a hut filled with incompetent rabble. Ayanokōji hopes an apparently incompetent person may eventually stand on the same field as a genius. Class D is therefore not only a strategic object. It is potential evidence against the father’s theory of human development.

But Ayanokōji’s own methods remain deeply White Room-like. He controls environments, exposes subjects to pressure, measures responses, and treats development as information about the designer’s model. He rejects being another person’s experiment while continuing to experiment on others.

Volume 7 completes the first major movement of Year 1 because it brings that contradiction into full view:

> **Ayanokōji has become free enough to reject ownership. He is not yet free of the need to create security through ownership-like control.**

# 2. Position in the Year 1 architecture

The second-pass progression now reads:

> **V1 — authored visibility**  
> **V2 — authored legibility**  
> **V3 — authored environment**  
> **V4 — authored dependency**  
> **V4.5 — counter-curriculum**  
> **V5 — controlled exposure**  
> **V6 — distributed integration under hidden contingency**  
> **V7 — ownership rejected, ownership reproduced**

Volume 6 established that Ayanokōji already knows lower-coercion ways to support development. He can stand back and let Airi choose entry into the group. Horikita can generate a strategic answer he did not design. The class is becoming less dependent upon one invisible author.

Volume 7 then tests whether Ayanokōji can relinquish control when the stakes are personal.

He does not.

He says he will withdraw from the class because Chabashira’s coercive leverage has collapsed. But the withdrawal is not a neutral return to ordinary life. He resolves the Ryūen problem through his most comprehensive act of environmental authorship so far:

- induce Kei’s abandonment anxiety;
- allow Ryūen to commit fully;
- secure witnesses and institutional narrative protection;
- enter only after the loyalty test reaches its decisive point;
- defeat every physical and psychological escape route;
- preserve Kei as a more reliable dependent;
- preserve Ryūen as a potentially more useful rival.

The volume therefore changes the meaning of “withdrawal.” Ayanokōji is not renouncing intervention. He is reclaiming the right to decide when intervention belongs to his own purposes rather than Chabashira’s.

It also introduces a new limit on authorship. Kei’s refusal is not a move Ayanokōji can reduce to the most obvious survival logic. Ryūen’s followers later preserve him through voluntary loyalty that fear alone cannot explain. Hiyori refuses participation in the rooftop operation. Kōenji remains outside every ordinary mechanism of class obligation. The social field is becoming populated by people whose agency cannot be counted simply as compliance with the strongest strategist.

# 3. Ryūen Kakeru — fear as sovereignty

Ryūen’s monologue is not decorative villain psychology. It supplies the political theory tested by the entire volume.

## 3.1 The snake and first victory

As a child, Ryūen sees adults and children panic around a large snake. No one attempts to remove it. He strikes its head with a stone.

The significant emotion is not courage in the heroic sense. He does not want to save classmates or become admired. He is curious why everyone else fears the animal, feels no equivalent fear himself, and experiences pleasure at the moment the opponent submits.

The act becomes his first `明確な勝利 / clear victory`.

Victory is therefore embodied before it is strategic. It is the conversion of another being from threatening autonomy into helpless matter.

## 3.2 `恐怖` and `愉悦`

Ryūen states:

> `『恐怖』と『愉悦』は表裏一体。`

Fear and pleasure are two sides of one structure because another person’s fear is evidence of his power. The more complete the submission, the more complete the pleasure.

He then defines worldly `実力` through violence. This is not merely a claim that fighting skill matters. Violence is the final authority because it can override:

- reputation;
- discussion;
- rules;
- friendship;
- and a person’s declared intention.

His politics grows from the body outward.

## 3.3 Defeat without fear

Ryūen has lost many fights. What distinguishes him is refusal to treat temporary defeat as final.

He thinks only about:

- retaliation;
- reversal;
- and the later moment when the opponent learns that vigilance can never safely end.

This is why one victory over Ryūen normally cannot establish sovereignty. An opponent must believe that Ryūen can return:

- tomorrow;
- while the opponent is alone;
- when friends are absent;
- or when institutional protection fails.

His authority is built on endlessness.

## 3.4 Boredom as the cost of domination

Once everyone around him appears predictable and governable, victory loses intensity. No opponent can overturn his self-conception. The only imagined answer is death.

This boredom links Ryūen to Ayanokōji more closely than either initially understands.

Ayanokōji later reflects that being called the White Room’s greatest masterpiece is also boring. Learning had meaning while higher standards remained. If no one stands above him, the hierarchy closes.

Both boys therefore desire something beyond effortless superiority:

- Ryūen wants an opponent who can make domination uncertain.
- Ayanokōji wants evidence that the ceiling defined by his father is not final.

Their rooftop confrontation is not simply hunter versus hidden prey. It is a meeting between two adolescents whose superiority has made ordinary competition feel empty.

# 4. Ordinary life as the positive object of freedom

The father confrontation defines freedom negatively—freedom from ownership, confinement, and another person’s assigned purpose. The winter chapters define it positively.

## 4.1 Snow

Ayanokōji has apparently never watched ordinary snowfall. His anticipation has a childlike quality precisely because his education was extraordinary.

The White Room gave him knowledge far beyond his age and withheld experiences most children acquire without instruction.

Snow therefore belongs to the same counter-curriculum as:

- ice cream in Volume 4.5;
- movies in Volume 6;
- the Manabu relay in Volume 5;
- and casual friendship-group conversation.

None is difficult. None requires elite ability. All matter because no institution assigned their purpose.

## 4.2 The Ayanokōji Group

The group meets two or three times per week without a fixed obligation. Members can leave without punishment. Airi increasingly joins conversation naturally. She says she has never had so much fun merely talking.

This is the healthiest relationship structure Ayanokōji has encountered so far:

- belonging does not require constant utility;
- there is no host and parasite;
- no one controls expulsion from the friendship through secret leverage;
- and ordinary time is enough reason to remain together.

The relationship remains informationally unequal. The group believes Horikita authored the island victory. They know Kiyopon, not X or the White Room survivor. But the comfort is not false because disclosure is incomplete.

## 4.3 Books and Hiyori

Hiyori reaches Ayanokōji through shared reading rather than crisis, blackmail, or class strategy.

She recognizes Chandler, recommends Dorothy L. Sayers, carries mystery novels in hopes of meeting someone with matching taste, and invites him to lunch. Ayanokōji admits that he likes mystery fiction.

This is one of the first clear cultural preferences the narration establishes for him.

He initially suspects a trap. That suspicion shows how difficult it is for him to interpret unassigned friendliness. Yet he can empathize with Hiyori’s anxiety about using an unfamiliar cafeteria by comparing it to his own first encounter with a coffee machine.

The analogy is modest and important. He does not simply diagnose her. He gives her a piece of himself that makes the unfamiliarity less embarrassing.

## 4.4 Friendship against class logic

The volume repeatedly places ordinary relationships beside the school’s competitive constitution.

Airi dislikes the thought that reaching Class A requires other students to lose. Hiyori wants students from different classes to get along. Ayanokōji enjoys a Sunday of shopping, lunch, and karaoke even while Sakayanagi, Ryūen, Nagumo, and his father convert the school into increasingly dangerous political terrain.

The school teaches that every cross-class relationship may become leverage.

The ordinary scenes test whether people can continue valuing one another beyond that assigned antagonism.

# 5. Ayanokōji and his father — the masterpiece rejects the owner

## 5.1 A relation existing mainly on paper

Neither man performs familial warmth.

Ayanokōji asks whether a parent may unilaterally distort a child’s hope. His father replies by asking whether Kiyotaka ever regarded him as a parent. Kiyotaka says no. He doubts the man ever regarded him as a son.

The relationship is biological and administrative rather than reciprocal.

## 5.2 `所有物`

The father’s central sentence is exact:

> `おまえは俺の所有物だ。`

He does not merely demand filial obedience. He claims property jurisdiction. Ownership supposedly gives him every right, including the right to decide life and death.

This is the clearest statement of the ideology against which Ayanokōji’s freedom must be measured.

The father believes that because he created the developmental system that produced Kiyotaka’s ability, the resulting person belongs to the creator’s project.

The error is not simply cruelty. It is a theory of authorship:

> **Causal authorship over development creates moral ownership over the developed person.**

Ayanokōji rejects that conclusion when applied to himself. Volume 7 asks whether he can recognize the same problem in his own authorship of other people’s development.

## 5.3 Matsuo — evidence, threat, and epistemic caution

The father says he:

- dismissed Matsuo;
- destroyed the man’s reputation;
- prevented the son from continuing education;
- and forced the family into precarity.

He then says Matsuo killed himself by self-immolation.

The previous analysis treated much of this as settled fact. The second pass must preserve the source layer accurately.

The report comes from Ayanokōji’s father. Ayanokōji thinks the earlier punishment is likely real and says the man could produce a death registration if demanded. But the novel does not independently verify the suicide or every detail of the son’s condition inside Volume 7.

The correct formulation is:

> **The father presents a plausible and partially corroborable account of retaliatory destruction; the exact details remain paternal testimony at the local boundary.**

This caution does not weaken the scene’s political meaning. Ayanokōji identifies the purpose correctly. The father is not chiefly seeking guilt. He is demonstrating that anyone who assists Kiyotaka’s autonomy may be punished, including family members who were not directly involved.

The threat converts freedom into outward collateral damage.

## 5.4 Ayanokōji’s reply

He refuses the intended guilt structure.

If Matsuo truly died while knowingly accepting the risk required to place him in the school, leaving would betray the purpose of the sacrifice. Ayanokōji does not accept the father’s attempt to make the victim’s death evidence that the child must submit to the perpetrator.

His response sounds cold because it refuses the emotional performance the father is trying to produce. The moral responsibility remains with the person who imposed the punishment.

# 6. The White Room — effective education and incomplete humanity

Volume 7 does not permit the simplest anti-White-Room reading.

Ayanokōji does not claim that the institution failed to teach him.

He says it may be among the world’s most efficient facilities for developing a human being. It taught:

- academic disciplines;
- martial arts;
- self-defense;
- social survival;
- and more knowledge than most people acquire across a lifetime.

He credits it with forming his abilities and much of his personality.

Its failure is not ineffectiveness. It is totalization.

The White Room decides in advance which dimensions of life are unnecessary and cuts them away. The child receives no authority to determine what knowledge might matter to him.

This produces Ayanokōji’s most explicit positive intellectual rebellion:

> `俗世間`  
> `自由とは何か、縛られないことの意味とは何か`

He wants to learn the ordinary world his father dismissed. He wants to know what freedom is and what it means not to be bound.

The formulation is epistemic as much as emotional. Ayanokōji has learned enough to discover that curiosity is not naturally finite. The White Room’s declaration that some human experiences are useless therefore becomes intellectually intolerable.

## 6.1 Class D as counterevidence

His father calls the school a hut filled with irredeemable people at the bottom.

Ayanokōji responds that the school may provide one answer to whether people are equal. He hopes an apparently incompetent person may eventually stand on the same field as a genius.

This gives his Class D involvement a deeper possible meaning than Chabashira’s coercion.

If:

- Sudō can become disciplined;
- Horikita can become relational;
- Airi can become socially agentic;
- Kei can choose beyond survival;
- and fragmented abilities can become a functioning class,

then human development cannot be reduced to the White Room’s centralized manufacture of excellence.

The class may become evidence that development can arise through:

- relationship;
- ordinary life;
- failure;
- and self-authored desire.

This remains a possibility rather than a fully articulated project at the Volume 7 endpoint. But the source supports it strongly enough to matter.

# 7. Chairman Sakayanagi — protection without ownership

Chairman Sakayanagi provides the volume’s strongest alternative model of protection.

He personally admitted Ayanokōji despite the school’s preselection process. He refuses the father’s demand that the student be removed against his will. Yet he does not claim that Kiyotaka is exempt from rules.

The protection has three features:

1. **It respects expressed choice.** Ayanokōji may remain unless he chooses to leave.
2. **It is institutionally bounded.** Legitimate expulsion grounds still matter.
3. **It does not demand personal submission.** The chairman does not convert refuge into ownership.

This is what protection looks like when it preserves rather than absorbs autonomy.

The contrast with Ayanokōji’s relation to Kei is severe. Ayanokōji’s protection is more personalized and operationally absolute. It also depends upon secrecy, leverage, manufactured need, and his continued position as indispensable host.

The chairman’s model is weaker in immediate force and stronger in legitimacy.

# 8. Chabashira Sae — protection distorted into coercion

Chabashira’s lie is not a minor teacher deception. It reveals how a nominally protective institution can reproduce the logic from which it meant to shelter a student.

## 8.1 Authentic facts supporting a false causal story

Chabashira knows enough about:

- Ayanokōji’s father;
- his abnormal past;
- and the school’s protective role

to make her fabricated threat credible.

This continues the series’ truth/proof architecture. A lie becomes persuasive because it is built around real information.

## 8.2 Attachment through fear of loss

The chairman and Chabashira originally wanted Ayanokōji to become attached to the school so that he would choose to remain.

Chabashira converts that protective objective into a coercive method:

> help my class reach A, or the school you value will be taken from you.

She attempts to manufacture attachment through threatened expulsion.

That method reproduces the father’s structure in miniature. The person controlling access to the future demands obedience in the present.

## 8.3 Her lost authority

Once Ayanokōji recognizes that she is obsessed with Class A and that the current class has a plausible path upward, her threat collapses. A teacher who needs his ability cannot easily destroy him.

He tells her to perform the role the institution should have assigned her:

- guide Horikita and the class;
- teach;
- stop using him as substitute for unresolved adult failure.

This is not merely revenge. It distinguishes education from instrumentalization.

# 9. Withdrawal — freedom from obligation, not the end of intervention

Ayanokōji repeatedly says his participation in class advancement is over.

That declaration is partly true.

Chabashira’s leverage forced him to treat Class A as an assigned objective. Once the threat is exposed, he no longer accepts a duty to solve every examination or preserve every class advantage. Horikita, Hirata, Keisei, Sudō, and the class’s new distributed infrastructure appear capable of continuing without constant correction.

But “withdrawal” contains several different possibilities:

- ceasing to act for Chabashira’s objective;
- ceasing to act for Class D;
- ceasing to act in response to threats;
- ceasing to control people;
- and ceasing to desire challenging opposition.

Volume 7 establishes only the first two with any confidence.

Ayanokōji does not renounce intervention. He reclaims its authorship.

## 9.1 Kei’s severance as a designed event

The phone call begins with an ordinary conversational failure. Ayanokōji tries to sound casual; Kei immediately recognizes that he has no interest in meaningless small talk. Her perception matters because it shows that the relationship already includes knowledge of his habits rather than pure operational contact.

She asks why he hides his ability. He says he never wanted involvement and acted only because circumstances forced him.

He then announces:

- he will stop helping the class;
- he will stop contacting her;
- he has deleted her number;
- and their relationship was always cold.

Kei experiences the announcement as loss, not liberation.

The ethical problem appears in the narration after the call. Ayanokōji predicts that:

- anxiety will grow;
- loneliness will intensify;
- and Ryūen’s pressure in that state may cause psychological collapse.

The severance is therefore not an innocent consequence of withdrawal. It is a knowingly destabilizing intervention inside a conflict he expects to resolve.

## 9.2 Boredom and the masterpiece

During his room reflection, Ayanokōji says he was not dissatisfied with White Room life. He then identifies a different problem.

If he is already the father’s greatest masterpiece, what remains above him?

Learning carried meaning while there was always something beyond his current reach. A closed hierarchy produces boredom.

This clarifies several seemingly unrelated Year 1 choices:

- running fully against Manabu;
- accepting Ryūen’s escalation with slight enjoyment;
- responding to Sakayanagi’s attention;
- and preserving opponents with future developmental value.

Ayanokōji’s ordinary-life desire and his attraction to difficult opposition are not opposites. Both are searches for experience beyond the ceiling imposed by his father.

## 9.3 The remaining thread

He would prefer Ryūen never reach him. Yet the line from Ryūen to Kei remains even after direct contact is severed.

Ayanokōji rejects an indefinite `いつか / someday`. He does not want to remain passively vulnerable to a confrontation whose timing belongs to someone else.

So he controls the timing.

His withdrawal from class obligation becomes the precondition for a personally authored resolution.

# 10. Horikita, Sudō, and a class increasingly able to continue

Horikita does not control Volume 7’s climax. Her development nevertheless forms the background against which Ayanokōji can plausibly withdraw.

## 10.1 Sudō’s restraint becomes durable

Sudō reports provocation rather than retaliating. He obeys Horikita’s prior instruction and trusts that she will determine the next response.

This is more significant than a single moment of self-control at the sports festival. The behavior persists after the public climax that originally motivated him.

Horikita has begun converting:

- attraction;
- recognition;
- study;
- and leadership

into a durable behavioral structure.

## 10.2 Horikita sees beyond her own public role

She correctly recognizes that Ryūen is not satisfied with the explanation that she alone produced Class D’s victories. She worries about Ayanokōji’s exposure.

This is an important change from Volume 3, where she accepted a public role whose full strategic foundation she did not understand. She now sees that her visible leadership and the hidden actor’s authorship are distinct.

## 10.3 Considerate ordinary contact

Her loan of *Farewell, My Lovely* and practical library assistance are easy to overlook beside the rooftop.

They matter because Horikita increasingly knows how to help without turning the interaction into:

- command;
- debt;
- or proof of superiority.

She does not demand emotional disclosure. She identifies a preference and makes it easier for another person to pursue it.

The action is small evidence of a leader learning ordinary consideration.

## 10.4 Ayanokōji’s assessment

When he tells Chabashira that Horikita and the others can continue, the claim is not purely self-serving. Volumes 5–7 supply supporting evidence:

- Sudō is more disciplined;
- Keisei teaches;
- Hirata stabilizes;
- Kei controls a social network;
- Horikita produces independent strategies;
- and the friendship/class infrastructure no longer requires one person to solve every local problem.

The class is not independent of Ayanokōji in an absolute sense. It has become independent enough that his withdrawal is no longer obviously equivalent to immediate collapse.

# 11. Hiyori — quiet agency and ethical refusal

Hiyori occupies two distinct positions in Volume 7.

She creates one of Ayanokōji’s healthiest new ordinary relationships, and she supplies the clearest internal refusal inside Ryūen’s operation.

## 11.1 A friendship founded in taste rather than rescue

Their connection begins with literature.

No one is being blackmailed. No class point depends upon the lunch. Neither person needs the other’s protection. The relationship is possible because both like mystery novels.

This makes it unusually valuable evidence for Ayanokōji’s ordinary self. He can identify a preference and share it without converting the interaction into a problem to solve.

## 11.2 Fear of unfamiliar ordinary space

Hiyori’s hesitation about the cafeteria shows that social difficulty need not be dramatic to be meaningful. Ayanokōji recognizes the pattern through his own unfamiliarity with an ordinary machine.

The moment is reciprocal because his self-disclosure is not offered to obtain leverage. It reduces embarrassment.

## 11.3 Refusal of the rooftop operation

When Ryūen explains that he will target Kei, Hiyori asks whether finding X is truly necessary. She refuses to join.

This is not rebellion against Class C as a whole. She remains willing to help if a real need arises. Her refusal is bounded and ethical:

- she does not accept the hunt as self-justifying;
- she does not allow Ryūen’s authority to define every conflict as necessary;
- and she preserves the right not to become a piece in a cruel operation.

At the Volume 7 boundary, Hiyori does not yet possess the power to stop Ryūen. She does establish that Class C contains judgment independent of him.

# 12. Kōenji Rokusuke — `非常識` as radical autonomy

Kōenji is the volume’s alternative to both Ryūen and Ayanokōji.

Ryūen seeks freedom through sovereignty over others.

Ayanokōji seeks freedom through control of the environment so that no one can force his choices.

Kōenji seeks freedom through refusal to recognize ordinary claims upon him.

## 12.1 No class as moral owner

Kōenji says he solved the zodiac problem because he wanted the meetings to end, not because he cared about Class D’s result. The class benefited accidentally from his desire for personal freedom.

His ability is real and socially unreliable.

He does not accept the institution’s assumption that talent creates a duty to make talent collectively available.

## 12.2 Outside ordinary prediction

Ayanokōji identifies `非常識 / outside ordinary common sense` as a possible strength.

Ryūen’s method depends on finding the fear, desire, reputation, or obligation through which a person can be controlled. Kōenji refuses many of those stakes at the level of premise.

That makes him difficult to govern even when his behavior is selfish.

## 12.3 Beauty, youth, and romance

Kōenji says that he values youth, romance, and beauty. The statement is narcissistic but analytically useful.

Unlike Ayanokōji, he does not require a theory justifying ordinary pleasure. Unlike Ryūen, he does not need conflict to feel alive. His freedom already contains positive preferences.

Its ethical limitation is equally clear: the cost of his refusal is carried by classmates who cannot exempt themselves from the school’s collective consequences.

# 13. Ryūen’s hunt for X — surveillance as theater

Ryūen’s search is more sophisticated than a list of suspects.

## 13.1 The target must know he is being hunted

The visible surveillance campaign is designed to create pressure. X should see:

- classmates followed;
- candidate names tested;
- confrontations expanding;
- and the search approaching the hidden connection.

This is counterintelligence through spectacle.

Ryūen wants X to become afraid of exposure and act in ways that reveal the protected interest.

## 13.2 The Kōenji confrontation as smoke test

The direct confrontation with Kōenji serves multiple purposes:

- test whether Kōenji is X;
- observe how Class D responds to a threatened member;
- identify who follows, speaks, or intervenes;
- and force the hidden actor to understand that public pressure is expanding.

The chosen target is partly a sensor.

## 13.3 Similarity as investigative hypothesis

Ryūen concludes that X thinks like he does.

This is why Kōenji ultimately appears wrong for the role. Kōenji is too self-contained. X has repeatedly manipulated:

- other people;
- class outcomes;
- evidence;
- and rival expectations.

Ryūen is searching for structural resemblance.

The inference is partly narcissistic. It is also why he comes closer to Ayanokōji than other first-year students do.

# 14. The rooftop as institutional experiment

Ryūen does not treat the rooftop only as a place where cameras can be disabled.

He treats the school itself as an object of experiment.

He has tested:

- camera coverage;
- bribery;
- rule enforcement;
- communication boundaries;
- destruction of property;
- and how much misconduct becomes actionable without a complainant.

The assault on Kei extends that program.

## 14.1 Mutual secrecy as privatized law

Ryūen assumes neither side can safely report what occurs.

He risks punishment for:

- assault;
- coercion;
- camera damage;
- and group participation.

Ayanokōji risks exposure of:

- X;
- Kei’s past;
- the Manabe operation;
- and covert class manipulation.

The rooftop becomes a privatized jurisdiction in which official law is suspended by mutual incrimination.

## 14.2 Trauma as a technology of disclosure

Ryūen does not simply ask for information while inflicting pain. He deliberately recreates the structure of earlier abuse:

- group observation;
- cold water;
- physical restraint;
- filming threat;
- bodily exposure;
- and the expectation that no adult will intervene.

He wants the body’s memory to overwhelm the present person’s strategic calculation.

## 14.3 Truth as the decisive weapon

The cold water matters. The most damaging instrument is accurate information.

Ryūen tells Kei that Ayanokōji:

- arranged the earlier isolation;
- used Hirata’s name;
- allowed Manabe’s group to attack;
- obtained evidence;
- and converted the incident into control.

The account is substantially true.

Kei cannot dismiss it as enemy propaganda.

Volume 6 showed Kushida weaponizing confidential truth. Volume 7 shows Ryūen weaponizing causal truth. Factual accuracy does not make the disclosure humane. Its purpose is to destroy the relational meaning through which Kei had survived the earlier operation.

# 15. Kei Karuizawa — the parasite chooses a self

Kei’s rooftop monologue is the volume’s most important character development.

## 15.1 Trauma returns through the body

Her present environment collapses into the past.

Cold, observation, humiliation, and helplessness recreate a world in which no resistance appears capable of changing the outcome. Her narration moves toward the old survival method: accept that the abuse is happening and reduce the continuous shock of hoping it will stop.

The return is not evidence that her school identity was purely fake. It shows that adaptation remains vulnerable when the conditions that produced it are deliberately recreated.

## 15.2 Self-blame and moral complication

Kei also recognizes that her own aggressive treatment of others helped create hostility.

That recognition should be kept proportional.

She can be responsible for:

- arrogance;
- bullying conduct;
- and social harm

without becoming responsible for torture, retraumatization, or coordinated assault.

The text’s self-blame is psychologically credible and morally dangerous if treated as justification.

## 15.3 The rational case for betrayal

Naming Ayanokōji appears to offer:

- immediate relief;
- protection of her public status;
- and the possibility of continuing at school.

Refusal appears to offer nothing.

This is why the choice has moral weight.

## 15.4 The reversal

Her speech initially sounds as though the name is coming. Then the sentence changes:

> `何度聞かれても……絶対に、言わない。`

The decisive point is not that she knows Ayanokōji will rescue her. The narration explicitly strips that certainty away.

She says what she must believe is neither Ryūen’s words nor Kiyotaka’s presence.

The unstated third term is the self choosing.

## 15.5 Dignity rather than purity

Kei does not claim moral sainthood. Her formulation is colloquial:

> she wants to look admirable about something until the end.

The modesty is essential.

She wants one act that is not simply:

- attachment to the strongest protector;
- social performance;
- or avoidance of immediate pain.

She feels pride even while expecting her current school life to disappear.

## 15.6 Beyond the parasite model

The choice does not erase dependency. She still hopes he may come. She remembers that working with him was exciting and slightly fun. She is grateful that he helped her in some form.

But the refusal proves that dependency is not total explanation.

A person may be:

- dependent;
- grateful;
- manipulated;
- angry;
- frightened;
- and still capable of a self-authored moral decision.

Ayanokōji later interprets the result as evidence that she will not betray him under extreme pressure.

The reader has more evidence available than that operational conclusion.

# 16. Ayanokōji’s severance and delay — controlled crisis, controlled rescue

Ayanokōji’s plan should be described precisely.

He does not create Ryūen’s cruelty. Ryūen independently chooses:

- surveillance;
- abduction-like coercion;
- torture;
- and a violent confrontation.

Ayanokōji does create or intensify several conditions that make the crisis more useful to him:

- he previously sent the recording that made the Manabe connection discoverable;
- he knows the search is converging on Kei;
- he severs contact while predicting psychological destabilization;
- he receives the emergency message and does not respond immediately;
- he waits after inferring that cold water is being carried to the rooftop;
- and he enters only after the ordeal has produced maximum information about Kei and Ryūen.

This is not mere failure to rescue quickly.

It is **delayed rescue as diagnostic and relational engineering**.

## 16.1 Compartmentalization

After seeing the message, Ayanokōji continues spending time with the friend group. He accepts the possibility of a future outing with Satō and participates in ordinary conversation.

The scene can look like emotional indifference. It is better understood as extraordinary compartmentalization.

He does value ordinary life. He is also capable of keeping a known crisis outside the visible emotional field until the selected time of action.

The coexistence matters more than either extreme interpretation:

- the ordinary boy is not fake;
- the strategic actor is not erased by ordinary feeling.

## 16.2 Chabashira as witness and object lesson

Ayanokōji brings Chabashira near the scene for several reasons:

- she becomes a time and location witness;
- her presence limits later fabrication;
- he can use the delay to gather institutional information;
- and he demonstrates that any future class involvement belongs to his own decision rather than her command.

The teacher is both insurance and audience.

## 16.3 Manabu as post-conflict authority

Manabu’s placement below the rooftop is particularly elegant environmental control.

He does not need to witness every blow. Every participant leaving must pass him. He can observe:

- who was present;
- visible physical condition;
- timing;
- and the fact that Ayanokōji anticipated institutional aftermath.

Ayanokōji secures not only physical victory but the narrative through which the victory can survive later accusation.

## 16.4 The rule labyrinth

While Kei suffers, Ayanokōji asks about hidden school rules:

- buying examination points;
- concealment of violence;
- expulsion reversal;
- and thousands of codified provisions disclosed only under relevant conditions.

The contrast is ethically brutal and institutionally revealing.

The school’s formal order is not a transparent civic system. It is a conditional labyrinth whose rules become available when the right trigger is activated. Students with superior curiosity, resources, and strategic timing can therefore treat the institution as another information market.

# 17. Arrival — the hidden actor becomes visible

Ayanokōji identifies himself directly to Albert.

He does not preserve the ambiguity Ryūen spent months trying to remove.

That decision is possible because he has already arranged:

- the witnesses;
- the alibi;
- the camera timeline;
- mutual secrecy;
- and the deterrence structure governing the aftermath.

The revelation of identity occurs only after exposure has become containable.

His first words to Kei are:

> `遅くなって悪かったな。`

The apology acknowledges lateness without disclosing that the lateness was selected.

He then invokes the promise to rescue her.

The emotional force of the scene depends upon a truth Kei does not possess at that moment:

> the rescuer intentionally allowed the rescue to become more psychologically absolute.

# 18. Environmental authorship — letting Ryūen believe he chose the battlefield

Ayanokōji’s explanation to Ryūen makes Volume 7 the culmination of the authorship sequence begun in Volume 1.

He sent the recording knowing it would trigger a search for the Manabe betrayal. Ryūen’s temperament made the later steps predictable:

- surveillance;
- candidate testing;
- use of Kei as leverage;
- camera disabling;
- and reliance upon violence.

The key formulation is:

> `手のひらで泳がされているように見せて、こっちが泳がせてた。`

He allowed Ryūen to believe Ayanokōji was being made to swim in Ryūen’s palm, while actually making Ryūen swim.

This is mature environmental authorship:

> **another person’s autonomy becomes the engine of the designer’s plan.**

Ryūen remains intelligent, active, and self-motivated. Ayanokōji does not command him directly. He selects the conditions through which Ryūen’s own motives lead toward the prepared result.

That method is more powerful than simple coercion because the subject experiences his path as self-chosen.

It is also ethically more difficult to recognize and contest.

# 19. The rooftop fight — violence without emotional heat

The fight confirms Ayanokōji’s extraordinary physical ability. Its greater significance lies in how he uses violence.

## 19.1 Calibrated force

Against Ishizaki and Albert, he uses:

- timing;
- balance;
- anatomical targets;
- nerve and throat control;
- and carefully measured impact.

Against Ibuki, he deliberately avoids potentially fatal head trauma.

The violence is not uncontrolled.

That makes it safer in one sense and more disturbing in another. He possesses enough control to select the amount of harm required for each objective.

## 19.2 Ryūen’s courage is real

Ryūen continues after recognizing the gap.

He is not merely a bully whose authority collapses when somebody stronger appears. He has been defeated before and built his rule through repeated returns.

The rooftop therefore validates part of his self-conception:

- he can endure pain;
- he can continue fighting;
- and temporary bodily defeat does not normally govern him.

Ayanokōji must defeat more than the body.

## 19.3 Accepting pain as experiment

Ayanokōji deliberately allows Ryūen to strike him.

He wants to test the proposition that sufficient pain will awaken fear.

This is not required for physical victory. It is an experiment conducted on himself and on Ryūen’s theory.

## 19.4 Ayanokōji’s account of fear

He does not claim simple ignorance of fear.

He has watched people collapse. He knows the misery of defeated persons. At some point, those observations ceased producing fear because he learned that another person’s destruction does not injure him if he can protect himself.

His formulation is close to:

> the self that remains safe is the winner.

This is one of the clearest traces of White Room conditioning in Year 1.

The child learns not that suffering is unreal, but that suffering belongs to another person unless it reaches the self.

## 19.5 Breaking the heart after the fight is already won

Ayanokōji continues striking Ryūen after the physical result is certain.

His goal is to remove every expectation that the result can later be reversed:

- physical resistance fails;
- provocation fails;
- future retaliation is institutionally contained;
- subordinates are incapacitated;
- and no emotion appears available for Ryūen to manipulate.

When Ryūen’s heart takes longer to break than expected, Ayanokōji does not revise the method. He extends it.

The violence is pedagogical in the darkest sense:

> pain is applied until the subject learns the lesson the instructor has selected.

## 19.6 No pleasure, anger, or triumph

Ryūen asks what Ayanokōji feels.

The answer is not excitement or vengeance. The event is too trivial to move him.

This should not be generalized into total emotional absence.

The same volume shows:

- friendship pleasure;
- literary preference;
- curiosity about snow;
- interest in ordinary social life;
- boredom;
- and desire for challenging opposition.

The rooftop establishes a narrower but important point:

> violence and domination do not themselves provide the emotional heat Ryūen expects.

Ayanokōji’s violence is procedural.

Ryūen’s is personal and theatrical.

# 20. Three theories of fear

Volume 7’s conflict can be summarized through three distinct relationships to fear.

## 20.1 Ryūen — sovereignty through denied fear

Ryūen believes the ruler must find and control the fear of others while refusing fear’s authority over himself.

His mistake is not believing fear powerful.

It is believing fear determinative.

## 20.2 Kei — courage as action while afraid

Kei never ceases being terrified.

Her courage consists in making fear’s preferred action—betrayal—nonbinding.

She demonstrates that fear can remain fully present without possessing the final vote.

## 20.3 Ayanokōji — fear made strategically unavailable

Ayanokōji neutralizes fear through:

- preparation;
- conditioned emotional distance;
- superior force;
- and control over aftermath.

This is not necessarily courage in the same sense as Kei’s choice. He makes the feared outcome appear impossible or irrelevant before it can govern him.

## 20.4 Ryūen’s first fear

Ryūen finally experiences fear when he loses not only a fight but every route through which defeat could remain temporary.

The fear marks the collapse of sovereignty.

It may also become the beginning of genuine revision.

# 21. Is Ayanokōji heroic on the rooftop?

Only under a sharply qualified definition.

## 21.1 The strongest case for heroism

He:

- comes personally;
- accepts exposure;
- defeats active tormentors;
- protects Kei’s secret;
- fulfills an explicit promise;
- and ends Ryūen’s immediate capacity to repeat the abuse.

The protection is materially effective.

## 21.2 The strongest case against the heroic reading

He:

- knows Kei is likely to become the target;
- destabilizes her deliberately;
- waits after learning the abuse is in progress;
- uses the ordeal as a loyalty test;
- and allows retraumatization to deepen dependence upon his rescue.

A faster intervention would likely have reduced harm.

## 21.3 Better formulation

Ayanokōji is neither a conventional savior nor simply identical to Ryūen.

He is:

> **the architect of a controlled crisis who ensures that the decisive rescue belongs to him.**

Ryūen wants information and domination.

Ayanokōji wants final resolution, loyalty data, and a more stable dependent asset.

The methods overlap in treating Kei’s suffering as usable.

The objectives and degree of control differ.

# 22. Kei after rescue — protection becoming possession

The aftermath refuses sentimental purification.

## 22.1 “You betrayed me”

Kei says directly that Ayanokōji betrayed her.

He does not deny it.

This preserves the moral truth of the relationship’s origin even at the moment of greatest protective intimacy.

## 22.2 `呪縛`

Ayanokōji says the old curse has ended.

Externally, much has changed:

- Ryūen has been neutralized;
- the Manabe group is contained;
- the secret is less usable;
- and Kei can return to ordinary school life.

Psychologically, the claim is much less complete.

Her trauma remains. Her need for a protector remains. The new protection is stronger because it is privately concentrated in Ayanokōji.

The old curse has been reduced.

It may have been replaced by a more intimate structure of dependency.

## 22.3 `おまえを傷つけたのはオレだ`

Ayanokōji admits that he hurt her.

This is unusually direct.

He does not ask forgiveness and does not renounce the method. Recognition of responsibility does not yet become moral regret.

## 22.4 `宿木`

He calls himself her host.

The language is not reciprocal partnership. It treats safety as a biological dependency:

- she cannot exist at school without him;
- he supplies the environment of survival;
- and her continued value belongs inside his structure.

## 22.5 Either outcome becomes usable

The most ethically severe point is that Kei could not truly fail the test from Ayanokōji’s strategic perspective.

- Refusal makes her a reliable secret-holder.
- Betrayal would create guilt he could later use.

The subject’s response changes the mechanism of control, not whether control remains available.

## 22.6 Yet she still exceeds him

Ayanokōji’s interpretation is evidence, not the final meaning of Kei’s act.

The choice she made belongs partly to:

- dignity;
- gratitude;
- attachment;
- pride;
- and a desire to become someone she can respect.

His asset language cannot erase that agency.

# 23. Ryūen after defeat — what he loses

Ryūen loses more than physical authority.

## 23.1 The right to rule on his own terms

He believes tyrannical conduct remains tolerable only while it produces results.

The rooftop operation:

- uses violence;
- risks subordinates;
- targets a traumatized student;
- and fails comprehensively.

By his own political standard, he no longer possesses the right to continue ruling as before.

## 23.2 Fearlessness

The identity organized around never knowing fear has been disproved.

This does not automatically reform him. It removes the certainty through which the previous self operated.

## 23.3 Interpretive sovereignty

Ryūen believed every person could be understood by locating the correct fear.

Kei and Ayanokōji both violate the model:

- Kei fears and refuses;
- Ayanokōji makes fear unusable.

## 23.4 Purpose

The hunt for X restored excitement to a bored ruler.

After the loss, emptiness returns in more severe form. He initially treats withdrawal as the only honest response.

# 24. What Ryūen gains

## 24.1 A defeat capable of teaching

Previous losses preserved the expectation of later reversal.

Ayanokōji defeats:

- body;
- strategy;
- emotional theory;
- and future retaliation.

The result demands conceptual revision rather than simple recovery.

## 24.2 Voluntary loyalty

Ibuki waits for hours and challenges him with his own doctrine.

Ishizaki and Albert wait at the school, refuse to let him take sole responsibility, and threaten to leave if he leaves.

These actions cannot be explained only by immediate fear.

Ryūen’s coercive system has produced:

- attachment;
- expectation;
- and belief in his capacity to lead them upward.

This does not redeem the violence through which the hierarchy formed. It reveals that its social consequences have become more complicated than his own theory allows.

## 24.3 The 800-million-point calculation

Ibuki finds a three-year calculation sufficient, in principle, to move the entire class to Class A.

Ryūen says it is merely a play memo and claims he intended to save only himself.

The correct local status is unresolved but strongly suggestive:

- the plan is unnecessary for one-person transfer;
- its amount corresponds to the whole class;
- but the author denies collective intention.

It should be treated as evidence against the claim that he sees classmates only as disposable instruments, not as conclusive proof of secret altruism.

## 24.4 A possible succession structure

Ryūen names Hiyori and Kaneda as possible leaders.

The statement shows that he can imagine the class continuing without his visible command. Whether that belief is accurate remains open.

# 25. Who preserves Ryūen?

The answer is distributed.

Ayanokōji tells Ryūen that leaving without understanding defeat would end his growth. He promises not to report the incident and supplies strategic reasons for keeping Ryūen at school.

Ibuki challenges his surrender.

Ishizaki and Albert make withdrawal self-defeating by tying their own futures to his.

A Class D student has already accepted part of the camera-repair cost.

Ryūen infers that Ayanokōji planted the idea of the followers’ threatened withdrawal. The text does not directly confirm that specific authorship.

The prior analysis was too confident in making Ayanokōji the sole designer of Ryūen’s preservation.

The stronger source-grounded formulation is:

> **Ayanokōji creates institutional space for Ryūen to remain and explicitly argues for continued growth; Ryūen’s followers supply the voluntary attachment that makes withdrawal personally and politically costly. The exact source of the follower ultimatum remains unresolved.**

This matters because it gives the followers real agency.

Ryūen is not preserved simply as another piece in Ayanokōji’s game. He is also preserved by people whose loyalty his own theory of fear cannot fully explain.

# 26. Ability and legibility in Volume 7

Volume 7 substantially expands the project’s `実力` taxonomy.

## 26.1 Ryūen’s definition

Ability is violence plus conquest of fear.

This model is embodied and sovereign. It underestimates:

- institutional control;
- moral courage;
- and the strategic value of relationships that are not reducible to fear.

## 26.2 Ayanokōji’s displayed ability

He reveals:

- overwhelming combat skill;
- environmental prediction;
- psychological modeling;
- institutional literacy;
- and narrative control over aftermath.

His highest demonstrated ability is not punching power.

It is the capacity to make:

- the opponent’s choice of battlefield;
- the timing of exposure;
- the availability of witnesses;
- and the meaning of the result

serve one integrated design.

## 26.3 Kei’s moral and generative ability

Kei’s refusal is a form of ability the school does not measure.

It is:

- moral courage;
- identity generation;
- and action not fully contained by the designer’s survival model.

She produces an answer that matters because it is not merely the optimal response predicted by power.

## 26.4 Kōenji’s unusable ability

Kōenji’s capacity remains difficult to convert into collective action because he refuses the class’s moral claim.

He is powerful precisely because ordinary systems cannot easily make him care.

## 26.5 Horikita’s developmental ability

Her leadership is becoming socially usable across time. Sudō’s continued restraint and her ability to identify the X hunt show that development is not confined to examination-day performance.

# 27. Relationship architecture

## 27.1 Ayanokōji–Kei

| Dimension | Volume 7 state |

| Public form | virtually nonexistent |
| Private form | covert protection, information exchange, coercive dependency |

## 27.2 Ayanokōji–Ryūen

Their relation moves from hidden pursuit to recognized adversarial equality of method, not equality of power.

Ryūen is the first first-year student to identify the structural type of person he seeks because he recognizes similarity:

- hidden causation;
- willingness to use people;
- and attention to fear.

Ayanokōji preserves him partly because future opposition may remain useful or interesting.

## 27.3 Ayanokōji–Horikita

Horikita now worries about his exposure and offers ordinary assistance. Ayanokōji trusts her enough to believe the class can continue, but he still withholds the largest conflict and decides the point of withdrawal unilaterally.

## 27.4 Ayanokōji–Hiyori

The relationship is low leverage, cross-class, and founded in shared taste. At this endpoint it is one of the clearest examples of voluntary ordinary contact.

## 27.5 Ryūen–followers

Fear remains part of the structure. The aftermath reveals voluntary loyalty, belief in his strategic promise, and willingness to share institutional risk.

## 27.6 Ayanokōji–father

The relation is explicit conflict over authorship and ownership. Neither side recognizes the other through ordinary family reciprocity.

## 27.7 Ayanokōji–Chabashira

Her authority shifts from apparent coercive control to exposed dependence upon the student she tried to command. The relationship may now become more openly transactional or pedagogical; the local text does not yet settle which.

# 28. Institutional and political development

## 28.1 Admission is curated before examination

Chairman Sakayanagi reveals that candidates are selected in advance and that formal entrance tests are largely camouflage.

The school’s meritocracy therefore begins after a nontransparent act of curation.

The institution decides who is worthy of entering before claiming that later competition reveals worth.

## 28.2 The school as refuge and experiment

The school protects Ayanokōji from direct paternal sovereignty.

It also:

- hides rules;
- permits private-point purchase of exceptional outcomes;
- makes surveillance partial;
- and creates conditions sophisticated students can exploit.

It is neither simple refuge nor simple White Room analogue.

## 28.3 Student-council expansion

Manabu’s request concerning Nagumo moves the political scale beyond the first-year class system.

The hidden strategist is being recruited into a conflict over the school’s governing philosophy.

## 28.4 Class C after Ryūen

The visible ruler steps back. Hiyori, Kaneda, Ishizaki, Ibuki, and Albert become more politically legible.

The class’s future constitution now depends upon whether its members can convert attachment to Ryūen into distributed authority rather than mere waiting for his return.

# 29. Ethics and autonomy

Volume 7 is the most demanding Year 1 case so far for separating effectiveness from legitimacy.

## 29.1 Father

The father’s position is illegitimate at the level of premise. He denies Kiyotaka’s personhood by treating developmental causation as ownership.

## 29.2 Chairman Sakayanagi

The chairman’s protection is legitimate precisely because it preserves the student’s choice and remains bounded by general rules.

## 29.3 Chabashira

Her original protective task becomes illegitimate when she fabricates an expulsion threat and makes continued safety conditional upon serving her private ambition.

## 29.4 Ryūen

Ryūen’s operation is coercive, violent, and deliberately retraumatizing. His curiosity about school boundaries does not convert assault into legitimate experiment.

## 29.5 Ayanokōji

The strongest ethical defense of his conduct is consequential:

- the Ryūen threat is ended decisively;
- Kei’s secret becomes safer;
- Kei discovers a capacity for loyalty beyond immediate survival;
- and future attacks are deterred.

The strongest criticism is equally substantial:

- he destabilizes a trauma survivor;
- knowingly delays intervention;
- uses suffering as diagnostic information;
- and makes the final safety structure more dependent upon himself.

The developmental outcome does not settle whether the method was justified.

## 29.6 Kei’s agency after coercion

A coerced environment does not make every choice inside it meaningless.

Kei’s refusal retains moral agency because:

- betrayal remains available;
- she believes rescue may not come;
- and the choice contradicts the strongest immediate survival incentive.

Yet the choice cannot be used retroactively to consent to the operation that produced it.

## 29.7 Ibuki’s partial boundary

Ibuki refuses active participation in the water torture and objects to Ryūen’s violence.

She nevertheless remains on the rooftop, helps restrain Kei in the visual framing, and prioritizes seeing X.

Her boundary is real and insufficient.

# 31. Japanese narration, voice, and register

## 31.1 Ryūen’s monologue

Ryūen’s voice is unusually declarative and bodily.

Repeated terms—`恐怖`, `愉悦`, `暴力`, `勝利`, `実力`—turn personal memory into political doctrine. His sentences move quickly from one snake to a universal claim about the world.

This is characteristic of his thinking:

> a successful local domination becomes proof of a total law.

## 31.2 Father and proprietary grammar

The father’s `所有物 / property` language is categorical. He does not argue from affection, duty, or even conventional parental authority. The grammar reduces Kiyotaka to an object whose owner possesses every right.

Ayanokōji’s reply is comparatively plain. He refuses the premise rather than performing filial outrage.

## 31.3 `俗世間`

The word carries a mildly dismissive sense of ordinary worldly life. Ayanokōji adopts the father’s hierarchy and reverses its value.

What was cut away as vulgar, mundane, or useless becomes precisely the knowledge he wants.

## 31.4 `自由とは何か、縛られないことの意味`

The formulation does not define freedom as power.

It defines freedom as an object of learning. Ayanokōji does not claim he already understands it. He entered the school because the White Room made understanding impossible.

## 31.5 Kei’s fragmented refusal

> `何度聞かれても……ぜっ『た』いに、言わない`

The broken phonetic rhythm matters. Certainty is not delivered from physical composure. It is constructed through cold, fear, and near-collapse.

The interruption inside `絶対` makes the insistence feel bodily rather than rhetorical.

## 31.6 `最後まで格好つけたい`

Kei does not use elevated moral language. `格好つける` can mean putting on a brave or admirable front.

That modest phrasing prevents the scene from becoming self-sanctification. She wants one act she can consider cool or worthy.

## 31.7 `呪縛`

Ayanokōji calls Kei’s prior condition a binding curse.

The word includes:

- trauma;
- exposure risk;
- Manabe/Ryūen leverage;
- and reliance upon visible protectors.

The irony is that his remedy strengthens another binding relation.

## 31.8 `宿木`

The host metaphor preserves the parasite framework after Kei has acted beyond it.

Ayanokōji’s language lags behind the agency the reader has just witnessed.

## 31.9 `操られるような心`

Ayanokōji says he does not possess a heart Ryūen can manipulate.

The line should remain context-specific. It describes the absence of usable emotional levers during the rooftop violence, not proof that he has no feelings anywhere.

## 31.10 `非常識`

Kōenji is not merely eccentric. He operates outside the ordinary common sense through which institutions and rivals predict obligation, fear, and self-interest.

The word becomes a theory of ungovernability.

## 31.11 `利用価値`

Ayanokōji closes the volume by saying Ryūen still possesses utility.

Even after acknowledging similarity, preserving him, and inviting future growth, Ayanokōji returns to the language of usable value.

The wording does not prove the absence of every personal interest. It shows the interpretive vocabulary through which he makes preservation safe to himself.

# 32. Illustrations and embodied characterization

## 32.1 Cover — two forms of Class C influence

The cover pairs Hiyori and Ryūen.

Hiyori’s quiet composure and Ryūen’s predatory confidence represent two political modes within the same class:

- observation and restraint;
- domination and spectacle.

The image anticipates the volume’s revelation that Ryūen’s class contains judgment not identical to his own.

## 32.2 Kamuro and mutual leverage

The elevator illustration places Kamuro and Ayanokōji in close bodily proximity. The scene can superficially resemble romantic pressure.

The actual relation is reciprocal surveillance. Each holds enough information to make exposure costly.

Visual proximity therefore masks political distance.

## 32.3 The father’s seated authority

The father occupies the chair expansively, with broad shoulders and contemptuous stillness. His body is drawn as institutional mass rather than familial warmth.

The visual hierarchy supports the proprietary language of the scene.

## 32.4 Chabashira’s formal surface

Chabashira’s polished adult presentation preserves the image of teacherly authority at the exact point where her hidden leverage is being exposed as dependent upon Ayanokōji’s incomplete information.

## 32.5 Haruka and Airi in winter

Their paired winter image visually reinforces Airi’s movement into ordinary companionship. The contrast with Kei’s later isolated bodily exposure is severe:

- one girl is incorporated into warmth through voluntary friendship;
- another is stripped of warmth to make dependency legible.

## 32.6 Kōenji’s theatrical self-sufficiency

Kōenji is surrounded by visual sparkle and poses as though the ordinary school environment exists for his self-display.

His autonomy is not hidden. It is performed openly and without apology.

## 32.7 Kei’s restraint and exposure

The illustrations of Ibuki restraining Kei and Kei kneeling wet foreground:

- bodily helplessness;
- fear;
- loss of controlled social presentation;
- and the humiliating gaze of the group.

They prevent the rooftop from being read only as a clever strategic mechanism.

The battered close-up removes the relaxed smile through which he usually governs the scene. Sweat, widened eyes, and physical disarray make the internal collapse visible.

Ayanokōji is illustrated mid-strike with concentration rather than rage. The image supports the prose’s presentation of violence as controlled execution rather than emotional discharge.

# 33. Formal and literary strengths

## 33.1 The prologue and ending form a closed fear experiment

The snake monologue establishes Ryūen’s identity. The final dream returns to the same snake after fear has entered his self-understanding.

The structure makes defeat a revision of origin rather than merely a lost fight.

## 33.2 Ordinary life is structurally necessary

The friend-group, snow, reading, lunch, and shopping scenes establish what Ayanokōji is defending from his father and what he risks losing through exposure.

Without them, “freedom” would remain abstract.

## 33.3 The father and rooftop mirror each other

The father claims Kiyotaka through ownership.

Ayanokōji later describes himself as Kei’s host and determines the conditions of her protection.

The volume does not make them morally identical. It places structural resemblance where the protagonist’s conscious rebellion is strongest.

## 33.4 Kei receives the decisive moral action

The fight reveals power.

Kei’s refusal reveals character.

Placing the refusal before rescue prevents Ayanokōji from owning the entirety of her development.

## 33.5 The institutional aftermath is prepared before the spectacle

Manabu, Chabashira, the camera, the alibi, and mutual disclosure show that the physical victory is only one component of the operation.

The fight succeeds because the political environment has already been won.

## 33.6 Ryūen remains a person after defeat

The final chapter returns to his perspective, followers, shame, ambition, and possible succession.

He is not discarded as a completed villain. The defeat changes the kind of problem he represents.

## 33.7 Multiple protection models are compared without an explicit lecture

The volume presents:

- fatherly ownership;
- chairman rule-bound refuge;
- Chabashira coercive attachment;
- Hirata bounded care;
- Ryūen conditional relief;
- and Ayanokōji absolute but dependency-producing rescue.

The comparison emerges through parallel action rather than external moral commentary.

# 34. Critical reservations and strongest counterarguments

## 34.1 Does the volume romanticize retraumatization?

Yes, potentially.

Kei’s suffering produces:

- courage;
- loyalty;
- relationship intensity;
- and Ayanokōji’s dramatic revelation.

The admirable response should not be mistaken for evidence that the ordeal was necessary for growth.

## 34.2 Is Ayanokōji’s delay strategically necessary?

The text presents it as useful, not as the only available option.

He could likely have intervened earlier and accepted greater uncertainty about Kei’s loyalty or Ryūen’s full commitment. The additional suffering purchases information and relational control.

## 34.3 Does Kei’s refusal prove independence?

Not complete independence.

Her action exceeds immediate survival and the parasite model, but she still hopes for rescue and remains structurally dependent after the event.

The better claim is **a self-authored act inside continuing dependency**.

## 34.4 Is Ayanokōji identical to his father?

No.

The father wants Kiyotaka as property serving an imposed political destiny.

Ayanokōji seeks:

- freedom;
- ordinary knowledge;
- challenge;
- and development of people dismissed as inferior.

The structural resemblance lies in method:

- controlled environments;
- pressure;
- assigned developmental outcomes;
- and causal authorship interpreted as authority.

Difference of purpose does not erase similarity of form.

## 34.5 Does Ayanokōji feel nothing during the rooftop?

He reports no emotional movement from the violence itself.

The broader volume disproves global emotionlessness. A precise reading should say that the combat context offers Ryūen no usable emotional lever.

## 34.6 Is Ryūen’s 800-million-point plan proof of secret class altruism?

No.

It is powerful counterevidence to his claim that he planned only for himself. He explicitly dismisses it as an idle calculation. The collective interpretation remains strong inference, not settled fact.

## 34.7 Did Ayanokōji single-handedly save Ryūen?

The source does not support that total claim.

He creates room for Ryūen to remain and argues against premature withdrawal. Ibuki, Ishizaki, and Albert supply independent loyalty. Ryūen infers that the follower ultimatum originated with Ayanokōji, but the narration does not directly verify the inference.

## 34.8 Is Chairman Sakayanagi’s protection fully noncoercive?

It is the strongest legitimate model in this volume, but still institutionally paternal. He made the exceptional admission decision without Kiyotaka’s prior participation. What distinguishes the later protection is that he respects the student’s stated choice and does not make continued refuge conditional upon personal service.

# 38. Cumulative delta through Volume 7

## 38.1 Ayanokōji delta

Volume 7 provides the clearest local account yet of both his freedom and his failure to generalize it.

He explicitly rejects:

- paternal ownership;
- assigned political destiny;
- and coercive teacher authority.

He positively values:

- ordinary social knowledge;
- literature;
- friendship;
- snow;
- and the possibility that people classified as inferior may develop.

At the same time, he treats Kei’s crisis through White Room-like environmental control. The cumulative Ayanokōji thesis therefore becomes:

> **He wants freedom as self-authorship for himself while still treating authored development as a legitimate way to secure other people.**

His rooftop violence also refines the emotionless-mastermind question. He is not globally without feeling. He is exceptionally insulated from the emotional levers Ryūen expects violence to activate.

## 38.2 Kei delta

Kei’s Volume 4 self-model as parasite is no longer sufficient.

Volume 7 establishes:

- continuing trauma;
- real dependency;
- growing attachment;
- knowledge of Ayanokōji’s betrayal;
- and the capacity to choose dignity against immediate survival.

The cumulative formulation should now be:

> **Kei remains dependent, but dependency no longer exhausts her agency.**

## 38.3 Ryūen delta

Ryūen moves from feared sovereign to defeated actor confronting a crisis of identity and legitimacy.

His followers’ conduct reveals voluntary attachment. His 800-million-point calculation suggests a class-wide ambition he verbally denies. His fear theory is broken but not replaced.

He is no longer simply a first-year antagonist. He has become an unresolved political subject capable of reconstruction.

## 38.4 Horikita and class-leadership delta

Horikita’s absence from the rooftop should not obscure the cumulative gains:

- Sudō’s restraint is durable;
- she recognizes the X hunt;
- she worries about Ayanokōji’s exposure;
- she provides ordinary considerate help;
- and Ayanokōji judges the class capable of continuing.

The class is becoming sufficiently distributed that his withdrawal is plausible, though not yet proven.

## 38.5 Relationship delta

- **Ayanokōji–Kei:** coerced protected collaboration becomes a more emotionally consequential and more deeply dependent structure. Kei adds chosen loyalty; Ayanokōji adds an absolute protection promise and stronger asset control.
- **Ayanokōji–Hiyori:** first substantial cross-class friendship founded primarily in shared taste.
- **Ayanokōji–Ryūen:** hidden pursuit becomes recognized rivalry and developmental preservation.
- **Ayanokōji–Horikita:** strategic asymmetry remains, but ordinary consideration and concern over exposure increase.
- **Ryūen–followers:** fear-based hierarchy reveals voluntary loyalty and collective expectation.

## 38.6 Institution delta

Volume 7 establishes:

- curated preselection before nominal admission;
- Chairman Sakayanagi’s bounded protective authority;
- conditional disclosure of extensive hidden rules;
- student-council succession as a school-wide political problem;
- and the ease with which mutual secrecy can privatize violence outside normal adjudication.

ANHS becomes more clearly both refuge and experimental institution.

## 38.7 Ability/merit delta

The major forms of ability now include:

- Ryūen’s coercive violence;
- Ayanokōji’s integrated environmental/institutional authorship;
- Kei’s moral courage and self-authored refusal;
- Kōenji’s ungovernable autonomy;
- Horikita’s developmental leadership;
- and Hiyori’s quiet ethical judgment.

`実力` is therefore increasingly impossible to compress into one scale.

## 38.8 Ethics/autonomy delta

The project now has a particularly sharp contrast among protection models:

- father — ownership;
- chairman — rules-bound autonomy;
- Chabashira — coercive attachment;
- Hirata — bounded, nonexclusive care;
- Ryūen — conditional relief in exchange for disclosure;
- Ayanokōji — highly effective, secret, dependency-producing protection.

## 38.9 Truth/proof/record delta

The Matsuo report requires source-layer caution. The rooftop demonstrates how true information can be used to destroy trust. Ayanokōji’s witness/alibi structure shows that physical victory remains vulnerable until the official narrative has also been secured.

## 38.10 Ordinary-life delta

`俗世間` becomes the strongest conceptual term yet for the ordinary-life counter-curriculum.

The volume gives Ayanokōji explicit preferences and pleasures:

- mystery fiction;
- friendship-group time;
- unfamiliar seasonal experience;
- and ordinary social outings.

These are not distractions from freedom. They are what freedom is beginning to contain.

## 38.11 Open-question delta

The largest new unresolved questions are:

- whether Ayanokōji can protect without manufacturing need;
- whether Kei’s agency can grow outside host logic;
- whether Ryūen’s follower loyalty changes his rule;
- whether Horikita’s class can function without hidden correction;
- and whether Ayanokōji’s interest in developing “inferior” people can escape his father’s experimental logic.

# 40. Final synthesis

Volume 7 begins with Ryūen killing a snake because he cannot understand why everyone else is afraid.

It ends with Ryūen wondering whether fear would have changed that first choice.

The movement between those moments is the volume’s real victory condition.

Ryūen has spent his life turning temporary defeats into later domination. Pain does not govern him because every loss remains reversible. His opponent may win a fight but cannot sleep safely afterward. Fear belongs to everyone else because Ryūen controls the future continuation of conflict.

Ayanokōji defeats him by taking that future away.

The physical gap is enormous. The deeper defeat is architectural.

Before entering the rooftop, Ayanokōji has already arranged:

- a public alibi;
- Chabashira’s nearby presence;
- Manabu’s authoritative witness position;
- knowledge of the camera timeline;
- mutual-incrimination deterrence;
- and a narrative in which reporting the fight would damage Ryūen’s class more than it would expose Ayanokōji.

Ryūen’s promise of endless retaliation loses credibility because the environment in which retaliation would occur has been made hostile to him.

The rooftop therefore reveals the highest form of ability displayed in Year 1 so far:

> not merely winning inside a rule system, but arranging physical, psychological, social, and institutional conditions so that the opponent’s preferred strategy becomes the mechanism of his own defeat.

Yet the volume refuses to let that ability settle the moral argument.

Ayanokōji’s father claims ownership because he authored the developmental conditions that created Kiyotaka’s ability. Chabashira claims obedience because she appears to control access to the school. Ryūen claims sovereignty because he controls the future possibility of violence.

Ayanokōji defeats all three claims when they threaten him.

He then tells Kei that he is her host.

The structural resemblance is not accidental.

Ayanokōji’s purpose differs from his father’s. He does not want Kei to become an instrument of national ambition. He offers safety, preserves her school life, reduces external blackmail, and recognizes capabilities others overlook. But he also creates the conditions under which his protection becomes psychologically absolute.

The volume’s deepest question is therefore not whether Ayanokōji is better than his father.

He plainly differs in important ways.

It is whether he has recognized the principle that makes the father wrong:

> **No amount of causal authorship over another person’s development creates ownership over the developed person.**

Kei’s rooftop refusal supplies the strongest answer available at this stage.

Ayanokōji creates the test.

Ryūen creates the immediate cruelty.

Neither man controls the final meaning of her response.

Kei learns that Ayanokōji arranged the earlier assault and may have abandoned her. Her rescue is no longer certain. Betrayal offers relief. She is terrified.

She refuses anyway.

The act is not pure independence. She remembers gratitude, excitement, and the possibility that he may still come. But the text explicitly moves the object of belief away from both men. What remains is the decision she can call her own.

That decision breaks the parasite model without erasing dependency.

Kei does not become suddenly healed or self-sufficient. She proves that a person may be dependent without being wholly determined by dependence.

This is a form of development the White Room cannot easily describe. It is not produced by acquiring a higher test score or following the optimal survival path. It is the creation of a self through a choice whose value belongs partly to the chooser.

Ayanokōji sees reliability.

The reader can see self-authorship.

The distance between those interpretations is where his own development remains incomplete.

The father confrontation makes that incompleteness more painful because Ayanokōji gives one of the clearest accounts of freedom in the series.

He does not deny what the White Room gave him. His critique is more sophisticated than simple rejection. The institution taught efficiently because it determined in advance which things a human being required. Its violence lies partly in denying the learner authority over the purpose of learning.

Ayanokōji wants the ordinary world precisely because it was classified as unnecessary.

The volume then shows what that world contains:

- a girl carrying novels in hope of meeting someone who likes them;
- friends who gather without a fixed objective;
- a shy student discovering that ordinary conversation can be fun;
- a first snowfall;
- shopping, lunch, karaoke, and talk of Christmas;
- a rival who values youth and beauty;
- and a boy realizing that he prefers time with people to another class-strategy discussion.

These scenes provide the positive content of his rebellion.

Freedom is not only the absence of a father’s command.

It is the right to discover that mystery novels, snow, friendship, competition, or another person matter before someone else has assigned their value.

This is why Class D may become more than Chabashira’s route to Class A.

Ayanokōji hopes the apparently incompetent can stand on the same field as genius. The statement is an intellectual challenge to the father’s hierarchy and to the White Room’s monopoly on development.

Horikita, Sudō, Airi, Kei, and the class’s distributed infrastructure provide emerging evidence that people can become capable through relationships and self-directed experience rather than only optimized institutional pressure.

But Ayanokōji still responds to uncertainty by building a smaller White Room around the problem.

He:

- controls the environment;
- selects the pressure;
- withholds intervention;
- measures response;
- and retains the person as evidence of successful design.

The rooftop is therefore both his liberation and his repetition.

He frees himself from Chabashira’s coercive objective.

He repeats developmental coercion against Kei.

He destroys Ryūen’s belief that fear governs everyone.

He uses fear and dependency to secure his own structure.

He tells his father that each person should determine his own path.

He determines when other people have suffered enough to learn the lesson he wants.

Volume 7 does not resolve this contradiction because resolution would require a form of relationship Ayanokōji has not yet learned:

> protection that does not need to remain indispensable; education that does not claim the student’s future; intimacy in which the other person possesses enough knowledge and safety to refuse.

The chairman provides an institutional approximation. He protects Ayanokōji’s choice without making the student his private instrument.

Hirata provides a relational approximation. His protection of Kei is limited, but it is designed to become unnecessary.

Hiyori provides a moral approximation. She refuses the rooftop operation without demanding control over everyone else.

The Ayanokōji Group provides an ordinary approximation. Its members can come, leave, talk, and belong without constant proof of usefulness.

Ayanokōji can recognize these models.

He has not yet made them the governing structure of his closest relationship.

Ryūen’s final chapter offers one more challenge to total authorship. Ayanokōji leaves room for him to remain and explicitly argues that departure would end his growth. But Ryūen is also preserved by Ibuki, Ishizaki, and Albert—people who choose him after fear has ceased to function as sufficient explanation.

The followers reveal a political reality the ruler did not understand.

A system built through coercion may generate real attachment. That attachment does not justify the original coercion. It means people exceed the categories through which their leaders understand them.

Kei exceeds “parasite.”

Ryūen’s followers exceed “subordinates governed by fear.”

Horikita exceeds “visible decoy.”

Hiyori exceeds “conflict-averse piece.”

Kōenji exceeds ordinary institutional usefulness.

Ayanokōji’s greatest limitation is increasingly not failure to understand behavior. It is the tendency to translate every meaningful response back into the language of utility, reliability, and developmental design.

The volume ends with him calling Ryūen similar and still useful.

That may be true.

It is not necessarily the whole truth of why he wants the defeated rival to remain.

Volume 7 therefore completes the first major movement of *Classroom of the Elite* not because Ayanokōji finally shows that he is strongest.

The novels had already supplied ample evidence of concealed ability.

It completes the movement because the source now places his central moral contradiction beside his central desire with unusual clarity:

> He wants a life no one else has authored for him.  
> He still feels safest when he authors the lives around him.

The next stage of Year 1 will test whether ordinary attachment can change that asymmetry—or merely make the authored environment more intimate.
