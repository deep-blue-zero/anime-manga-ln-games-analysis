# 1. Executive thesis

Volume 9 is about **who possesses the right to determine what a true fact means about a person**.

Ichinose did shoplift.

The volume never solves the crisis by declaring that allegation false. Nor does it suggest that poverty, family love, or adolescent desperation converts theft into a morally neutral act. Ichinose herself rejects that escape. She repeatedly calls what she did wrong, remembers her mother forcing her to return the item, and accepts that the past cannot be made not to have happened.

What she finally refuses is a different proposition:

> because one bad act is true, every later act of goodness must be fraudulent, and the person who committed it must remain permanently available to be named by that act.

Sakayanagi’s attack works by collapsing **event into identity**. A past theft becomes `犯罪者`—criminal—as a totalizing noun. Once the label is established, it can contaminate everything else:

- perhaps Ichinose’s kindness is hypocrisy;
- perhaps her pooled private points are an elaborate future theft;
- perhaps silence proves guilt;
- perhaps self-defense proves panic;
- perhaps public confession proves the rumor was justified.

Volume 9 therefore develops the series’ old truth/evidence problem into a problem of **authored reputation**. Truth does not speak for itself. Someone still decides:

- which truth is selected;
- when it is disclosed;
- what context is attached;
- what other allegations are placed beside it;
- which interpretation becomes socially dominant;
- and whether the target is allowed to change afterward.

Ayanokōji defeats Sakayanagi’s first-stage information architecture by refusing to restore a clean information environment. He makes it worse. He obtains secrets from Kushida, mixes truth and falsehood across multiple classes, uses Kiriyama as the posting proxy, and deliberately pushes rumor circulation until the school and student council can no longer treat the matter as ordinary gossip. His strategy is not:

> replace lies with truth.

It is:

> **increase the institutional cost of unregulated rumor until regulation becomes rational.**

That maneuver saves Ichinose from continued reputational attack. It also exposes uninvolved students to new humiliation and violates confidences Kushida had been given under expectations of privacy.

At the personal level, Ayanokōji does something equally difficult to classify. He repeatedly visits Ichinose while she isolates herself, sits outside her closed door, offers a form of nonjudgmental listening, and gives her one of the volume’s most humane propositions:

> `すべての人間には許される権利がある`
>
> Every human being has the right to be forgiven.

But he also calls the remaining phase `一之瀬帆波の心を壊す作業`—the work of breaking Ichinose Honami’s heart—and explicitly argues that supportive kindness alone is insufficient; she must be placed under enough pressure to confront what she has buried.

The contradiction is not accidental.

> **Ayanokōji saves Ichinose from someone else’s authorship by temporarily claiming substantial authorship over the conditions of her recovery.**

Volume 9 therefore binds together three of Year 1’s largest themes:

1. **truth does not determine institutional reality by itself;**
2. **development can be real even when its conditions are coercively authored;**
3. **a human being must not be reduced to the worst true statement that can be made about them.**

# 2. Position in the Year 1 architecture

Volume 8 enlarged the series’ political scale. Nagumo demonstrated that the person who can influence the rules need not win inside the announced contest. He could author the environment within which another person’s trust, obligation, and class resources became vulnerable.

Volume 9 moves the same problem from **rules** to **reputation**.

The governing progression is now:

> **V1 — authored visibility**  
> **V2 — authored legibility**  
> **V3 — authored environment**  
> **V4 — authored dependency**  
> **V4.5 — counter-curriculum**  
> **V5 — controlled exposure**  
> **V6 — distributed integration under hidden contingency**  
> **V7 — sovereignty over futurity**  
> **V7.5 — negotiated indispensability**  
> **V8 — institutional authorship**  
> **V9 — authored reputation / information-environment authorship**

The important shift is that the object being controlled is no longer simply what another person can **do**. It is what other people are allowed to **believe that person is**.

This makes Volume 9 a natural culmination of several earlier Year 1 threads:

- Sudō’s truthful account in Volume 2 could not become credible without a usable evidentiary frame;
- Sakura’s truthful testimony was weakened by visible anxiety;
- Ryūen repeatedly arranged real injuries into misleading causal narratives;
- Kushida weaponized true secrets stripped from the confidential relationships that gave them context;
- Volume 8 showed trust itself becoming accumulated political access.

Volume 9 combines all of them. A true wrongdoing, false rumors, real secrecy, strategic leaks, anonymous posting, physical notes, social-network architecture, and institutional liability are folded into one reputational battlefield.

# 3. Narrative architecture

The substantive novel is organized into nine named movements.

## 3.1 `一之瀬帆波の独白`

Ichinose defines herself before the main narration does. The prologue establishes:

- an ordinary successful childhood;
- a mother whose moral expectation was straightforwardness;
- friends and school success;
- a single serious wrongdoing;
- family pain;
- long withdrawal;
- and ANHS as an attempted restart that does not require pretending the past never happened.

The structure matters. The reader is told that there is a real `過ち / error` before learning its exact content. The volume therefore does not stage a mystery in which the central question is whether Ichinose is secretly bad. It stages a problem in which the reader must decide what a concealed bad act should be allowed to mean.

## 3.2 `生徒会長の意向`

Nagumo and Sakayanagi establish the institutional field before the attack becomes public. We learn that:

- Nagumo helped obtain Ichinose’s secret;
- he regards her partly as future personal property;
- Sakayanagi requests freedom to attack her;
- Nagumo authorizes more than a neutral council should;
- and council authority can selectively permit conditions under which harassment and even limited violence become thinkable.

The reputational attack therefore begins inside a prior architecture of power.

## 3.3 `変わっていく関係`

The novel broadens beyond Ichinose:

- Kei ends the public Hirata relationship;
- classmates respond differently to the breakup;
- rumors proliferate;
- Yamauchi becomes the object of Sakayanagi’s staged attention;
- Kanzaki confronts Hashimoto;
- Akito discloses his own past without losing his friends.

This chapter is the first major counterexample to permanent labeling. Akito’s history becomes information *inside* an existing relationship rather than a weapon used to overwrite it.

## 3.4 `変わらないつもり`

Ayanokōji experiences several forms of ordinary relation—Mii-chan’s romantic advice, Hiyori’s friendship, Horikita’s concern for Ichinose—while continuing to process them strategically. Ichinose insists on silence as her chosen defense.

Her position is understandable but unstable: the strategy assumes the attacker will eventually tire before discovering a more concrete way to force interpretation.

## 3.5 `一之瀬の秘密、神室の秘密`

The anonymous attack becomes physical through the note `一之瀬帆波は犯罪者である`.

Kamuro then supplies the mirror case. She also has a shoplifting past. But the meaning of the act differs:

- Ichinose steals once in a family crisis;
- Kamuro steals repeatedly for guilt, thrill, and psychic stimulation.

Sakayanagi coerces Kamuro through secrecy, yet the arrangement also gives Kamuro a stable role and ends the behavior. The novel thus creates a disturbing parallel to Ayanokōji’s own developmental interventions: coercion can produce genuine behavioral change without becoming ethically legitimate because of that success.

## 3.6 `蔓延する噂`

Valentine’s Day places adolescent ordinary life beside growing reputational warfare. Ayanokōji wants chocolate, tutors Kei, and stages part of their meeting for Hashimoto’s benefit. Surveillance and intimacy coexist in the same scene.

## 3.7 `曖昧なもの`

Hashimoto’s first-person perspective reveals an explicit insurance politics. The second rumor campaign then expands across classes. The effects become physically real: anger, confrontation, possible fighting, and escalating institutional risk.

Ichinose finally speaks publicly and reclaims the story of her own wrongdoing.

## 3.8 `すべてのカラクリ`

The novel rewinds again and reveals that much of the apparent rumor escalation was Ayanokōji’s counteroperation. This chapter is indispensable because it prevents a morally flattering retrospective reconstruction. He did not merely stand beside Ichinose until she chose recovery. He:

- bought confidential information;
- coerced a posting proxy;
- manipulated a surveillance observer;
- used Hiyori’s concern as part of the environment;
- and deliberately created pressure on Ichinose.

## 3.9 `復帰`

The final chapter returns agency to Ichinose. She explicitly tells Ayanokōji that he was decisive, but he refuses the role of savior as total author:

> he was a trigger; she had to break with the past herself.

The late Valentine chocolate then closes the volume not with public exoneration but with a voluntary private gesture.

# 4. Rumor as a technology of social authorship

A rumor is not powerful because everyone believes it.

Volume 9 repeatedly shows that very little consensus is required.

If nine people dismiss an allegation and one accepts it, the target’s social world still changes. The target must now decide:

- whether to deny;
- whether to ignore;
- whether to investigate;
- whether to report;
- whether to explain private history;
- whether to burden friends with the defense;
- and whether every future interaction is being filtered through the accusation.

The attack therefore imposes **decision costs** even when belief remains low.

The novel makes this asymmetry especially clear through negative proof. When Yamauchi demands proof that Shinohara did *not* engage in compensated dating, the burden becomes impossible by design. There is no finite evidence set that establishes every event that never occurred.

Hashimoto names the structure `悪魔の証明 / devil’s proof` during the later confrontation. The phrase matters because it describes more than a debate trick. It is the social technology of the campaign:

> the attacker makes a claim cheap to produce and expensive or impossible to eliminate.

The target then supplies energy merely by attempting to answer.

Ichinose’s initial silence is consequently rational. She understands that public denial cannot close an unbounded rumor channel. But silence also has limits once the attacker possesses one true fact and can make the accusation more specific.

The volume’s central epistemic proposition is therefore not:

> people are gullible.

It is:

> **reputation can be damaged even among skeptical people when the accused is forced to live inside a question no available evidence can conclusively close.**

# 5. Ichinose Honami: guilt, wrongdoing, and moral identity

The second pass substantially strengthens Ichinose as one of Year 1’s most philosophically important characters.

Her problem is not simply low self-esteem.

She has constructed a moral identity around a paradox:

> **I did something wrong, therefore I must never become the kind of person who treats wrongdoing lightly; but if I can never be more than the wrongdoer, no later moral action can ever count.**

Her mother’s response is crucial. The mother does not tell Ichinose that the theft is understandable and therefore acceptable. She:

- recognizes the stolen item;
- strikes her;
- removes it from the sister;
- returns to the store despite illness;
- and apologizes.

This gives Ichinose something many characters in the series lack: a moral authority who combines love with real correction.

The family does not cast her out.

The action is condemned.

The child remains loved.

That distinction is exactly what Ichinose has difficulty internalizing afterward. She collapses the moral statement:

> **you did wrong**

into the ontological statement:

> **you are wrong.**

Sakayanagi exploits that collapse. Her most effective move is not exposing the theft itself. It is extending the fact forward into a theory of the present:

> if Ichinose once stole a hair clip, perhaps her current kindness is merely cover for stealing the enormous private-point reserve later.

This is not logically compelled. It is reputational narrative construction.

Ichinose’s recovery is powerful because she does not answer by producing an immaculate counteridentity. She does not declare herself pure. Her decisive language is closer to:

> I did it. I will not deny it. I also refuse to spend the rest of my life performing penance for a punishment that no longer exists.

`これで私の懺悔は終わり`—my penance ends here—therefore means more than emotional catharsis. It is a claim to **temporal personhood**:

> the present self is continuous with the past self, but not exhausted by it.

This becomes the ethical center of the volume.

# 6. Context is not exculpation

Volume 9 handles the shoplifting story better when we resist two symmetrical simplifications.

## Simplification A: “She stole for her sick family, so she did nothing wrong.”

The text rejects this.

Ichinose herself says the act remains wrong regardless of explanation. Her mother’s response reinforces that position. Economic hardship and love for her sister explain why the temptation became acute. They do not magically produce ownership of another person’s goods.

## Simplification B: “Crime is crime, therefore context is morally irrelevant.”

Sakayanagi pushes toward this position strategically. The text rejects it just as strongly.

Context matters for:

- motive;
- recurrence risk;
- character judgment;
- proportionate response;
- possibility of repair;
- and whether the act should define later relationships.

The contrast with Kamuro proves the point. Both incidents fall under the same broad legal category. They are psychologically and morally different.

Kamuro steals repeatedly for stimulation and has normalized the practice.

Ichinose steals once in an acute family context, experiences immediate guilt, is forced into restitution, and spends months in disproportionate self-punishment.

A legal label can classify the act.

It cannot by itself exhaust the person.

# 7. Forgiveness as a right to continue becoming

Ayanokōji’s statement that every human being possesses a right to forgiveness is one of the most striking ethical propositions he has articulated so far.

It should not be read as a formal legal doctrine. Nor does the text establish a universal duty for every victim to reconcile with every offender.

Its force is developmental.

A person cannot become ethically better if social judgment recognizes only two states:

- innocence;
- permanent contamination.

Under such a model, confession becomes irrational. Admitting wrongdoing supplies others with a permanent weapon while offering no pathway back into ordinary moral community.

Ayanokōji’s reflection on concealed wrongdoing explicitly notices this. Many people wear the skin of goodness because openly acknowledging a fault is dangerous. Public righteousness can therefore coexist with private concealment precisely because the social cost of confession is potentially unlimited.

The healthier structure modeled by Ichinose’s mother and later by Class B is:

> wrong act → acknowledgment → consequence / restitution → continued relationship → future conduct.

The action remains in the biography.

It does not become a life sentence against personhood.

This is why Volume 9 belongs not only in the truth/reputation thread but in the series’ recurring **right to continue developing** thread. A person who can never cease being the worst thing they have done has no meaningful developmental future.

# 8. Nagumo: trust as access and possession

Volume 8 already established Nagumo’s theory that trust can be accumulated and spent.

Volume 9 shows the theory operating before the reader had fully understood its reach.

Ichinose entered the school wanting student-council membership. Manabu refused her without explaining the eventual timing. Nagumo then tells a targeted lie: Manabu is classist, attached to A-class pedigree, and unlikely ever to accept a B-class student.

The narration explicitly corrects him. Manabu’s actual judgment is much closer to the opposite—he cares about underlying human qualities.

Nagumo’s lie is effective because it is **psychologically plausible from Ichinose’s position**. She has already received an inexplicable B placement and a rejection from the institution she wants to join. Nagumo supplies a theory that makes the rejection legible and then offers himself as the exception.

He already possesses information from Hoshinomiya suggesting that Ichinose’s record contains a concealed cause. He uses that asymmetry to infer a secret and conditions council access on disclosure.

The crucial point is that Ichinose does not confess because Nagumo threatens her directly.

She confesses because she decides that after losing trust before, she must become someone capable of trusting.

Nagumo captures a moral aspiration and converts it into informational access.

That is a particularly corrosive form of power.

His repeated `私物 / personal property` language makes the structure explicit. He does not initially imagine Ichinose only as a talented future council officer. Beauty, access, loyalty, and institutional opportunity are being folded into a proprietary model of leadership.

Volume 9 therefore sharpens Nagumo’s meritocracy:

> **mobility under his system may be real, but mobility increasingly arrives through personal incorporation into the sovereign’s network.**

That is not the same thing as an impersonal meritocratic institution.

# 9. Sakayanagi: experiment, theatricality, and the lure of Ayanokōji

Sakayanagi attacks Ichinose for several reasons that should not be collapsed into one.

At the class-political level, destroying the leader of Class B is rational. Ichinose’s cohesion and private-point network make her a serious long-term obstacle.

At the ideological level, Ichinose’s reputation for goodness is an attractive target. Sakayanagi is interested in what remains when a socially admired identity is forced into contradiction with a hidden past.

At the personal level, however, the later reveal is decisive:

> **Ayanokōji’s intervention is part of the desired outcome.**

Sakayanagi intentionally leaves a thread he can detect. The apparent fresh proof of Kamuro’s shoplifting is staged from the old incident; the beer-can date lets Ayanokōji realize that the scene has been manufactured.

This corrects an easy misreading of the middle chapter. Kamuro does **not** straightforwardly recommit theft in the present simply to prove her story. The physical evidence itself is part of Sakayanagi’s authored path.

Sakayanagi is therefore conducting two experiments at once:

1. Can Ichinose’s moral identity survive exposure?
2. Will Ayanokōji choose to enter the field if given enough reason and enough evidence?

The first person becomes material in the second relationship.

This is an ethical failure even if Sakayanagi correctly anticipates that Ayanokōji may save Ichinose. She is prepared for the possibility that he will not and that Ichinose will break.

Her later willingness to stake expulsion on a direct contest further clarifies the intensity of the rivalry. She does not want generic proof that Ayanokōji is capable. She wants a mutually acknowledged field in which the hidden child she remembers must answer her directly.

# 10. Ayanokōji’s counterattack: rumor saturation

The most important strategic correction in the second pass is that Ayanokōji does **not** defeat rumor with accurate fact-checking.

He builds a larger rumor crisis.

The stages are precise.

## 10.1 Acquire confidential social data

He goes to Kushida because her social network contains information no official class structure can see.

## 10.2 Mix truth and falsehood

Pure fabrication would be too weak. A rumor ecosystem becomes difficult to dismiss when some items contain recognizable interpersonal truth while others are absurd or false.

## 10.3 Spread the attack across classes

Ichinose can be framed as one interpersonal dispute. Multiple first-year classes experiencing anonymous defamatory postings become a school-governance problem.

## 10.4 Increase expected institutional liability

Ayanokōji explicitly thinks beyond immediate embarrassment. If unchecked harassment contributes to withdrawal, expulsion, or suicide, the school’s reputation is endangered.

## 10.5 Force regulatory intervention

Once the cost of neutrality exceeds the cost of action, the institution announces that repeated defamatory rumor-spreading may itself be punishable.

The elegance of the strategy lies in incentive design.

Ayanokōji does not need the administration to care about Ichinose as much as her classmates do.

He needs the administration to care about **its own legitimacy**.

That is classic environmental authorship.

He changes what response is rational for the institution.

# 11. The ethical cost of rumor saturation

Strategic elegance must not obscure what the counterattack actually does.

Ayanokōji obtains secrets that were disclosed to Kushida under expectations of interpersonal confidence. Kushida herself initially objects to the transaction on exactly those grounds. The fact that she later sells the information does not retrospectively create consent from the people whose private lives become material.

The resulting rumors affect students who are not responsible for Sakayanagi’s campaign.

Shinohara must respond to a sexualized allegation.

Hondō becomes the target of a humiliating claim about his preferences.

Satō and Onodera’s actual tension is dragged into public circulation.

The strategy works partly because it is harmful enough to create institutional risk.

That is morally important.

The cleanest description is:

> **Ayanokōji converts other students’ privacy into regulatory pressure.**

This is not equivalent to Sakayanagi’s original attack. His objective is defensive and he is trying to terminate a harmful rumor system rather than destroy a particular student.

But defensive purpose does not erase third-party cost.

The volume therefore provides another case in which Ayanokōji’s intervention is simultaneously:

- intelligent;
- effective;
- developmentally consequential;
- and ethically invasive.

# 12. The “door” intervention: nonjudgmental presence

Ayanokōji’s repeated visits to Ichinose are among the most humane scenes in his Year 1 characterization if read at the level of *what he provides*.

He does not begin with a lecture.

He does not demand that she open the door.

He does not tell her that her classmates are disappointed.

He sits outside.

Eventually he says that he is waiting for her to `吐き出す`—to let out what she is carrying.

His metaphor is especially important:

> `オレは今扉だ`
>
> I am a door right now.

A door has:

- no judging face;
- no immediate touch;
- no demand for reciprocal emotional performance.

For someone ashamed of being seen, this lowers the cost of confession.

Ayanokōji’s insight is psychologically precise. Ichinose can rescue other people but cannot rescue herself because her social identity makes her feel responsible for remaining the reliable, good, nonburdensome person.

By becoming almost less than a conventional interlocutor, he gives her permission to speak without immediately managing his feelings too.

This is not fake care.

The text gives strong reason to treat the listening itself as real and useful.

# 13. The same intervention as coercive pedagogy

The ethical difficulty appears because Ayanokōji is not only a listener.

He is also the person deliberately refusing to let avoidance continue.

His narration names the final operation harshly:

> `一之瀬帆波の心を壊す作業`

He has already decided that Class B’s supportive kindness is **correct for support but wrong for correction**. In his view, they are cushioning Ichinose well enough that she may continue living around the wound rather than through it.

He therefore supplies pressure.

This is recognizably White Room-shaped reasoning:

> diagnose developmental blockage → create sufficient stress → produce adaptation.

The difference from the White Room is substantial but incomplete.

He is not trying to break Ichinose into obedience.

He wants her to reclaim agency, stop self-punishment, and become capable of living in relationship again.

He offers forgiveness rather than performance criteria.

He listens rather than prescribing the content of the confession.

But he still privately decides:

- that confrontation must happen now;
- that current support is insufficient;
- that greater pressure is justified;
- and that he is entitled to continue appearing until the desired threshold is reached.

The result is therefore best classified as **autonomy-directed paternalism**.

The desired endpoint is more autonomous than the starting point.

The route is not fully autonomous.

# 14. White Room memory becomes empathy rather than only technique

A crucial line in the intervention connects Ichinose to Ayanokōji’s childhood without making their experiences equivalent.

He remembers children in the White Room who had nobody to whom they could disclose weakness and who collapsed under their own unresolved pressure.

This is significant because White Room memory often appears in his strategic competence as:

- training;
- bodily conditioning;
- learning;
- competition;
- or adversarial adaptation.

Here it becomes **relational knowledge of isolation**.

He understands the danger of a person who can perform competence publicly while possessing no socially legitimate place to fail.

This is cognitive empathy rooted in autobiographical recognition.

The moral limitation remains the same one identified throughout Year 1:

> he can understand suffering extremely well without reliably concluding that the suffering person should control the intervention.

But Volume 9 also shows that his unusual history can generate more than manipulative accuracy. It can generate a form of solidarity:

> I have seen what happens when nobody can receive the part of a person that is failing.

That is one of the strongest arguments against treating Ayanokōji’s human development as mere performance.

# 15. Kushida: information as distributed social infrastructure

Volume 9 gives the clearest technical account yet of Kushida’s real ability.

It is not merely:

> she knows many secrets.

Her power is architectural.

She understands the difference between a public friendship group and its shadow copies. A group of five may have a hidden group of four that excludes the disliked member and becomes the real site of gossip, complaint, and coalition-building. Multiply that structure across dozens of relationships and Kushida possesses a map of the school that formal class leadership cannot reproduce.

This is **social-network intelligence**.

It includes:

- who is connected;
- who is excluded;
- which friendships are performative;
- what information flows through which subgroups;
- who trusts whom enough to disclose;
- and where resentment accumulates before it becomes public conflict.

In the ability taxonomy, Kushida therefore possesses unusually high:

- socially usable ability;
- political information ability;
- and relational legibility.

Her pathology and her competence share the same origin. She built the network partly to secure admiration, control exposure, and defend the public self.

This is why simply calling her “fake” is analytically weak. The public social labor is real. The surveillance motive is also real.

The same structure can welcome a lonely student and destroy a person who trusted her.

# 16. Ayanokōji and Kushida: contract without trust

Their late-night negotiation is one of the cleanest examples of relationship-by-proof rather than relationship-by-faith.

Kushida records the conversation.

Ayanokōji detects the recording because it changes her speech.

She stops the phone recorder.

Later, she reveals a second device.

Both sides understand the logic perfectly:

> a promise that cannot be trusted must be made usable as evidence.

Ayanokōji then offers an extraordinary price: half of his future private-point income until graduation.

At first glance this appears like altruistic sacrifice for Ichinose. He explicitly corrects that interpretation.

The payment buys two things:

1. the information necessary for the rumor operation;
2. a reason for Kushida not to target him immediately, because his continued enrollment becomes economically valuable to her.

The relationship is therefore designed as **mutual deterrence**.

The most severe revelation comes afterward. Saving Ichinose is not the sole hidden purpose. Ayanokōji wants to measure the quantity and quality of Kushida’s information network so that he can eventually expel her more cleanly.

This should modify any reading that Volume 6’s “leave Kushida to Horikita” meant he had abandoned removal planning in principle. Through Volume 9, he has not acted against Horikita’s stated project, but he is preserving and improving a contingency for elimination.

His version of patience remains armed.

# 17. Hashimoto: insurance politics as a worldview

Hashimoto’s first-person section is one of Volume 9’s most useful ensemble expansions.

He is not best described merely as disloyal.

He treats **exclusive loyalty as concentrated risk**.

His strategy is to maintain multiple relationships across political centers so that the failure of one leader does not destroy his own future.

This explains several behaviors coherently:

- attention to Nagumo;
- curiosity about Ryūen;
- observation of Ayanokōji;
- continued service under Sakayanagi;
- willingness to preserve romantic or social threads he does not emotionally value.

Hashimoto is therefore a political portfolio manager.

That model is ugly in intimate terms because people easily become insurance instruments. But it is rational inside a school where:

- leaders may fail;
- expulsion exists;
- class position determines material outcomes;
- and information about exceptional actors has enormous future value.

Ayanokōji responds not by demanding trust but by feeding Hashimoto a controlled interpretation. The Valentine meeting with Kei becomes a piece of staged causal evidence designed to make Hashimoto feel that he has solved the mystery.

This is a particularly refined form of authored legibility:

> the observer is not blinded; he is given a satisfying explanation that ends his search in the wrong place.

# 18. Kiriyama and coerced complicity

Kiriyama’s role prevents the anonymous-posting operation from becoming an abstract protagonist trick.

Someone has to perform the act.

He initially sees no reason to accept the risk. Anonymous defamation could compromise:

- his standing;
- the council;
- and his own position in the Manabu–Nagumo conflict.

Ayanokōji then constructs a coercive choice.

He offers plausible benefits:

- Ichinose is useful to the student council;
- containing the rumor protects institutional credibility;
- reputational trouble can indirectly burden Nagumo.

But when persuasion is insufficient, he supplies leverage: refusal can itself be narrated as evidence that Kiriyama has already become one of Nagumo’s people.

Kiriyama yields.

This is not free collaboration.

It is not violent coercion either.

It is **reputational coercion**—the threatened authorship of another person’s political identity.

The symmetry with Ichinose’s crisis is deliberate enough to matter analytically. Ayanokōji is fighting a battle over who gets to define a person socially while using the threat of social definition to recruit one of his own tools.

# 19. Hiyori: quiet agency becomes political agency

Hiyori’s development in Volume 9 is easy to underrate because she does not become loud.

She begins from a personal moral fact:

> Ichinose treated even an isolated student like her without distinction.

That memory creates obligation.

Hiyori tells Ayanokōji that she dislikes hurting people but believes there are occasions when defending a friend requires fighting. Later, when Ishizaki and Ibuki are prepared to force a confession from Hashimoto physically, she becomes the person who stops them.

Her reasoning is precise:

> a confession extracted by force does not become proof.

She nevertheless refuses passivity. Her promise that the responsible party will `必ず報いを受けてもらいます`—certainly receive consequences—shows moral restraint without political resignation.

This strengthens the developing Hiyori profile:

- she can recognize harm;
- she can form cross-class attachment;
- she can restrain her own side;
- she can distinguish evidence from vengeance;
- and she can sustain a demand for accountability without needing domination.

Her influence is not sovereign.

That is part of its importance.

# 20. Horikita: strategic realism without ownership of another class

Horikita’s response to Ichinose displays a different sort of development.

She wants to help.

She also understands that Ichinose is a future opponent and that some information cannot be demanded simply because two classes currently cooperate.

Ichinose explicitly invokes this boundary:

> we are friends and temporary allies, but we will eventually fight.

Horikita accepts the refusal.

That is significant. Her early Year 1 self often assumed that if information was relevant to correct decision-making, the rational thing was to obtain it. Here she tolerates another leader’s private sphere even when uncertainty creates risk.

Ayanokōji then gives the cold strategic counterargument:

> if Sakayanagi destroys Ichinose, Class C may benefit.

Horikita does not simply absorb his model. She treats the decision about intervention as her own responsibility.

This is another small but important sign of generative leadership before the later taxonomy formally exists:

> she is becoming capable of receiving Ayanokōji’s analysis as **input**, not command.

# 21. Ordinary Ayanokōji survives inside the information war

Volume 9 is particularly damaging to the “emotionless machine” reading because some of its most revealing lines have no strategic grandeur at all.

He wants Valentine chocolate.

The narration explicitly places this desire before the larger question of wanting a girlfriend. The wish is almost anthropological and adolescent at once:

> this is a normal high-school ritual; I would like to know what it is like to be included in it.

He is also genuinely pleased when Mii-chan reports that Hirata called him especially reliable.

Neither response is operationally necessary.

His conversation with Mii-chan further shows the unevenness of his development. He can give reasonable romantic advice and listen carefully. At the same time, he notices that his mind automatically evaluates information for later usefulness.

The ordinary and strategic layers therefore do not replace one another.

They coexist:

> **the boy who wants chocolate is the same boy who stages a Valentine meeting as counter-surveillance evidence.**

That is a much stronger characterization than either:

- secretly normal teenager pretending to be cold;
- or cold mastermind pretending to be normal.

He is becoming socially real without ceasing to process social reality instrumentally.

# 22. Kei: intimacy inside counter-surveillance

Kei’s Valentine scene is structurally double.

At the ordinary level:

- she brings chocolate;
- hides vulnerability behind teasing cover;
- asks whether Ayanokōji expected the gesture;
- probes whether other girls gave him anything;
- and participates in the sort of ambiguous adolescent exchange Volume 4.5 and 7.5 taught him to value.

At the strategic level, Ayanokōji selected the timing because Hashimoto is watching. He wants the observer to see a relationship and draw a manageable conclusion.

This creates one of the volume’s recurring formal patterns:

> **a genuine event can also be strategically staged.**

Kei’s chocolate is not fake because Ayanokōji uses the meeting.

The meeting is not strategically innocent because the chocolate is genuine.

That distinction is central to *Classroom of the Elite*. Authentic feeling and authored environment are not mutually exclusive.

The ethical question is who knows they are inside both structures.

Ayanokōji does.

Kei only partially does.

# 23. Kamuro as a mirror of coerced development

Kamuro’s backstory is one of the volume’s strongest internal mirrors for Ayanokōji.

Sakayanagi catches a repeated harmful behavior.

She identifies the underlying pattern.

She obtains leverage.

She offers secrecy in exchange for service.

The arrangement gives Kamuro a role.

The harmful behavior stops.

This is precisely the kind of outcome that tempts consequentialist justification:

> if coercion produced improvement, perhaps coercion was good.

The volume does not permit such an easy conclusion.

Kamuro explicitly rejects Sakayanagi’s language of friendship. The relationship began without free consent and remains structured by asymmetrical knowledge.

At the same time, narration recognizes that being needed may have filled the psychological space in which the thrill-seeking theft operated.

Both can be true:

> **the intervention helped Kamuro; Sakayanagi still lacked clean moral entitlement to construct the relationship that way.**

That is almost a laboratory version of the project’s central Ayanokōji problem.

# 24. Class B: solidarity survives factual shame

Sakayanagi’s central assumption is not irrational.

Ichinose’s authority depends heavily on trust. A revelation proving that she concealed serious wrongdoing could plausibly destabilize a class whose political constitution is built on personal confidence.

The decisive counterevidence is Class B itself.

The classmates do not preserve Ichinose because the rumor turns out to be entirely false.

They preserve her **after learning it contains a true core**.

This is much more important.

Their loyalty demonstrates that solidarity can distinguish:

- past action;
- present conduct;
- contextual explanation;
- and continuing membership.

It does not require a leader to be spotless.

Kanzaki’s earlier stance already foreshadows this. He trusts Ichinose despite her refusal to deny the rumors because he has enough relational evidence to judge the person over time.

That is not blind faith in the sense of believing she has never done wrong.

It is **diachronic trust**:

> judging a person across accumulated behavior rather than allowing one discovered fact to erase the rest of the record.

This makes Class B’s constitution stronger than a simple “everyone is nice” description.

# 25. Institutions respond to thresholds, not truth alone

The school does not intervene at the first rumor.

It does not intervene because Ichinose privately suffers.

Even the physical note does not automatically produce decisive action while the target refuses to report.

The response comes when the information environment becomes systemically dangerous:

- multiple classes;
- many rumors;
- repeated anonymous posting;
- confrontation;
- potential violence;
- and plausible reputational liability for the school.

This is consistent with the broader Year 1 institutional model.

ANHS is not an omniscient moral arbiter.

It responds to what becomes:

- legible;
- enforceable;
- costly;
- or institutionally salient.

Ayanokōji wins because he understands the threshold.

This also means the victory is not simply proof that “the school protects students.”

The more precise conclusion is:

> **the school can be induced to protect students when private harm is translated into institutional risk.**

That is a much less reassuring proposition.

# 26. Japanese voice and register

Volume 9 relies heavily on contrast between moral content and speaker register.

## Ichinose

Her language remains warm, apologetic, and relational even under attack. She repeatedly worries about the burden placed on other people. This is not merely “nice-girl role speech.” It is part of the pathology: she finds it easier to protect others from her trouble than to permit them to protect her.

Her recovery is therefore audible in increased declarative force. `これで私の懺悔は終わり！` is striking because it is not an apology. It is a boundary.

## Sakayanagi

Refined politeness continues to package predatory content. The controlled register increases rather than reduces the violence of propositions like permanent criminal identity or expulsion wagers.

## Nagumo

Nagumo’s casual masculine confidence turns institutional authority into personal social dominance. `私物` is especially revealing because he speaks proprietary logic with little need to conceal it.

## Kushida

The recording scene gives a rare technical view of her performance voice. Ayanokōji notices that active recording makes her language unnaturally self-controlled. Once the phone recording stops, her expression and speech loosen—until the second recorder reveals that even “relaxed” interaction still exists inside evidentiary calculation.

## Hashimoto

His internal voice is transactional without being grandiose. He narrates social ties through backup, insurance, and future use, making opportunism sound like practical risk management.

## Ayanokōji

His internal register moves effortlessly between:

- Valentine insecurity;
- dry romantic observation;
- social pleasure;
- legal/institutional reasoning;
- coercive planning;
- and philosophical claims about forgiveness.

The coexistence of those registers remains central to reading him correctly.

# 27. Visual and embodied characterization

The illustrations reinforce the volume’s movement from imposed visibility toward chosen visibility.

## Ichinose and the note

The note illustration makes reputational authorship physical. A sentence about her identity is something she must literally hold and confront.

## Kamuro

Her seated portrait and flashback imagery keep her from functioning only as Sakayanagi’s information conduit. The body under coercion remains a person with boredom, thrill-seeking behavior, and an independently unwanted history.

## Mii-chan

The blushing café image is formally valuable because it places ordinary adolescent vulnerability inside a novel dominated by surveillance. Romantic embarrassment is not narratively trivial here; it is one example of private information that matters precisely because it belongs to someone.

## Kei and Valentine’s Day

The illustration carries genuine social warmth while the prose simultaneously reveals strategic staging. Image and narration therefore produce two valid layers rather than one “true” and one “fake” scene.

## Ichinose’s confession

The public confession illustration recenters the scene on the person speaking rather than on the rumor as abstract information. Her body becomes the source of the narrative rather than the passive surface onto which others write.

## Closing chocolate

The final image completes that inversion. She began the volume forced to hold a statement authored about her. She ends it making a private gesture authored by herself.

That is the volume’s visual arc in miniature:

> **object of disclosure → subject of disclosure → agent of voluntary expression.**

# 28. Ability after Volume 9: information is not a scalar capacity

Volume 9 adds several forms of ability that a simple academic/physical ranking cannot capture.

## Kushida — network intelligence

She maps informal groups, exclusions, secrets, and conversational routes.

## Ichinose — relational legitimacy

Her class accepts damaging truth because trust has been accumulated through daily behavior rather than charisma alone.

## Sakayanagi — narrative attack design

She selects a fact capable of contaminating a whole identity and scripts evidentiary breadcrumbs for a second target.

## Hashimoto — portfolio politics

He distributes loyalty risk across competing centers.

## Hiyori — epistemic restraint

She knows when anger has outrun proof and can stop her own allies without surrendering the demand for accountability.

## Ayanokōji — information-environment authorship

He does not need perfect control of belief. He manipulates the volume, distribution, and institutional cost of information until other actors rationally change behavior.

These capacities strengthen the emerging Year 1 argument:

> **ability is not simply what an individual can do. It includes what social structures the individual can read, build, preserve, redirect, or destabilize.**

# 29. The four class constitutions after Volume 9

## Horikita / Class C — developmental pluralism under strategic uncertainty

The class increasingly possesses distributed academic and social support. Horikita is willing to engage another class’s crisis without claiming entitlement to its secrets. The unresolved threat remains Kushida and Ayanokōji’s private contingency planning around her.

## Ryūen / Class D — authority in partial suspension

Ryūen is not the central actor of this volume, but his class’s confrontation with A-class rumor politics shows that the social body still reacts strongly without requiring him to command every response. Hiyori’s restraint becomes an important non-Ryūen source of authority.

## Ichinose / Class B — solidarity tested by true disclosure

This class receives its strongest ideological test so far. It does not merely protect a falsely accused leader. It decides that a leader with a real past wrongdoing remains worthy of trust and membership.

That is a stronger constitution than innocence-based cohesion.

## Sakayanagi / Class A — aristocratic authorship

Sakayanagi treats people as experimental pieces with different degrees of awareness:

- Kamuro as coerced subordinate;
- Yamauchi as vanity target;
- Ichinose as political and psychological object;
- Ayanokōji as the real desired respondent.

The system is intelligent and centralized. Its moral risk is equally centralized: one sovereign decides whose suffering may become material for another test.

# 31. Ethical evaluation of the major interventions

## Sakayanagi’s attack

**Strategic effectiveness:** high.  
**Subject autonomy:** very low.  
**Third-party harm:** moderate to high.  
**Ethical judgment:** illegitimate reputational violence.

She possesses a true fact and uses it to author a permanent identity, while deliberately accepting the possibility of severe psychological collapse.

## Nagumo’s disclosure architecture

**Strategic effectiveness:** high.  
**Consent quality:** compromised by deception and institutional leverage.  
**Ethical judgment:** proprietary misuse of trust.

Ichinose voluntarily tells him the secret, but he created the decision environment through misinformation and undisclosed prior knowledge.

## Ayanokōji’s rumor saturation

**Strategic effectiveness:** extremely high.  
**Defensive objective:** real and important.  
**Privacy cost:** substantial.  
**Third-party consent:** absent.  
**Ethical judgment:** effective defensive escalation using uninvolved people as means.

The plan terminates a larger harmful system by making institutional nonresponse more expensive. It should not be sanitized as harmless because the final regulatory result is good.

## Ayanokōji’s intervention with Ichinose

**Care:** genuine.  
**Understanding:** unusually high.  
**Subject benefit:** strong.  
**Timing/autonomy:** partially overridden.  
**Ethical judgment:** autonomy-directed paternalism with real humane content.

He offers what she genuinely lacks: a nonjudgmental receiver and a conceptual path out of permanent self-condemnation. He also privately decides that sufficient pressure must be applied until she confronts the past.

## Class B’s response

**Coercion:** low.  
**Recognition:** high.  
**Ethical judgment:** strong model of accountability without exclusion.

The class neither denies the wrongdoing nor converts it into permanent social death.

# 35. Cumulative delta through Volume 9

## Ayanokōji

- His ability to author **information environments** becomes explicit and school-scale.
- He demonstrates genuine autobiographical empathy through memories of isolated White Room children.
- He articulates a universalistic-sounding right to forgiveness.
- He still chooses intervention timing and pressure unilaterally.
- He converts Kushida’s confidential network and Kiriyama’s political vulnerability into tools.
- His ordinary adolescent desires continue to become more concrete.
- His strategic and social selves are increasingly simultaneous rather than alternating masks.

## Ichinose

- Her hidden past is no longer merely inferred; the local text establishes the shoplifting incident and its family context.
- Her political legitimacy survives factual shame.
- She moves from self-protective silence to self-authored disclosure.
- She rejects indefinite penance without denying responsibility.
- Her class demonstrates that solidarity can survive disillusionment.
- Her personal trust in Ayanokōji deepens substantially.

## Sakayanagi

- Her rivalry with Ayanokōji now justifies risking another student’s psychological destruction in order to draw him onto the board.
- She reveals sophisticated authorship of evidence and causal interpretation.
- Her treatment of Kamuro becomes an important coerced-development mirror.

## Nagumo

- Meritocratic institutional reform is further entangled with personal incorporation and proprietary access.
- Trust is not only something he spends politically; he acquires it through deception when useful.
- His council authority can create selective zones of tolerated aggression.

## Kushida

- Her information skill is upgraded from “knows secrets” to a genuine model of hidden social-network intelligence.
- Ayanokōji’s elimination contingency remains active and becomes better informed.
- Their relationship becomes an evidence-backed deterrence contract.

## Hiyori

- Quiet empathy becomes quiet political authority.
- She rejects violence that cannot generate proof while maintaining a demand for consequences.

## Hashimoto

- Opportunism becomes a coherent risk-diversification ideology.
- His surveillance demonstrates how observers can be controlled by giving them a plausible wrong answer.

## Institutional theory

- The school responds to private harm when it becomes institutionally costly enough.
- Rumor is shown as a cheap claim with expensive defense.
- Factual truth and reputational meaning are decisively separated.
- Privacy becomes political capital.

# 37. Final synthesis

Volume 9 begins with a girl remembering one thing she did wrong.

The entire political conflict emerges from everyone else trying to decide what that thing is allowed to mean.

For Ichinose, the theft initially means:

> I failed the moral person my mother wanted me to be.

For Nagumo, it becomes:

> a secret that can purchase trust, access, and future dependence.

For Sakayanagi, it becomes:

> a fault line through which a leader’s public identity can be broken—and a lure capable of bringing Ayanokōji into open contest.

For rumor consumers, it becomes:

> one true core that can make unrelated falsehoods feel newly plausible.

For Class B, after disclosure, it means:

> something our leader did and must own, but not the total explanation of who she is now.

For Ayanokōji, it becomes two different problems.

The first is institutional:

> how do you stop a rumor architecture when direct denial strengthens it?

His answer is ruthless. Saturate the environment. Make the school itself vulnerable to the consequences of continued nonresponse. Use privacy, anonymous posting, network knowledge, and reputational risk as pressure.

The second is human:

> how do you help someone who can save everyone except herself?

His answer is more intimate. Sit outside the door. Do not display a judging face. Wait. Make it possible for the weakness to be spoken.

And then, because he is still Ayanokōji, the humane answer is folded back into developmental authorship. He has already decided that waiting cannot continue indefinitely. He will supply enough pressure to force the crisis toward resolution.

That contradiction is why Volume 9 matters so much.

A lesser reading turns the story into:

> genius boy saves nice girl from evil rumor.

The Japanese text is more difficult.

Ayanokōji recognizes a deeply humane principle:

> **a person must be allowed to exist after wrongdoing.**

He simultaneously acts according to a much less liberal principle:

> **if I understand what will make you stronger, I may be justified in shaping the conditions until you face it.**

Ichinose’s actual victory lies between those propositions.

She is pressured.

She is helped.

She is attacked.

She is defended.

But the decisive public statement becomes hers.

She does not defeat Sakayanagi by proving that the past was imaginary.

She does not defeat guilt by deciding that theft was acceptable.

She defeats the totalizing narrative by saying, in effect:

> **That happened. I am still here. I will not spend the rest of my life allowing that act to write every sentence that follows.**

This is also why Class B’s response matters more than perfect exoneration would have. They know.

And they stay.

Their solidarity therefore ceases to depend on innocence.

It becomes a community capable, at least in this moment, of holding wrongdoing and continuing membership in the same frame.

The closing chocolate gives the volume an appropriately small endpoint.

Ichinose begins holding an object containing someone else’s statement about her identity.

She ends holding out an object of her own choosing.

The public record is not erased.

The private future reopens.

Volume 9’s deepest claim can therefore be stated simply:

> **Truth matters. Context matters. Responsibility matters. But no one true fact—especially the worst true fact—should possess unlimited sovereignty over a human being’s future.**

The unresolved challenge is that Ayanokōji understands this principle most clearly while still reserving for himself extraordinary power to decide how other people will be brought to that future.
