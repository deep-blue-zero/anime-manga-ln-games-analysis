# 1. Executive thesis

Year 1 Volume 1 is not merely the story of students discovering that a generous school was secretly grading their conduct. It is the foundational argument of the series about **who has the authority to define ability and what an institution may do to people after defining them**.

Three systems of judgment emerge.

**The school’s system** says that value is what its concealed criteria can convert into class points, private income, status, and continued enrollment.

**Horikita Suzune’s initial system** says that value lies primarily in demonstrated individual competence, especially academic competence; people who cannot keep pace are burdens whose removal may improve the whole.

**Ayanokōji Kiyotaka’s emerging system** is less settled. His behavior suggests that ability is contextual: physical talent, social influence, observation, pedagogy, information acquisition, self-control, and rule interpretation may all become decisive under different conditions. He begins preserving classmates whom a static ranking would discard—not because he already possesses a humane universal philosophy, but because friendship, curiosity, prior commitment, and a desire to understand “true ability” have begun competing with his preference for detached noninvolvement.

The first month at Advanced Nurturing High School is therefore a contest over **legibility**.

The students believe they possess freedom because visible supervision has disappeared. The school knows that every late arrival, conversation, nap, phone use, and spending choice is already becoming institutional data. Freedom exists, but the consequences governing it are asymmetrically known.

Ayanokōji understands this structure faster than most. He also refuses to let the structure understand him completely. He calibrates test scores, minimizes public credit, delegates visible action to socially credible classmates, and turns the school’s own claim—that points can buy anything—into a transaction that saves Sudō from expulsion.

Yet the second pass sharpens an important qualification to the earlier thesis that he simply “refuses to be measured.” He does more than evade measurement. He **authors what others are allowed to measure**:

- an average-looking academic record;
- a modest swimming time;
- Kushida’s apparent authorship of the past-exam solution;
- Horikita’s visible leadership;
- and a classwide result whose causal structure is known only to a few people.

This is the earliest form of a method that may later become either education or control.

The volume’s most important achievement belongs not to the hidden strategist alone, however. Horikita changes. She begins by asserting that weak students should simply be removed. She ends by risking her own score to lower the failing threshold, apologizing to Sudō after researching the difficulty of his chosen profession, and proposing that a “defective product” may become sound through repair or change.

Her development provides the volume’s strongest provisional answer to its opening question about equality:

> People are not equal in ability, but unequal ability does not prove that any person’s current institutional classification is final—or that weakness in one context exhausts the person’s possible value.

Volume 1 does not yet offer an egalitarian political philosophy. It does establish the conflict that will govern the series:

> **A meritocracy may recognize real differences while still misunderstanding people, manufacturing failure, and confusing what it can measure with what a human being can become.**

# 2. Second-pass findings at a glance

The fresh reread confirms the first analysis’s strongest conclusions, but revises their emphasis.

## Confirmed

- The school’s first “gift” of freedom is a hidden behavioral examination.
- `実力` is already broader than academic ranking.
- Ayanokōji’s Japanese narration is socially anxious, comic, sexually curious, and interested in ordinary adolescence.
- Horikita’s first major arc is the conversion of individual excellence into socially usable ability.
- Kushida’s public kindness is real social labor even though her private hostility is also real.
- The midterm rewards reading the environment around the formal test, not merely mastering the stated curriculum.

## Strengthened

- The volume’s theory of ability is explicitly supported by the author’s afterword: study matters, but study is not everything, and human aptitude is diverse.
- The school’s “fairness” problem is more precise than simple hidden rules. Ayanokōji distinguishes factual inequality from the need for institutions to maintain minimally credible procedure.
- Horikita’s change is not sudden sentimental softening. It proceeds through investigation, pedagogical failure, observation of effort, and deliberate risk to her own score.
- Ayanokōji’s intervention is motivated partly by genuine friendship and commitment, not only strategic curiosity.
- Kushida’s public and private selves are less binary than “mask versus truth”: she explicitly says she dislikes Ayanokōji while also enjoying speaking with him.

## Corrected or qualified

- “The boy who refuses to be measured” is too simple. He does not merely refuse measurement; he controls the visible data and the public attribution of causality.
- “Passivity” is not one undifferentiated moral failure. The bus scene separates nonintervention grounded in utility, autonomy, discomfort with public attention, and genuine indifference. Ayanokōji’s nonintervention requires scene-specific judgment.
- The first victory is not authored by Ayanokōji alone. Horikita’s instruction, Kushida’s network and credibility, the students’ effort, and the private-point contribution all remain causally necessary.

## Newly foregrounded

- The bus scene introduces three political models before the school is entered: Kōenji’s radical autonomy, Kushida’s social mobilization, and Ayanokōji/Horikita’s nonintervention for different reasons.
- Ayanokōji’s decision to withhold the past exam until the final day is the first clear case of shaping another person’s motivation through controlled information.
- Chabashira’s knowledge of his contact with an upperclassman shows that school surveillance includes social transactions and cross-year activity, not cameras alone.
- The final line—wanting to see Horikita smile—places personal curiosity beside strategic involvement from the very beginning.

# 3. Position in the Year 1 architecture

Volume 1 performs five foundational tasks.

## 3.1 It makes the institution itself mysterious

The school is introduced through claims that sound almost utopian:

- guaranteed advancement or employment;
- a self-contained campus;
- private rooms;
- extensive consumer facilities;
- 100,000 points per student;
- and broad freedom to spend and socialize.

The students are not initially told that class conduct governs future point allocation. The novel therefore makes institutional interpretation a survival skill before it introduces any elaborate special examination.

## 3.2 It makes the protagonist part of the mystery

Ayanokōji is not initially framed as an openly dominant genius. The reader instead receives discrepancies:

- exact fifty-point entrance scores;
- a correct solution to unusually advanced mathematics;
- a trained body beneath an average presentation;
- unusual movement against Manabu;
- immediate attention to cameras and institutional wording;
- and repeated self-explanations that reduce sophisticated plans to laziness, bad ideas, or the search for an easy route.

The problem is not merely “how strong is he?” It is how much of his own self-description the reader should trust.

## 3.3 It establishes Class D as a problem of integration

Class D is not a room of uniformly weak students. It contains:

- academic excellence without empathy;
- social leadership without coercive authority;
- physical talent without discipline;
- popularity without transparent loyalty;
- extraordinary capability without cooperation;
- ordinary practical or social strengths that the school’s academic surface does not immediately display;
- and concealed ability that deliberately appears average.

The class’s initial defect is political: these capacities cannot yet become a shared system.

## 3.4 It turns ordinary life into evidence

Friendship, swimming, shopping, sexual banter, meals, game ignorance, and dormitory privacy are not filler. They establish what Ayanokōji actually wants from school and what the White Room-like rhetoric of “ability” threatens to recapture.

## 3.5 It creates the first developmental intervention

The remedial study group becomes a prototype.

Ayanokōji does not simply give answers. He:

- identifies who can recruit;
- leaves teaching to the more suitable people;
- challenges Horikita’s theory of disposability;
- purchases a backup mechanism;
- controls when the backup becomes known;
- and conceals public authorship.

The result enlarges the students’ chance to remain. It also places Ayanokōji in the position of deciding what information they should receive and when.

# 4. Narrative and chapter architecture

The chapter sequence is itself an argument moving from abstract equality toward embodied, institutional judgment.

| Chapter | Narrative function | Core analytical movement |
|---|---|---|
| **日本社会の仕組み** | Philosophical prologue | Equality is disputed before the institution defines merit |
| **ようこそ、夢のような学校生活へ** | Entrance, bus, class formation | Freedom, autonomy, social influence, and hidden risk are introduced |
| **Ｄクラスの皆様方** | Ordinary routines and social attempts | Friendship and institutional authority begin developing together |
| **男性諸君、お待たせしました** | Pool comedy and embodied comparison | Adolescent desire coexists with deliberate performance suppression |
| **友達** | Failed social engineering | Friendship cannot be forced into existence merely because it is strategically useful |
| **終わる日常** | Last days of apparent paradise | The class settles into unregulated habits before consequence arrives |
| **ようこそ、実力至上主義の世界へ** | Zero-point revelation | Private conduct becomes collective liability and class caste |
| **集え赤点組** | First remedial intervention | Pedagogy fails when it begins from contempt and wrong assumptions |

The title sequence is revealing. The “dreamlike school life” does not simply end when the points disappear. What ends is the students’ belief that ordinary conduct exists outside institutional consequence. The “beginning” chapter arrives only after the apparent midterm result, because the true beginning is the first discovery that the system can be interpreted and negotiated rather than merely obeyed.

# 5. The school as experimental institution

## 5.1 Freedom is granted under asymmetric knowledge

Chabashira tells the class that each student has received 100,000 points and may spend them freely. She also says that points can buy anything inside the school and may be transferred. The students interpret the allocation as confirmation that admission itself established their value.

Ayanokōji and Horikita sense that the arrangement is excessively generous. Their suspicion is not paranoia. The school has supplied freedom while withholding the rule connecting class conduct to future income.

The asymmetry matters:

- students know they can act;
- the school knows what those actions will cost;
- students do not know that the cost is collective;
- teachers observe without correcting behavior that will later be punished.

The first month is therefore a behavioral laboratory.

The school is not testing whether students can follow a newly disclosed rule. It is testing whether ordinary standards remain internalized when visible correction disappears.

That has a defensible educational rationale: mature people should not require constant policing to arrive on time or listen in class.

It also has an ethically troubling form. The institution deliberately allows preventable failure to accumulate because the failure produces clearer data.

## 5.2 Collective class points create forced community

Late arrival, private conversation, sleeping, and phone use appear individually chosen. The school converts them into shared economic harm.

The mechanism forces students into collective responsibility before they have voluntarily formed a community.

This creates an important political sequence:

1. individuals act as though the class is merely a shared room;
2. the institution reveals that every person’s conduct affects everyone’s income and rank;
3. classmates acquire incentives to regulate one another;
4. friendship, resentment, leadership, and exclusion become economically consequential.

The school does not wait for community to emerge. It makes interdependence unavoidable and observes what political order the students construct.

## 5.3 The class hierarchy becomes social caste

Upperclassmen immediately use `不良品 / defective product` to identify Class D students. Class placement therefore becomes more than an internal learning group. It licenses assumptions about intelligence, discipline, and human quality.

The hierarchy can become self-confirming:

- lower placement produces humiliation;
- humiliation reduces confidence or cooperation;
- resulting dysfunction appears to validate the original placement.

Volume 1 does not yet reveal the full admission or placement procedure. It already gives reason to reject the idea that the class letter is a transparent description of total ability.

## 5.4 The school’s criteria remain intentionally opaque

When challenged, Chabashira refuses to provide the detailed calculation behind class points. She compares the system to corporate personnel assessment: people are judged without receiving a complete formula.

The school thereby teaches two lessons at once:

- real institutions often do contain opaque evaluation;
- people who understand only declared metrics remain vulnerable to hidden judgment.

But realism is not legitimacy. Reproducing an unjust or unaccountable feature of adult institutions does not by itself justify it educationally.

## 5.5 Surveillance exceeds cameras

Ayanokōji notices cameras in the convenience store and later uses the school’s broad surveillance environment in his reasoning. More revealingly, Chabashira knows he contacted a third-year student to purchase past examination papers. She says upperclassmen have their own assigned matters, implying that vertical social contact is visible to the school.

The institution can monitor:

- bodies in classrooms;
- transactions;
- point transfers;
- cross-year contact;
- and perhaps the social routes through which information moves.

Ayanokōji’s concealment is therefore never simple invisibility. It is the management of what an observer can plausibly infer from the visible trace.

## 5.6 The range-change “mistake” is a stress test

Chabashira claims she forgot to tell Class D that the midterm range had changed. Her lack of concern, the other teachers’ lack of reaction, Hoshinomiya’s amused gesture, and Chabashira’s later deliberate waiting strongly support the inference that the inequity was not an ordinary administrative mistake.

The class is being tested for its ability to:

- detect inconsistency;
- respond under compressed time;
- mobilize social networks;
- acquire information;
- and discover that formal equality of outcome does not compensate for unequal access to the relevant rules.

The exact degree of deliberate design remains unconfirmed within Volume 1, so the claim should remain a strong inference rather than textual fact.

# 6. Equality, fairness, and the instability of merit

## 6.1 The opening does not settle the philosophy

The first line asks whether people are equal. The narration rejects both naive equality and the easy emotional acceptance of inequality. It invokes natural differences, social outcomes, education, and Fukuzawa’s famous language about human rank.

The prologue is deliberately provocative. It is not the final doctrine of the volume.

By the end, at least four questions have separated:

1. Are people equal in capacity? — plainly not.
2. Are different capacities equally visible to the school? — no.
3. Does unequal capacity make unequal procedure legitimate? — no.
4. Does present weakness establish permanent worthlessness? — the volume increasingly says no.

## 6.2 Ayanokōji’s procedural claim

When Sudō faces expulsion, Ayanokōji does not claim that every student deserves the same outcome regardless of performance. He argues that Class D was subjected to the same test and punishment after receiving the relevant range later than its rivals.

His key formulation is:

> **「少なくとも平等に見えるようにはしなければならない」**  
> At minimum, it must be made to appear equal.

This may sound cynical. It is politically serious.

Institutional authority requires procedural credibility even in an unequal society. If the institution does not maintain minimally defensible procedure, evaluation becomes arbitrary power disguised as merit.

Chabashira does not accept the argument as a direct appeal to justice. She leaves a linguistic opening and waits for Ayanokōji to convert the objection into a permitted transaction.

The school therefore treats the capacity to make justice administratively legible as another form of ability.

## 6.3 The volume’s contextual theory of ability

The students’ capacities diverge by context.

| Character | Possessed or emerging ability | Why it is initially unusable or misread |
|---|---|---|
| Horikita | academics, prediction, discipline | contempt, poor pedagogy, isolation |
| Sudō | exceptional athletic ability, persistence | anger, low academics, poor self-regulation |
| Kushida | social recruitment, memory, atmosphere control | hidden hostility and approval dependence remain unresolved |
| Hirata | legitimacy, inclusion, voluntary coordination | cannot compel people who reject his social authority |
| Kōenji | intellectual and physical exceptionality | refuses collective obligation |
| Ike | social ease and ordinary-group belonging | immaturity and weak discipline dominate early perception |
| Ayanokōji | observation, strategy, physical training, rule interpretation | deliberate concealment and reluctance to accept social responsibility |

The important word is not “equal.” It is **convertible**.

Can the person’s capacity be converted into:

- a class outcome;
- a relationship;
- an institutional record;
- or future development?

The author’s afterword reinforces this. Kinugasa states that study is important but is not everything, and points to sport, art, comedy, and diverse vocations. This does not govern fictional fact, but it confirms that the novel’s widening of “ability” beyond academic ranking is deliberate.

## 6.4 Developmental ability

Sudō is not yet academically competent. The study group reveals something static scores cannot show: he can endure effort when the purpose becomes meaningful and the social environment stops treating him as merely stupid.

Horikita’s judgment changes because she observes the process.

This introduces a category the school’s initial ranking cannot measure cleanly:

> **developmental ability — the capacity to become more capable or socially usable when given time, motivation, and an appropriate relation.**

The right to continue developing becomes inseparable from the later question of expulsion.

# 7. Ayanokōji Kiyotaka
## The adolescent observer who begins choosing involvement

## 7.1 The Japanese narrator is not emotionally blank

Volume 1’s Ayanokōji is socially anxious, comic, sexually attentive, and eager for ordinary experience.

He:

- rehearses possible friendship openings;
- panics after another student befriends the boy he considered approaching;
- wonders what exactly makes someone a friend;
- wants a moderate number of friends;
- finds Horikita beautiful;
- notices Kushida’s appearance, scent, body, and physical proximity;
- participates in adolescent sexual banter to remain inside the male group;
- fantasizes about girls’ swimsuits;
- feels genuine excitement at obtaining girls’ contact information;
- stays up watching online videos;
- does not know common games;
- and interprets surviving the midterm together as a form of youth.

This is not a minor tonal curiosity. It establishes what he came to the school to obtain.

The student who says `オレは自由だ / I am free` after entering his private dorm room values the school partly because it gives him:

- privacy;
- separation from family;
- ordinary leisure;
- and a social life not wholly assigned in advance.

## 7.2 His public averageness is authored

The volume supplies several discrepancies:

- exact fifty-point entrance scores;
- a correct solution to advanced mathematics;
- a trained body noticed at the pool;
- evasion of Manabu’s attack;
- fast institutional inference;
- and a strong reaction to hereditary hierarchy.

Ayanokōji does not simply hide “the answer.” He produces a coherent average profile.

At the pool he chooses a mediocre time. In testing he chooses an apparently ordinary score. When his past-exam strategy succeeds, he assigns public credit to Kushida.

The reason is partly self-protection and partly functional. Kushida’s credibility makes the class more likely to accept the material. Hidden authorship and effective delegation coincide.

## 7.3 He is not a solitary mastermind in this volume

The midterm solution depends upon several independent capacities.

- Horikita narrows the curriculum and adapts her teaching.
- Kushida recruits the students, communicates the changed range, and presents the past exam with sufficient credibility.
- Sudō, Ike, and Yamauchi perform the effort and memorization.
- Horikita risks lowering her own English score.
- Ayanokōji acquires the past exam and later identifies the point-purchase route.
- Horikita contributes the private points necessary to make the purchase possible.

Ayanokōji is the hidden architect of several crucial links. He is not the sole cause.

## 7.4 The first environmental intervention

Ayanokōji withholds the past exam until the day before the test because he predicts early certainty would weaken motivation. He wants the students to develop basic study habits and then use the old questions as emergency insurance.

This is effective.

It is also the earliest clear example of controlling another person’s informational environment for a developmental purpose.

The ethical severity is limited compared with later possibilities:

- he does not create the expulsion threat;
- he does not harm the students;
- he expands their chance to remain;
- and the withheld information is supplied in time to help.

But the structure is already present:

> he decides what they should know, when they should know it, and what motivational state the delay should produce.

## 7.5 Why he saves Sudō

No single motive is sufficient.

### Friendship

Ayanokōji explicitly says Sudō and Ike are friends he does not want to see expelled. This should not be reduced to camouflage.

### Commitment

He tells Chabashira that he decided to help and does not want to stop while another route remains.

### Curiosity

He says he entered the school partly to learn what equality and true ability mean. Sudō is a test case for whether visible academic weakness exhausts human potential.

### Institutional interest

The expulsion crisis reveals how far the points system extends and whether formal judgments can be negotiated.

### Aversion to passive disposability

Ayanokōji challenges Horikita’s immediate conclusion that the weakest students should simply be removed.

The act is not pure altruism. It is already more than strategic indifference.

## 7.6 The bus scene and nonintervention

The bus scene should not be flattened into “Ayanokōji is passive.”

Kōenji refuses to give up his seat because he recognizes no obligation to sacrifice personal comfort for social contribution.

Kushida mobilizes the wider group through public moral appeal.

Horikita refuses because she sees no meaning in the act and regards personal conviction as sufficient.

Ayanokōji remains seated partly because he avoids attention and partly because he reasons that younger people may possess greater future social utility than the elderly woman.

His reasoning is cold and contestable. It is not absent.

Later, he also refuses to follow Horikita when she leaves the club meeting because he recognizes her right to act alone. Nonintervention may express respect for autonomy in one scene and moral avoidance in another. The volume does not support one global verdict.

## 7.7 His earliest political philosophy

Ayanokōji accepts factual inequality but rejects arbitrary institutional conversion of inequality into consequence. He also recognizes that conventional measures may miss context-specific capacity.

His own position remains unstable:

- he rejects Horikita’s disposability;
- he accepts transactional exploitation of school rules;
- he threatens the upperclassman with total retaliation if betrayed;
- he minimizes public responsibility;
- and he still wonders whether involvement will pull him back into the world he thought he had escaped.

The final reaction to `実力至上主義` is not triumph. It is unease:

> **「遠ざかったつもりだったのにな、そういう世界からは」**  
> He believed he had moved away from such a world.

The volume ends with a personal reason for continuing:

> he wants, at least, to see Horikita smile.

This is not yet a declaration of romance or altruistic leadership. It is evidence that personal curiosity has begun competing with withdrawal.

# 8. Horikita Suzune
## Excellence becoming socially usable

## 8.1 Initial doctrine: static competence

Horikita begins by treating academic and self-regulatory competence as the clearest evidence of value. She does not merely dislike weak students. She believes accommodating them wastes resources that should be directed toward capable people.

Her isolation is therefore partly ideological.

She interprets friendship as needless dependency and believes that she can pursue Class A through individual excellence.

## 8.2 Dependence hidden inside solitude

Manabu’s confrontation reveals the contradiction.

Suzune’s supposedly self-authored excellence is organized around him:

- she followed him to the school;
- fears his judgment;
- and accepts physical intimidation that she would reject from almost anyone else.

Manabu says she confuses `孤高 / proud or elevated solitude` with `孤立 / isolation`.

His diagnosis is important. His violence is abusive and cannot be justified as a necessary pedagogical technique.

The scene also reveals Ayanokōji’s concealed physical capacity, but Suzune’s fear should not be reduced to a device for displaying male power.

## 8.3 Failed pedagogy

Horikita’s first study group fails because she assumes that understanding the correct explanation is sufficient to teach.

She begins from conceptual knowledge the students do not possess, humiliates them when they cannot follow, and treats Sudō’s basketball ambition as delusion without understanding the field.

Her failure is not lack of knowledge. It is failure to model another person’s starting position.

## 8.4 Ayanokōji’s challenge

Ayanokōji asks her to imagine Sudō’s background and the reason he chose this school. He also points out that:

- Sudō possesses physical ability she does not;
- Ike may possess social or communicative capacities she lacks;
- and dismissing people before discovering the relevant context is itself a defect.

This does not instantly convert Horikita into a compassionate leader. It makes her static model intellectually unstable.

## 8.5 Reconstructed teaching

Horikita changes the lesson design.

She:

- reduces the goal to passing rather than abstract mastery;
- uses basketball analogy;
- identifies point-heavy and short-answer items during the test;
- and distributes teaching labor with Kushida.

Her pedagogy becomes more effective when she stops treating the students’ ignorance as moral insult and starts treating it as a problem of translation.

## 8.6 Research before apology

Her apology to Sudō is more analytically significant than a spontaneous burst of kindness.

She researches professional basketball, its difficulty, and the economic risk of the path. She then concludes that someone who does not understand the field has no right to ridicule the person pursuing it.

This is an emerging epistemic ethic:

> **judgment requires understanding the domain and the person’s actual commitment.**

The lesson directly revises her initial universalization of academic merit.

## 8.7 Strategic self-risk

Horikita intentionally lowers her English score in an attempt to reduce the class average and save Sudō from the failing threshold. She cannot safely lower it without limit; a further reduction might endanger her own enrollment.

This is her first major conversion of personal excellence into collective protection.

The attempt fails because she lacks full information about rounding. The failure matters. Intelligent action can still be defeated by hidden rules.

## 8.8 “Defective” is not “refuse”

At the volume’s climax, she distinguishes `不良品 / defective product` from `クズ / refuse or trash`.

A defect may be repaired or changed into a good product.

The industrial metaphor remains dehumanizing. Its use by Horikita is nevertheless a major development because she reverses its implication. Current defect no longer establishes permanent disposal.

This is not full humanism. It is the beginning of developmental meritocracy.

## 8.9 End-of-volume state

Horikita remains:

- blunt;
- contemptuous;
- unable to offer easy warmth;
- and committed to Class A partly for personal reasons.

But she has begun to:

- work with Kushida despite mutual dislike;
- alter her method;
- recognize Sudō’s effort and domain-specific talent;
- risk her own score;
- join a celebration she would previously have rejected;
- and accept that reaching A requires more than solitary excellence.

Her leadership has not formed. The necessary premise has appeared: other people may contain value she has not yet learned how to see.

# 9. Kushida Kikyō
## Sociality as labor, recognition, and coercive credibility

## 9.1 The bus scene establishes her method

Kushida does not defeat Kōenji’s argument. She changes the audience.

She appeals to the entire bus and makes continued nonintervention socially uncomfortable. Another passenger eventually yields.

Her strength is not abstract philosophical superiority. It is mobilization.

## 9.2 She understands accommodation

During introductions, nervous Inogashira freezes. Generic encouragement increases pressure. Kushida says:

> take your time; do not hurry.

The difference is relational. She adjusts the group’s pace to the speaker rather than demanding the speaker match the group immediately.

This is genuine social intelligence.

## 9.3 Her public kindness produces real outcomes

Kushida:

- exchanges contact information across the class;
- remembers people;
- recruits Sudō, Ike, and Yamauchi;
- communicates the changed test range;
- distributes the past exam;
- stabilizes conflict;
- and makes collaboration socially acceptable.

Without her, the rescue plan is substantially less likely to work.

## 9.4 The private Kushida does not erase the public one

Her private rant reveals intense hatred toward Horikita and contempt for classmates. She then uses a possible fabricated sexual-assault allegation as leverage over Ayanokōji, physically arranging fingerprint evidence.

The threat demonstrates:

- acute awareness of credibility;
- willingness to weaponize gendered reputational asymmetry;
- and a capacity to turn physical evidence into an authored narrative.

Yet the private self is not simply the “truth” and the public self a complete lie.

Later she tells Ayanokōji that she dislikes him while also finding conversation with him enjoyable. Her social pleasure and resentment can coexist.

The public persona is labor performed under a psychological need for recognition. It is also a real competence through which other people receive help.

## 9.5 Horikita and Kushida as opposite failures

Horikita preserves self-consistency at the cost of social relationship.

Kushida preserves social relationship at the cost of exposing only a tightly controlled self.

Horikita would rather be disliked than perform warmth she does not feel.

Kushida needs to be liked so strongly that hidden resentment becomes explosive.

Their cooperation in the study group is therefore politically significant. Two incompatible social strategies become mutually useful before either conflict has been solved.

## 9.6 Relationship with Ayanokōji

Ayanokōji’s accidental knowledge gives him leverage. Kushida’s ability to author a credible allegation gives her counter-leverage.

Their relationship begins through mutual risk rather than trust.

When he gives her public credit for the past exam, he uses her legitimacy and preserves his invisibility. She gains prestige; he gains concealment. The operation benefits both, but the information asymmetry remains.

# 10. Class D as a collection of unconverted capacities

## 10.1 Hirata Yōsuke: visible legitimacy

Hirata repeatedly performs the class-facing work Horikita cannot.

He:

- initiates introductions;
- asks rather than commands;
- forms an open study group;
- permits people to enter or leave;
- and immediately asks whether Sudō can be saved after the failing result.

His leadership is voluntary and socially legible.

Its limitation is also visible. Sudō rejects him, and Hirata cannot compel participation without becoming something other than the person the class trusts.

## 10.2 Sudō Ken: embodied potential and wounded dignity

Sudō’s academics and anger make him an obvious target for disposal. His body and commitment to basketball reveal a capacity the initial school environment does not value clearly.

His change begins when other people treat the dream as serious enough to understand rather than ridicule.

The study period does not make him academically strong. It reveals that he can endure effort and accept help when the relation preserves dignity.

## 10.3 Ike and Yamauchi: immaturity and social belonging

They are undisciplined, sexually preoccupied, and academically weak. They also form the earliest ordinary male social network through which Ayanokōji experiences friendship.

The volume does not yet establish their deeper capacities. It already shows why a purely academic judgment is incomplete: the class’s social life is partly built by students whom Horikita would classify as burdens.

## 10.4 Kōenji Rokusuke: radical autonomy

Kōenji’s refusal on the bus is internally consistent. He recognizes no obligation to subordinate personal comfort to public moral appeal. His swimming and quiz performance suggest exceptional capability.

But capability that refuses availability cannot be treated as ordinary class power.

He raises a problem that the school’s collective system has not solved:

> Does exceptional ability create an obligation to contribute, or does collective need illegitimately claim ownership over the individual?

## 10.5 Ichinose Honami: mediation without humiliation

Ichinose’s brief intervention in the Class C confrontation is structurally important.

She:

- stops Sudō without insulting him;
- rebukes the provocateurs;
- invokes institutional reporting only as a boundary;
- and leaves the group able to continue studying.

Ayanokōji notices the contrast with Horikita. Correct content is not enough; social resolution depends on how truth is delivered.

## 10.6 The class’s actual defect

Class D lacks a functioning political division of labor.

It has:

- Horikita’s analysis;
- Hirata’s legitimacy;
- Kushida’s network;
- Sudō’s physical potential;
- Kōenji’s exceptional ability;
- and Ayanokōji’s hidden architecture.

None can yet be relied upon as a coherent system.

The midterm rescue is the first temporary proof that unequal capacities can become mutually usable.

# 11. The midterm examination
## The formal test is only one layer

## 11.1 Formal mechanism

The class learns that a student scoring below half the class average, after the school’s rounding rule, receives a failing result and immediate expulsion. The exact threshold is not disclosed until the result crisis.

Class D also receives the corrected test range later than its rivals.

The point system permits transfers and, when interpreted literally, the purchase of an examination point.

## 11.2 The surprise quiz as signal

The earlier quiz contains several questions far beyond the expected first-year curriculum. Ayanokōji later confirms that the same questions appeared in an older quiz.

The difficult questions therefore measure more than ordinary knowledge. They reward students who discover precedent or already possess unusual knowledge.

## 11.3 The past-exam market

Ayanokōji identifies a resource-poor third-year Class D student by watching who selects the free mountain-vegetable meal. He purchases the past midterm and quiz for 15,000 points.

This scene establishes the political economy of the school:

- poverty becomes bargaining leverage;
- point transfers leave records;
- old information becomes market commodity;
- and cross-year inequality creates opportunities for trade.

Ayanokōji’s threat of comprehensive retaliation if cheated shows that transactions remain socially enforced even when institutionally permitted.

## 11.4 Information timing

He delays disclosure of the past exam until the final day so it cannot replace the two weeks of study.

The strategy separates two objectives:

- development of minimal study discipline;
- emergency survival through memorization.

It is more educational than immediately giving the answers. It is still a unilateral decision about other people’s motivational environment.

## 11.5 Kushida’s public authorship

Ayanokōji asks Kushida to claim she acquired the material. This works because:

- she is trusted;
- an upperclass friendship is plausible for her;
- she can address the entire class;
- and her public identity converts the information into coordinated action.

The scene shows that truth’s social effectiveness depends on the speaker’s credibility.

## 11.6 Horikita’s two interventions

First, Horikita has already taught enough of the likely curriculum that many questions remain manageable even without rote memorization.

Second, she deliberately lowers her English score to reduce the average threshold.

Her failed manipulation of the average is a serious protective act rather than a symbolic gesture.

## 11.7 Purchasing one point

Ayanokōji returns to the premise stated on the first day: nothing inside the school is beyond purchase.

He asks for the price of one English point.

Chabashira sets the price at 100,000 private points—more than one ordinary student is likely to retain after the first month. Horikita joins him, making the transaction possible.

The solution is elegant and morally strange.

Sudō’s academic result remains a failure. His institutional status changes because classmates correctly interpret a market rule.

From the school’s perspective, this may count as “true ability.” It is also proof that academic judgment is not sacred. It is another tradable institutional object.

## 11.8 What the examination actually measures

The midterm measures at least:

- academic preparation;
- willingness to seek help;
- pedagogical flexibility;
- social recruitment;
- information acquisition;
- point liquidity;
- institutional language interpretation;
- and the ability to recognize that the formal test is nested inside a broader environment.

The school is not simply selecting students who know the subject.

It is selecting students who can survive the system around the subject.

## 11.9 Ethical evaluation

The strongest defense of the school is that students are being prepared for an adult world in which:

- criteria are incomplete;
- resources are unequal;
- information must be acquired;
- and consequences cannot always be appealed away.

The strongest criticism is that the school manufactures avoidable harm, withholds crucial information, and places a sixteen-year-old’s entire future behind a threshold whose rules are disclosed only after he fails.

That is not merely realism. It is pedagogical cruelty used to produce diagnostic clarity.

# 12. Relationship architecture

## 12.1 Ayanokōji–Horikita

### Public form

Seatmates who frequently argue and become associated in classmates’ gossip.

### Private form

Ayanokōji recognizes her competence and isolation. Horikita identifies anomalies in his ability and increasingly uses him as a recruitable resource.

### Information asymmetry

Ayanokōji knows more about his own ability, institutional observations, and several plans. Horikita knows enough to suspect concealment but cannot define its extent.

### Leverage

Horikita uses meals, prior deception, and direct pressure to recruit him. He uses superior situational insight to reshape her decisions without fully disclosing why.

### Development

They move from mutual avoidance to a functional partnership. The partnership is not equal, but it already includes genuine influence in both directions.

### Volume-end question

Can Horikita become a leader rather than the visible surface of a person whose methods she does not understand?

## 12.2 Ayanokōji–Kushida

### Public form

Friendly classmates, with Kushida actively trying to connect.

### Private form

Mutual knowledge and counter-leverage after her hidden rant and fabricated-assault threat.

### Trust

Low. Operational usefulness is high.

### Development

Their shared secret and past-exam operation create a limited bond. Kushida’s “thank you” remains ambiguous: gratitude for preserving her secret, giving her credit, or allowing a less performative conversation may all be involved.

## 12.3 Horikita–Sudō

The relationship begins in contempt and aggression.

It changes through:

- teaching;
- visible effort;
- researched understanding;
- apology;
- score sacrifice;
- and Sudō’s recognition that Horikita tried to protect him.

His sudden romantic attraction is comic, but the underlying shift is serious: he has encountered recognition from the person whose judgment previously humiliated him.

## 12.4 Horikita–Kushida

They remain incompatible.

Their cooperation proves that political usefulness does not require affection. Horikita explicitly accepts the fact of Kushida’s hatred as clearer and easier to manage than false warmth whose meaning she cannot verify.

This is a limited but important tolerance for coexistence.

## 12.5 Class network

The victory creates a small coalition:

- Horikita as analyst and teacher;
- Kushida as social communicator;
- Ayanokōji as hidden institutional reader;
- Sudō, Ike, and Yamauchi as preserved participants;
- Hirata as broader class legitimacy.

The coalition is provisional. Its existence is the volume’s first answer to the problem of Class D.

# 13. Japanese narration, voice, and register

## 13.1 Ayanokōji

Ayanokōji uses casual `オレ`. His interior voice is loose, self-mocking, referential, and frequently sexually comic. It contrasts sharply with the economical, low-profile voice he presents to classmates.

Characteristic Year 1 Volume 1 features include:

- internal rhetorical questions;
- sudden Kansai-flavored comic phrasing such as `あかん` in self-mockery;
- exaggerated fear of social failure;
- quiet observational precision;
- and self-explanations that minimize ability as “bad ideas” or laziness.

The shift into serious strategic speech is marked by greater directness and fewer comic detours.

## 13.2 Horikita

Horikita’s speech is complete, controlled, and often marked by feminine declarative endings such as `～わ`, `～よ`, and `～かしら`. She rarely uses vulgarity. Her cruelty takes the form of a polished judgment.

During change, the same voice begins stating respect without becoming conventionally warm. Her apology to Sudō remains formally precise because she has not acquired an easy emotional idiom.

## 13.3 Kushida

Public Kushida uses soft questions, diminutives, inclusive language, and emotional cushioning. Her `ゆっくりでいいよ` adapts the group to the anxious speaker.

Private Kushida shifts toward blunt hatred, rougher phrasing, and repeated pressure. The private register exposes resentment but does not eliminate her capacity for genuine social pleasure.

## 13.4 Hirata

Hirata’s `僕` and polite inclusive requests establish a leadership style based on voluntary consent. He invites rather than orders and explicitly allows people to leave the study group.

## 13.5 Sudō

Sudō’s rough `俺`, contractions, imperatives, and insults make bodily aggression audible. His speech begins acquiring apology, requests, and acknowledgment without losing blunt masculinity.

## 13.6 Kōenji

Kōenji speaks theatrically, with self-mythologizing formality and amused superiority. His voice refuses the premise that others possess ordinary claims over him.

## 13.7 Chabashira

Chabashira’s terse declaratives, contemptuous address, and strategic omissions make her sound more like an examiner than a teacher. She uses language as bait, repeatedly leaving openings for students who notice exact phrasing.

# 14. Appearance, illustrations, and embodied characterization

## 14.1 Cover and color front matter

The cover places Horikita prominently in the foreground while Ayanokōji stands slightly behind and turned away. The composition prefigures their initial political arrangement: she is the visible exceptional student; he occupies a quieter position whose importance is not yet legible.

The color front matter also foregrounds the volume’s contrast between institutional uniform and individual presentation. Horikita’s immaculate, severe appearance differs from Kushida’s approachable softness and the boys’ looser social postures.

One color image is overtly sexualized. It establishes the light novel’s willingness to frame female embodiment as reader-facing fanservice even while the prose later takes privacy, coercion, and reputation seriously. That tension should remain part of the formal assessment.

## 14.2 Kushida’s visual openness

Several illustrations show Kushida leaning forward, blushing, placing a hand near her chest, or being physically framed between male figures. Her visual language emphasizes:

- accessibility;
- softness;
- direct eye contact;
- and a body that becomes both social invitation and later coercive evidence.

The illustration near the fabricated-assault threat intensifies the ethical ambiguity by eroticizing a scene whose narrative function is intimidation and control of credibility.

## 14.3 The pool illustration

Horikita and Kushida are shown in swimsuits, making the male gaze and reader-facing fanservice materially visible. The prose simultaneously uses swimming to disclose comparative physical ability and Ayanokōji’s deliberate suppression.

The illustration therefore joins two modes that do not sit comfortably together:

- embodied competence;
- sexualized spectacle.

## 14.4 Chabashira at the podium

The monochrome illustration of Chabashira looming over the class during the point revelation visually centralizes the institution. Students appear below the adult who controls the information capable of redefining their entire first month.

## 14.5 Manabu and Ayanokōji

The action illustration captures the instant when Ayanokōji’s apparently passive body becomes technically responsive. It is the volume’s clearest visual contradiction between public averageness and concealed training.

## 14.6 Ichinose’s mediation

The illustration of Ichinose between aggressive boys frames her as socially composed and physically unthreatening while still commanding the scene. It visually contrasts mediation with Horikita’s confrontational truth-telling.

## 14.7 Final class-facing image

The late illustration pairing Horikita and Kushida presents the two girls as complementary class functions: severe analysis and social warmth. The image is more optimistic than their private relationship warrants, but accurately captures the temporary coalition on which the midterm rescue depends.

# 15. Motifs and recurring language

## 15.1 `不良品 / defective product`

The school hierarchy turns people into products. Upperclassmen use the term as insult; Chabashira uses it as classification; Horikita eventually reverses it by arguing that defects may be repaired.

The metaphor remains dangerous because it assumes a standard of “good product” defined by the institution.

## 15.2 `自由 / freedom`

Freedom first means separation from family, privacy, and relief from ordinary supervision.

The school then reveals freedom as a test whose consequences were hidden.

Ayanokōji’s “I am free” is sincere. It is also provisional.

## 15.3 `友達 / friend`

Ayanokōji repeatedly asks what makes friendship real. The answer emerges through unplanned repetition rather than formal declaration: meals, group chat, invitations, swimming, study, and shared relief.

## 15.4 Points

Points convert:

- value;
- consumption;
- information;
- social debt;
- and eventually an examination score

into one institutional language.

## 15.5 Visibility

The volume repeatedly asks who is seen and how:

- cameras observe;
- teachers permit misconduct while recording it;
- Kushida manages public appearance;
- Ayanokōji suppresses measurable performance;
- Horikita is visibly excellent but politically isolated;
- class letters become public identities.

## 15.6 Repair

Horikita’s repair metaphor is the first articulation of developmental possibility. It does not yet escape product language, but it changes the direction from disposal toward transformation.

# 16. Formal and literary strengths

## 16.1 Genre conversion

The volume begins as school comedy and gradually reveals an institutional thriller. The tonal shift works because the earlier ordinary scenes become evidence retrospectively:

- spending becomes behavioral data;
- jokes become proof of adolescence;
- friendship attempts become class infrastructure;
- a free meal becomes a market signal;
- exact wording becomes a loophole.

## 16.2 The protagonist remains partially unreadable

The first-person perspective gives direct access to embarrassment and desire while withholding a stable account of capacity and motive. This creates a productive asymmetry: the reader may know what Ayanokōji notices without knowing how much he planned before reporting the observation.

## 16.3 Character conflict carries the philosophy

The volume does not leave equality and meritocracy as abstract debate.

- Horikita and Sudō embody competing capacities.
- Kushida demonstrates social power.
- Kōenji tests radical autonomy.
- Hirata tests voluntary leadership.
- Chabashira embodies opaque institutional judgment.
- Ayanokōji turns procedure into outcome.

## 16.4 The first victory is genuinely collective

The causal complexity prevents the volume from becoming a simple mastermind reveal. Every major contributor supplies something the others cannot.

## 16.5 The afterword clarifies the artistic project

The author’s statement that study is not everything and that human aptitudes vary supports the reading of the volume as a critique of narrow academic merit, without dictating the fictional ethical verdict.

# 17. Critical reservations

## 17.1 The school’s pedagogy depends on manufactured preventable harm

Withholding the point rule and corrected test range may produce useful information about adaptability. It also threatens expulsion for failure under conditions the institution deliberately made harder.

The school’s defenders may call this preparation for society. That does not explain why an educational institution should reproduce the least accountable features of society rather than teach students how to challenge them.

## 17.2 The opening rhetoric is philosophically rough

The equality prologue uses broad statements about sex, disability, work, and social value. It functions as provocation, not a fully defensible theory. The volume’s later treatment is substantially more nuanced.

## 17.3 Sexual comedy conflicts with the privacy theme

Ayanokōji’s adolescent gaze is important characterization. Extended breast jokes and sexualized illustrations sometimes reduce girls to spectacle in a volume that also asks the reader to take bodily privacy and reputational coercion seriously.

## 17.4 The Kushida threat uses an inflammatory trope

Her fabricated-assault threat efficiently establishes her ruthlessness and command of credibility. It also relies on a sensational gendered scenario that can overwhelm the more subtle institutional point.

## 17.5 Manabu’s violence is not adequately challenged

His diagnosis of Suzune may be perceptive. His physical conduct is abusive. The volume uses the scene partly to reveal Ayanokōji’s physical ability, risking displacement of Suzune’s experience.

## 17.6 Development is achieved through partial manipulation

Ayanokōji’s delayed release of the past exam is mild and beneficial compared with more severe possible interventions. It still normalizes the idea that a superior observer may decide what others should know in order to shape their effort.

## 17.7 Several characters remain archetypal

The volume deliberately introduces recognizable school types. It begins complicating them, but firm conclusions about many secondary students would be premature.

# 21. Cumulative delta after Volume 1

## Ayanokōji delta

The corpus begins with a protagonist whose ordinary adolescent desires are already real but whose public identity is deliberately engineered. He has not yet accepted general responsibility for the class. He has made the first choice to intervene after friendship, curiosity, and a prior commitment become sufficient reasons.

## Horikita and class-leadership delta

Horikita moves from static individual merit toward developmental judgment. Her leadership remains crude, but she has learned that teaching requires adaptation and that another person’s field must be understood before it is judged.

## Relationship delta

- Ayanokōji–Horikita becomes a functional, unequal partnership.
- Ayanokōji–Kushida becomes a relationship of mutual secrets and operational value.
- Horikita–Sudō moves from contempt toward recognition and attraction.
- Class D develops a temporary coalition rather than a stable community.

## Institutional delta

The school is established as an enclosed, data-producing environment where:

- behavior becomes class economy;
- points can translate almost any institutional object;
- evaluation criteria are deliberately incomplete;
- and teachers may allow failure to develop in order to observe adaptation.

## Ethics and autonomy delta

The first major rescue is beneficial and partially manipulative. Ayanokōji respects some forms of refusal while controlling information in others. Horikita’s initial willingness to discard people is challenged, not fully abandoned.

## Ability and merit delta

Five ability dimensions are already visible:

- possessed;
- displayed;
- measured;
- socially usable;
- developmental.

The school measures only some of them and mistakes the result for total value.

## Open-question delta

The volume leaves unresolved whether Ayanokōji’s interventions will remain limited, consent-compatible forms of assistance or evolve into a private right to author other people’s development.

# 23. Final synthesis

Volume 1 begins by asking whether people are equal and ends by showing why the question cannot be answered through one scale.

Horikita is more academically capable than Sudō.

Sudō possesses a body and professional ambition she cannot imitate.

Kushida can move a group in ways Horikita cannot.

Hirata can create voluntary participation but cannot command someone who rejects his legitimacy.

Kōenji possesses extraordinary power that the class cannot count on.

Ayanokōji can read the whole environment but refuses to let the environment read him in return.

The school compresses these differences into a class letter and a point total.

That compression is politically useful. It is not the same as understanding the people being ranked.

The first month demonstrates how easily “freedom” becomes a trap when the authority governing consequences possesses information the subject does not. The students are free to spend, talk, sleep, and ignore class. They are not informed that these choices determine the economic fate of everyone around them.

Chabashira’s defense—that ordinary conduct should not require explicit explanation—has force. Students should know that lateness and disruption are irresponsible.

It does not answer the whole criticism.

The school withholds not only moral instruction but the scale and distribution of consequence. It turns individual misconduct into collective deprivation, then calls the result a neutral measurement of merit.

Ayanokōji’s first major intervention operates inside this contradiction.

He does not defeat the system by demanding compassion. He learns the system’s language:

- old tests may be purchased;
- information may be timed;
- social credibility may be delegated;
- class averages may be manipulated;
- and an examination point may be bought.

Justice becomes possible only after it is translated into a valid transaction.

That is an impressive victory and an indictment of the institution.

The person most changed by the operation is Horikita.

Her initial worldview is not refuted by the claim that everyone is secretly equal. It is refuted by evidence that she did not know what she was measuring.

Sudō’s academic weakness is real.

So are:

- his effort;
- his athletic talent;
- his professional commitment;
- and his ability to respond when another person recognizes those things seriously.

Horikita’s apology marks the beginning of a more demanding meritocracy. She must understand a person’s domain, starting point, and developmental possibility before declaring the person worthless.

Her later distinction between a defective product and refuse remains trapped inside the institution’s industrial vocabulary, but it reverses the direction of judgment. A defect may be repaired. A current classification may not be final.

Ayanokōji stands at the center of this change without fully joining it.

He wants friends, freedom, women, games, leisure, and ordinary youth. He also studies people as problems of motivation and information. His choice to save Sudō is partly friendship and partly experiment. His choice to conceal authorship is partly self-protection and partly sound delegation. His choice to withhold the past exam is educational and paternalistic at once.

Volume 1 therefore establishes the precise ambiguity that the rest of the series must test:

> **Ayanokōji may be uniquely capable of seeing what another person can become. That does not yet establish that he has the right to determine the conditions under which the person becomes it.**

The class’s first success is not the emergence of a single elite ruler. It is the temporary coordination of unlike capacities:

- analysis;
- teaching;
- social recruitment;
- embodied effort;
- financial sacrifice;
- and institutional interpretation.

The novel’s answer to “true ability” is therefore still provisional, but already broader than rank:

> **True ability is not merely what one person possesses. It is what a person or community can make possible under the relevant conditions—and whether those conditions allow the people involved to remain more than instruments of the outcome.**

The volume ends before that ethical standard can be met.

It ends with the class having survived, Horikita having changed, and Ayanokōji deciding—uneasily, personally, and still from the shadows—to continue.
