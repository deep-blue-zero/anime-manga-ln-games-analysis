# 1. Executive thesis

Volume 1 asked what happens when an institution observes students according to criteria the students do not know. Volume 2 asks the harder epistemic question:

> **What happens when something is true, but the people who know it cannot make that truth legible in a form the institution is willing to recognize?**

Sudō tells the truth about the immediate incident: Class C students provoke and attack him first. Yet the visible record points against him. He has the established reputation of a violent troublemaker; three injured opponents corroborate one another; he is physically unharmed; and his previous behavior makes the accusation plausible.

Sakura also tells the truth. She witnessed the encounter and possesses photographs that establish her presence. Yet the photographs do not capture the initiating blow, and her status as a Class D student gives her a material interest in Sudō’s exoneration. Her social anxiety further produces the very behaviors—hesitation, poor eye contact, difficulty speaking—that an adversarial hearing can misread as signs of deception.

The volume therefore distinguishes at least five epistemic layers:

1. **event truth** — what actually happened;
2. **evidentiary capacity / 証拠能力** — whether something can legitimately enter deliberation as evidence;
3. **probative force / 証明力** — how much that evidence should persuade;
4. **credibility** — how much a speaker’s social identity, conduct, timing, and reputation affect belief;
5. **institutional outcome** — what the school is actually prepared to record and enforce.

This is more precise than a simple truth-versus-lie conflict. Class C’s story is false, but its injuries and coordinated testimony are real facts. Sakura’s story is true, but the proof available to her is incomplete. The school cannot access the event directly; it can only interpret representations of it.

The second pass also reveals that the same problem structures Sakura’s personal story. Sakura does not lack a self. She lacks a safe way to make different parts of herself socially legible. Her withdrawn school persona, the self-photographed idol identity `雫 / Shizuku`, her witness identity, and her frightened but connection-seeking interior are not cleanly divisible into “false mask” and “true person.” Her opening paradox—only by ceasing to be herself can she become herself—makes the mask an enabling technology as well as a concealment.

That gives the camera motif unusual coherence. A camera can:

- let Sakura author how she appears;
- accidentally make her a witness;
- turn behavior into institutional data;
- facilitate predatory attention;
- create protective evidence;
- and, finally, become a fabricated sign of omniscient surveillance.

The volume’s climactic bluff does not prove Sudō innocent. It changes the decision environment so that the guilty students themselves make continued lying too dangerous. The fake cameras work because Ishizaki, Komiya, and Kondō know the hidden truth and are denied the time, information, and external advice necessary to verify whether the threatened proof exists.

Ayanokōji’s role is therefore more consequential than “solving the case.” He begins to exercise **environmental authorship**. He does not directly hand Horikita the answer. He stages the premises from which she can produce it: he raises surveillance, brings her to the unmonitored corridor, emphasizes the evidentiary stalemate, and introduces the conceptual possibility that a lie can acquire the force of truth. Horikita recognizes the synthesis, verifies feasibility, assumes responsibility for the gamble, recruits Ichinose and Kanzaki, and publicly owns the operation.

The authorship is shared but asymmetrical:

> **Ayanokōji authors the path of discovery; Horikita authors the publicly actionable strategy.**

That distinction matters because it is the earliest strong example of a method that can be read in two ways. It can be developmental pedagogy: he gives another person room to think, decide, and lead. It can also be covert control: he determines what information she encounters while denying that he has done so.

The volume gives us reason to retain both readings.

Most importantly, Ayanokōji’s strategic mode does not erase his human one. He is preoccupied with whether friends will invite him to lunch, pleased to be contacted by girls, physically affected by Ichinose’s proximity, comfortable enough with Horikita to speak near his `素 / unguarded self`, and deeply moved by Sakura’s isolation. His statement that he once desperately wanted someone to know the pain of fighting alone is among the strongest early indications that his concern for Sakura is not merely instrumental.

Yet even care is already entangled with calculation. He locates Sakura through phone tracking; at the stalker confrontation he initially waits for a more decisive evidentiary moment before intervening; and he turns the man’s fear of public exposure into leverage.

Volume 2 therefore advances the Year 1 problem from **hidden evaluation** to **controlled legibility**:

> **Power belongs not only to the person who knows what is true, but to the person who can determine what others see, what they can verify, whom they believe, and what choices remain rational under uncertainty.**

The ethical question is already present inside the success:

> **When does helping another person become authorship over the conditions in which that person must act?**

# 2. Second-pass findings at a glance

The prior Volume 2 reading was strong. The second pass largely confirms it while making the volume’s central structure more exact.

## Confirmed

- The disciplinary conflict is fundamentally about unequal credibility rather than uncertainty over whether violence occurred.
- Sudō is truthful about Class C initiating the immediate fight, but his reputation functions as accumulated adverse evidence.
- Sakura’s witness story connects truth, visibility, surveillance, and self-presentation.
- The fake-camera operation is a bluff that bypasses formal adjudication rather than producing direct proof.
- Horikita becomes the visible author of the plan while Ayanokōji conceals the extent of his contribution.
- Ayanokōji’s ordinary interior remains socially anxious, heterosexual, comic, and sincerely interested in friendship.
- Ichinose and Kanzaki introduce a more organized model of class cooperation than Class D currently possesses.
- The stalker subplot mirrors the main plot by turning evidence and feared exposure into the decisive protective weapon.

## Strengthened

- `証拠能力` and `証明力` make the volume’s epistemology more technical than the earlier shorthand “truth / proof / credibility.”
- Sakura’s masks should be understood as **authored forms of visibility**, not simply false selves to be removed.
- Horikita’s leadership development begins not only in taking public credit but in accepting **responsibility for a gamble whose failure would worsen Sudō’s position**.
- Ayanokōji’s care for Sakura is more explicit than the first analysis emphasized: he grounds his advice in his own remembered desire for someone to recognize his pain.
- Ayanokōji’s autonomy language is already internally contradictory in a productive way. He strongly condemns friends violating his privacy and explicitly tells Sakura she need not testify, yet he is simultaneously willing to shape Horikita’s informational environment without disclosure.
- Class B’s strength is not merely “good people.” Ichinose and Kanzaki show early evidence of self-created governance, informational infrastructure, and principled limits on competition.
- The point economy becomes more politically expansive: individual class transfer is priced at twenty million private points, extracurricular performance can create value, and even dispute withdrawal incurs administrative point costs.

## Corrected or qualified

- The previous reading sometimes described Horikita as moving from present-value meritocracy to developmental thinking too cleanly. In this volume she still explicitly says that someone who is not useful **now** has no value for the immediate Class A project. Her final defense of Sudō’s `進歩 / progress` is therefore a **new breach in that framework**, not its complete abandonment.
- The earlier description of Ayanokōji “using Kushida’s attractiveness as bait” overstates her operational role in the final execution. The Class C boys are brought to the corridor under a pretext connected to Kushida, but the decisive confrontation is conducted by Ayanokōji and Ichinose; the analytical emphasis belongs on informational and environmental control rather than sexual manipulation.
- The fake-camera victory is not simply “Ayanokōji’s solution assigned to Horikita.” Horikita independently recognizes the synthesis after his cues, verifies that the required equipment can be obtained, requests Class B’s points, accepts debt, and owns the formal risk. The correct model is **seeded discovery plus autonomous operationalization**.
- Sakura’s final willingness to reveal more of Shizuku should not be treated as proof that the idol persona was merely an unhealthy false mask. The prologue itself establishes that performed identity is one of the few spaces where she can access otherwise unavailable parts of herself.

## Newly foregrounded

- Volume 2 contains a recurring distinction between **chosen visibility and imposed visibility**. Sakura’s self-photography is chosen; the stalker’s gaze is imposed; testimony is chosen but institutionally exposing; phone-location access is structurally available; the fake cameras impose imagined observation on Class C.
- Ayanokōji’s line that friendship does not permit unlimited intrusion is an early ordinary-life autonomy principle with substantial later analytical value.
- The volume gives a small but important direct Horikita POV passage, exposing insecurity and responsibility that Ayanokōji’s external narration alone could not fully establish.
- Chabashira explicitly frames Ayanokōji himself as potentially the most dangerous `不良品 / defective product` in Class D because high capability can become catastrophic when mishandled. The school’s defect language is thus detached from simple low ability.
- The ending does not merely reveal a stronger rival. It shows Ayanokōji becoming mentally excited by the competitive landscape and then recoiling from that excitement. His desired ordinary life and his attraction to difficult problems are already in conflict.

# 3. Position in the Year 1 architecture

Volume 1 established three foundational propositions:

1. institutional rules can govern students before students know what the rules are;
2. ability is contextual and can remain invisible to the metric being applied;
3. Ayanokōji is willing to intervene while suppressing public attribution.

Volume 2 moves all three propositions from **measurement** into **epistemology**.

The school no longer merely asks, “How should these students be ranked?” It must decide, “Whose account of reality should count?” That question exposes several weaknesses in the school’s competitive architecture.

First, the class system gives witnesses an automatic conflict of interest. Sakura’s testimony loses force because saving Sudō protects Class D’s points. A system designed to intensify class solidarity simultaneously makes intra-class testimony less trustworthy.

Second, teacher behavior affects competitive information. Class B receives useful information about extracurricular rewards and teacher incentives that Class D did not. Student competition is therefore mediated by adult disclosure practices.

Third, private points increasingly function as political resources rather than consumer currency. They can buy information, transfer between students, theoretically purchase class mobility, finance equipment for a deception operation, and pay administrative costs.

Fourth, the volume introduces **student-authored examinations**. Whoever organized the attack on Sudō has taken one of his known weaknesses—anger backed by overwhelming physical advantage—and constructed a situation where winning the physical encounter produces institutional vulnerability. The school designs environments to reveal students; students are already beginning to do the same to one another.

Finally, Volume 2 changes Ayanokōji’s position. In Volume 1 he solved emergencies while attempting to disappear into the causal background. In Volume 2, Horikita begins to notice that the background itself may have been arranged. The concealment problem is no longer only whether he will display high ability. It is whether people will realize that some of their own “discoveries” were made inside environments he deliberately shaped.

This makes Volume 2 the first strong bridge from **hidden competence** toward **hidden authorship**.

# 4. Narrative and chapter architecture

The substantive narrative is organized around seven named movements:

1. `佐倉愛里の独白` — Sakura Airi’s Monologue
2. `波乱の幕開けは突然に` — The Turbulent Opening Arrives Suddenly
3. `ウィークポイント` — Weak Point
4. `意外な目撃者` — An Unexpected Witness
5. `それぞれの思惑` — Respective Motives
6. `真実と嘘` — Truth and Lies
7. `たったひとつの解決策` — The One and Only Solution

The architecture is unusually coherent because the opening monologue does not merely introduce Sakura. It supplies the formal vocabulary for the whole book.

## 4.1 From mask to contested event

Sakura opens by asking whether everyone else also lives through `偽りの仮面 / false masks`. Immediately afterward she witnesses the fight and takes photographs almost involuntarily.

The sequence creates two distinct image problems:

- the image Sakura **chooses** to make of herself;
- the image her camera accidentally makes of an event.

Neither image produces total truth.

## 4.2 “Weak point” is both character flaw and attack surface

The title `ウィークポイント` applies to more than Sudō’s temper.

A weak point may be:

- Sudō’s aggression and reputation;
- Sakura’s fear of public attention;
- Horikita’s destabilization around Manabu;
- Ayanokōji’s desire to remain socially inconspicuous;
- Class D’s poor cohesion;
- or the school’s dependence on evidence rather than omniscience.

The plot advances by discovering which weakness can be converted into leverage.

## 4.3 “Unexpected witness” expands the meaning of ability

The search for a witness introduces Sakura, Ichinose, class-transfer rules, extracurricular point rewards, teacher incentives, and the possibility that class placement does not simply mirror descending ability.

What appears to be an evidentiary chapter becomes a broad institutional-information chapter.

## 4.4 “Respective motives” refuses a single ethical model

Horikita, Kushida, Ayanokōji, Ichinose, Kanzaki, Sakura, and Sudō all have different reasons to continue.

- Horikita cares about class damage and whether Sudō can become useful.
- Kushida treats friendship as a reason to support and confront.
- Ayanokōji mixes friendship, curiosity, sympathy, and strategic interest.
- Ichinose objects to rule-external malicious lying as a threat to the school’s competitive order.
- Kanzaki supports that norm but remains more willing to accept compromise.
- Sakura wants to act without being consumed by the visibility action requires.

The volume’s ethical structure is therefore plural rather than protagonist-centered.

## 4.5 “Truth and lies” makes the hearing fail on purpose

The hearing establishes that the school can evaluate evidence but cannot access certainty. Sakura’s testimony matters enough to alter the balance but not enough to produce complete exoneration.

The procedural compromise offered afterward is rational precisely because no side can prove its entire claim.

Horikita’s refusal of compromise therefore represents a deliberate move **outside the existing evidentiary game**.

## 4.6 “The one and only solution” is not one solution

The final chapter resolves two different visibility crises:

- Sudō’s institutional case;
- Sakura’s private confrontation with her stalker.

Both are solved by producing feared evidence and controlling the social story around it.

In the first case, the decisive evidence is false as an institutional fact: the cameras were not school cameras and hold no footage of the original fight.

In the second, the evidence is real: Ayanokōji photographs the stalker’s physical aggression.

The parallel matters. The same strategic mind can protect someone through actual documentation or manipulate someone through fabricated documentation. The ethical difference lies not in technique alone, but in target, truth status, alternatives, and consequences.

# 5. The Sudō case: event truth, reputation, and institutional reality

## 5.1 What happened is comparatively clear

The prologue gives Sakura direct visual access to the aftermath of a one-sided confrontation. Sudō has beaten three Class C students badly. Later evidence and the conspirators’ own behavior establish that they deliberately provoked and initiated the encounter as part of a setup.

The important distinction is:

> Sudō is **factually innocent of initiating this specific attack** while remaining deeply responsible for the reputation and behavioral history that make the false accusation plausible.

Those propositions do not cancel each other.

## 5.2 Reputation is not proof, but it changes the prior probability of belief

Ayanokōji recognizes the problem quickly. When direct evidence is scarce, institutions and ordinary people rely more heavily on whatever information remains available.

For Sudō, that includes:

- repeated threats;
- readiness to fight;
- hostility toward classmates;
- contemptuous language;
- his overwhelming physical advantage;
- and a pattern of conflict.

This creates **reputational debt**.

A person cannot reasonably demand that every new claim be evaluated as though nobody knows how he habitually behaves. Yet using character history as substitute proof is also dangerous: once someone is socially classified as violent, every ambiguous event can reinforce the classification.

Volume 2 does not resolve that tension. It dramatizes it.

## 5.3 Horikita distinguishes incident innocence from causal responsibility

Horikita’s initial refusal to rescue Sudō is among the book’s strongest arguments.

Her position is not that Class C was justified. It is that Sudō repeatedly creates social conditions in which violence becomes likely. Saving him from the current false allegation without forcing him to confront his own contribution risks merely resetting the cycle.

That is a meaningful causal argument.

It is also ethically dangerous when turned into pedagogy through abandonment. Horikita is prepared to let an unjust institutional punishment occur because she believes the larger lesson may improve him.

Kushida resists precisely this move: if a friend has a serious flaw, tell him and remain involved rather than converting suffering into the teacher.

Neither position is sufficient by itself.

## 5.4 Basketball changes Sudō’s institutional legibility

One of the volume’s sharpest moments comes when Chabashira reveals that extracurricular achievement can produce points. Sudō’s basketball skill ceases to be merely a private aspiration and becomes something the school may convert into institutional value.

Ayanokōji immediately asks Horikita whether Sudō now has greater value to save.

Her reaction exposes an important limit in her early meritocracy. She can recognize ability more readily when the institution gives that ability a currency conversion.

This is not hypocrisy. It is a problem of legibility.

Sudō’s athletic talent existed before Horikita learned the rule. What changed was not his body but the **institutional translation function** attached to it.

The scene therefore anticipates a central Year 1 distinction:

> **possessed ability and institutionally usable ability are not the same thing.**

## 5.5 Sudō’s final development is deliberately non-magical

After the complaint is withdrawn, Chabashira refuses to let Sudō interpret survival as vindication of his entire conduct. She tells him that his everyday behavior created the conditions for the crisis.

Sudō then recognizes something new: his actions are no longer purely private because he is a member of a class whose points, reputation, and opportunities can be damaged by him.

This is not a complete reform.

The text explicitly rejects overnight transformation. Chabashira predicts future trouble; Horikita agrees. What Horikita adds is more important:

> today, Sudō made one piece of progress; tomorrow’s Sudō can therefore be better than today’s.

This is the first clear breach in her earlier static present-value model.

But the second pass should not overstate it. Earlier in the same volume she says that future potential is irrelevant if someone is not useful for the immediate Class A project. She has not become a general philosopher of developmental inclusion. She has begun to recognize **trajectory as an additional form of value**.

That is a smaller and more credible change.

# 6. Sakura Airi: masks, chosen visibility, and the right to be believed

## 6.1 The prologue rejects a simple authentic/false binary

Sakura’s opening is the conceptual center of the volume.

She cannot comfortably:

- maintain eye contact;
- inhabit crowds;
- approach people;
- or sustain ordinary face-to-face connection.

Yet she knows she cannot truly live without others. Her solution is paradoxical: wear a false mask and hide the “real” self. Only then can she become herself.

That formulation makes it a mistake to say that the story simply wants Sakura to “take off the mask.”

Her performed identity can be alienating, but it also grants access to capacities that ordinary school Sakura cannot exercise. As Shizuku she can:

- pose confidently;
- smile into a camera;
- maintain a public page;
- answer fan comments;
- attract thousands of followers;
- and experience controlled recognition.

The performance is constructed. The desires it expresses are not therefore unreal.

A better formulation is:

> **Sakura’s mask is a technology for making otherwise inaccessible parts of herself livable.**

## 6.2 Kushida correctly complicates Ayanokōji’s “real face” question

Ayanokōji initially wonders whether the glamorous Shizuku might be Sakura’s hidden true self.

Kushida’s answer is more sophisticated. Makeup, clothing, self-suggestion, and the act of entering a role can produce another mode of personality without making either mode the singular true core.

This matters because Kushida herself is a specialist in socially authored identity.

The volume therefore puts three forms of masking beside one another:

- Sakura enlarges herself through a mediated persona;
- Kushida maintains expansive social warmth while concealing hostility;
- Ayanokōji reduces himself through ordinariness and under-attribution.

A mask can conceal, enable, defend, attract, or control. “Mask” is a formal category, not a moral verdict.

## 6.3 Photography gives Sakura authorship over the gaze

Sakura’s relationship with cameras begins in self-authorship.

The camera allows her to decide:

- angle;
- expression;
- timing;
- styling;
- audience;
- and which version of herself will circulate.

This is radically different from the stalker’s gaze. He treats visibility as entitlement: because he has seen Shizuku, admired Shizuku, and found Sakura, he imagines a reciprocal intimacy that does not exist.

The conflict is therefore not simply private versus public identity.

It is:

> **Who controls the terms under which Sakura becomes visible?**

## 6.4 The witness problem exposes the cost of public existence

Sakura’s photographs prove something important but limited: she was present.

They do not capture the first attack.

To convert her knowledge into institutional evidence she must testify. That act forces her into exactly the form of exposure she has organized her life to avoid.

Ayanokōji’s first response is ethically important: `無理しなくていい / You do not have to force yourself.` He does not initially treat the class’s need as sufficient reason to conscript her.

This is one of the clearest autonomy-respecting acts in the first two volumes.

Later, when she chooses to testify, he again reframes the motive. He tells her to discard Sudō, Kushida, and the class as the primary reasons. She should speak because **she herself** wants to tell the truth she witnessed.

The advice places self-authorship before collective utility.

## 6.5 Ayanokōji’s autobiographical empathy

The narration becomes unusually revealing when Ayanokōji explains why someone reaching out matters.

He says, in effect, that he once desperately wanted someone to recognize the pain of fighting alone.

The volume gives no further explanation and this document will not import one.

What matters locally is that his empathy is not merely deductive. Sakura activates a remembered emotional structure:

- isolation;
- pain witnessed by nobody;
- desire for recognition;
- and the value of another person voluntarily saying, “I see that you are struggling.”

That is strong evidence against readings that treat all early relational concern as performance.

## 6.6 “I believe Sakura” is not an evidentiary claim

After Manabu explains why Sakura’s evidence cannot fully establish the case, Ayanokōji answers that he believes her.

Manabu interprets this as class interest: naturally a Class D student wants to believe another Class D student.

Ayanokōji corrects the grammar. He is not saying “I want to believe.” He is saying “I believe.”

This is not proof that Sakura is truthful. The reader independently knows she is.

It is evidence about Ayanokōji’s chosen relationship to her. He is making a **personal commitment of trust** in a context where the institution cannot certify her.

That distinction is one of the volume’s most humane counterpoints to institutional epistemology:

> **proof determines what an institution may responsibly enforce; trust determines what one person may choose to risk in relation to another.**

The two should not be confused, but neither is reducible to the other.

## 6.7 “For myself” complicates his care rather than cancelling it

Ayanokōji later promises to help Sakura again if she is in trouble. The narration says he says the words `オレ自身のために / for my own sake`.

The strongest reading is not that the promise is secretly selfish and therefore false.

He is attempting to become someone for whom friendship is a real relation. Saying the words for himself means that the act helps author **his own desired self** as well as comfort Sakura.

This creates an important parallel:

- Sakura experiments with a self she can inhabit through performed visibility;
- Ayanokōji experiments with a self who can say and mean the language of friendship.

Both are learning that identity can be partly constructed without being unreal.

## 6.8 The stalker confrontation: courage before rescue

The stalker subplot should not be reduced to Ayanokōji saving a vulnerable girl.

Sakura first makes the difficult decision herself. She takes the large bundle of letters, confronts the man, explicitly tells him the contact is unwanted, and attempts to terminate the relationship he has imagined.

The physical danger escalates because he refuses her autonomy.

Only after he grabs her and pushes her against the shutter do Ayanokōji and Ichinose intervene.

That sequence matters. Sakura’s development precedes the rescue.

## 6.9 Ayanokōji’s rescue is effective and ethically imperfect

Ayanokōji reaches the scene through the school phone’s location-sharing function. He then waits while listening and observing because he wants a more decisive evidentiary moment; Ichinose pulls at his sleeve when the physical aggression becomes intolerable.

The method is characteristic:

- locate the endangered person through surveillance-capable infrastructure;
- gather evidence;
- appear only when intervention produces maximum leverage;
- photograph the assault;
- construct a threatening public narrative;
- and create an escape path so the offender can flee rather than fight.

The result is protective.

The delay remains ethically relevant. Ayanokōji’s strategic instinct continues operating during another person’s danger. He weighs the value of stronger proof against immediate intervention.

The scene is therefore neither evidence of cold indifference nor uncomplicated heroic rescue. It is an early example of **care filtered through strategic optimization**.

## 6.10 Sakura’s final movement is small enough to be believable

After the crisis she initiates a morning greeting.

She does not become extroverted. She still struggles with eye contact and still considers how much of Shizuku to reveal.

The important movement is from:

> visibility as something done **to** her

or

> visibility possible only through a fully mediated persona

into:

> **small acts of chosen direct presence.**

Her growth is not the destruction of her masks. It is increased control over when she needs them.

# 7. Ayanokōji Kiyotaka: ordinary desire, relational recognition, and covert authorship

## 7.1 The ordinary adolescent layer is not camouflage

Volume 2 contains a large amount of low-stakes interior narration that has no reason to exist as performance for other characters.

Ayanokōji worries about being a low-ranked friend. He notices that having some friends can feel worse than having none because exclusion becomes interpretable. He is delighted when Hirata invites him somewhere. He notices Kushida’s attractiveness repeatedly, reacts physically to Ichinose’s close distance and scent, feels excitement about phone contacts with girls, and thinks in recognizable adolescent-romantic categories.

These passages are not strategically useful to him.

They establish a baseline:

> **Ayanokōji does not lack ordinary desire. He lacks an ordinary developmental history for integrating desire, trust, vulnerability, and action.**

## 7.2 Horikita has become a low-performance relationship

Horikita notices that he has become slightly more talkative.

Ayanokōji says that around her he can speak closer to his `素に近い自分 / self near his unguarded state`. The key reason is not romantic excitement but the absence of pressure. Silence between them does not become awkward.

This is a meaningful preference.

Ayanokōji appears to value relationships in which he does not have to continuously perform social fluency.

At the Volume 2 boundary, the text supports:

- habitual proximity;
- intellectual familiarity;
- mutual observation;
- comfort with silence;
- growing private access;
- and a shared tendency to hide vulnerability.

It does **not** require a romantic interpretation.

## 7.3 His ordinary privacy ethic is stronger than his covert operational ethic

When classmates copy his dorm-room key without permission, Ayanokōji internally objects that friendship does not permit people to do anything they want to one another.

This is a simple line with large analytical value.

He possesses an intuitive ordinary-life principle:

> **relationship does not erase boundaries.**

He similarly tells Sakura that witness participation cannot simply be demanded from her because the class would benefit.

Yet he does not apply the same transparency standard to his strategic guidance of Horikita. He is willing to manipulate what she notices while concealing his authorship.

That contradiction should be preserved rather than solved too quickly.

It may indicate that Ayanokōji understands autonomy more readily in **personal boundary situations** than in **developmental or strategic situations**, where he thinks in terms of outcomes and system design.

## 7.4 He does not simply solve Horikita’s problem; he authors the discovery path

The end of the volume gives us unusually strong evidence because Horikita reconstructs his sequence explicitly.

She identifies three interventions:

1. he first puts surveillance cameras into the conceptual field;
2. he brings her to the special-building corridor so she notices the absence of cameras;
3. he introduces the idea that a lie can obtain the force of truth when the evidentiary stalemate seems complete.

Ayanokōji denies intentionality, but his later narration concedes that he moved too much and needs to reduce the visibility of his thinking.

The denial is therefore not persuasive as literal explanation.

The important question is what kind of authorship this is.

He does **not**:

- issue an order;
- dictate the final plan;
- force Horikita to use it;
- or publicly take credit.

He instead controls the information sequence so that Horikita can generate the solution herself.

That makes the intervention simultaneously:

- pedagogical;
- manipulative;
- empowering;
- self-concealing.

The categories are not mutually exclusive.

## 7.5 Horikita’s agency remains real

The second pass should resist giving Ayanokōji total authorship merely because he planted the cues.

Horikita:

- recognizes the synthesis;
- leaves the hearing to verify feasibility;
- determines what equipment is required;
- explains the complete plan to Ichinose and Kanzaki;
- persuades them despite the moral discomfort;
- accepts a debt of points to Class B;
- delegates technical implementation;
- chooses to reject the compromise;
- and privately accepts responsibility if the gamble worsens Sudō’s punishment.

This is not puppet theater.

Ayanokōji increases the probability that she will arrive at a particular class of solution. Horikita still has to transform possibility into political action.

The best phrase for the relationship here is:

> **seeded discovery, autonomous operationalization.**

## 7.6 His strategic method is increasingly environmental

The final bluff works because of environmental design, not merely verbal cleverness.

The Class C students are placed in conditions of:

- heat;
- urgency;
- guilty knowledge;
- incomplete information;
- visible technical props;
- peer disagreement;
- fear of expulsion;
- and restricted access to outside advice.

Ayanokōji and Ichinose do not need to prove the camera story. They need only make verification impossible before the targets must choose.

This is a crucial conceptual development.

> **The objective is not to make the lie certainly believed. It is to make disbelief too risky.**

That is a more general form of power than deception.

## 7.7 Friendship is a stated motive, not merely an external interpretation

When Horikita presses him on why a supposed conflict-avoider keeps helping, Ayanokōji says perhaps he wanted to help the first friends he made.

The qualifier `多分 / probably` preserves ambiguity. But it should not be dismissed merely because he is hard to read.

Volume 2 repeatedly gives relational evidence consistent with the claim:

- he worries about friendship rank;
- he values being selected;
- he helps Sakura without class necessity alone;
- he says he believes her;
- he speaks of friendship partly for his own sake;
- and he experiences Horikita as unusually comfortable company.

The strategic and relational explanations can both be true.

## 7.8 He is afraid of his own engagement

The ending is one of the volume’s strongest character passages.

Ayanokōji begins analyzing:

- the likely Ryūen threat;
- Ichinose and Kanzaki;
- the unknown strength of Class A;
- and the near-impossibility of Horikita reaching Class A in her current form.

He then catches himself becoming emotionally engaged in the competitive problem.

His reaction is not triumph but recoil. He reminds himself that he chose this school because he did not want to live this way. He wants a quiet, ordinary life. He says he knows how defective, foolish, and frightening he can be.

At this boundary, the text does not tell us what precisely he fears about himself.

It does establish a central internal conflict:

> **the difficult system attracts the part of him that he came to school hoping not to need.**

That makes “peaceful school life” more than a cover story. It is a normative preference struggling against an ingrained or intrinsic attraction to mastery.

# 8. Horikita Suzune: from correct judgment to responsible leadership

## 8.1 She begins from a coherent but narrow position

Horikita’s refusal to save Sudō should not be flattened into cruelty.

She has a real theory:

- his repeated aggression creates foreseeable crises;
- rescuing him without change encourages repetition;
- Class D cannot rise if it continually absorbs the costs of his conduct;
- and consequences may be the only language he takes seriously.

The weakness lies in assuming she may choose unjust punishment as a developmental tool for another person.

That is already a version of the Year 1 control problem.

## 8.2 She can read people when she chooses to look

Horikita identifies Sakura as the likely witness from a subtle behavioral reaction.

This qualifies the idea that she is simply poor at understanding humans.

She is capable of acute observation. Her difficulty is more specifically relational and normative:

- she often observes in order to classify;
- she underweights emotional cost;
- and she has difficulty translating recognition into a form another person can accept.

In other words, the missing ability is not perception alone. It is **socially usable perception**.

## 8.3 Manabu exposes the difference between competence and autonomy

At the student-council hearing, Horikita freezes when she realizes Manabu is presiding.

The scene matters because her normal polished control collapses not under an intellectual challenge but under one person’s gaze.

Ayanokōji physically snaps her out of paralysis, after which she resumes competent questioning.

This confirms that her apparent self-sufficiency remains organized around a relationship she cannot yet emotionally govern.

## 8.4 She initially fails inside the formal evidentiary system

Horikita asks good questions:

- why would a supposedly attacked group arrange the meeting this way?
- why does a capable fighter like Ishizaki appear to have offered no meaningful resistance?
- why does the physical pattern look so asymmetric?

These questions expose implausibility.

They do not create proof.

The hearing therefore teaches Horikita that being logically persuasive is not identical to meeting the institution’s evidentiary threshold.

This is an important leadership lesson. A leader does not merely need the better explanation; she needs a solution that survives the actual decision procedure.

## 8.5 Rejecting compromise is her first major assumption of political risk

The offered compromise is rational. It reduces Sudō’s punishment and avoids the possibility of total defeat.

Horikita rejects it after recognizing the alternative route.

Before the final adjudication she privately admits that if Sudō receives a heavier penalty, responsibility will be hers.

That is a meaningful change from Volume 1.

Leadership is beginning to mean not only judging other people’s deficiencies, but **owning the downside of decisions made for the group**.

## 8.6 The fake-camera plan violates one of her own preferred norms

Horikita’s plan is explicitly a lie. Ichinose and Kanzaki recognize that it conflicts with their moral preference for rule-bound competition and truthfulness.

Horikita’s justification is consequential:

> a lie began the incident; only a lie may be able to end it without unjust punishment.

This is not moral relativism. She sees the method as exceptional and uncomfortable.

Still, it marks an important development: she is learning that institutional strategy may require operating through the weaknesses of the actual system rather than demanding that the system become ideal first.

## 8.7 Her POV reveals internal responsibility concealed by her public register

The late shift into Horikita’s internal perspective is formally valuable.

We learn directly that she:

- fears having made the wrong choice;
- anticipates blame if the gamble fails;
- experiences relief when the plan works;
- recognizes Ayanokōji’s importance reluctantly;
- and begins questioning whether his “conflict avoidance” description is coherent.

The external Horikita sounds categorical. The internal Horikita is increasingly capable of uncertainty.

That difference is itself development.

## 8.8 Sudō’s `進歩` introduces trajectory into her value system

Her final statement to Sudō is one of the volume’s most important changes.

She does not say he is now good, safe, or reliable.

She says that today he noticed one of his mistakes, and therefore tomorrow’s version of him can be better.

This is a more developmental vocabulary than the one she began with.

But it remains an opening rather than a completed conversion. She is still intensely outcome-oriented and still evaluates people by their usefulness to the Class A project.

The second-pass conclusion is therefore:

> **Volume 2 does not turn Horikita from static meritocrat into developmental humanist. It teaches her that trajectory is evidence too.**

# 9. Kushida Kikyō: social labor, boundaries, and unstable authenticity

## 9.1 Her public self remains socially real

Volume 1 already complicated any reading of Kushida as “fake kindness covering the real hateful person.” Volume 2 strengthens that correction.

Her public competence repeatedly produces benefits that do not vanish merely because the performance requires effort.

She:

- mobilizes classmates who would otherwise abandon Sudō;
- maintains relationships across classes and years;
- gives emotionally legible form to other people’s concerns;
- helps identify and approach Sakura;
- creates a low-pressure social environment on the Sunday outing;
- and calibrates how quickly to ask Sakura for greater familiarity.

Ayanokōji notices the last point explicitly. Kushida encourages Sakura to drop formal speech, but when Sakura struggles, Kushida does not keep pushing. She understands how much invitation the other person can currently absorb.

That is genuine social ability.

## 9.2 Kushida and Horikita offer different theories of helping

Their disagreement over Sudō clarifies both characters.

Horikita believes consequences may be necessary to force self-recognition.

Kushida believes a friend should:

- remain involved;
- tell the person what is wrong;
- and help them change rather than strategically abandon them.

Kushida’s position is more immediately humane.

It is not necessarily free from self-interest. Being the person who can connect, mediate, and help is central to her social identity. But self-investment does not make the care fictional.

The volume repeatedly resists the notion that motive must be pure before an action can be sincerely prosocial.

## 9.3 She has unusual authority on the problem of masks

Kushida’s interpretation of Shizuku matters because it comes from someone who understands how behavior changes under an authored persona.

She does not need to declare one Sakura “true” and the other “false.” A role can create real behavior.

That insight indirectly applies to Kushida herself.

Her warm public register is constructed and effortful, yet its effects are real. Her private hostility is also real. Neither automatically invalidates the other.

## 9.4 Ayanokōji’s relationship to Kushida is becoming ordinary

He describes conversation with her as increasingly comfortable. He accepts that she may dislike him internally and says that ordinary liking and disliking are natural.

This is a surprisingly tolerant position.

He is not demanding moral purification as the price of continued acquaintance. At the same time, he remains strongly attracted to her and susceptible to the social force of her proximity and warmth.

At this point the relationship supports:

- friendship;
- attraction;
- curiosity;
- guarded trust;
- knowledge of a dangerous private side.

It does not support the conclusion that he understands her.

## 9.5 Her unresolved late-volume request preserves rather than solves the mystery

Kushida’s private tone can change sharply, and the volume ends without establishing what she ultimately wants from Ayanokōji.

The correct local conclusion is therefore not “Kushida’s true self is X.”

It is:

> **Kushida possesses several socially functional modes whose relationship to one another remains unresolved.**

That uncertainty is part of the character, not a temporary information gap to fill from later volumes here.

# 10. Ichinose Honami and Kanzaki Ryūji: early Class B constitutionalism

## 10.1 Ichinose’s kindness is already institutional

Ichinose’s importance in Volume 2 is not that she is nicer than Horikita.

She displays a larger-scale capacity to turn social trust into organization.

We learn that Class B has created internal roles such as class representative, deputy, and secretary even though the school did not command it. Ichinose also handles dormitory concerns, uses public and digital information channels, coordinates classmates, offers point rewards for useful information, and moves comfortably among students outside her own class.

That is an early model of **self-created governance**.

Class B’s cohesion is not merely a personality aggregate. It has infrastructure.

## 10.2 She gives a principled reason to help an opponent

Ichinose explicitly rejects the idea that the incident is “Class D’s problem.”

Her argument has two layers.

First, she personally dislikes allowing malicious lying to determine someone’s fate.

Second, she recognizes a systemic threat: if class competition makes false accusations outside the accepted competitive rules a viable weapon, every class becomes less safe.

Her solidarity is therefore not simple sentimentality.

It is partially constitutional:

> **competition requires some limits on what competitors may legitimately do to one another.**

## 10.3 Kanzaki makes the norm explicit

Kanzaki says that competition under rules is welcome; the current attack occurs outside those rules and should not be accepted.

This is an early indication that Class B’s identity includes a distinction between:

- adversarial competition;
- and destructive bad-faith conduct that undermines the possibility of competition itself.

He is also more cautious than Ichinose. He sees the appeal of accepting a compromise once the hearing reaches evidentiary stalemate.

That difference is healthy at this stage. Ichinose is not surrounded only by affirmation.

## 10.4 Their partnership models initiative plus internal resistance

Ichinose brings:

- social reach;
- resource commitment;
- optimism;
- persuasive energy;
- improvisational execution.

Kanzaki brings:

- skepticism;
- procedural seriousness;
- willingness to voice risk;
- and less attachment to dramatic total victory.

The pair therefore offers a contrast with early Class D, where Horikita often has no legitimate internal partner capable of challenging her reasoning from a position of trust.

## 10.5 The unexplained point balance is an epistemic clue, not yet a conclusion

Ayanokōji glimpses part of Ichinose’s private-point screen and is shocked by what he sees. The text deliberately withholds the exact explanatory mechanism from the reader and does not give us a legitimate basis for solving it.

The correct evidence status is `UA / unresolved ambiguity`.

What can be said is narrower:

- Ichinose has access to an unexpectedly large private-point balance or arrangement;
- she knows how to move points through school systems;
- and Ayanokōji begins treating her as a larger strategic obstacle than her friendly presentation initially suggested.

The mystery is important precisely because Volume 2 does **not** explain it.

## 10.6 The fake-camera plan compromises their preferred ethics

Ichinose and Kanzaki both recognize that Horikita’s plan depends on a lie.

Ichinose is visibly uncomfortable. Kanzaki says it may conflict with her moral rules.

They still cooperate because the deception is being used to prevent a malicious falsehood from successfully weaponizing the school’s disciplinary system.

That decision should not be simplified into “lying is acceptable when the good guys do it.”

The more interesting point is that Class B already has a norm strong enough that violating it requires acknowledgment and justification.

The plan therefore creates a debt in two senses:

- Class D literally borrows points;
- Horikita also asks Class B to spend some of its moral comfort on her behalf.

# 11. Hirata, Kei, and the social infrastructure of Class D

## 11.1 Hirata’s moral authority is a real form of ability

Hirata states a clear priority: classmates should be helped, and he is willing to bear personal costs for that principle.

He also physically places himself between Sudō and Kōenji when conflict threatens.

The relevant ability is not strategic brilliance.

It is legitimacy.

People listen to Hirata because he has accumulated credibility through reliable social conduct. In a volume obsessed with credibility, that is not secondary power.

## 11.2 Class D’s female social structure is already differentiated

The narration distinguishes Kushida’s broad, soft, cross-group popularity from Kei’s harder status influence among girls.

Neither has Horikita’s academic profile.

Yet the class would be substantially less governable without them.

This strengthens the Year 1 argument that institutional or academic rankings miss forms of ability that determine whether a community can actually act.

## 11.3 Ike and Yamauchi expose the volume’s uncomfortable continuum of looking

The boys’ sexualized commentary and location-based attempts to manufacture encounters with Kushida are largely played for comedy.

The volume later condemns the electronics-store employee’s predatory surveillance much more seriously.

The behaviors are not morally identical. The stalker escalates to repeated unwanted correspondence, violation of Sakura’s private space, physical restraint, and threatened sexual violence.

Still, the juxtaposition matters.

The book itself provides the conceptual vocabulary to ask where ordinary objectifying attention becomes entitlement.

Its comedy does not always interrogate that boundary as sharply as the stalking plot does.

# 12. The school as an economy of information, points, and credibility

## 12.1 Class points remain dynamic but no longer carry invisible debt from April

Class D receives 87 class points after the midterm-period adjustment. Hirata correctly notices that the class’s earlier violations are not continuing as an invisible negative balance that automatically consumes all future gains.

This is an important institutional clarification.

The school can punish harshly without making recovery mathematically impossible.

## 12.2 Private points are now explicitly political currency

The school’s rule that things can in principle be bought with points becomes more concrete.

A student can theoretically purchase transfer to a desired class for **20,000,000 private points**.

No student is known to have completed this. A prior student accumulated twelve million through large-scale fraud against inexperienced first-years and was expelled before reaching the threshold.

This tells us several things.

First, the existence of a legal price does not mean ordinary access is equal.

Second, the point system creates incentives for entrepreneurial discovery and predation at the same time.

Third, the school distinguishes exploitation of legitimate rules from conduct it classifies as rule-breaking, but it does not eliminate the incentive to search aggressively for loopholes.

## 12.3 Information asymmetry exists between teachers as well as students

Ichinose knows that:

- extracurricular results can produce points;
- charitable or public activities may be evaluated;
- and teacher incentives are tied to class success.

Class D had not been told all of this.

Chabashira’s claim that she “forgot” does not erase the effect.

A competition among students is also partly a competition among information environments created by adults.

This weakens any simplistic claim that class outcomes represent pure student merit.

## 12.4 The school prices procedure itself

When Class C withdraws the complaint, Manabu notes that the withdrawal requires payment of administrative costs in points.

Even procedural reversal has an exchange value.

The point system therefore reaches into:

- consumption;
- academic survival;
- information;
- class mobility;
- extracurricular reward;
- and dispute administration.

The school increasingly appears as a political economy in which many qualitatively different things can be translated into one numerical medium.

## 12.5 Credibility is structurally contaminated by class competition

The disciplinary process makes a reasonable assumption: a witness from Sudō’s own class has an incentive to protect him.

But the assumption creates a perverse consequence.

Because class members share point consequences, **solidarity automatically devalues testimony**.

The school creates collective incentives and then treats those incentives as grounds for distrusting collective support.

This is not necessarily avoidable in adjudication. It is still an institutional tension produced by the school’s own design.

## 12.6 The school may treat crises as educational environments—but the final bluff remains an inference

Ichinose and Ayanokōji tell the Class C students that the school is deliberately waiting to see whether students can solve the conflict themselves.

This is part of the bluff.

The broader school pattern makes the theory plausible, but Volume 2 does not establish that the administration actually knew Class C was lying or had deliberately structured the delay as a hidden examination.

The local evidence status should therefore remain `SI / strong inference` at most for the general institutional tendency and `TF / false operational claim` for the specific assertion that the school had camera proof.

That distinction matters.

# 13. Truth, proof, credibility, and the authored record

## 13.1 The hearing gives the volume its most exact vocabulary

Manabu’s explanation to Sakura should govern the epistemic analysis.

Her testimony and photographs have enough `証拠能力` to be introduced as evidence.

Their `証明力` is weaker because:

- she is a Class D student;
- the photographs establish presence, not initiation;
- and the timing of the disclosure creates an interested-party problem.

The school therefore does not say, “Sakura is definitely lying.”

It says, in effect, “this evidence is insufficient to establish the complete proposition at the required level of confidence.”

Sakura experiences that distinction as devastating because, subjectively, she knows she told the truth.

The institutional and personal meanings of “believed” are not identical.

## 13.2 Visible injury is powerful but underdetermined evidence

Class C has the strongest obvious material fact: three injured bodies.

The injuries prove that Sudō hit them.

They do not prove who initiated violence.

This is the volume’s clearest example of a broader epistemic rule:

> **evidence may be genuine while the narrative built from it is false.**

The solution to misinformation is therefore not simply “find real facts.” Real facts are already present on both sides.

The problem is causal interpretation.

## 13.3 Reputation supplies causal plausibility

Sudō’s reputation makes one causal story more believable before adjudication begins.

The institution is not irrational for considering it. It would be irrational to treat prior behavior as conclusive proof.

Volume 2 lives in that middle space, where credibility is necessary for social life and dangerous for justice.

## 13.4 The fake cameras weaponize the gap between truth and proof

The conspirators know the event truth.

They also know the school lacks proof.

Horikita’s plan changes only the second belief.

The cameras do not make the truth more true. They make the targets believe that the truth has become **institutionally retrievable**.

This is why the bluff works only against guilty actors. Their private knowledge supplies the emotional force that the fake evidence itself lacks.

## 13.5 The result is not formal vindication

The complaint is withdrawn.

Sudō avoids suspension and formal problem-student treatment.

But the school never issues a definitive ruling saying that Class C initiated the assault.

The narrative therefore ends with a pragmatic restoration rather than epistemic purification.

That is important for the series’ politics.

Ayanokōji and Horikita learn that it may be easier to change the **decision context** than to make the institution discover the truth.

# 14. Surveillance and the politics of the gaze

Volume 2 contains enough forms of looking that surveillance should be treated as one of its organizing motifs.

## 14.1 Institutional surveillance

Classroom cameras contribute to behavioral evaluation. Students know some spaces are watched, but not every corridor is visibly covered.

Ayanokōji studies these gaps.

## 14.2 Peer surveillance

The school phone’s location-sharing functions can reveal friends’ positions when permissions are enabled. Ike uses this socially intrusive capacity to manufacture “chance” encounters with Kushida.

Ayanokōji later uses the same infrastructure to find Sakura during an emergency.

The moral difference lies in purpose and urgency, but both acts reveal that the school’s communication platform makes location legible among peers.

## 14.3 Predatory surveillance

The stalker collapses fandom into possession. He tracks Sakura’s public identity into her private life, sends repeated letters to her dormitory, and interprets his own intensity as evidence of a mutual bond.

His language is a pathological version of the volume’s credibility theme:

> he treats the strength of his own belief as proof that the relationship exists.

## 14.4 Self-authored visibility

Sakura’s Shizuku photographs are the countermodel.

She chooses the image.

This is not freedom from being seen. It is a claim to **jurisdiction over the form of being seen**.

## 14.5 Protective documentation

At the stalker scene, Ayanokōji creates real photographic evidence of physical aggression.

The camera becomes protective because it changes what the offender believes can be publicly proven.

## 14.6 Fabricated institutional gaze

The fake network cameras are the most abstract use of surveillance.

Their power does not depend on recording anything.

It depends on being recognized as the eye of an institution that might have been watching already.

The volume thus progresses through:

> **being watched → choosing to be seen → fearing unwanted observation → documenting harm → fabricating the appearance of observation.**

The recurring proposition is:

> **Visibility is power only when someone controls its terms.**

# 15. The fake-camera operation as environmental authorship

## 15.1 Formal objective

The formal problem is to prevent the school from imposing meaningful punishment on Sudō without possessing evidence strong enough to establish full innocence.

## 15.2 Strategic environment

The operation depends on several asymmetries:

- Class C knows it lied;
- Class D knows that Class C knows it lied;
- the conspirators do not know what surveillance infrastructure the school may possess;
- they do not have time to investigate calmly;
- they fear expulsion more than they value continuing the attack;
- and at least two of the three are less committed than Ishizaki.

## 15.3 Material resources

The plan requires:

- Class B private points;
- purchasable network cameras;
- technical help from Sotomura;
- physical installation;
- a plausible school-security story;
- and allies willing to participate in a lie.

This is an important reminder that “genius” alone does not execute the outcome. Strategy consumes social, economic, and technical infrastructure.

## 15.4 Psychological pressure

Ayanokōji’s narration is explicit that heat and cognitive load matter. The targets are kept in an uncomfortable corridor and denied time to verify the story.

Ichinose then progressively closes escape routes:

- the cameras are supposedly special-building security exceptions;
- there are two, making student installation seem less plausible;
- their own admission that they checked for cameras becomes incriminating;
- school delay is reframed as intentional testing;
- and camera review is associated with likely expulsion for coordinated lying.

The bluff does not need absolute credibility.

It needs sufficient credibility under a catastrophic downside.

## 15.5 The decisive choice is complaint withdrawal

Ayanokōji offers a face-saving equilibrium:

- Class C withdraws;
- Class D does not demand camera review;
- no one receives the maximum institutional penalty;
- and both sides can stop before the hidden truth is formally adjudicated.

The solution is coercive in the technical sense that it intentionally creates overwhelming pressure.

It is also less destructive than the threatened alternative.

## 15.6 Ethical assessment

### Strongest defense

- Class C initiated the scheme and knowingly lied.
- The ordinary hearing could not adequately protect Sudō from a false allegation.
- The bluff creates no fabricated footage and does not falsely accuse an innocent party of a new act.
- It gives the conspirators a route away from likely expulsion.
- It restores Sudō’s ability to continue basketball without forcing Sakura to carry the impossible burden of proving more than she witnessed.

### Strongest criticism

- The plan normalizes deception as a substitute for transparent adjudication.
- It manipulates the targets’ informational environment so they cannot make a fully informed decision.
- It relies on psychological pressure rather than accountable procedure.
- Ayanokōji conceals from Horikita how deliberately he shaped her path toward the plan.
- Success may reinforce a lesson that superior environmental control is preferable to institutional reform.

### Provisional verdict

The operation is **defensible as a proportionate bluff against knowingly malicious actors under a defective evidentiary situation**, but it is not ethically neutral.

Its deepest significance is developmental rather than legal:

> Ayanokōji and Horikita learn that human behavior can be redirected by changing perceived constraints rather than changing underlying truth.

That technique has much broader potential than this relatively bounded use.

# 16. Ryūen Kakeru and the arrival of student-authored adversarial environments

Ryūen is largely offstage, and the local analysis should respect that limited exposure.

What Volume 2 does establish is enough to change the scale of the series.

The setup against Sudō appears designed around known characteristics:

- he is physically dominant;
- he is easy to provoke;
- his reputation supports an aggression narrative;
- his basketball future gives the punishment unusually high leverage;
- and beating three students without being injured makes the visible aftermath favor the weaker attackers.

Whoever designed the scheme understands that a person’s **strength can be turned into evidence against him**.

That logic resembles the school’s hidden evaluations. The difference is purpose. The school claims educational or competitive goals; the student attacker uses environmental design as a weapon against a rival class.

The final appearance of the likely Ryūen therefore introduces a crucial Year 1 development:

> **students are not merely solving institutional tests; they are beginning to design tests for one another.**

At the Volume 2 boundary, the responsible interpretation should remain modest:

- he is connected to the operation strongly enough to be treated as its likely higher-level author;
- he is confident and deliberately provocative;
- he intends further direct conflict;
- his complete methods, motives, and leadership system remain unknown.

# 17. Ability and legibility after Volume 2

Volume 2 expands the Year 1 ability taxonomy significantly.

## 17.1 Possessed ability

Examples:

- Sudō’s basketball and fighting capacity;
- Sakura’s photographic skill and mediated self-presentation;
- Ayanokōji’s environmental reasoning;
- Ichinose’s organization;
- Kushida’s social fluency.

## 17.2 Displayed ability

Ayanokōji continues to suppress attribution. Sakura displays confidence only inside particular contexts. Horikita displays more strategic leadership once she chooses to own the camera plan.

## 17.3 Measured ability

The in-volume database inserts literalize the school’s classificatory gaze. They present students as profile objects with grades and standardized ability categories.

The narrative repeatedly demonstrates that such profiles are incomplete.

## 17.4 Socially usable ability

This becomes especially important.

- Kushida can mobilize people.
- Hirata can stabilize conflict.
- Kei carries social influence among girls.
- Ichinose can organize a class and external information search.
- Kanzaki can provide internal resistance.
- Sotomura can solve a technical implementation problem.

Ability that cannot enter collective action is strategically different from ability that can.

## 17.5 Developmental ability

Sakura’s morning greeting and Sudō’s new recognition of class responsibility are both intentionally small examples.

The text resists transformation fantasies. Progress can consist of one new capacity becoming possible.

## 17.6 Political ability

Ichinose is the clearest new case. Her influence crosses group boundaries because people recognize her as socially credible and organizationally capable.

Horikita’s political ability is beginning but remains fragile because her public legitimacy is not yet broad.

## 17.7 Moral ability

Volume 2 also suggests that judgment under uncertainty is an ability in its own right.

- Can a person distinguish incident guilt from broader causal responsibility?
- Can one compete without accepting false accusation as legitimate strategy?
- Can one use deception without losing the ability to name it as deception?
- Can one help without requiring another person to become useful first?

The school does not yet provide a metric for these capacities.

# 18. Relationship architecture

## 18.1 Ayanokōji–Horikita

**Public form:** neighboring classmates and reluctant collaborators.

**Private form:** increasingly habitual companionship, comfortable silence, room access, mutual observation, and strategic cooperation.

**Information asymmetry:** severe. Horikita knows he is suppressing ability but not why or how much.

**Leverage:** Horikita invokes his promise to help reach Class A; Ayanokōji controls information she does not know he is feeding her.

**Refusal capacity:** meaningful but imperfect. He repeatedly says he does not want Class A, but continues to help; she cannot actually compel his full ability.

**Volume 2 change:** Horikita stops treating his competence as incidental and starts explicitly investigating him.

**Unresolved question:** can their collaboration become more reciprocal if he continues to conceal the architecture of his interventions?

## 18.2 Ayanokōji–Sakura

**Public form:** barely connected classmates becoming friends.

**Private form:** trust formed through witness support, ordinary outing, privacy protection, and rescue.

**Information asymmetry:** Ayanokōji knows Shizuku and the stalking risk before Sakura knows how much he understands; Sakura knows little about him.

**Leverage:** relatively low in the relationship itself. He explicitly tells her she need not testify and seeks permission before disclosing Shizuku to Ichinose after the rescue.

**Volume 2 change:** Sakura hears someone call her a friend for the first time; Ayanokōji uses friendship language partly to construct his own desired relation to her.

**Possible romantic coding:** Sakura’s desire for one-on-one contact and interrupted attempts to say something may indicate attraction, but Volume 2 does not require a definitive romantic classification.

## 18.3 Ayanokōji–Kushida

**Public form:** friendly classmates.

**Private form:** attraction, comfort, partial knowledge of her hostile side, and mutual uncertainty.

**Volume 2 change:** Ayanokōji explicitly accepts that she may dislike him while still valuing their current interaction.

**Unresolved question:** what does Kushida ultimately want from him, and how stable is the division among her social modes?

## 18.4 Horikita–Sudō

**Public form:** frequent conflict; Sudō is increasingly attached to her judgment.

**Power asymmetry:** Horikita possesses evaluative authority in his eyes that she does not fully accept as a relationship.

**Volume 2 change:** she moves from willingness to let him suffer for the lesson toward active rescue plus explicit developmental judgment.

**Risk:** Sudō may interpret strategic preservation as personal devotion more quickly than Horikita intends.

## 18.5 Horikita–Manabu

**Public form:** student versus student-council president; siblings known to each other.

**Private structure:** Horikita’s competence remains vulnerable to his gaze.

**Volume 2 change:** no reconciliation; the hearing reveals how incomplete her emotional independence remains.

## 18.6 Ichinose–Kanzaki

**Public form:** Class B leadership pair.

**Private political function:** initiative plus internal skepticism.

**Volume 2 change:** they demonstrate that a cohesive class can contain disagreement without treating disagreement as disloyalty.

# 19. Japanese narration, voice, and register

## 19.1 Ayanokōji: comic adolescent voice beside strategic declaratives

The Japanese narration continues to undermine the stereotype of a uniformly flat protagonist.

His internal register moves easily among:

- self-deprecating social comedy;
- exaggerated worries about friendship rank;
- physical attraction;
- casual slang;
- analytical probability;
- legalistic reasoning;
- and terse strategic statements.

The contrast becomes strongest during the fake-camera operation. The same narrator who worries about girls and invitations can calmly reason about reducing another person’s capacity for logical and creative thought through heat, urgency, and cognitive load.

The important literary feature is not that one voice is “fake.”

It is that both registers belong to him.

## 19.2 Horikita: polished certainty with newly visible private uncertainty

Horikita’s outward speech remains complete, controlled, and often sharply feminine in sentence-final form. Her contempt tends to arrive as polished judgment rather than rough aggression.

The late internal POV reveals that the certainty of her public register does not imply absence of fear.

This is a valuable methodological warning:

> **voice presentation is characterization, but it is not transparent access to total interior state.**

## 19.3 Sakura: formality as defensive distance

Sakura’s hesitant keigo and difficulty dropping formal speech mark social distance rather than elite status.

Her growth is audible when she experiments with ordinary greetings and greater directness.

The voice change remains modest, fitting the volume’s rejection of instant personality transformation.

## 19.4 Kushida: cushioning and calibrated invitation

Kushida’s ordinary register is warm, responsive, and full of social cushioning. She makes requests feel optional and notices when another person needs pressure reduced.

Her sharper private modes therefore matter because they are register changes, not merely different semantic opinions.

## 19.5 Ichinose: colloquial warmth plus public-command fluency

Ichinose is notably capable of keeping a friendly, almost playful register while directing an adversarial confrontation.

During the camera bluff she can sound conversational and theatrical while systematically closing the targets’ options.

This combination helps explain her political effectiveness: authority does not require her to become linguistically severe.

## 19.6 Sudō: roughness begins to acquire self-reflection

Sudō’s rough masculine speech does not disappear when he admits fault.

That is important. Development is not represented by replacing his entire social register with polite speech.

The change lies in what he becomes capable of acknowledging inside the same voice.

# 20. Appearance, illustration, and embodied characterization

The visual resources support the prose’s concern with legibility rather than providing an independent hidden answer.

## 20.1 Sakura / Shizuku

The strongest visual contrast is between Sakura’s subdued school presentation and the gravure-idol image associated with Shizuku.

The contrast emphasizes:

- glasses versus exposed face;
- guarded posture versus confident pose;
- dark, socially minimizing presentation versus highly authored camera awareness.

The visual evidence should not be interpreted as “Shizuku is the real Sakura.” It demonstrates how radically presentation can reorganize what other people perceive from the same body.

## 20.2 Character-database inserts

The volume contains several profile/database pages that reduce students to standardized categories and letter grades.

As paratext, these pages materially reinforce the institutional-legibility theme.

They invite the reader to participate in the school’s classificatory gaze while the prose itself keeps showing capacities the boxes fail to capture.

The proper analytical use is therefore double:

- they are useful snapshots of official/editorial characterization;
- they are also formal reminders that a profile is not a whole person.

## 20.3 Sakura’s testimony illustration

The hearing illustration foregrounds the difficulty of public speech rather than transforming Sakura into a suddenly confident performer. Her body remains visibly tense and effortful.

This matches the prose’s developmental scale: courage is acting while fear remains present.

## 20.4 Final rival image

The late image placing Ayanokōji in the foreground with Horikita behind him visually anticipates the new asymmetry created by the volume: she is beginning to see that the supposedly passive boy in front of her may be controlling more of the route than he admits.

This is editorial emphasis rather than independent proof of motive, but it aligns strongly with the ending conversation.

# 21. Motifs and recurring language

## 21.1 `偽り / falsehood` and `真実 / truth`

The obvious pair is not used only for the Class C lie.

It extends to:

- personas;
- reputations;
- testimony;
- fabricated cameras;
- self-description;
- and Ayanokōji’s “conflict-averse” identity.

The volume repeatedly asks whether false presentation can produce true effects.

## 21.2 Cameras

Cameras function as:

- self-authorship;
- witness;
- surveillance;
- evidence;
- threat;
- fiction.

Few motifs in the early series carry so many distinct forms of power.

## 21.3 `不良品 / defective product`

Chabashira’s late use of the school’s defect language against Ayanokōji is crucial.

She says the most capable product may also be the most dangerous to handle.

“Defect” therefore cannot mean merely low academic or physical ability.

It may refer to incompatibility between capacity and the institution’s desired mode of use.

## 21.4 Friendship

Friendship appears in several incompatible grammars:

- “friends should help”;
- “friends still have boundaries”;
- “I believe my friend even when I cannot prove her claim”;
- “I want evidence that other people choose me”;
- “I may be helping because these are the first friends I made.”

The concept is becoming both emotionally important and ethically unstable.

## 21.5 `進歩 / progress` and `成長 / growth`

The ending uses developmental language for Sudō without claiming completed change.

This becomes a useful distinction for the larger Year 1 method:

> progress can be locally real without making the person globally transformed.

## 21.6 Peace / `平穏`

Ayanokōji’s desire for quiet ordinary life returns precisely when he finds himself intellectually stimulated by the competitive landscape.

`平穏` therefore functions not merely as stated preference but as a boundary he is trying to impose on himself.

# 22. Formal and literary strengths

## 22.1 The prologue governs the entire book

Sakura’s monologue initially appears like character-specific setup. By the end, “mask” has become the formal structure of almost every major conflict.

This is efficient thematic architecture.

## 22.2 The disciplinary mystery is not solved by discovering a hidden clue

The reader already has access to enough truth to know Sudō did not simply attack three innocent students.

The suspense instead concerns whether truth can become actionable.

That makes the volume closer to an epistemic and institutional thriller than a conventional whodunit.

## 22.3 The stalker subplot rhymes with the main plot rather than merely interrupting it

Both crises involve:

- disputed or unwanted narratives;
- evidence;
- visibility;
- bodily vulnerability;
- credibility;
- and a decisive threat of public exposure.

The parallel gives Sakura’s personal story structural relevance to the central case.

## 22.4 The perspective shifts are strategically placed

Sakura opens the book and Horikita receives direct interiority near the end.

Those shifts limit Ayanokōji’s interpretive monopoly.

The reader can compare his model of another person with that person’s own fear and self-understanding.

## 22.5 The solution distributes competence

The operation needs:

- Ayanokōji’s conceptual seeding and pressure design;
- Horikita’s synthesis and risk ownership;
- Ichinose’s money, political credibility, and performance;
- Kanzaki’s assent and caution;
- Sotomura’s technical help;
- Sakura’s earlier courage;
- and the conspirators’ guilty knowledge.

This is important for a series often remembered through protagonist genius alone. The outcome is networked.

## 22.6 The ending creates a strong double reveal

Two things become visible at once:

- an external rival likely capable of designing adversarial environments;
- Horikita’s growing recognition that Ayanokōji has been designing environments too.

The competitive story and the protagonist mystery advance through the same concept.

# 23. Critical reservations

## 23.1 The stalking plot remains structurally gendered

Sakura receives meaningful agency, but the climax still places a socially anxious girl in sexual danger so that Ayanokōji can deploy competence and protection.

The scene is better than a pure damsel construction because:

- Sakura initiates the confrontation;
- she clearly articulates refusal;
- she attempts to reclaim her boundaries;
- and her courage is recognized independently of the rescue.

Still, the danger is narratively useful to Ayanokōji’s characterization in a way worth naming.

## 23.2 Ayanokōji’s intervention delay is ethically underexamined by the prose

He explicitly wants a more decisive moment before revealing himself, and only intervenes once the stalker physically restrains Sakura and Ichinose signals that waiting has become intolerable.

The plan produces excellent evidence, but a stronger normative framing would ask more directly whether gaining better proof justified the additional danger.

## 23.3 The volume’s sexual comedy weakens its surveillance critique

Ike and Yamauchi’s location monitoring, body commentary, and invasive fantasies are treated with substantial comic indulgence.

The later stalking plot asks the reader to take unwanted observation and entitlement seriously.

The behaviors differ dramatically in degree, but the volume is not always interested in examining the continuum it has created.

## 23.4 Some legal vocabulary should not be mistaken for doctrinal authority

Ayanokōji and others reason about self-defense, burden of proof, evidence, and proportionality in ways that are useful for the narrative.

This artifact treats that language as **character/institutional reasoning inside the novel**, not as an authoritative statement of Japanese law.

## 23.5 The camera bluff depends on unusually favorable target psychology

The operation succeeds because the conspirators are:

- guilty;
- hot;
- rushed;
- divided;
- relatively unsophisticated;
- and prevented from consulting the unseen higher-level actor.

A more disciplined adversary could inspect the equipment, refuse immediate decision, or demand institutional verification.

The fragility does not make the plan implausible. It limits how generalizable the victory should be considered.

## 23.6 The school’s procedure remains only partially specified

The volume provides enough information to understand the hearing dramatically, but not enough to reconstruct a complete formal disciplinary code.

We should therefore avoid overclaiming:

- exact burden-of-proof standards;
- exact precedent rules;
- or whether every similar dispute would be handled identically.

# 24. Strongest competing interpretations

## 24.1 “Ayanokōji solved everything; Horikita merely received credit”

This interpretation has real support because he deliberately seeds the conceptual chain and hides that fact.

It nevertheless underweights Horikita’s independent contribution. She must:

- recognize the synthesis;
- test feasibility;
- commit resources;
- persuade external allies;
- reject the compromise;
- and own failure risk.

A more accurate conclusion is asymmetrical collaboration rather than puppet/master simplicity.

## 24.2 “Sakura’s arc is about becoming her true self by abandoning the fake idol persona”

The prologue strongly resists this.

Sakura says the mask is precisely how she can sometimes become herself. Shizuku is authored, but authored identity can still express real desire and capacity.

The arc is better understood as increasing control over transitions among selves and audiences.

## 24.3 “The volume proves reputation-based judgment is unjust”

Too simple.

Sudō’s reputation is causally relevant because he created it through repeated behavior. Institutions cannot operate without credibility judgments.

The injustice emerges when reputation substitutes for specific proof or becomes impossible to revise.

## 24.4 “Ayanokōji’s care for Sakura is purely strategic”

The source does not support such a narrow reading.

He gives up potential class utility by telling her she need not testify, connects her isolation to his own remembered pain, chooses personal trust in her, and explicitly speaks the language of friendship partly for his own sake.

His care is strategically mediated. It is not therefore strategically fabricated.

## 24.5 “The fake-camera bluff is straightforwardly righteous because the targets were guilty”

Guilt strengthens the defense of the tactic but does not erase the ethical mechanism.

The plan deliberately restricts information and choice under severe threat. The fact that the targets deserve no success in their false accusation does not make manipulation disappear as a category.

# 28. Cumulative delta after Volumes 1–2

Volume 1 established the school as a system that withholds evaluative meaning and forces students to discover how their behavior becomes collective consequence. Volume 2 does not simply add a disciplinary subplot. It changes the cumulative model by showing that **ability and innocence are politically inert unless they can become legible in an institutionally actionable form**.

## 28.1 Ayanokōji delta

After Volume 1, Ayanokōji could be described as a concealed high-capacity student who manipulates his own visibility, seeks ordinary friendship, and quietly supplies solutions while allowing Horikita to occupy the public leadership role.

Volume 2 adds five important refinements.

### 1. Concealment becomes environmental authorship

He does not merely hide what he can do. He increasingly determines **what other people will be positioned to notice and infer**.

The camera plan is the clearest example. He does not deliver a finished answer to Horikita. He instead controls the sequence of available cues until she can discover the answer herself. This is a more sophisticated form of influence than direct instruction.

### 2. Friendship becomes an admitted motive

His uncertainty about whether he is genuinely considered a friend, his comfort in silence with Horikita, and his later statement that he probably wanted to help the first friends he had made make ordinary social belonging harder to dismiss as camouflage.

### 3. Personal trust can exceed proof

His declaration that he believes Sakura even when the institution cannot yet prove her account establishes a distinction between interpersonal trust and public adjudication.

### 4. Empathy is real but does not prevent optimization

Ayanokōji connects Sakura's isolation to his own remembered pain and gives her meaningful choices about testimony and disclosure. Yet during the stalker confrontation he also wants a more decisive evidentiary moment before intervening.

The volume therefore already refuses the simple alternatives:

> caring person **or** manipulator.

He can care and optimize the person he cares about within the same episode.

### 5. He begins to fear his own strategic appetite

The closing monologue is especially important cumulatively. Competition interests him. The thought of testing himself against stronger actors excites him. He then recoils toward the peaceful, ordinary school life he says he wants and names himself as frightening.

The central Ayanokōji question after Volume 2 is therefore sharper than after Volume 1:

> **Is concealment protecting an ordinary self from the competitive strategist—or is ordinary life the environment in which that strategist is learning new and more intimate forms of control?**

## 28.2 Horikita and class-leadership delta

Horikita's Volume 1 problem was not lack of ability but inability to translate individual excellence into shared movement.

Volume 2 advances that arc without completing it.

She still displays a severe present-usefulness ethic. Undeveloped ability has no immediate value to her class project. But Sudō's case forces her to confront **trajectory**.

By the end she can tell him that today's progress implies the possibility of greater growth tomorrow.

Equally important, the fake-camera operation shows a more developed form of leadership than merely receiving Ayanokōji's answer:

- she recognizes a seeded concept;
- tests it;
- commits resources;
- recruits allies;
- bears the public risk;
- and accepts deception as a morally compromised instrument she herself is willing to own.

Her independence remains incomplete because Ayanokōji shaped the discovery environment and Manabu can still destabilize her emotionally.

The cumulative formulation is therefore:

> **Horikita is moving from excellence as exclusion toward leadership as the recognition and activation of trajectories, but she remains dependent upon external structures she does not yet fully understand.**

## 28.3 Relationship delta

Volume 2 broadens the series' relationship vocabulary.

### Ayanokōji–Horikita

Their connection gains a low-pressure private register. Silence itself can be comfortable. At the same time, information asymmetry grows because Ayanokōji deliberately conceals the extent to which he authors her inference environment.

### Ayanokōji–Sakura

This becomes the first relationship in which the volume explicitly juxtaposes:

- recognition;
- privacy;
- trust;
- permission to refuse;
- autobiographical empathy;
- protective surveillance;
- and strategic delay.

That combination will be important for later relationship analysis even without importing later developments.

### Ichinose–Kanzaki

The pair establishes an early model of leadership that can contain internal disagreement. Kanzaki is not merely subordinate support; he can object to risk while continuing to serve the group's chosen action.

### Horikita–Sudō

Their relationship shifts slightly from contempt toward conditional recognition of development. The important change is not affection. It is that Horikita begins to recognize a person as a **trajectory** rather than only a present score.

## 28.4 Institutional delta

Volume 1 established hidden evaluation and collective class points.

Volume 2 expands the institution in several directions:

- private points can purchase class mobility;
- extracurricular activity enters evaluation;
- teachers have material incentives attached to class success;
- school phones create location visibility;
- student council adjudication converts evidence and reputation into enforceable outcomes;
- withdrawal itself has point consequences;
- and a student's prior reputation can alter the practical force of present testimony.

The school therefore becomes more than an exam designer.

It is an institution that builds **markets, surveillance infrastructure, records, reputational economies, and adjudicative procedures** through which students become legible.

## 28.5 Ethics and autonomy delta

Volume 1 already showed Ayanokōji willing to intervene indirectly while hiding authorship.

Volume 2 makes the ethical structure more explicit.

Positive autonomy evidence includes:

- telling Sakura she need not testify;
- urging her to act for herself rather than the class;
- respecting her permission regarding Shizuku;
- praising her self-directed confrontation.

Counterevidence includes:

- tracking her location;
- withholding immediate intervention while seeking stronger evidence;
- engineering the Class C boys' decision environment;
- deliberately denying verification time.

The cumulative conclusion is not that Ayanokōji either respects or disregards autonomy.

It is that he appears to value **the production of capable choice** while reserving for himself considerable authority over the environment in which choice becomes possible.

That distinction should remain central in later volumes.

## 28.6 Ability and merit delta

The first two volumes now support a more differentiated ability model.

### Possessed ability

A person may possess academic, athletic, social, informational, or strategic capability.

### Displayed ability

Ayanokōji suppresses his; Sakura disguises hers behind contextual identity; Sudō displays physical ability but also destructive behavior.

### Measured ability

The school quantifies academics and other performance domains, and the database inserts visually reinforce this classificatory impulse.

### Socially usable ability

Ichinose immediately sees that Sudō's athleticism could become class value. Horikita initially has more difficulty seeing abilities not yet organized into present utility.

### Developmental ability

Sudō's progress forces the first explicit recognition that tomorrow's person may be more valuable than today's measurement.

### Political ability

Ichinose's network, trust, points, and command presence make her effective across class boundaries even though none of those capacities is reducible to a test score.

Volume 2 therefore strengthens the emerging proposition:

> **`実力` is not merely what a person can do. It also concerns whether ability can be made visible, trusted, organized, and converted into collective action.**

## 28.7 Open-question delta

Volume 2 closes some Volume 1 questions and generates sharper replacements.

The question is no longer merely whether Ayanokōji is hiding greater ability. Horikita and Chabashira now explicitly suspect that he is.

The new questions concern:

- why he suppresses it;
- what happens if concealment fails;
- whether Horikita can become independently generative rather than merely well-guided;
- whether Sudō's development will become durable;
- what Ichinose's unexplained points signify;
- what kind of leader the Class C strategist will prove to be;
- and whether Ayanokōji can preserve ordinary friendship while increasingly treating social environments as systems he can author.

# 29. Open questions at the Volume 2 endpoint

These questions are deliberately limited to what remains unresolved after Volume 2. They should not be answered using later volumes.

1. **Why does Ayanokōji suppress his real level of ability so aggressively?**  
   The volume makes concealment harder to deny but does not supply a complete motive.

2. **What exactly does Chabashira know about Ayanokōji?**  
   Her description of him as Class D's greatest defective product implies unusual knowledge or inference, but the source does not yet fully explain its basis.

3. **Will Horikita become capable of producing solutions without Ayanokōji first arranging the path toward them?**  
   Volume 2 shows meaningful autonomous operationalization, not yet independent strategic generation.

4. **Can Horikita's emerging recognition of growth survive conflict with her immediate-usefulness logic?**

5. **Will Sudō's recognition of collective responsibility persist once the immediate threat of expulsion or punishment recedes?**

6. **What is the source and significance of Ichinose's extraordinary private-point situation?**  
   The volume deliberately leaves this unresolved.

7. **How durable is Class B's combination of solidarity, points, information, and internal dissent?**

8. **Who is the Class C actor who recognizes the camera operation's significance, and what kind of conflict does he intend to create?**

9. **How much of Sakura's progress will survive outside the immediate crisis?**  
   Has she become more capable of choosing when to reveal herself, or merely more comfortable with Ayanokōji and a small trusted circle?

10. **Can Sakura integrate Airi and Shizuku without treating one as the false identity and the other as the only authentic one?**

11. **How far will Ayanokōji go in allowing danger to develop when stronger evidence or a better strategic position can be obtained by waiting?**

12. **Does Ayanokōji want merely to avoid conflict, or does he also enjoy difficult competition enough that peace and challenge are already competing desires?**

13. **What happens when more classmates notice the gap between Ayanokōji's public mediocrity and the causal influence surrounding him?**

14. **Is the school's reputation-sensitive disciplinary process capable of revising its judgment of students whose past conduct made them initially unbelievable?**

15. **How often will the institution itself distinguish truth from what can be successfully entered into the record?**

# 30. Final synthesis

Year 1 Volume 2 is a novel about **the politics of legibility**.

Its apparent problem is simple: Sudō says he was attacked; Class C says he attacked them; somebody must determine what happened.

But the novel repeatedly refuses to let truth function by itself.

Sudō may be telling the truth, yet:

- his body;
- his temper;
- his reputation;
- the visible injuries;
- the order in which accusations were made;
- and the class interests surrounding the dispute

all shape how much force his truth possesses.

Sakura has relevant truth, yet her fear of becoming visible makes truth politically inert until she chooses to enter the record.

Her problem is even more intimate than Sudō's. The person the school sees as Sakura Airi and the person the camera sees as Shizuku are not cleanly divided into false and true selves. Her opening paradox suggests that the authored mask is sometimes precisely the instrument through which a less socially constrained self can exist.

Ichinose demonstrates another solution to legibility. Her power comes from making herself credible across boundaries. People know her, trust her, answer her calls, lend her legitimacy, and allow her to coordinate information. Kanzaki's disagreement does not destroy that structure; it proves the structure can contain dissent.

Horikita begins learning that leadership requires more than seeing ability correctly. It requires creating conditions under which ability can become usable. Her decision to run the camera bluff marks an ethically important change: she is no longer only the visible face onto which Ayanokōji projects an answer. He seeds the path, but she recognizes it, verifies it, finances it, recruits allies, bears the risk, and owns the lie.

That makes the volume's authorship structure deliberately asymmetric.

Ayanokōji remains the hidden environmental designer. His characteristic intervention is becoming more sophisticated. Rather than simply tell another person what to do, he can arrange:

- what they notice;
- when they notice it;
- what alternatives appear available;
- what another actor fears;
- and how long anyone has to verify the situation.

A fake camera therefore becomes real in its consequences.

The device does not record anything. What matters is that the Class C boys inhabit an environment in which they cannot safely act as though it is fake.

This is an early miniature of a much larger problem already latent in the series:

> **the person who controls the conditions under which others decide what is true can exercise power without ever issuing a command.**

Yet Volume 2 is equally important because Ayanokōji's control does not exhaust him.

He worries about whether other boys really count him as a friend. He finds silence with Horikita comfortable. He believes Sakura before he can prove her. He remembers the pain of fighting alone. He lets Sakura refuse testimony. He asks her to act for herself. He hesitates before revealing her private identity. He says he probably wanted to help because these were his first friends.

And after beginning to feel excitement at the prospect of stronger conflict, he turns inward with something close to fear.

He wants peace.

He also appears capable of enjoying the very kind of strategic contest that threatens peace.

The most important development is therefore not that Volume 2 reveals a hidden mastermind. That formulation is too shallow.

It reveals a person developing two capacities at once:

> **greater attachment to ordinary human beings, and greater skill at authoring the environments in which those human beings act.**

Those capacities can support one another. Understanding Sakura enables him to help her. Understanding Horikita enables him to teach indirectly. Understanding the Class C boys enables him to protect Sudō.

But they can also come into ethical conflict, because the better he becomes at understanding people, the easier it becomes to make another person's sincere choice serve a result he selected first.

Volume 1 asked whether apparently defective people could become useful members of a community.

Volume 2 adds the harder question:

> **Who gets to decide what version of a person becomes visible enough to count—and what kind of power belongs to the person capable of arranging that visibility?**

That is the volume's decisive contribution to the Year 1 architecture.
