# 1. Executive thesis

Volume 4 is the point at which *Classroom of the Elite* stops treating trust merely as a condition of cooperation and begins treating it as **extractable political capital**.

The zodiac-group examination divides the first-year students into twelve mixed-class groups and places one secret `優待者 / preferentially treated student` inside each. The school publicly asks students to transcend class identity, discuss the answer, and reach a mutually beneficial result. It simultaneously offers a more profitable route in which one non-target student identifies the target early, acquires private points, transfers class points, and ends the group's examination through betrayal.

The institution therefore does not simply ask whether students can cooperate. It asks whether they can determine:

- what another person knows;
- whether the person is telling the truth;
- what proof can be made visible;
- what relationship makes disclosure rational;
- who can be induced to accept risk;
- and when a performance of trust is more valuable than trust itself.

The examination's official object is the hidden `優待者`.

Its real object is the **architecture of belief**.

Ayanokōji, Ichinose, Ryūen, and Katsuragi each respond through a different political model.

- **Katsuragi** protects Class A's lead by refusing discussion. His fortress converts nonparticipation into rational defense.
- **Ichinose** creates voluntary interaction, studies behavior through games and conversation, and uses apparently cooperative openness as strategic cover once she knows Class B cannot be harmed in the Rabbit group.
- **Ryūen** centralizes phones, information, and obedience, then searches for the deterministic rule hidden behind the school's claim of `厳正な調整 / strict adjustment`.
- **Ayanokōji** builds a layered deception around phones and identities—but his more consequential operation is the acquisition of Kei Karuizawa as the class's missing social network.

The first three volumes had already shown that Ayanokōji can author what other people and institutions are able to see. Volume 4 moves into a darker domain. He begins authoring **what another person believes she must depend upon in order to survive**.

Kei's opening monologue names herself a `寄生虫 / parasite`: a weak organism that cannot live alone. The second pass must resist turning that language into an omniscient diagnosis. It is Kei's trauma-authored theory of herself. Severe bullying taught her that low social status could become bodily danger, that friendship could fail, and that survival required attachment to a socially stronger person. Her dominance inside Class D, her false relationship with Hirata, and her rapid use of Machida inside the Rabbit group are not evidence that she is naturally empty or helpless. They are a sophisticated survival system built around the conviction that unprotected independence is unsafe.

Hirata supplies one model of protection. He agrees to a false relationship without demanding romance or sex. He regards Kei as one important classmate among others and will intervene against harm. But his universalism places a limit on exclusivity: he refuses to injure Manabe or any other student for Kei's benefit. He wants a reconciliation in which nobody is abandoned.

Ayanokōji recognizes that this moral limit makes Hirata an imperfect host for the security Kei seeks.

He then manufactures the alternative.

He:

1. identifies Kei's social power and hidden instability;
2. deliberately reactivates Manabe's conflict with her;
3. acquires Hirata's trust and information;
4. uses Hirata to lure Kei to an isolated lower deck;
5. manipulates Manabe through an anonymous identity and promises of revenge;
6. creates a closed environment whose escalating violence he explicitly compares to the Milgram experiment;
7. refuses to intervene even if the assault becomes worse than expected;
8. leaves while Kei's cries continue;
9. returns after her other protectors have failed to appear;
10. uses blackmail, physical restraint, exposure of trauma, and a direct threat of sexual assault;
11. then promises protection more certain than Hirata's or Machida's in exchange for cooperation.

This is not merely recruitment. It is **manufactured dependency**.

Ayanokōji does not discover a naturally occurring bond and negotiate access to it. He breaks the credibility of competing protectors, recreates the conditions of helplessness through which Kei learned to seek hosts, makes himself the person who knows everything, and offers safety from a danger he helped create.

The resulting arrangement is operationally powerful. Kei possesses what he lacks:

- command of the girls' social hierarchy;
- access to information inaccessible to him;
- rapid situational intelligence;
- and the ability to make Class D's fragmented female network politically usable.

It may also protect her from future bullying.

Those benefits do not make the method consensual or humane.

Volume 4 therefore sharpens the project's governing hypothesis. The relevant opposition is not simply development versus control. A person may genuinely gain safety, status, and future agency through a relationship created by coercion. The difficult question is:

> **What moral authority does an effective protector acquire when he first manufactures the conditions that make his protection indispensable?**

The volume deliberately refuses to let Ayanokōji's cruelty erase his ordinary adolescent self. The same narrator:

- wants friends and a girlfriend;
- recognizes Horikita, Sakura, Yukimura, and others as emerging friends;
- becomes awed by a star-filled sky;
- panics when Kushida embraces him;
- finds Ichinose attractive enough to imagine liking her;
- enjoys low-pressure companionship;
- and values the school because it offers a life outside the world seeking to reclaim him.

The disturbing conclusion is not that these feelings are fake.

It is that genuine loneliness, attraction, and desire for ordinary life coexist with a conditioned strategic mode capable of turning another adolescent's bodily terror into a class resource.

Volume 4's largest recorded outcome is Ryūen's class-point victory.

Its most consequential unrecorded outcome is that Ayanokōji acquires the first person whose social life he can use as a distributed extension of his own sight.

# 2. Second-pass findings at a glance

## Confirmed

- The examination is a mixed-class trust-and-betrayal mechanism rather than a simple logic puzzle.
- Kei's public dominance is a survival construction organized around protection and status.
- Hirata and Kei's relationship is explicitly false and protective rather than romantic.
- Ayanokōji intentionally permits and intensifies harm to recruit Kei.
- His threat of sexual assault is direct textual fact, not merely suggestive intimidation.
- Ichinose combines genuine warmth with high-level strategic deception.
- Ryūen's greatest strength is centralized information control and pattern discovery, not violence alone.
- Ayanokōji's ordinary adolescent desires remain fully visible beside the coercive operation.

## Strengthened

- **Trust as political capital:** every major strategy depends on who can turn another person's belief into action.
- **Institutional legibility:** the school makes an unalterable email into formal proof, but Ayanokōji exploits the unstated assumption that the phone displaying the proof identifies its owner.
- **Kei's ecological ability:** her importance lies in command of a social environment, not conventional academic or strategic ranking.
- **Hirata's universalism:** his inability to privilege one person is both a moral strength and a practical limitation.
- **Ichinose's bounded honesty:** she respects voluntary disclosure while using cooperation rhetoric to create profitable betrayal by other classes.
- **Ayanokōji's network problem:** he explicitly identifies the need for `目と耳 / eyes and ears`; Kei is recruited as institutional infrastructure.

## Refined / corrected

- `寄生虫 / parasite` should be analyzed first as Kei's trauma-based self-conception. It is not an objective statement that she possesses no independent capacity.
- Ichinose's openness is neither innocence nor hypocrisy. It is a consensual political technique bounded by Class B's interests.
- The Rabbit-group phone operation is not an isolated clever trick. It is a study in the gap between **proof of content** and **proof of identity**.
- The earlier account sometimes spoke of Kei as a “partner” too quickly. At the Volume 4 endpoint, **coerced hidden collaborator**, **protected asset**, or **new dependency relation** is more precise than reciprocal partnership.
- The content of the paper Ayanokōji gives Hirata as a second form of insurance is not explicitly disclosed. Its exact function remains unresolved rather than available for confident reconstruction.

## Newly foregrounded

- Volume 4 links the examination and the Kei operation through the same grammar: knowledge of another person's secret creates leverage over the person's future.
- The school officially asks students to disregard class boundaries while making class-point incentives the most consequential part of the exam. The contradiction is structural, not accidental.
- Absence of complaint is repeatedly treated as operational consent: Ryūen can coerce phones so long as nobody reports him; Ayanokōji can exploit harm so long as no evidence becomes actionable.
- Ayanokōji's ability to enjoy ordinary companionship is expanding at the same time as his capacity for intimate coercion.
- Teachers are already using group composition for personal and investigative purposes, complicating the idea that the exam is a neutral measurement system.

# 3. Position in the opening four-volume architecture

## Volume 1 — hidden evaluation

The school withholds the criteria through which freedom becomes collective judgment. Horikita's narrow academic meritocracy fails to recognize context-dependent ability. Ayanokōji begins controlling the visibility of his own performance and interventions.

## Volume 2 — unequal legibility

Truth is separated from proof, credibility, and institutional action. Sakura can know the truth without being able to make it persuasive. Ayanokōji learns to shape what another person or authority can recognize as actionable.

## Volume 3 — environmental authorship

The deserted-island exam reveals ecological ability and collective conversion. Ayanokōji moves from solving rules to shaping the environment through which Ike, Sakura, Horikita, and others act, fail, or become socially visible.

## Volume 4 — dependency authorship

The unit of strategy contracts from the class environment to the intimate relationship. Ayanokōji now asks not only:

> What will this person do under pressure?

but:

> What must this person believe about safety, protection, and herself so that her future choices become predictable?

The progression is therefore:

> **hidden criteria → controlled legibility → authored environment → authored dependency**

This is the first volume in which Ayanokōji's developmental intelligence becomes unmistakably abusive at the interpersonal level.

# 4. Narrative architecture

The volume contains five named movements plus the afterword.

| Movement | Narrative function | Principal question |
|---|---|---|
| **軽井沢恵の独白** | Reveals the private trauma model beneath Kei's public dominance | What identity does survival require when friendship and youth have become dangerous? |
| **穏やかな日常は突然に……** | Reestablishes ordinary cruise life, Ayanokōji's adolescent voice, and the examination rules | Can apparent leisure remain outside institutional design? |
| **千差万別の想い** | Begins the mixed-group political contest and exposes class-specific interests | Can people with unequal starting positions genuinely prefer the same “cooperative” outcome? |
| **ダブルクエスチョン** | Moves from the formal double question of the exam to Ayanokōji's private double operation against Kei | Who is the target, and what does the target fear losing? |
| **それぞれの差** | Reveals differences among strategies, social abilities, and moral limits | What distinguishes insight, control, cooperation, and betrayal when each can produce success? |

The title `ダブルクエスチョン / Double Question` is especially apt.

At the examination level, students must answer:

1. Who is the `優待者`?
2. Should that knowledge be shared, concealed, or betrayed?

At the character level, Ayanokōji asks two questions about Kei:

1. What is the private structure beneath her public authority?
2. Can that structure be made useful to him?

The narrative's formal cruelty lies in the fact that the reader receives Kei's private monologue before Ayanokōji begins exploiting precisely the structure it reveals. Her prologue does not merely create sympathy. It functions as operational disclosure.

# 5. The summer group special examination
## `Thinking` as the management of distrust

## 5.1 Official purpose

Mashima describes the school's model of adult foundational ability as three broad capacities:

- `アクション / action`;
- `シンキング / thinking`;
- `チームワーク / teamwork`.

The island examination emphasized teamwork. The new examination emphasizes thinking, defined as the ability to:

- analyze the present situation;
- clarify the problem;
- identify and prepare the process required for resolution;
- exercise imagination;
- and produce new value.

This definition is broad enough to sound almost apolitical. The actual rules immediately make thought political. Students are not solving an impersonal problem. They are deciding what another person is, who may be trusted, and when disclosure should become betrayal.

## 5.2 Mixed-class zodiac groups

All first-year students are distributed among twelve groups named after the zodiac. Each group contains members from all four classes, generally around fourteen students. Students are told to suspend their normal class relationships and act as members of the temporary group.

The instruction is deliberately paradoxical. The school's reward structure continues moving class points between the home classes. A student is therefore asked to become a Rabbit-group member while remaining materially accountable to Class A, B, C, or D.

The exam creates overlapping identities:

- temporary group member;
- home-class member;
- possible VIP;
- possible betrayer;
- private-point seeker;
- and social actor whose reputation survives after the group dissolves.

The institution does not remove class loyalty. It asks students to behave as though class loyalty has been suspended while preserving every incentive that makes suspension difficult.

## 5.3 The VIP

One student in each group is secretly selected as `優待者`. The VIP receives an official school email naming the status and is also the correct answer to the group's examination.

The VIP cannot submit an answer personally. This asymmetry is crucial. The person who is the object of knowledge cannot exercise the same formal power as those trying to identify the object.

The school constructs a miniature political situation in which:

- one person has privileged knowledge;
- everyone else has the power to classify that person;
- and the classified person can survive only by controlling others' beliefs.

## 5.4 Four possible results

| Result | Condition | Class-point effect | Private-point effect | Political logic |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **1: collective success** | Every required non-VIP student submits the correct VIP after the exam | none | VIP receives 1,000,000; others receive 500,000 each | universal disclosure and coordinated trust |
| **2: VIP preserved** | At least one required student is wrong or does not answer after the exam | none | VIP receives 500,000 | secrecy, confusion, or incomplete cooperation |
| **3: correct betrayal** | A non-VIP identifies the VIP during the answer window | betrayer's class +50; VIP's class −50 | betrayer receives 500,000 | individual/class gain through exposure |
| **4: incorrect betrayal** | A non-VIP submits the wrong person | betrayer's class −50; VIP's class +50 | VIP receives 500,000 | misclassification rewards the concealed target |

A member of the VIP's own class cannot trigger Results 3 or 4 by naming the VIP. This prevents the simplest form of collusive class-point transfer.

## 5.5 Anonymity and private reward

The school announces only:

- the group result;
- and each class's aggregate point change.

It does not publish:

- the VIP;
- the betrayer;
- or the recipient of the private reward.

Students may use temporary IDs or divide the payment so that wealth does not reveal the beneficiary.

This changes the moral economy of betrayal. A student can damage the temporary group or home-class trust structure without having to own the act publicly. Anonymity makes private rationality easier to separate from social responsibility.

## 5.6 Official truth and device identity

The school prohibits copying, deleting, transferring, or altering the official VIP email. Ayanokōji interprets this as making the email `１００％の真実証明 / one-hundred-percent proof of truth`.

The rule does not solve the epistemic problem. It relocates it.

The message is authentic. But:

- who owns the device?
- who entered the password?
- whose phone number will ring?
- can a SIM lock be removed?
- and what false inference will observers draw after one deception is exposed?

The decisive strategies do not falsify the official message. They manipulate the chain connecting authentic message to assumed person.

## 5.7 Prohibited coercion and practical coercion

The rules threaten expulsion for:

- stealing a phone;
- forcing or threatening someone to reveal information;
- using another person's device to submit an answer;
- or falsely claiming coercion.

The school also claims it will investigate suspicious conduct and monitors digital activity.

Ryūen nevertheless obtains Class C's devices through authority and fear. If a classmate formally hands over a phone and does not complain, the difference between voluntary cooperation and coerced compliance becomes difficult for the institution to observe.

The prohibition therefore reveals a familiar institutional limitation:

> The school can regulate legible force more easily than domination that has already shaped what the subordinate is willing to call voluntary.

## 5.8 The examination as modified prisoner dilemma

Collective success produces the largest total private reward. But any student who believes the VIP identity can gain personally and improve the home class by betraying before others do.

The structure is not a simple prisoner's dilemma because:

- class blocs are uneven;
- the VIP's class receives distinct incentives;
- players can coordinate within home classes;
- public reputation survives after the one-shot group ends;
- and information is distributed asymmetrically.

It is better understood as a **nested betrayal market**:

- the temporary group offers collective wealth;
- the home class offers political loyalty;
- the individual offers private gain;
- and the institution offers anonymity.

## 5.9 What the exam actually measures

The exam makes at least seven forms of ability visible:

1. **social access** — who can contact people in other groups;
2. **behavioral reading** — who can detect strain, avoidance, or scripted speech;
3. **network authority** — who can obtain information from a whole class;
4. **proof architecture** — who understands phones, passwords, official emails, and device identity;
5. **risk calibration** — who knows when not to answer;
6. **political imagination** — who can understand another class's incentives;
7. **identity authorship** — who can make one person appear to be another.

The school calls these capacities `thinking`, but the exam's strongest students are not simply the best abstract reasoners. They are the students who can make other people socially legible—or strategically illegible.

## 5.10 Required examination table

| Dimension | Finding |
|---|---|
| Official goal | Test `thinking`: analysis, problem clarification, preparation, imagination, and creation of value |
| Hidden practical goal | Force students to manage mixed loyalty, social information, anonymity, and betrayal incentives |
| Key asymmetry | The VIP knows the truth but cannot answer; others can expose or misclassify the VIP |
| Main exploit | Home-class networks, device ownership, layered phone identity, VIP allocation pattern, and socially compelled disclosure |
| Public winner | Class C / `POLITY-RYUEN`, with +150 class points |
| Hidden authors | Ryūen at the class-wide pattern level; Ayanokōji and Ichinose in Rabbit; Katsuragi through defensive nonengagement |
| Human cost | Distrust, coercion, surveillance, intra-class secrecy, Manabe's assault on Kei, and Kei's coerced recruitment |
| Institutional lesson | Social knowledge and betrayal can be turned into measurable competitive value |
| Ethical problem | The school prohibits direct coercion while rewarding structures in which domination makes coercion appear voluntary |
| Longitudinal effect | Creates Class D's shadow information network and establishes Ryūen as the visible rival searching for its hidden author |

# 6. Four class strategies and five political systems

Volume 4 is not simply a contest among four clever students. It compares political systems: ways of organizing information, risk, loyalty, and authority.

## 6.1 Katsuragi Kōhei
### The fortress of the status quo

Katsuragi's strategy is non-engagement.

He calls the exam `シンキング / thinking`, then demonstrates that thinking need not produce speech. Class A has the largest point lead. It gains comparatively little from opening channels through which lower classes can extract favored-person identities.

His logic is defensive:

- do not reveal;
- do not negotiate;
- do not increase the number of people capable of exploiting a mistake;
- accept neutral outcomes over high-variance cooperation.

This is rational within Class A's structural position. A lower class needs movement. The leading class often benefits from preventing movement.

### Strength

Katsuragi recognizes that communication is not automatically productive. In an adversarial environment, every statement can become information.

### Weakness

Class A cannot prevent other classes from coordinating externally. Defensive silence protects each individual group imperfectly when Ryūen discovers the favored-person allocation structure and directs coordinated attacks.

Katsuragi's model is therefore strong against conversational manipulation and weak against systemic inference.

## 6.2 Ichinose Honami
### Consensual legitimacy and selective openness

Ichinose does not begin by imposing herself as Rabbit's leader. She waits to see whether someone else will establish direction. When no legitimate center emerges, she proposes that the group pursue Result 1.

Her method has several layers.

### She asks before leading

Ichinose frames cooperation as a shared preference rather than a command. This matters because the group contains rival classes that owe her no formal obedience.

### She makes trust socially comfortable

She uses conversation and card play to lower defensive tension. These activities are not distractions from strategy. They allow her to observe:

- reaction speed;
- comfort with risk;
- interpersonal alignment;
- and who becomes uneasy when favored-person questions arise.

### She receives voluntary delegation

Her Class B classmates trust her enough to give her broad discretion without visible coercion. That is political capital accumulated before the exam.

### She remains capable of deception

Ichinose later says that she may lie in special examinations even though she prefers honesty in ordinary life. The distinction is crucial. Her legitimacy does not come from an inability to deceive. It comes from others believing she will not use deception casually against the community that trusts her.

### She identifies the phone operation

Ichinose tests the phone/SIM assumption with Hamaguchi, detects the Yukimura decoy, notices that Ayanokōji is too obvious as the supposed second-layer favored person, and infers Kei as the protected center.

She then refuses to betray.

That refusal is not naïve mercy. Her strategy benefits if another class makes the wrong early accusation. She uses insight without automatically converting it into immediate predation.

### Political meaning

Ichinose's authority is the volume's strongest example of **consensual information sovereignty**. People give her access because they expect her to use it within an understood moral and strategic framework.

## 6.3 Ryūen Kakeru
### Coercive information sovereignty

Ryūen governs Class C through centralized compulsion.

He forces students to surrender phones, threatens those who conceal information, and builds an internal map of favored persons. He seeks not merely individual guesses but the underlying rule through which the school distributed the identities.

His method is powerful because it eliminates internal uncertainty faster than a voluntary process could.

It is also politically expensive.

### Strength

- rapid class-wide disclosure;
- centralized analysis;
- coordinated early answers;
- willingness to accept hostility;
- and ability to convert individual risk into class strategy.

### Weakness

- no meaningful refusal capacity;
- dependence on one center;
- silence mistaken for consent;
- and a pattern of control visible enough that sophisticated opponents can infer his methods.

Ryūen's system produces the largest class-point gain. The result demonstrates strategic effectiveness, not moral legitimacy.

## 6.4 Ayanokōji Kiyotaka
### Layered identity and environmental sovereignty

Ayanokōji does not attempt to dominate all twelve groups. He recognizes that his current social isolation makes that impossible.

His response is twofold:

1. build a local deception in Rabbit;
2. use the exam to acquire future information infrastructure.

The Rabbit plan is not merely a phone swap. It is an **interpretive ladder**:

- Yukimura appears to be favored;
- phone-number/SIM analysis exposes the apparent swap;
- Ayanokōji then appears to be the true favored person behind Yukimura;
- a second clue encourages an A-class participant to act on that conclusion;
- the wrong accusation protects the actual favored person, Kei.

The strongest feature is that the deception rewards intelligence.

An unsophisticated observer believes Yukimura.

A moderately sophisticated observer detects the phone anomaly and believes Ayanokōji.

Only a still more sophisticated observer asks why the second answer has been made so satisfying and looks for the third layer.

## 6.5 Hirata Yōsuke
### Voluntary social legitimacy as a separate political system

Hirata is not a class strategist comparable to the four leaders in exam architecture. He is a system of social trust without which Class D's strategies cannot scale.

His authority comes from:

- availability;
- empathy;
- restraint;
- consistency;
- and the belief that he will not exploit private weakness for personal gain.

This is why his decision to involve Ayanokōji in Kei's problem matters so much. It is not merely a plot handoff. Hirata transfers part of his legitimacy to someone he recognizes as unsettling but potentially useful.

His social constitution differs from Ichinose's in scale and from Ayanokōji's in transparency.

He wants to protect everyone. That universalism creates trust and makes exclusive guarantees impossible.

# 7. The Rabbit-group phone strategy
## Identity layered upon identity

## 7.1 The actual favored person

Kei is Rabbit's favored person.

Because her social behavior is erratic and publicly self-interested, she is difficult to protect through simple trust. Ayanokōji instead protects her through layered misdirection.

## 7.2 The first layer: Yukimura

Kei's favored-person email exists on a phone whose identity can be manipulated through the school's point economy and device/SIM conditions.

The operation makes Yukimura appear to carry the favored person's device identity and behave like the person under protection.

His serious demeanor and willingness to perform the role reinforce the inference.

## 7.3 The second layer: Ayanokōji

A skilled observer may identify that Yukimura's phone identity has been switched.

That observer then finds Ayanokōji as the satisfying hidden answer behind him.

Ayanokōji deliberately places himself in the position of “real favored person exposed by clever detection.”

## 7.4 The protected center: Kei

The second answer is still false.

The operation works because most strategic inference stops once the observer feels rewarded for penetrating a deception.

The actual favored person is protected by another student volunteering to become the apparent hidden center.

## 7.5 The A-class early answer

Ayanokōji delegates an additional insurance mechanism through Hirata so that an A-class participant is induced to submit Ayanokōji's name early.

The source establishes the function and outcome more clearly than every internal transmission detail. The exact memo/communication chain should not be reconstructed beyond the text.

The wrong early answer produces Result 4:

- Class A loses 50 class points;
- Class D gains 50 class points;
- Kei receives the favored-person reward.

## 7.6 Ichinose's detection

Ichinose tests whether phone identity can be separated from the apparent user and sees through the Yukimura layer.

She then questions the convenience of Ayanokōji as the second answer and reaches Kei.

This is an important ability distinction.

Ayanokōji excels at designing the interpretive route.

Ichinose excels at recognizing when an apparently successful inference has been made too easy.

## 7.7 Why Ichinose does not betray

Ichinose's non-betrayal is not an inability to act ruthlessly.

She has a different expected-value calculation. If another class sends the wrong answer, Class B benefits relatively without assuming the direct risk of an early accusation.

She also preserves the legitimacy on which her political authority depends.

Her strategy combines morality and interest rather than requiring one to cancel the other.

## 7.8 `ダブルクエスチョン`

The phone plan embodies the title.

The first question is:

> Who is the favored person?

The second is:

> Which answer has been prepared for the person proud enough to believe they solved the first question?

This is the step from deception to **meta-deception**.

# 8. Ayanokōji Kiyotaka
## From environmental author to intimate sovereign

Volume 4 is not the first time Ayanokōji manipulates another person. It is the first time the manipulation reaches so deeply into bodily fear, sexual vulnerability, and the conditions of personal survival.

## 8.1 The ordinary adolescent layer remains explicit

The opening chapter gives unusually direct evidence against an emotionally blank reading.

Ayanokōji imagines romance aboard the cruise, jokes that male students are hungry carnivores, notices newly formed couples, complains that he lacks both friends and a girlfriend, and understands himself as the sort of low-ranked friend invited once for every ten invitations extended to someone closer.

He is not merely performing this social uncertainty for other characters. Much of it occurs in private narration. He also says that his current life is genuinely satisfactory. Being able to speak with Sudō, Ike, Yamauchi, Horikita, Kushida, and Sakura already makes school life feel worthwhile.

This is a modest but important change from Volume 1's almost abstract wish for friendship. He has begun evaluating ordinary life through particular people.

His humor is equally revealing. Hirata's intense attention makes him joke internally about being pushed onto the bed and losing control. His lack of experience with Werewolf becomes self-deprecating evidence that he never belonged to the kind of friend group that plays party games. He notices food, water, scent, bodily warmth, and attractive women with ordinary adolescent awkwardness.

None of this mitigates the later abuse.

It establishes that the person committing it is not an unfeeling machine unable to understand ordinary humanity. He is someone capable of ordinary desire who can place another person's autonomy beneath a strategic objective when he decides the objective is important enough.

## 8.2 The school as chosen refuge

Ayanokōji restates why he entered the school: its prohibition on outside contact offered enforced separation from the external world. He calls the school a `楽園 / paradise` from which Chabashira threatens to expel him if he does not help pursue Class A.

The truth of her claim remains unverifiable. His response is practical: because he cannot prove it false and the cost of ignoring a true threat would be severe, he must act as though it may be true.

He also immediately considers counteraction. A brief inner `devil` suggests that he could move first and find a way to force Chabashira's resignation. The passage is humorous in presentation, including an impossible Dragon Ball fantasy, but the political logic is serious:

> when an authority attempts to control his future through unverifiable threat, his instinct is to acquire information and eventually reverse the power relation.

This is the same logic he later applies to Kei at a smaller scale.

## 8.3 Direct cooperation as an ideal he does not follow consistently

When Hirata asks Ayanokōji to become an invisible intermediary with Horikita, Ayanokōji advises him to speak with her directly. `他人と協力し合うとはそういうことだ / that is what cooperating with another person means.`

The judgment is ethically sound. Cooperation requires accepting the risk that another person will hear the actual speaker and may refuse.

Ayanokōji then constructs the relationship with Kei through the opposite principle:

- he conceals authorship;
- arranges intermediaries;
- manufactures the crisis;
- withholds relevant information;
- makes refusal catastrophically expensive;
- and defines the terms after the subject has been isolated.

The contradiction is not merely hypocrisy. It shows that he understands reciprocal cooperation conceptually but does not consider it adequate for every objective. Where he wants ordinary friendship, directness has value. Where he wants reliable infrastructure, he chooses control.

## 8.4 The `eyes and ears` problem

Ayanokōji's most important strategic realization occurs before the assault.

The zodiac structure is too distributed for one hidden actor. Even complete cooperation from Horikita and Hirata would leave large areas of the year socially inaccessible. He therefore concludes that his priority is not the present point total but acquiring future eyes and ears.

This is a transition from event solving to institution building.

He no longer asks only:

> How do I protect this VIP?

He asks:

> What social system will allow me to know and shape future class environments without becoming publicly visible?

The answer is gendered by the class's current social ecology:

- Hirata possesses broad male and general trust;
- Kei can organize the girls;
- Horikita can issue visible strategic judgments;
- Ayanokōji can coordinate them privately.

The social world he once lacked becomes political infrastructure.

## 8.5 Trauma as information

Ayanokōji reads Kei with extraordinary precision.

He sees that her behavior changes according to whether a protective male is nearby. He notices that her apparent dominance weakens around girls from other classes. He recognizes that public aggression toward Rika is partly a display performed before her own female allies. He infers that Hirata's neutrality limits the protection he can provide and that Kei knows this.

He also recognizes resilience. After hearing what she endured, he judges the core of her personality unusually strong because she returned to school life and rebuilt a social position.

These insights are not false simply because they become instruments.

The ethical problem is that understanding does not produce restraint. It allows him to calibrate pressure more accurately.

## 8.6 Concealment and plausible liability

The Manabe operation demonstrates his ability to construct both the action and the future evidentiary story.

He chooses a place with:

- one practical entrance;
- little foot traffic;
- poor phone reception;
- and no immediate help.

He uses Hirata to summon Kei and obtain Manabe's contact information. He creates a second social-media identity and presents himself as another Class D victim of Kei. He gives Manabe enough traceable material to believe that if the operation is exposed, the anonymous planner can be identified and punished. The apparent self-risk generates trust.

At the same time, he retains an alternative plan: photographs from the earlier stairwell confrontation can be used to threaten Manabe directly if persuasion fails.

This is characteristic Ayanokōji authorship. He does not merely choose one path. He creates an environment in which each likely response already has a corresponding continuation.

## 8.7 Outcome without accountability

Hirata asks him not to make Kei sad.

Ayanokōji anticipates that Hirata would become furious if he knew what was planned. His response is not that the anger would be mistaken. It is that the final absence of an actionable problem is what matters. He compares undetected harm to a murder that cannot be legally judged without evidence.

The analogy is revealing. His ethical frame temporarily collapses into enforceability:

> if the break is repaired and the seam remains invisible, the harm need not become a social fact.

This is the logic of authored record from Volumes 2–3 taken into private ethics. The victim's experience is subordinated to whether the event produces a punishable trace.

## 8.8 The deliberate withholding of rescue

Ayanokōji does not merely fail to rescue Kei quickly.

He states that even if the assault becomes worse than expected, he has no intention of intervening midway. Later, he concludes that thoroughly breaking her once will save effort during reconstruction.

This removes several possible defenses:

- he is not surprised by the violence;
- he is not temporarily unable to intervene;
- he does not misread Manabe's intentions;
- and he does not decide under immediate emergency pressure.

The suffering is a planned stage of recruitment.

## 8.9 Sexuality as an instrument of control

The sexualized threat after the assault is deliberately calibrated.

Ayanokōji touches Kei's thigh, physically prevents escape, threatens to expose her, orders her to spread her legs, sounds his belt, and observes whether preserving her public position matters enough for her to accept sexual violation.

He then says the body was never the objective. The threat was meant to prove that he would do anything necessary.

That explanation describes instrumental intent. It does not reduce the severity of the act.

The coercion works because he has already established:

- physical superiority;
- informational control;
- social power over the secret;
- isolation;
- and the credibility of extreme conduct.

The scene is sexual violence as political communication even though the threatened assault is not completed.

## 8.10 Recognition and invasion

Ayanokōji's recognition of Kei's scar is narratively complex.

He sees that the violence she described does not fully explain the depth of fear. He follows a glance toward her left side, touches the area, and forcibly lifts her clothing to reveal the injury. He recognizes that it implies danger beyond ordinary school bullying and feels respect for the strength required to return to social life.

This can be read as one traumatized person recognizing another's darkness.

It is also bodily invasion.

The language of darkness risks aestheticizing a situation in which one person has just used terror to gain access to another person's most guarded evidence of harm. Recognition does not become mutual simply because he understands what he sees.

## 8.11 Protection as political contract

Ayanokōji finally changes tone and offers a contract:

- Kei will act for him;
- he will act for her;
- personal liking is irrelevant;
- and he will protect her more reliably than Hirata or Machida.

He immediately proves practical capacity by showing the earlier photograph that can deter Manabe.

The offer is effective because it addresses a real need. It is not freely negotiated because Ayanokōji has just demonstrated that he controls:

- the threat;
- the secret;
- the evidence;
- and the available protector.

The contract is therefore materially reciprocal and procedurally coercive.

## 8.12 The end-of-volume emotion: `interesting`

Ryūen's success produces two reactions in Ayanokōji:

- recognition that Class D may face repeated danger;
- and the emergence of an unknown feeling he calls `面白い / interesting`.

The word matters because the threat is not only aversive. Ayanokōji is drawn toward an opponent capable of producing uncertainty. This is consistent with his broader dissatisfaction with environments he can solve completely.

Ryūen becomes dangerous not merely because he may discover Ayanokōji.

He becomes interesting because his independent reasoning may generate a future Ayanokōji has not already closed.

# 9. Karuizawa Kei
## Social dominance as trauma adaptation

Kei's monologue and the later assault create the most complete Year 1 portrait so far of a public identity built for survival.

## 9.1 “I did not change”

Kei begins by rejecting the language of transformation. Entering the school changed nothing because, perhaps, she never intended to change.

She knows:

- boys and girls dislike her;
- her strengths and weaknesses;
- and the distance between her public posture and private state.

She no longer experiences being disliked as simple pain. She may even want it. Hostility is safer than intimacy if intimacy creates opportunities for betrayal or downward status movement.

## 9.2 The scar and bodily memory

The scar is not an abstract symbol. Seeing it in the mirror produces dizziness, nausea, and retching. The past persists through bodily response even when the social environment has changed.

The text therefore resists any simple claim that a new school produces a fresh start. Trauma is not reset by enrollment.

## 9.3 Rejection of youth and friendship

Kei says she does not need youth or friends. What matters is protecting herself, and she will do anything required.

This is not ordinary antisocial preference. It is a hierarchy of values produced by prior danger:

> belonging is valuable only if it contributes to safety.

Friendship, romance, and status become technologies rather than ends.

## 9.4 The parasite metaphor

`寄生虫` is Kei's own word before it becomes Ayanokōji's.

It expresses:

- perceived inability to survive alone;
- necessity of attaching to a stronger host;
- and shame that survival requires using another person.

But the metaphor underdescribes her activity.

Kei does not simply cling. She:

- constructs a gyaru-coded public persona;
- manages female hierarchy;
- performs aggression when allies are watching;
- chooses protectors according to context;
- lies quickly;
- gathers information;
- and reads power accurately.

She is dependent and politically active.

A more precise local formulation is:

> Kei has developed **relational survival intelligence** inside a self-concept that interprets every stable relationship as parasitic attachment.

## 9.5 Hirata as host and shield

The false relationship with Hirata gives Kei:

- a respected male partner;
- high status among Class D girls;
- deterrence against direct bullying;
- and a socially intelligible reason for protection.

Hirata asks no romantic payment. His achievement, as he understands it, is that she can attend school without becoming a victim again.

Yet Kei knows the protection's limit. Hirata is everyone's ally. He will mediate and defend her, but he will not become an exclusive instrument of retaliation.

This is why she attaches herself temporarily to Machida when conflict with Manabe escalates. She is not replacing one romantic affection with another. She is seeking situational protection where Hirata's absence and neutrality create uncertainty.

## 9.6 Victim and aggressor

Kei's history does not make all her current aggression innocent.

She pushes Rika and refuses apology partly because backing down before her own friends would weaken the persona protecting her. Her status performance imposes harm on someone more timid.

The volume therefore avoids a morally simple victim/perpetrator division. Trauma explains why humiliation has become intolerable. It does not make humiliating another person harmless.

This complexity is one reason Manabe's grievance can be activated. Ayanokōji does not invent the original conflict. He deliberately inflames it and creates the conditions in which it becomes violence.

## 9.7 Social intelligence

Ayanokōji initially worries that Kei is insufficiently dominant inside Rabbit. He expects her to control the room as she does among Class D girls.

Her restraint is evidence of intelligence, not incapacity.

She knows:

- Hirata cannot openly privilege her;
- Machida's protection is conditional;
- the mixed group does not recognize her status automatically;
- and pushing too hard without a secure host may expose her.

Her social power is ecological. It depends on a network, not an intrinsic aura.

## 9.8 The assault and reactivation of learned helplessness

Manabe's group recreates elements of prior victimization:

- isolation;
- numerical superiority;
- physical confinement;
- forced apology;
- hair pulling;
- slaps;
- kicks;
- and the gradual targeting of places less likely to reveal evidence.

Kei initially tries to preserve the dominant persona. As physical control becomes impossible, her body returns to terror and submission.

The violence demonstrates that her new identity is powerful within the social environment she built but not independent of it.

## 9.9 The post-assault choice is not consent

Ayanokōji interprets Kei's inability to report him as proof that she is usable. She fears losing the reconstructed social position more than she fears obeying his demands.

This should not be described as free selection of a better protector.

At the moment of agreement:

- she has been assaulted;
- he has revealed that he knows the assault and past;
- he controls incriminating evidence;
- he threatens exposure and sexual violence;
- he has physical control;
- and he offers the only guaranteed route out.

Her acceptance is rational under coercion.

## 9.10 What she gains

The moral criticism should not erase the material benefit.

By the end of the operation, Kei gains:

- evidence capable of deterring Manabe;
- a protector who can act covertly and decisively;
- access to a strategist powerful enough to stabilize her public life;
- and a role in Class D's hidden political structure.

These gains are real.

They do not retroactively authorize the process.

## 9.11 What remains hers

Even at her lowest point, Kei retains forms of agency:

- she insults Ayanokōji;
- refuses to interpret his conduct as legitimate domination;
- explains her own learned helplessness with clarity;
- recognizes the difference between force and affection;
- and later participates in the phone strategy competently.

Ayanokōji controls her options. He does not wholly author her perception of what he has done.

# 10. Ayanokōji's operation against Kei
## Protection manufactured through terror

The operation should be reconstructed carefully because its ethical meaning depends upon which events he merely uses and which he actively authors.

## 10.1 Target selection

Ayanokōji does not select Kei only because she is vulnerable.

He selects her because she combines:

- social centrality;
- female-network access;
- rapid situational intelligence;
- a hidden history;
- an existing protector arrangement;
- and limited confidence in independent survival.

The operation is therefore not simple sadism or opportunism. It is strategic recruitment through trauma.

## 10.2 Reactivating Manabe's grievance

Ayanokōji lies to Manabe that he witnessed Kei bullying another girl.

He wants Manabe to act and to observe Kei's reaction.

This is the first clear step in manufacturing the later crisis. He does not merely notice a naturally developing conflict. He injects information intended to keep resentment active.

## 10.3 First observation and delayed intervention

When Manabe's group corners Kei, Yukimura moves to intervene.

Ayanokōji restrains him.

His rationale is that stopping the confrontation now will merely defer it and remove the chance to acquire leverage capable of preventing recurrence.

This is a consequentialist argument with a real strategic basis.

It also means Kei is exposed to risk without knowledge or consent because Ayanokōji prefers a more decisive future structure.

## 10.4 Learning from Hirata

Through the midnight meeting, Ayanokōji confirms:

- the romance is false;
- Kei requested retaliation;
- Hirata will not provide exclusive violence;
- Kei's social dominance is linked to self-protection;
- and severe past bullying is the origin of the arrangement.

Hirata's trust gives Ayanokōji enough information to design the next stage.

## 10.5 Selecting the battlefield

Ayanokōji chooses an isolated lower floor with:

- a single obvious entry path;
- limited passing traffic;
- low visibility;
- and enough enclosure to make interruption unlikely.

He understands the space as part of the operation.

This is environmental authorship in its clearest Year 1 form so far.

## 10.6 Luring Kei and Manabe

He uses Hirata's name to get Kei to the location.

He manipulates Manabe anonymously and creates the expectation that this is a safe opportunity for revenge.

Manabe's group still chooses to come. Ayanokōji controls neither every movement nor every exact injury.

He controls the conditions that make violence attractive and apparently consequence-free.

## 10.7 “Break and patch”

Ayanokōji explicitly treats breaking and repairing as acceptable if the overall plan does not become a public problem.

He compares hidden wrongdoing to murder without evidence: an event not institutionally provable cannot be punished as the event it was.

This is not merely a neutral observation about evidence. It is the governing logic of his plan.

He optimizes not for absence of harm but for absence of legible harm.

## 10.8 Non-rescue

Before Manabe's group arrives, Ayanokōji decides that he will not intervene even if Kei suffers more than expected.

During the assault, he repeats the position.

He then leaves the scene so the violence can continue beyond his direct observation.

The stated logic is that a thorough collapse reduces the work required for reconstruction.

This is the volume's most explicit instance of developmental control treating psychological destruction as productive raw material.

## 10.9 The Milgram frame

Ayanokōji narrates the assault through the Milgram experiment.

The analogy is revealing because he understands how:

- closed environments;
- authority or expectation;
- gradual escalation;
- and group pressure

can make ordinary people perform cruelty.

But the analogy also understates his own role.

The historical experiment involved an authority instructing participants. Ayanokōji's situation contains additional active authorship:

- he revives the grievance;
- discloses Kei's vulnerability to Manabe;
- selects the site;
- encourages expectation of retaliation;
- removes witnesses;
- and guarantees nonintervention.

He is not merely observing situational cruelty. He is the designer of the situation.

## 10.10 Harm minimization is not moral exoneration

Ayanokōji advises or structures the assault so publicly visible injuries will be limited. He later notes with satisfaction that the marks should be difficult to detect.

This can be defended as reducing bodily harm.

It also makes the abuse harder to report and easier to conceal.

The plan minimizes evidence more reliably than it minimizes trauma.

## 10.11 Post-assault timing

He waits until Manabe's group leaves and Kei has begun regaining composure.

He does not immediately comfort her. He watches, waits, and enters at the point when the previous protector has apparently failed and the threat of recurrence feels unavoidable.

The timing is recruitment timing.

## 10.12 The lie about Hirata

Ayanokōji says he came because Hirata was called away and asked him to substitute.

The lie temporarily fills the gap left by the absent protector.

He first positions himself as extension of Hirata's protection, then demonstrates that he can provide something Hirata cannot.

## 10.13 Exposure as leverage

He reveals knowledge of:

- the bullying;
- the false relationship;
- the social performance;
- and the scar.

He frames himself as holding `生殺与奪 / the power of life and death, or power to grant and take away` over her school identity.

The language is political sovereignty applied to one adolescent's social existence.

## 10.14 Sexualized coercion

Ayanokōji touches Kei's thigh without consent, orders her to open her legs, simulates unfastening his belt, restrains her, and makes sexual violation seem possible.

His private narration indicates that the threat is instrumental.

Kei cannot know the stopping point.

The relevant ethical fact is therefore not only what he secretly intends. It is what a reasonable person in her position is made to believe.

She believes rape may occur and submits.

Calling the action a bluff would describe Ayanokōji's private plan while erasing the victim's experienced coercion.

## 10.15 The scar

He exposes the scar she hides.

This confirms his knowledge and collapses her remaining ability to preserve one private boundary.

He does not react with disgust. That may later carry interpersonal significance.

At this moment, the non-disgust occurs inside violation. Recognition and abuse are not mutually exclusive.

## 10.16 `闇を持つ者は惹かれあう`

Ayanokōji says people carrying darkness are drawn to one another.

The line creates apparent intimacy through shared concealment.

But the darkness is not equally disclosed.

Kei's history is exposed in detail.

Ayanokōji gives only enough of himself to suggest similarity while preserving the actual source, scale, and implications of his past.

The relationship is emotionally suggestive and informationally unequal.

## 10.17 The offer

He proposes a reciprocal formula:

> she acts for him; he acts for her.

On the surface, this is exchange.

In structure, it is asymmetrical:

- he controls the evidence;
- he can expose her;
- he understands the operation;
- she does not;
- he can protect her from Manabe;
- and her social identity has just been made dependent on his discretion.

## 10.18 The new host

The endpoint narration interprets the result as a transfer of parasitic attachment.

Ayanokōji sees himself becoming the new host and narrows Kei's available path toward him.

That is the volume's clearest protection/ownership problem.

He offers a more effective shield than Hirata.

He also makes leaving the shield dangerous.

# 11. Hirata Yōsuke
## A protector whose moral universality becomes a practical limit

Hirata's role in Volume 4 is easy to underestimate because Ayanokōji uses him as an information and access node. The second pass instead treats him as an independent theory of protection.

## 11.1 Social centrality and the cost of availability

Hirata is trusted so broadly that he has little private time. He notices isolated students, invites Ayanokōji to lunch, prioritizes conversation with a boy over a prearranged meal with several girls, and wants to integrate Horikita before the class's temporary momentum disappears.

Ayanokōji recognizes the cost. Hirata's time is continuously appropriated by people who need him. Personal worry can remain unspoken because the role of universal helper does not easily permit him to become the person requiring help.

This is an early sign that social usefulness can become another form of enclosure.

## 11.2 His attempt to use Ayanokōji as intermediary

Hirata asks Ayanokōji to translate between himself and Horikita while hiding his involvement. The proposal is unusually indirect for someone committed to trust.

Ayanokōji interprets it correctly as anxiety. Hirata is afraid that a direct approach will fail and that Class D will lose the chance to integrate the person publicly credited with the island victory.

When challenged, Hirata revises his position immediately. He accepts that cooperation requires facing another person directly rather than outsourcing the relational risk.

This flexibility is one of his strongest abilities. He can recognize when an apparently kind plan denies the other person's right to respond to the actual speaker.

## 11.3 The false relationship

Hirata confirms that his relationship with Kei is protective rather than romantic. She asked for help after years of severe bullying, and he agreed to become her boyfriend so that status and his reputation would deter further victimization.

He does not present himself as savior in a triumphant sense. He says his only merit is that she has been able to attend school without being bullied.

This modest formulation matters. Protection is judged by the protected person's ability to inhabit ordinary life, not by the protector's ownership of her gratitude.

## 11.4 The moral limit of protection

When Ayanokōji asks how far Hirata would go for Kei, Hirata makes the boundary clear. She is an important classmate. He wants to protect her. He cannot harm another person simply because she asks him to.

For Kei, this is insufficient. She wants a protector who will guarantee that she never becomes vulnerable again, regardless of competing claims.

For the ethical analysis, the limit is essential:

> Hirata's unwillingness to become an exclusive coercive weapon is not a defect in care. It is care constrained by recognition that other people also possess moral claims.

Ayanokōji sees the same limit and turns it into a recruitment opportunity.

## 11.5 Sugimura and the origin of universal protection

Hirata tells Ayanokōji that a friend, Sugimura, suffered severe bullying and attempted suicide, leaving him effectively brain-dead. Hirata's failure to recognize and stop the harm became the foundation of his present drive to protect people nearby.

This history explains both the strength and fragility of his leadership.

He is unusually attentive because inattentiveness once had catastrophic consequences. He also experiences group fracture as evidence that he may be repeating the same failure.

## 11.6 Delegating to Ayanokōji

Hirata knows Ayanokōji is not ordinary. He sees the coldness and logic through which Ayanokōji deduces the false relationship. He still gives him Kei's history and asks for help.

The trust is understandable:

- Ayanokōji contributed during earlier crises;
- has no obvious public motive to exploit Kei;
- and appears capable of solutions Hirata cannot produce.

It is also a serious misjudgment. Hirata asks him not to hurt Kei but does not know enough to assess the method he is authorizing indirectly.

The episode reveals a vulnerability in benevolent delegation:

> a protector who recognizes his own limits may hand power to someone more effective without understanding the ethical system through which that effectiveness operates.

## 11.7 Contrast with Ayanokōji

| Dimension | Hirata | Ayanokōji |
|---|---|---|
| Protection | broad, publicly legitimate, nonexclusive | absolute, covert, conditional on obedience |
| Use of harm | refuses to harm another person for Kei | engineers harm to make himself indispensable |
| Information | shares under trust and requests restraint | withholds authorship and controls evidence |
| Goal | allow Kei to attend school safely | secure Kei as social intelligence and guarantee her safety |
| Exit structure | relationship is nominally protective and can theoretically end | relationship begins by making alternatives less viable |
| Ethical strength | respects competing human claims | can act decisively where moral hesitation prevents action |
| Ethical danger | may fail the person needing exclusive protection | transforms effective protection into private sovereignty |

Hirata's protection is less effective in the narrow crisis Ayanokōji constructs. It is more compatible with Kei eventually becoming free of the protector.

# 12. Ichinose Honami
## Trust as method, observation as politics, and restraint as strategy

Volume 4 is the first sustained demonstration that Ichinose's warmth and strategic competence are not opposing layers.

## 12.1 Facilitation without formal command

Ichinose begins by acting where others hesitate. She introduces herself, invites the others to do the same, and explicitly offers the facilitator role to anyone who wants it.

The offer is structurally clever. A student who takes the role assumes responsibility for the room and exposes intention. A student who refuses leaves Ichinose with legitimate authority because she has given others an opening.

Her leadership is therefore consensual in form without being politically empty.

## 12.2 The social pressure inside openness

Her early question about whether students favor Result 1 creates pressure toward a publicly virtuous answer. Ayanokōji sees the risk immediately: endorsing collective success may be less a revelation of truth than compliance with the room's emerging norm.

Ichinose does not become villainous because her question has this effect. The scene shows that even warm facilitation authors an environment. Every leader shapes what becomes easy or costly to say.

## 12.3 Cards as behavioral evidence

Ichinose later proposes playing cards. Katsuragi dismisses the activity as irrelevant to trust. The activity is useful precisely because it appears low stakes.

Games make visible:

- risk tolerance;
- competitiveness;
- attention;
- willingness to cooperate;
- social ease;
- and the relationships forming across class lines.

Ichinose's political ability lies partly in turning ordinary interaction into knowledge without making every participant feel interrogated.

## 12.4 Bounded honesty

She says openly that she is not the VIP and that, if she were, she would conceal it. This may appear contradictory, but it defines her ethics of exam-specific deception.

She does not claim that a competitive rule requiring secrecy eliminates all honesty. She distinguishes:

- necessary role concealment;
- from a broader personality organized around falsehood.

She also declines to force Class B's VIPs to identify themselves to her. The class's leader does not automatically possess every member's secret.

This is a meaningful contrast with Ryūen.

## 12.5 The phone-number trap

Ichinose anticipates the most obvious device swap. Because a phone number remains attached to the device/SIM relation, calling the number can reveal that the apparent owner has changed.

The first layer of Ayanokōji's operation is designed to be exposed. Yukimura appears to possess the VIP phone. When that identity collapses, the observer is encouraged to conclude that Ayanokōji must be the hidden VIP.

Ichinose refuses the convenient second answer.

She verifies through Hoshinomiya that points can be used to remove the SIM lock and concludes that the phone identity could have been rearranged more deeply. She reconstructs Kei as the true VIP.

Her crucial insight is epistemological:

> after a visible lie is exposed, people are unusually vulnerable to mistaking the next explanation for final truth.

## 12.6 Nonintervention as active strategy

Ichinose does not send the answer immediately. She predicts that another class may act first and that Class A's internal division makes the Sakayanagi faction especially likely to betray.

Waiting has several advantages:

- B avoids the penalty of a wrong answer;
- a rival may reveal its confidence;
- and B can benefit from information without bearing the cost of first action.

Her restraint is therefore not indecision. It is a deliberate choice to let adversaries expose themselves.

## 12.7 Ayanokōji's revised evaluation

Ayanokōji realizes that Ichinose and her allies reached the same broad phone-swap concept he used and then found its weakness. He also recognizes that she is more risk-averse and strategically cautious than the simple “everyone should trust one another” performance suggests.

This is one of Volume 4's most important corrections to surface judgment.

Ichinose's friendliness is real. It is also a high-level political technology.

## 12.8 Her limits at this boundary

Ichinose's strategy depends on a room in which voluntary contact remains possible. Ryūen's class-wide coercion and Katsuragi's disciplined refusal sit partly outside her preferred mode.

The unresolved question is not whether she is intelligent.

It is whether a constitution based on voluntary trust can defend itself against actors who centralize information through fear or refuse the social field entirely.

# 13. Ryūen Kakeru
## Coercive information sovereignty and the birth of the X hunt

## 13.1 Information before violence

Ryūen's core strength in this examination is not physical intimidation by itself.

He centralizes information, identifies the allocation pattern, coordinates timing, and studies the causal style behind Class D's island victory.

Violence is one instrument within a broader sovereignty over Class C.

## 13.2 Phone collection and nominal consent

Ryūen pressures classmates into surrendering phones.

Because none reports him, the school's anti-coercion rules do not become actionable.

The episode reveals the insufficiency of procedural consent:

> the institution sees no complaint; therefore it sees no violation.

Social fear disappears from the record.

## 13.3 The recording phone

Ryūen leaves a phone recording beneath the chair during the private discussion. Ayanokōji notices and silently directs Horikita's attention to it.

The scene extends the volume's attention to devices as autonomous observers. A phone can carry:

- institutional proof;
- false identity;
- covert recording;
- or strategic bait.

## 13.4 Why the lower-class alliance fails

Ryūen's proposal to combine B, C, and D information against A is strategically plausible.

It fails because his past conduct destroys confidence in future compliance.

A written pledge cannot solve the problem when:

- betrayal may remain anonymous;
- enforcement is unclear;
- and Ryūen has already demonstrated willingness to exploit allies.

Trust is not moral decoration around strategy. It is infrastructure required to make the strategy possible.

## 13.5 Horikita is the visible target

Ryūen correctly concludes that Horikita's island performance does not explain the whole operation. He suspects a hidden collaborator but still directs pressure toward her.

Horikita helps preserve the cover by exaggerating Ayanokōji's ordinary incompetence and framing him as her only weak social contact.

The public strategist becomes both shield and target.

## 13.6 The unresolved source of certainty

Ryūen knows Kushida is the Dragon group's target and somehow produces a final Result 1 without exposing her early.

Ayanokōji doubts that body language alone could make every outsider trust the correct answer. He suspects an `絶対な根拠 / absolute basis sufficient for trust`.

At the Volume 4 boundary, the exact mechanism remains unresolved.

Likewise, the exact allocation formula is not fully explained in a form the local reader can confidently reproduce from the text alone. The second pass records Ryūen's pattern discovery as fact and the precise rule as an open problem.

## 13.7 “Interesting” danger

Ryūen's success produces an unusual reaction in Ayanokōji: interest.

The hidden strategist has found someone capable of:

- reading his effects;
- attacking the decoy;
- producing an unexplained result;
- and creating uncertainty.

Competition is beginning to become emotionally engaging rather than merely a threat to concealment.

# 14. Horikita Suzune
## Public legitimacy, concealed dependence, and the limits of reputation

## 14.1 Living inside a victory she did not author

Volume 3 gives Horikita public credit for the island victory. Volume 4 shows the political consequences.

Other students now interpret her as:

- a class strategist;
- a dangerous opponent;
- and someone capable of elaborate deception.

The reputation is false in causal origin and real in social effect.

It gives Horikita influence she did not previously possess.

## 14.2 Reputation changes the meaning of the same behavior

Horikita remains cold, severe, and suspicious.

Before the island exam, those traits signified isolation and social failure.

After the victory, rivals can interpret them as:

- strategic reserve;
- confidence;
- or concealed calculation.

The visible behavior has not changed enough to explain the new authority. The narrative assigned to the behavior has changed.

## 14.3 Shielding Ayanokōji

When Ryūen suspects hidden assistance, Horikita deliberately emphasizes Ayanokōji as:

- weak;
- ordinary;
- dependent;
- and socially insignificant.

This is not merely insult. It is counterintelligence.

Her ability to support the cover demonstrates growth in adaptive deception.

## 14.4 The morning-meeting error

Ayanokōji and Horikita meet in a relatively observable space to compare emails and discuss the allocation wording. He realizes that repeated direct contact may expose the connection between her visible success and his hidden involvement.

The scene demonstrates a cost of their incomplete communication system.

Concealment requires distance. Effective cooperation requires contact.

They have not yet built a secure interface between those needs.

## 14.5 Horikita and Ichinose

Horikita initially underestimates the possibility that Ichinose's assignment reflects exceptional social rather than formal leader status. She says leader quality and general excellence do not necessarily coincide—a true statement that also exposes her own developing understanding.

Her encounter with Ichinose and Ryūen places three models side by side:

- Horikita's still-forming visible leadership;
- Ichinose's voluntary social coordination;
- Ryūen's coercive command.

## 14.6 Defeat without full understanding

Horikita believes the Dragon group has protected Kushida and reached a genuine cooperative outcome.

Ryūen tells her he knew the answer.

The defeat matters because Horikita's current authority is partly supported by the belief that she understands and controls the class's strategic environment. Ryūen reveals that an opponent allowed the visible outcome.

The end of Volume 4 therefore destabilizes the island myth without yet exposing Ayanokōji.

## 14.7 Ayanokōji's expanding network

Ayanokōji tells Horikita that Hirata has been brought into cooperation and that another person will be introduced.

She dislikes discovering that the hidden structure around “her” leadership is expanding beyond her knowledge.

Her discomfort is justified.

Ayanokōji is building a shadow constitution inside the class while Horikita bears visible political responsibility.

# 15. Kōenji, Yukimura, Kushida, Sakura, and the wider ensemble

## 15.1 Kōenji Rokusuke: insight without obligation

Kōenji correctly identifies a VIP and ends the Monkey-group examination early. He characterizes the problem as a simple test of detecting lies.

His success confirms extraordinary perception.

Its class benefit is accidental. He acts for personal satisfaction and does not coordinate with Class D. The correct answer happens to prevent Class C from sweeping more completely, but no one could have relied on him to produce it.

He remains the clearest example of possessed ability that refuses social conversion.

## 15.2 Yukimura Teruhiko: academic hierarchy in a social-information exam

Yukimura resents placement in Rabbit because he believes his academic record should put him among the Dragon group's elite students.

The grievance reveals a narrow theory of merit. In an exam built around:

- social reading;
- group trust;
- deception;
- and device identity,

his written-test strength gives limited authority.

He tries to impose order through logical insistence but lacks the network legitimacy of Ichinose, Hirata, Kei, or Ryūen. He also becomes a piece in Ayanokōji's phone deception without knowing the complete structure.

The volume therefore distinguishes academic intelligence from political usefulness without denying that Yukimura is genuinely intelligent.

## 15.3 Kushida Kikyō: social accessibility as strategic vulnerability

Kushida's broad popularity makes her an obvious trusted person and therefore a high-value VIP. Horikita tries to protect her. Ryūen claims to read Horikita's effort.

The volume also gives Ayanokōji a quiet night scene with Kushida beneath the stars. Her fragrance, physical closeness, and sudden embrace produce unmistakable bodily reaction and social panic in him. The scene preserves his adolescent sexuality and Kushida's capacity to make intimacy appear spontaneous.

The local text does not fully resolve her motive. The important point is that social fluency can simultaneously create:

- genuine comfort;
- information;
- and an opening for strategic interpretation.

## 15.4 Sakura Airi: ordinary friendship and safe disclosure

Ayanokōji continues supporting Sakura without turning the interaction into direct leverage. She shares group information, accepts encouragement, and responds warmly.

This relationship is analytically useful because it demonstrates that his social life is not uniformly coercive. He can create low-pressure contact, offer availability, and refrain from converting every vulnerability into ownership.

That capacity makes the Kei operation a choice rather than an unavoidable consequence of incapacity.

## 15.5 Ichinose asleep: privacy as a boundary he still recognizes

When Ichinose falls asleep beside him, Ayanokōji notices her body and briefly considers what information might be available through the phone. He ultimately rejects the invasive act because it feels wrong and excessively creepy.

The boundary is neither perfect nor universal. It is evidence that he can experience privacy as a reason not to optimize.

The contrast with Kei is severe. Ayanokōji does not operate from one fixed rule about bodily or informational privacy. He decides whose privacy remains protected according to context and objective.

## 15.6 Career dreams and Class A

Ayanokōji and Ichinose discuss the school's advertised near-perfect education and employment outcomes. He notes that institutional guarantee cannot make an untrained person a professional athlete merely through connection, and that only a minority of children realize their stated dreams.

The conversation links Class A to the broader question of what institutional power can actually guarantee. A rank can create access and security. It cannot manufacture every desired identity.

This becomes a quiet counterpoint to the exam's manipulability. Institutions can control records and opportunity more easily than they can determine the complete human future.

# 17. The school, teachers, and the private-point economy
## Neutral procedure with non-neutral design

## 17.1 The school as designer of distrust

The school does not directly order betrayal.

It makes betrayal:

- legal;
- profitable;
- anonymous;
- and class-relevant.

It then observes what students do.

This is a characteristic ANHS method: create a harmful option, call the resulting choice evidence of student ability, and keep institutional responsibility at one remove.

## 17.2 Anti-coercion rules and enforcement limits

The school warns that:

- stealing phones;
- forcing disclosure;
- and making false coercion claims

can result in severe punishment.

But enforcement requires legible complaint or evidence.

Ryūen's class does not report him. Ayanokōji plans operations around the absence of proof.

The rule protects students only when power can be translated into an institutional record.

## 17.3 The email as certified truth

The school's unalterable message creates a powerful epistemic object.

It gives the holder something close to perfect proof of target status.

Ayanokōji and Ichinose demonstrate that perfect proof of one proposition can still produce a false conclusion about another.

## 17.4 Private points can purchase rule changes

Ayanokōji asks Chabashira how far private points can alter school rules. Her answer confirms that even apparently fixed institutional conditions may be purchasable.

This changes the meaning of points.

They are not merely consumer currency or exam rewards. They are a constitutional resource capable of changing what the institution permits.

He avoids email because he does not want the question preserved as a record.

Even his inquiry into rules is shaped by surveillance consciousness.

## 17.5 Teacher motives and group design

Ayanokōji overhears Hoshinomiya, Chabashira, and Mashima. The conversation suggests that Ichinose's placement in the Rabbit group was not simply generated by neutral numerical sorting. Hoshinomiya wants her to learn something and may be probing Ayanokōji through her.

At the Volume 4 boundary, the exact degree of teacher authorship remains unclear.

What is clear is that teachers possess:

- old relationships;
- class ambitions;
- suspicions;
- and some discretion over the social environments students enter.

The exam is therefore not a transparent machine standing above personal motive.

# 19. Ethics and autonomy
## Beneficial outcomes do not erase authorship of harm

The volume's strongest defense of Ayanokōji is consequentialist.

His operation may:

- prevent future bullying;
- preserve Kei's secret;
- give her a powerful protector;
- create a more unified Class D;
- and deter Manabe.

It may also be argued that he recognizes a brutal reality Hirata's conciliatory approach cannot solve quickly.

These points matter.

They do not settle legitimacy.

## 19.1 Goal selection

Ayanokōji decides:

- Kei should become his collaborator;
- her current protective arrangement is insufficient;
- and breaking her is an acceptable route toward recruitment.

Kei does not participate in selecting the developmental goal.

## 19.2 Information asymmetry

He knows:

- the Manabe manipulation;
- the isolated location;
- Hirata's actual position;
- the evidence he holds;
- and the intended recruitment outcome.

Kei knows none of these before the operation.

## 19.3 Alternatives constrained

He does not first attempt:

- direct negotiation;
- gradual trust-building;
- recruitment through class interest;
- cooperation mediated transparently by Hirata;
- or protection without exclusive control.

The text does not prove those alternatives would have succeeded.

It proves he prefers a route that produces faster certainty and deeper leverage.

## 19.4 Foreseeable harm

The assault, retraumatization, sexual fear, humiliation, and dependency are not accidental side effects.

They are foreseeable and partly intended.

## 19.5 Later agency

The bargain may expand Kei's future ability to act:

- she gains information;
- protection;
- and a role in class politics.

At the Volume 4 endpoint, however, these future benefits remain conditional and unverified.

The immediate relation clearly reduces refusal capacity.

## 19.7 The strongest counterargument

- bullying will not disappear through idealistic dialogue;
- Hirata cannot offer exclusive protection;

# 20. Japanese narration, voice, and register

## 20.1 Ayanokōji's first-person range

Volume 4 gives unusually clear evidence that Ayanokōji's narration cannot be reduced to a single flat register.

### Ordinary comic register

The cruise-ship opening uses:

- exaggerated animal metaphors for adolescent sexuality;
- self-pity about having no romantic developments;
- social-rank jokes;
- Dragon Ball fantasy;
- and comic panic at Kushida's embrace.

This register is loose, culturally ordinary, and recognizably teenage.

### Strategic compression

When Ayanokōji decides he needs “eyes and ears,” the prose becomes short and logistical:

- time is insufficient;
- connections are insufficient;
- the current exam cannot be controlled;
- future infrastructure must be acquired.

### Clinical coercive register

During the assault, he converts:

- bodies;
- trauma;
- location;
- visible injury;
- and psychological collapse

into controlled variables.

The same `オレ` voice moves among embarrassment, desire, tactical calculation, and severe dehumanization without announcing a clean personality switch.

That continuity is analytically important. The coercive strategist is not hidden beneath a false adolescent voice. Both modes belong to the narrator.

## 20.2 Kei's public and private `あたし`

Kei's public voice uses casual, assertive `あたし` and frequent emotional directness.

In social control mode, she:

- interrupts;
- mocks;
- demands;
- and assumes the right to define the immediate atmosphere.

Under fear, the same voice loses fluency:

- repetition;
- incomplete clauses;
- denial;
- pleading;
- and bodily reaction replace status performance.

Her monologue is more controlled than her crisis speech. It has the bleak coherence of someone who has turned trauma into a doctrine of survival.

The key contrast is not public lie versus private truth in a simple sense.

The public Kei is a real competence built to protect the private Kei.

## 20.3 Hirata's `僕` and the language of nonexclusive care

Hirata's `僕` register is gentle, explanatory, and normatively consistent.

He frequently:

- asks rather than commands;
- names other people's feelings;
- and frames disagreement as something to solve together.

His refusal of retaliation remains linguistically calm. He does not humiliate Kei for wanting revenge. He says the request violates the kind of protection he can offer.

His phrase that Kei is one precious classmate rather than an exclusively privileged romantic partner makes his politics audible: care is distributed through class membership rather than possession.

## 20.4 Ichinose's inclusive authority

Ichinose's language invites participation while steadily setting the group's agenda.

She often uses questions whose social function is double:

- sincere request for preference;
- and diagnostic observation of the answer.

Her politeness does not diminish authority. It creates the legitimacy through which authority is accepted voluntarily.

## 20.5 Ryūen's proprietary speech

Ryūen's language is rough, amused, and deliberately invasive.

He treats:

- other people's plans;
- class information;
- and even physical space

as things he may enter without permission.

His visible linguistic dominance matches his political method. He wants students to know that the central decision belongs to him.

## 20.6 Horikita's polished judgment

Horikita continues using controlled, complete, often feminine declarative forms. Her criticism of Kei arrives as a settled evaluation: defects, selfishness, no useful reason.

Ayanokōji's challenge exposes the limit of that voice. Polished judgment can sound complete before the underlying social system has been investigated.

## 20.7 Address and distance

The volume remains largely surname-based in the central relationships:

- `軽井沢`;
- `平田`;
- `堀北`;
- `一之瀬`;
- `龍園`.

That linguistic distance matters. Ayanokōji has acquired access to Kei's most private wound before the relationship has accumulated ordinary intimacy or reciprocal naming.

The informational distance has collapsed.

# 21. Appearance, illustration, and embodied characterization

## 21.1 Visual inventory

The EPUB contains twenty-five raster resources. The analytically relevant visual set includes:

- four color front-matter illustrations;
- four student-database inserts;
- ten monochrome narrative illustrations;
- cover/title/contents/branding material;
- and a short bonus-manga sequence.

## 21.2 Kei dominates the visual volume

The cover and color illustrations foreground Kei as:

- fashionable;
- confident;
- expressive;
- and visually central beside Ayanokōji.

That presentation establishes the public gyaru identity before the monologue reveals the scar, nausea, and parasite self-theory.

The monochrome sequence progressively strips that confidence away.

She is shown through:

- phone-centered social performance;
- surprise and defensive posture;
- restraint and exposure;
- the scar;
- and post-assault physical contraction.

The visual arc makes the collapse of status bodily.

## 21.3 The scar and the body as contested record

Kei's scar is something she wants to keep outside social visibility.

The institution's formal record does not contain the whole person. The body does.

Ayanokōji's exposure of the scar therefore functions as:

- confirmation;
- invasion;
- and seizure of private evidence.

## 21.4 The coercion illustration

The visual framing of Ayanokōji and Kei during the sexualized threat gives the scene strong dramatic and eroticized charge.

This creates a serious paratextual tension.

The prose establishes:

- credible fear of sexual assault;
- physical restraint;
- and consent collapse.

An illustration that aestheticizes proximity or vulnerability can make the scene memorable while weakening attention to the victim's lack of control.

The image should therefore be treated as evidence of editorial emphasis, not as moral neutralization.

## 21.5 Hirata's embodied composure

Hirata's appearance remains orderly even after waking in the middle of the night. Ayanokōji reads the grooming not as vanity but as consideration for people who may see him.

This small embodied detail supports his service orientation. His public competence is maintained through continuous attention to other people's comfort.

## 21.6 Database inserts and institutional legibility

The profile-like inserts frame students as measurable information objects.

Volume 4 simultaneously demonstrates the inadequacy of such files:

- Kei's visible ratings would not explain her social field control;
- Ayanokōji's official mediocrity actively deceives Ryūen;
- Hirata's public ideal-boy status conceals atonement and a false romance;
- Ichinose's approachable presentation conceals strategic counterintelligence.

The paratext therefore participates in the series' legibility problem even before *First File* makes the dossier aesthetic explicit.

# 22. Motifs and recurring language

## 22.1 Parasite and host

`寄生虫` converts relationship into survival biology.

A host supplies:

- safety;
- status;
- and continued life.

The metaphor excludes reciprocity almost by definition. It prepares Ayanokōji to understand protection as infrastructure rather than mutual care.

## 22.2 Eyes and ears

The phrase turns socially connected people into sensory extensions of the hidden strategist.

This is a political-body metaphor:

- Ayanokōji as concealed mind;
- socially central students as organs;
- class action as the body produced between them.

## 22.3 Masks, surface, and layers

The volume repeatedly distinguishes:

- appearance;
- visible personality;
- hidden motive;
- and a second hidden interpretation.

Ayanokōji explicitly says nearly everyone divides surface and reverse. The claim applies to him most of all.

## 22.4 Protection

`守る / protect` appears through incompatible models.

- Hirata protects without harming outsiders.
- Kei understands protection as absolute priority.
- Ayanokōji protects through control and deterrence.
- the school protects through formal rules that fail when victims cannot report safely.

The word is therefore ethically incomplete without asking:

> by what method, from what threat, at whose cost, and with what exit capacity?

## 22.5 Darkness

`闇 / darkness` creates intimacy through shared hiddenness.

Ayanokōji uses it to suggest kinship with Kei. The metaphor can recognize stigma without disgust.

It also allows him to imply reciprocity without disclosing equivalent information.

## 22.6 Strict adjustment

`厳正なる調整` presents the favored-person allocation as neutral institutional balancing.

Ryūen treats the phrase as evidence of an underlying pattern.

The official language of strictness and fairness becomes an attack surface for someone who assumes that administrative regularity must leave a detectable structure.

## 22.7 Double question

Every apparent answer creates a further question:

- Who is the favored person?
- Who wants you to think that?
- Who is Hirata to Kei?
- What does Kei's dominance protect?
- Is Ayanokōji offering rescue or ownership?
- Is ordinary adolescent desire evidence against strategic cruelty, or evidence that they coexist?

# 23. Formal and literary strengths

## 23.1 The Kei monologue changes the moral order of the volume

Opening with Kei prevents her from existing only as an object of Ayanokōji's analysis.

The reader knows before he does that:

- the scar matters;
- the public self is defensive;
- and protection is her governing need.

This produces dramatic irony and makes his later accuracy ethically more disturbing rather than more impressive.

## 23.2 Exam mechanics and relationship mechanics mirror one another

The favored person is:

- privileged;
- hidden;
- vulnerable to betrayal;
- and protected only through information control.

Kei occupies the same structure socially.

Her secret gives her special vulnerability; the person controlling the information can either protect or betray her.

## 23.3 The phone strategy embodies the volume's theory of identity

The strategy is not attached to the themes from outside. It formally reproduces them.

Visible user, device identity, true user, apparent hidden user, and actual favored person are separated into layers. The exam solution becomes a miniature of the characters' social masks.

## 23.4 Ayanokōji's voice preserves contradiction

The source does not force readers to choose between:

- comic adolescent;
- lonely student;
- romantic observer;
- analytical strategist;
- and coercive manipulator.

The power of the first-person form comes from making them coexist without immediate reconciliation.

## 23.5 Hirata receives independent moral intelligence

Hirata is not merely the nice boy whose methods fail so the protagonist can act.

He:

- recognizes the problem;
- identifies his own limit;
- understands that Ayanokōji is unusual;
- preserves privacy;
- refuses retaliatory violence;
- and delegates deliberately.

The volume gives the kinder model real strength before showing its practical limit.

## 23.6 Ichinose complicates the trust-versus-intelligence binary

Her detection of the phone plan prevents the novel from equating warmth with strategic inferiority.

She is both:

- socially open;
- and analytically dangerous.

## 23.7 The final score does not exhaust the result

Class D gains only 50 class points, but Ayanokōji acquires:

- Kei;
- stronger access to Hirata;
- information about Ryūen;
- and confirmation of Ichinose's ability.

The volume teaches the reader to distinguish official examination result from political aftermath.

# 24. Critical reservations and strongest counterarguments

## 24.1 The Milgram account is too neat

The narration uses Milgram to explain escalation as situational cruelty.

The analogy is rhetorically effective but simplified. It can make the assault appear like an objective psychological demonstration rather than an event whose central conditions were deliberately manufactured by Ayanokōji.

## 24.2 The sexual threat risks being treated as cleverness

Because Ayanokōji privately knows he is using the threat instrumentally, readers may be encouraged to classify the scene as a bluff rather than sexual violence.

The relevant moral perspective includes Kei's credible belief that assault may occur.

## 24.3 The successful outcome can retroactively sanitize the method

Kei gains protection and strategic significance.

That later benefit does not establish that the assault, non-rescue, and coercion were necessary.

The text provides no controlled comparison with a less harmful intervention.

## 24.4 Hirata's limitation can be overstated

It is tempting to say Hirata “cannot protect Kei.”

More precisely, he cannot provide the form of protection she demands:

- retaliatory;
- exclusive;
- and unconstrained by the rights of others.

That is a strategic limitation and an ethical strength.

## 24.5 Ichinose's openness can be romanticized

Her consensual leadership is impressive. It works partly because she has already accumulated reputation, social skill, and unusually loyal classmates.

A less trusted student could not reproduce the strategy simply by “being honest.”

## 24.6 Institutional rules are selectively realistic

The school writes detailed anti-coercion provisions while apparently accepting internal phone seizures and high-risk mixed-group conflict as long as no formal complaint reaches it.

The mechanism is thematically productive but institutionally severe.

## 24.7 Kei's victimization can become protagonist-centered

The scene is crucial to Ayanokōji's development and strategy. That framing risks treating Kei's trauma primarily as evidence of his power.

The opening monologue partially resists this reduction by giving her interiority first.

## 24.8 Strongest counterargument to the protector-monopoly thesis

A counterargument is that Ayanokōji does not create Kei's dependence; he recognizes a dependency already governing her life and replaces a protector unable to give her the needed security. He deters Manabe, does not expose the scar publicly, and offers operational reciprocity rather than passive victimhood.

This evidence matters. It shows that the relationship is not only imprisonment.

The thesis still holds because Ayanokōji deliberately destroys alternatives, withholds rescue, and uses threats Kei cannot safely distinguish from real intended harm. He does not merely become the best available protector. He engineers the comparison under which every other protector appears inadequate.

# 28. Cumulative delta through Volume 4

Volume 4 does not replace the first three volumes' model. It intensifies and reorganizes it.

## Ayanokōji delta

**Volume 1:** Ayanokōji authors what the institution and his classmates are permitted to measure. He hides ability, controls credit, and preserves ordinary school life through selective intervention.

**Volume 2:** He controls legibility: which truth becomes evidence, which person appears credible, and which threat becomes actionable.

**Volume 3:** He escalates into environmental authorship: shaping bodies, information, scarcity, and attribution so other people choose and develop inside conditions he has concealed.

**Volume 4:** He applies that architecture directly to an intimate relationship. He identifies a socially powerful but traumatized student, destroys confidence in existing protection, authorizes a new assault, delays rescue, introduces sexualized coercion, and then becomes the most credible source of future safety.

The progression is therefore:

> **authored visibility → authored legibility → authored environment → authored dependency.**

At the same time, Volume 4 offers some of the strongest evidence yet that his ordinary self is not an empty disguise. He wants friends, feels lonely, notices women, is moved by stars, experiences bodily warmth, and enjoys low-pressure companionship.

The ethical danger grows precisely because his human perception grows. He can understand what another person needs and convert that knowledge into both care and leverage.

## Horikita and class-leadership delta

Horikita is less centrally active than in Volume 3, but her political importance rises.

- The island victory has created a public image of her as Class D's strategist.
- Ryūen uses that image as the starting point for his hunt.
- She protects Ayanokōji by reinforcing the interpretation that he is merely her assistant.
- She notices structural clues such as `厳正な調整` and understands that public masks matter.
- Her legitimacy is now real enough to attract enemies even though the original victory narrative was incomplete.

This sharpens the earlier concern:

> **Manufactured legitimacy can become genuine authority before the person fully understands how the legitimacy was manufactured.**

Class D also begins developing distributed capacity:

- Hirata provides visible social legitimacy;
- Horikita provides public strategic identity;
- Yukimura provides academic/analytical seriousness;
- Kei becomes hidden social infrastructure;
- Ayanokōji controls the concealed architecture.

The class is becoming more powerful and more dependent upon informational asymmetry at the same time.

## Relationship delta

### Ayanokōji–Kei

The relationship begins as observation and becomes coercive recruitment. By the endpoint:

- he knows her hidden past and scar;
- she knows he is dangerous and unusually perceptive;
- he possesses evidence against Manabe;
- she possesses a socially useful network;
- he promises protection;
- she agrees to cooperate;
- her ability to refuse safely is severely limited.

The relationship is real, consequential, and radically unequal.

### Kei–Hirata

The false relationship remains a meaningful protective structure. Hirata's refusal of retaliation reveals the limit of universalist protection when the protected person demands exclusive sovereignty. His ethical boundary creates the opening Ayanokōji exploits.

### Ayanokōji–Hirata

Hirata voluntarily entrusts a private problem to Ayanokōji after recognizing both competence and danger. Ayanokōji's outward advice about direct cooperation is healthier than the hidden relationship he then builds with Kei.

### Ayanokōji–Horikita

Their cover arrangement becomes more politically consequential. Horikita protects his invisibility; he continues deciding what she is allowed to know about the threats produced by her public reputation.

### Ayanokōji–Ichinose

He develops genuine respect for her ability while remaining physically attracted to her. She sees through his layered deception more completely than most students, making her both an appealing social presence and a serious strategic observer.

### Ayanokōji–Ryūen

Ryūen becomes the first student adversary whose pursuit directly threatens Ayanokōji's hidden authorship. Ayanokōji is alarmed by his success and fascinated by the possibility of a worthy opponent.

## Institutional delta

The school now appears as a designer of **trust markets**.

It does not merely ask whether students can identify a secret. It assigns numerical consequences to:

- collective patience;
- favored-person selfishness;
- correct betrayal;
- incorrect accusation;
- phone identity;
- silence;
- and class reputation.

Teachers also shape the experimental population by placing conspicuous leaders into selected groups.

Private points acquire a new constitutional meaning: they can alter rules, conceal arrangements, and potentially purchase exceptions. The school is not a closed rule system. It is a polity in which rules may be negotiable for actors with sufficient resources and information.

Formal anti-coercion rules exist, but their protection depends upon reportable evidence and willingness to complain. Ryūen's internal domination and Kei's inability to expose harm show how private power can survive beneath formally protective procedure.

## Ethics and autonomy delta

The cumulative Year 1 intervention taxonomy now needs at least four categories:

1. **voluntary scaffolding** — assistance that leaves the subject's goal and refusal capacity substantially intact;
2. **bounded strategic influence** — concealed assistance or information control that affects choices without directly imposing bodily harm;
3. **coercive environmental authorship** — manufactured conditions, withheld rescue, or manipulated third parties that impose the developmental process;
4. **dependency authorship** — creating or monopolizing safety so that future cooperation becomes psychologically difficult to refuse.

Volume 4 enters the fourth category.

The governing ethical question is no longer only:

> Did the person become stronger afterward?

It is:

> **Did the intervention enlarge the person's ability to author later choices, or did it make the intervener the condition under which choice remains safe?**

## Ability and merit delta

The volume expands `実力` from ecological competence into **networked and political ability**.

Kei's most important capacity is not academic, physical, or formally measured. It is the ability to control atmosphere, status, access, and the girls' social network.

Hirata's ability is legitimacy: people trust his intentions and accept his mediation.

Ichinose's ability is consensual coalition-building across formal class boundaries.

Ryūen's is coercive coordination and information centralization.

Ayanokōji's limitation is not computation but reach. He needs eyes and ears.

This produces a stronger Year 1 thesis:

> **An isolated genius may understand the board but still require social actors who can make the board move.**

## Truth, proof, and record delta

Volume 2 distinguished event truth, evidence, credibility, and enforceable record.

Volume 3 showed that real objects and real pain can be arranged to produce false causal attribution.

Volume 4 adds identity layering:

- a genuine school email can be attached to a phone no longer carried by the expected person;
- a phone number can be preserved while the device changes;
- the apparent hidden strategist can be another decoy;
- public reputations can become shields or targets;
- silence can appear to institutional authority as consensual non-complaint.

The central epistemic problem becomes:

> **What does evidence prove when the identity linking person, device, role, and intention can itself be engineered?**

## Ordinary-life delta

Volume 4 places some of Ayanokōji's most human observations beside his most severe operation.

He wants friends and romance. He is moved by real stars. He experiences Kushida's warmth and Ichinose's attractiveness. He finds comfort in Yukimura's room.

The ordinary world is therefore not yet a moral cure.

It gives him more reasons to value life at school and more detailed knowledge of what other people need. That knowledge can support intimacy. It can also make manipulation more precise.

# 30. Final synthesis

Volume 4 transforms the political meaning of trust.

On the island, Class D's problem was whether fragmented people could become a functioning group under scarcity. The answer was partially yes. Ike's practical knowledge mattered. Hirata coordinated. Horikita bore responsibility. Ayanokōji secretly authored the decisive conditions. The class won through a mixture of real cooperation and false attribution.

The zodiac examination takes that new possibility and places a price on betraying it.

The school tells students to become Rabbit, Dragon, or another temporary collective rather than Class A, B, C, or D. It promises a shared reward if everyone reaches the answer together. It simultaneously pays more decisive political rewards to the student who identifies the favored person early and sends the correct accusation.

The examination is therefore not internally confused. Its contradiction is the curriculum.

Students are asked to discover whether they can create trust while knowing trust has an exchange value.

That design exposes four visible political answers.

Katsuragi protects Class A by refusing engagement. He recognizes that a leading class benefits most from preserving the status quo and avoiding unnecessary information exposure.

Ichinose tries to create a temporary consensual polity. She gives people room, uses games and conversation to make one another legible, proposes the outcome that benefits the group, and refuses to become the betrayer herself. Her openness is not stupidity. It is a bounded political technology dependent upon accumulated trust.

Ryūen centralizes phones and information through fear. He eliminates the uncertainty of voluntary choice by making disobedience personally dangerous. His class acts effectively because one person claims sovereignty over everyone else's privacy.

Ayanokōji builds a deception whose deepest protected layer is not himself but Kei. Device, phone number, SIM identity, visible carrier, hidden planner, and true favored person are separated. The result is a strategy that can survive an opponent seeing through the first trick because the first trick is itself bait.

But the volume's most important political system is not one of these class strategies.

It is the relationship Ayanokōji constructs with Kei.

Kei's opening monologue explains the problem before he enters it. She does not think school can transform her. She does not value youth or friendship. She understands herself as a parasite who must attach to someone strong enough to keep her safe.

That self-understanding was created through real harm. Her scar is not a metaphor. Her body reacts to memory before political calculation can stabilize it. Social hierarchy is not vanity for her. It is a technology developed after isolation became dangerous.

Hirata already protects her. His false romantic relationship gives her status and discourages attack. His care is real. It is also bounded. He will not assault Manabe's group on Kei's behalf. He will not make her safety the only moral claim in the class. When she asks whether he will protect her, he answers through a universal commitment to everyone.

Ayanokōji hears the difference.

He does not merely recognize that Hirata's protection is insufficient for Kei's psychology. He makes that insufficiency decisive.

He lies to Manabe, revives the conflict, selects an isolated location, uses Hirata's name to draw Kei there, prevents Yukimura from intervening, watches the assault, and explicitly decides not to help. He leaves while the violence is still continuing because a more complete break will require less work to reconstruct.

Then he returns.

The timing converts him from observer into apparent rescuer, though he did not stop the harm and helped create it. He reveals knowledge of Kei's hidden past, demonstrates his power to expose her, subjects her to sexualized coercion she cannot safely dismiss as bluff, examines the scar, and finally offers the thing she most wants: protection more absolute than Hirata's.

His bargain is effective.

It deters Manabe through evidence. It gives Kei a role rather than leaving her passive. It recognizes her genuine social power. It helps Class D gain points. It creates a relationship that may later develop in directions neither person can yet understand.

None of that changes the origin.

The decisive sentence is not merely that he will protect her.

It is his conclusion that she has found a new host and that her path has narrowed to one.

This is protection understood as monopoly.

The person who creates the strongest safety also controls the conditions under which safety is distributed.

That structure makes Volume 4 the first genuinely intimate expression of the Year 1 conflict between development and control.

Ayanokōji does not recruit Kei because he sees no humanity in her. The opposite is true. He notices more than the school measures.

He sees:

- the social intelligence beneath the gyaru performance;
- the trauma beneath dominance;
- the fear beneath aggression;
- the capacity to govern a female network;
- and the exact form of safety she is unable to provide herself.

His cognitive empathy is exceptional.

The ethical problem is what he believes that understanding authorizes him to do.

The volume gives us an Ayanokōji who is simultaneously more adolescent and more dangerous than a flat mastermind reading allows. He worries about friends. He wants romance. He is moved by the real sky. He panics at Kushida's bodily warmth. He notices Ichinose's attractiveness. He finds comfort in a classmate's room.

These experiences are not false.

They do not yet restrain his methods.

Ordinary life expands the range of things he can value. It also gives him more precise knowledge of loneliness, attraction, trust, shame, and the desire to belong. That knowledge can become the basis of reciprocal intimacy. In Volume 4, it also becomes the basis of intimate control.

The same contradiction appears at the institutional level.

Advanced Nurturing High School claims to test thinking. It creates mixed groups, temporary identities, anonymous communication, and carefully priced outcomes. It prohibits coercion and phone theft. Yet its protection depends on complaints that vulnerable students may be unable to make. It creates a market in trust, then treats the resulting betrayals as evidence of ability.

Ayanokōji is both victim and imitator of this system.

Chabashira threatens the school life he values so that he will use his ability for her objective. He, in turn, makes protection and exposure the conditions under which Kei will use her ability for his.

The purposes differ.

The structure rhymes.

This does not make Ayanokōji identical to Chabashira, Ryūen, or the school. Hirata voluntarily entrusts him with a problem. Kei receives real deterrence and a path to political action. Ayanokōji does not expose her publicly or abandon her after gaining information.

But benevolent or developmentally useful outcome does not settle moral authority.

Volume 4 asks the reader to hold both conclusions at once:

> **Kei is not merely victimized into passivity; she becomes a consequential political actor.**

And:

> **The person who makes that political role possible has engineered the terror through which her cooperation becomes difficult to refuse.**

This is why `寄生虫` cannot be allowed to become the final word about Kei.

It is the word through which she has learned to explain survival.

It is also the word Ayanokōji uses to make his own authorship appear inevitable.

The more challenging interpretation is that Kei's future agency may eventually exceed both descriptions.

At the Volume 4 endpoint, however, that possibility remains open rather than achieved.

The exam's final results matter because they establish the four classes' political capacities. Class B preserves itself through trust. Class C gains heavily through coercive coordination. Class A is punished by the wrong accusation. Class D gains modestly through layered deception.

The more lasting result is relational.

Ayanokōji now has the eyes and ears he said he lacked.

Kei now has the strongest protector she believes available.

Hirata has delegated a problem he could not solve within his ethical limits.

Horikita's public myth has become a target.

Ryūen has begun hunting the hidden source behind it.

The opening phase of Year 1 is therefore complete.

Ayanokōji has moved from hiding his own ability to building a private social infrastructure through which he can act across the class.

The unresolved question is no longer whether he can make other people useful.

It is whether anyone drawn into that infrastructure can become close to him without becoming dependent upon the conditions he controls.
