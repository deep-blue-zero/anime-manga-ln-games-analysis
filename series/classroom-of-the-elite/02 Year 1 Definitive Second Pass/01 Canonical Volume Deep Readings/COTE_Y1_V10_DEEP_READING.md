# 1. Executive thesis

Volume 10 is the first Year 1 volume in which the school openly says that **someone must be made disposable**.

Previous special examinations create danger, scarcity, conflict, deception, reputational exposure, and the possibility of expulsion. The Class Vote is different. The administration explicitly creates the examination because the first-year cohort has reached this point without losing a student. The absence of expulsion becomes the condition to be corrected.

The students are not primarily asked:

> Who knows the most?

or:

> Who can run the fastest?

or even:

> Which class can outmaneuver another class?

They are asked:

> **Which person among you should lose the right to continue?**

The examination therefore turns Year 1’s recurring argument about `実力 / jitsuryoku` into a political and ethical problem. Once continuation itself becomes scarce, different class constitutions reveal different answers to the question of human value:

- **Hirata** rejects the legitimacy of ranking classmates for sacrifice and ultimately offers himself instead.
- **Horikita** accepts that a decision is unavoidable and argues that leadership requires publicly owning a reasoned judgment.
- **Ichinose** refuses to convert differential ability into differential entitlement to remain, first seeking collective financial rescue and holding a lottery as the egalitarian fallback.
- **Sakayanagi** treats the vote as sovereign pruning: preserve strategically useful pieces, remove politically costly ones, conceal the true target when necessary.
- **Kōenji** openly argues for forward-looking latent capacity rather than sentiment or visible past contribution.
- **Ayanokōji** treats the exam as a rare chance to remove low-future-value members, while quietly protecting people he regards as strategically or relationally important.

The novel does **not** allow any one of these positions to become clean doctrine.

Its most important counterexample appears before the Class Vote even begins. Sudō’s academic improvement is now real enough that Horikita credits **his own effort**, and Chabashira observes that children’s growth is fascinating precisely because she cannot reliably predict who will develop:

> `誰が伸びてくるのか読み切れない。私の予想を簡単に裏切ってくる。`

That statement sits in direct tension with Ayanokōji’s later pruning logic. He knows that everyone may develop, but he also reasons that some people bloom late, some have narrow developmental ceilings, and therefore the vote should be used to remove an unnecessary student.

This is the volume’s hardest problem:

> **How can anyone justly terminate another person’s developmental future when developmental future is itself uncertain?**

The story gives no painless answer because the institution has intentionally removed the painless answer.

Horikita’s emergence as a leader matters for exactly that reason. Her achievement is not that she discovers an objectively correct human ranking. She does not. Her achievement is that she refuses to hide the judgment inside anonymous blocs. She names Yamauchi publicly, explains her criteria, exposes the betrayal behind his anti-Ayanokōji coalition, accepts that the class may retaliate against her, and thereby makes herself politically answerable for the decision.

This distinction is central:

> **Ayanokōji supplies concealed information and shapes the conditions under which the intervention becomes possible; Horikita authors the public justification and accepts visible responsibility for it.**

Manabu’s role strengthens rather than weakens her independence. Suzune asks him not for a tactical answer but for `勇気 / courage`. He tells her that she will not catch Ayanokōji—and, more importantly, that she does not need to:

> `おまえはおまえらしく成長すればいい`
>
> Grow as yourself.

That advice is explicitly anti-imitation. Volume 10 therefore marks one of Horikita’s strongest steps from **being developed** toward **becoming a source of political judgment in her own right**.

But the volume refuses to let visible leadership exhaust the problem. Ayanokōji is still hidden behind several outcomes:

- he passes the Yamauchi–Sakayanagi information chain toward Horikita;
- he correctly infers that Sakayanagi’s contradictory behavior means A-class praise votes will protect him;
- he uses Horikita’s intervention to redirect C-class criticism toward Yamauchi;
- he helps preserve Hirata by supporting the circulation of praise votes;
- he constructs a cross-class transaction that saves Ryūen while redirecting D-class expulsion toward Manabe;
- he uses that transaction to build further trust with Ichinose;
- and he privately recognizes that removing Manabe would also reduce Kei’s anxiety and could deepen Kei’s trust in him.

The last point is ethically severe. Protection, retribution, and dependency management become entangled. A person’s expulsion can become not only a class-political outcome but a resource in Ayanokōji’s private relationship architecture.

Ichinose provides the volume’s clearest countermodel. Nagumo offers the missing private points on the condition that she date him. She nearly accepts because she values classmates above herself, but her narration identifies the bargain as a threat to identity:

> `自分が自分でなくなってしまう`

Ayanokōji’s cross-class transaction gives her an alternative. It is strategically useful to him—he explicitly values the event for building trust with Ichinose—but it also preserves her ability to refuse an unwanted romantic relationship. The result is therefore both **instrumentalized by Ayanokōji** and **autonomy-enhancing for Ichinose**. Those propositions are not mutually exclusive.

Finally, the closing Tsukishiro confrontation changes the institutional frame. The Class Vote initially presents itself as a harsh but formally neutral developmental exercise. The ending reveals that a person connected to the administration has ordered Sakayanagi to expel Ayanokōji and that an earlier version of the exam reportedly would have allowed cross-class criticism votes—an architecture vastly easier to weaponize against one designated target. Tsukishiro then demonstrates the same logic physically: he replaces surveillance footage, uses official position as coercive leverage, and tries to make retaliation dangerous because any selected recording could be used against Ayanokōji.

The volume therefore closes on a reversal:

> **The institution that claims to teach students how to judge expendability may itself be captured by adults who have already decided which student should be expendable.**

Volume 10 is not simply “the expulsion volume.” It is a study of **manufactured scarcity, political responsibility, competing theories of human value, and the difference between being judged by a rule and having the rule itself authored against you**.

# 2. Position in the Year 1 architecture

The Year 1 progression through Volume 10 can now be stated as a sequence of increasingly powerful forms of authorship:

> **visibility → legibility → environment → dependency → counter-curriculum → controlled exposure → distributed integration → futurity → negotiated indispensability → institutional authorship → reputation → disposability**

Volume 9 showed that power belongs partly to whoever controls what social facts mean. Volume 10 asks the next question:

> What happens when the institution converts those meanings into the legal-administrative power to end someone’s participation altogether?

Several earlier threads converge here.

### `EXPULSION_DISPOSABILITY`

Expulsion ceases to be an exceptional penalty attached to failure. It becomes an administratively required output.

### `JITSURYOKU`

Ability can no longer be discussed as a simple present score. Students must decide whether they are judging:

- past contribution;
- current measurable performance;
- discipline;
- trustworthiness;
- political loyalty;
- future potential;
- social utility;
- or equal human entitlement to continue.

### `HORIKITA_LEADERSHIP`

Horikita moves from solving exams to publicly governing a class under moral scarcity.

### `HIRATA_CARE_LEADERSHIP`

The premise that nobody may be sacrificed is tested against a mechanism that makes non-sacrifice impossible without extraordinary money.

### `ICHINOSE_SOLIDARITY`

Class B converts solidarity into a constitution strong enough to accept either universal financial sacrifice or random equal risk.

### `AYANOKOJI_AUTHORSHIP`

Ayanokōji becomes increasingly capable of determining *which decision problem other people will face* without occupying the visible office that owns the decision.

### `ANHS_EXPERIMENT`

The school itself becomes less epistemically trustworthy. By the end of the volume, institutional design can no longer automatically be treated as pedagogically neutral.

# 3. Narrative architecture

The volume’s chapter order is unusually disciplined. It begins inside the psyche of the character least capable of surviving the coming examination and ends with an adult who reveals that the examination system itself may be subject to external capture.

| Spine | Chapter | Narrative function |
|---|---|---|
| S11 | `平田洋介の独白` | Hirata’s trauma genealogy; harmony as a reaction to an earlier coercive failure |
| S12 | `嵐の前の静けさ` | ordinary development before crisis; Sudō growth; sibling comparison; time moving differently for Ayanokōji |
| S13 | `クラス内投票` | formal mechanism; manufactured expulsion; initial class strategies; pruning theories |
| S14 | `救う難しさ` | relational consequences; Hirata, Ichinose, Nagumo, Ayanokōji’s rescue analysis |
| S15 | `兄と妹` | Suzune seeks courage; Manabu redirects her from imitation toward self-authorship |
| S16 | `善と悪` | C-class public confrontation; Yamauchi exposure; Hirata rupture; judgment under scarcity |
| S17 | `他クラスの考え` | D-, B-, and A-class constitutions revealed from inside; Sakayanagi and Nagumo manipulation |
| S18 | `退学者たち` | results, three expulsions, Ryūen rescue, B-class rescue, political aftermath, Tsukishiro institutional capture |

This architecture produces a significant formal movement:

> **private trauma → public rule → competing constitutions → irreversible outcome → exposure of the rulemaker.**

The prologue ensures that Hirata’s later collapse is not a plot twist attached to kindness. It is the return of a self he has been organizing his entire school identity to prevent.

The ending similarly ensures that the school’s brutality cannot be read only as “hard realism.” The adult intervention forces a second-order question: who gets to design the test that is supposedly revealing student worth?

# 4. The Class Vote as institutional mechanism

The Class Vote is simple enough mechanically that almost all of its complexity lies in the social field it creates.

Each student receives:

- **three praise votes** for classmates;
- **three criticism votes** for classmates;
- **one praise vote** for a student in another class.

Praise and criticism cancel. Ballots are anonymous. Students may not vote for themselves or duplicate the same person. The class’s net top scorer receives a non-transferable **Protection Point**. The net bottom scorer is expelled. Ties are rerun until a unique top and bottom exist.

The crucial administrative statement is that the examination exists to resolve `退学者の不在`—the absence of expellees.

That changes its ethical category.

A normal examination can be defended as measuring competence under a rule that may result in failure. Here, **expulsion is the required product**. The institution begins with the outcome “one person must leave” and then constructs a voting process to allocate that outcome.

## 4.1 The Protection Point

The `プロテクトポイント` is formally the opposite of expulsion: a one-use right to nullify a future expulsion penalty. Chabashira estimates its practical value at roughly twenty million private points, the same order of magnitude as the existing monetary escape from expulsion.

It therefore does two things at once:

1. makes continuation explicitly quantifiable;
2. gives the student most positively judged by peers an unequal future claim to survival.

The exam does not merely rank popularity. It converts popularity, usefulness, cross-class reputation, and political coordination into **differential security of personhood within the institution**.

Ayanokōji ultimately receives this asset because Sakayanagi’s A class overwhelmingly spends its external praise votes on him. An attempted expulsion environment therefore paradoxically leaves him harder to expel than before.

## 4.2 Anonymity and responsibility

The school makes individual ballots permanently anonymous. That design protects students from direct retaliation but also makes moral responsibility diffuse. Students can help expel someone without ever publicly owning the act.

Horikita’s later choice to name Yamauchi in open class discussion matters partly because she refuses that anonymity at the level of leadership. She cannot make each ballot public, but she can make **her own judgment** public.

## 4.3 Private points as a second constitution

The twenty-million-point exception means the vote is not absolute. A sufficiently capitalized community can buy its way out.

That makes private points more than currency. They become stored **continuation capacity**.

Class B’s year-long habit of pooling points—previously suspicious to outsiders—suddenly becomes a constitutional reserve capable of preventing expulsion. Ryūen’s accumulation of points through the Katsuragi contract similarly becomes the missing piece in a cross-class rescue.

Economic organization therefore determines who can refuse the institution’s demand for sacrifice.

# 5. The volume deliberately undermines simple pruning ideology

A shallow reading of Volume 10 could conclude:

> the school finally forces students to become realistic and eliminate weak members.

The text itself makes that conclusion unstable.

Before the Class Vote is announced, Sudō’s academic growth is foregrounded. Horikita refuses credit for the improvement and says:

> `彼自身の努力の結果よ`

His progress is the result of his own effort.

Chabashira follows with one of the most important statements in the volume:

> `誰が伸びてくるのか読み切れない。私の予想を簡単に裏切ってくる。`

A teacher who has watched many students explicitly acknowledges that developmental trajectories are difficult to predict.

Only later does Ayanokōji reason that the exam is an opportunity to remove a student with narrow developmental upside.

The sequence matters. The novel places the counterevidence **before** the pruning argument.

This yields a distinction essential to the project’s ability framework:

- **present ability** may be measurable;
- **displayed ability** may be observed;
- **socially usable ability** may be judged;
- **developmental ability** is partly retrospective;
- **future developmental ceiling** is uncertain.

The moment a leader uses the last category to justify irreversible expulsion, the epistemic problem becomes morally serious.

Ayanokōji may be unusually good at forecasting people. He is not omniscient. Horikita may have sensible comparative criteria. Those criteria do not transform a prediction into a fact about what a person could have become.

# 6. Ayanokōji: selective developmental utility under coercive scarcity

Ayanokōji’s response to the examination is neither simple cruelty nor humanitarian resistance.

He quickly recognizes what Kōenji states openly: because the school has already made an expulsion unavoidable, the class can use the event to remove someone it judges unnecessary.

His internal formulation is stark:

> `好き嫌いではなくクラスのために不要な生徒を選りすぐり消し去れるチャンスが来た。`

He immediately follows this with an acknowledgment that every human being possesses developmental possibility, but that development differs in timing and range. The conclusion he draws is not “therefore we cannot know.” It is:

> therefore this opportunity should not be wasted.

That is one of the clearest Year 1 examples of Ayanokōji’s White Room inheritance operating as an evaluative habit rather than merely a combat skill.

He translates people into:

- retained capacity;
- replaceability;
- future utility;
- political risk;
- developmental upside;
- and cost of removal.

Yet several details prevent him from becoming an abstract optimizer.

## 6.1 Kei is not placed into the general pool

When Kei fears that she may be targeted, Ayanokōji tells her that she is important to the class and that her expulsion is effectively outside his expected scenario.

This is not evidence of equal regard. It is evidence of **selective protection**.

By Volume 10, Ayanokōji’s moral-political world increasingly contains differentiated categories:

- people who may be pruned;
- people who should be retained for class development;
- people he wants retained for strategic reasons;
- people whose safety he has personally guaranteed;
- and people whose continued presence he simply wants.

Ryūen later enters the last two categories ambiguously. Ayanokōji admits that part of his motive is strategic—building trust with Ichinose—but also that he `何となく龍園を退学させたくなかった`: somehow, he did not want Ryūen expelled.

The adverb matters because it marks a desire not fully reduced to calculation.

## 6.2 He does not seize the public office of judgment

Ayanokōji could expose Yamauchi himself. He says he would have done so if Horikita failed to move.

Instead he routes the information through Manabu to Suzune and lets her become the visible leader.

This is characteristic environmental authorship. He does not need to speak the decisive public words if he can arrange a decision environment in which another person speaks them.

But this case also establishes a limit to the “Ayanokōji authored everything” interpretation. Horikita is not a puppet receiving a completed script. He supplies decisive hidden information; **she chooses the political theory by which that information will be used**.

## 6.3 He uses the exam to build relationships

The Ryūen/Ichinose transaction is explicitly valuable to him because it creates trust with Ichinose. The rescue is therefore not altruistically pure.

But purity is the wrong test. The stronger ethical question is whether his instrumental motive cancels the autonomy-enhancing consequence.

It does not.

Ichinose has two live choices at the moment of the transaction:

- accept Nagumo’s unwanted dating condition;
- or freely accept the cross-class exchange.

Ayanokōji’s intervention expands rather than contracts the feasible set available to her.

The same cannot be said of Manabe.

## 6.4 Manabe exposes the darkest side of protective authorship

When Ishizaki and Ibuki ask to save Ryūen, someone else must absorb D class’s expulsion. Ayanokōji has already considered Manabe a desirable target because she bullied Kei.

His narration goes further. If Manabe disappears, Kei may feel safer; if Ayanokōji allows Kei to infer that he engineered that disappearance, **her trust in him may increase**.

That is a severe convergence of motives:

- protect Kei;
- punish someone who harmed Kei;
- preserve Ryūen;
- improve D-class future competitiveness;
- and deepen Kei’s dependence/trust.

Manabe is not expelled because Ayanokōji personally orders her removal—Ibuki independently chooses her and accepts the risk of retaliation—but Ayanokōji is prepared to use the result relationally.

Protection here approaches ownership because the protector privately treats another person’s punishment as material for managing the protected person’s attachment.

# 7. Horikita Suzune: from solver to accountable political leader

Volume 10 is one of the strongest Horikita volumes in Year 1 because her growth is not measured by whether she discovers the hidden mastermind’s entire plan.

It is measured by whether she can **own a judgment in public**.

## 7.1 The Manabu encounter rejects bloodline determinism

Earlier in the volume, Manabu warns Ayanokōji not to estimate Suzune through him:

> `血縁関係だけで実力を推し量るな。俺がどうであったかと妹は一切の関係がない。`

The statement matters because Suzune herself has spent much of her adolescence doing the opposite. She has treated Manabu as the numerical specification of what excellence must look like.

Her inferiority is partly self-authored through imitation.

## 7.2 Ayanokōji nudges, but does not force, the sibling conversation

Ayanokōji recognizes that graduation will soon close the opportunity for reconciliation. He encourages Suzune to face Manabu, yet consciously stops short of forcing the meeting if she cannot choose it herself.

That restraint is significant within `AYANOKOJI_AUTHORSHIP`.

There are moments when he deliberately engineers another person’s environment until a desired choice becomes likely. Here he identifies a developmental opportunity but preserves the requirement that **Suzune initiate the decisive approach**.

## 7.3 `私に───勇気をください`

When Suzune finally meets Manabu, she does not ask for a plan, points, protection, or the identity of a sacrificial target.

She asks for courage.

Her fear is not primarily epistemic. She already knows the exam requires a decision. Her fear is political and personal: can she stand in front of the class, name what she believes, and remain answerable if they reject her?

Manabu asks whether school has become enjoyable. That question returns Year 1 to ordinary life. Suzune’s problem was never merely insufficient competence. It was tunnel vision so complete that school became only a ladder toward a brother’s acknowledgment.

He then tells her:

> `残念だが、おまえは綾小路には追いつけない`

but immediately refuses the hierarchy she might build from that fact:

> `だが、追いつく必要は全くない。おまえはおまえらしく成長すればいい`

This is not a humiliation scene. It is liberation from an imitation structure.

Manabu effectively says:

> You do not become worthy by becoming me. You do not become worthy by becoming Ayanokōji. The task is to become a developed Suzune Horikita.

## 7.4 Her class speech is leadership through named liability

Horikita’s public argument begins by attacking the exam’s political structure. Without class-wide deliberation, anonymous voting will devolve into coalition protection and may expel people whose continued presence is valuable. She says:

> `こんなもの、試験とは呼べないわ`

She then does something riskier than criticizing the institution: she supplies a name.

Yamauchi is her candidate because of a combination of:

- weak academic contribution;
- weak physical contribution;
- poor discipline and reliability;
- limited evidence of growth;
- and, decisively, willingness to sell out a classmate in exchange for Sakayanagi’s promise of external protection and romance.

The last factor prevents her decision from being a pure ability ranking. **Political betrayal matters.**

She also refuses to exempt Kushida from criticism merely because Kushida is popular. Kushida’s social network makes her participation in the coalition more consequential, not less.

This is an important evolution in Horikita’s ability model. Earlier she often treated people through relatively static visible capacity. Here she evaluates:

- contribution;
- trajectory;
- trustworthiness;
- effects on collective action;
- and political responsibility.

Her formulation remains contestable. She is still compressing plural human capacities into a retention decision under institutional coercion. But she makes the compression explicit and accepts that the class may judge **her** for it.

## 7.5 Sudō becomes evidence that her leadership has generated someone capable of resisting her

Sudō initially resists Yamauchi’s expulsion because Yamauchi is his friend.

Horikita does not simply command obedience. She forces the consequence into view: save Yamauchi, and another person must be exposed instead.

Sudō’s difficulty is morally important. Friendship is not stupidity here. It is a rival value that cannot be dissolved by utility arithmetic.

Ayanokōji’s narration recognizes Sudō’s growth. The boy who once reacted almost entirely through anger is now capable of understanding a collective tradeoff even when it hurts a friendship.

That development is partly a success of Horikita’s pedagogy—and therefore another warning against overconfident early pruning.

# 8. Hirata Yōsuke: care, coercion, and the collapse of anti-ranking leadership

Hirata’s crisis is the volume’s strongest argument against reading kindness as a stable personality trait detached from history.

His prologue establishes that:

> `僕にとって、大切なのはクラスなんだ。`

He corrects “friends” into “class.” That distinction reveals his governing structure. He protects individuals through the preservation of the social whole.

The origin is traumatic.

A childhood friend was bullied and attempted suicide. Hirata responded to the class’s failure not by becoming naturally gentle but by forcibly suppressing conflict:

> `間違ったやり方で、クラスを押さえつけた。`

The fighting stopped. So did the smiles.

He therefore enters ANHS carrying two incompatible convictions:

1. uncontrolled hierarchy and cruelty can destroy a person;
2. coercive suppression of conflict can destroy a community.

His current smiling mediator identity is an attempt to find a third way.

The Class Vote removes that third way.

## 8.1 The exam recreates the original trauma structurally

The school tells Hirata that he must participate in deciding who should be excluded from the group he has defined himself by preserving.

His usual methods—listen, mediate, distribute support, lower conflict, keep everyone inside—cannot solve a rule that requires one body outside.

His insomnia and physical deterioration are therefore not overreaction. The exam attacks the premise on which his social self rests.

## 8.2 `堀北……ちょっと黙れよ`

When Horikita publicly argues for Yamauchi’s removal, Hirata’s polished register collapses. He kicks a desk and tells her to shut up in the same rough phrase previewed by the prologue.

The formal design matters: the text gives us the language of rupture **before** showing the situation that evokes it.

The “good Hirata” has not been exposed as fake. Rather, the volume reveals that his prosocial identity was built in active opposition to a coercive self he knows he can become.

## 8.3 His anti-ranking position has moral force

Hirata argues that classmates do not possess the right to decide another classmate’s worth.

That position should not be dismissed as childish merely because he cannot produce a workable exam solution. It identifies a genuine moral problem:

> compelled choice does not automatically create legitimate jurisdiction over another person’s future.

Chabashira counters that future managers and leaders will have to make such cuts in society. Her speech is institutionally coherent. It is **not equivalent to authorial proof** that the Class Vote is ethically justified.

The novel itself has already shown that:

- developmental potential is uncertain;
- political manipulation shapes voting;
- wealth can purchase exemption;
- leaders can deceive their own class;
- and the exam may have been designed under external pressure to target a particular student.

Hirata is operationally paralyzed, but his objection remains morally live.

## 8.4 Self-sacrifice becomes coercive care

Unable to choose someone else, Hirata tries to make himself the sacrifice. He asks classmates to write his name as their criticism vote and tells them he hates them in an attempt to produce the desired outcome.

This is not pure selflessness.

It is **manipulative self-erasure**.

He is still trying to control the class’s pain: if somebody must be harmed, he will author himself as the acceptable target so nobody else has to own another person’s removal.

The class refuses him. Students independently circulate support and praise votes, with Ayanokōji helping through Kei’s network. Hirata places near the top rather than the bottom.

His year of care has accumulated as social capital powerful enough to defeat his attempt to disappear.

That is one of the volume’s most important demonstrations of relational reciprocity:

> the person who thinks he must save everyone discovers that other people can choose to save him against his own plan.

The outcome does not heal him. Yamauchi is still expelled, and Hirata ends the volume psychologically devastated. But the class’s response establishes that his relationships are not merely one-way dependency structures.

# 9. Kōenji: latent capacity as aristocratic self-certification

Kōenji’s response is the cleanest elitist position in the class.

He does not care about promises of future reform from students he regards as low-capacity. He argues that hidden or latent ability matters more than sentimental attachment or unverified intention.

There is a serious insight inside the arrogance:

> present visible performance is not identical to total ability.

That principle is compatible with the project’s broader `実力` taxonomy.

The problem is epistemic and political. Kōenji treats his own judgment of latent ability as effectively self-validating while refusing the ordinary participation through which others might evaluate his social usefulness.

His doctrine is therefore:

> **future capacity matters—but I reserve the right to know whose future capacity is real.**

That is less a meritocracy than an aristocracy of private judgment.

The result-day scene complicates him further. He repeatedly provokes the expelled Yamauchi while Yamauchi is emotionally collapsing. Ayanokōji briefly considers whether Kōenji may be absorbing Yamauchi’s hatred and redirecting it away from the class; Kōenji denies altruistic intent and claims he merely wanted the best view.

The correct local classification is **unresolved motive**. The behavior may produce a protective function without establishing that protection was the reason.

# 10. Yamauchi: culpable actor and manipulated experimental subject

Yamauchi is easy to reduce to “the obvious loser finally gets expelled.” Volume 10 is more ethically interesting if that reduction is resisted.

He is culpable.

He willingly joins a conspiracy to direct criticism votes toward Ayanokōji because he believes Sakayanagi will reward him with A-class praise and future romance. He recruits classmates and accepts betrayal as a route to self-preservation.

But he is also being manipulated by someone with radically superior information and strategic capacity.

Sakayanagi:

- cultivates his romantic fantasy;
- promises praise votes;
- implies a future relationship;
- instructs him not to preserve evidence of the bargain;
- suggests Kushida as an information/recruitment node;
- uses Kamuro to help select Ayanokōji as the visible target;
- deliberately lets information leak so Ayanokōji can discover the plot;
- and privately calls Yamauchi an `実験体` while describing him as being `調教中`.

Yamauchi therefore occupies two moral positions simultaneously:

> **agent of betrayal** and **object of experiment**.

Acknowledging the second does not erase the first. It prevents the analysis from turning strategic inferiority into proof that a person deserved institutional annihilation.

His breakdown illustration reinforces that point. Once the result is final, the novel presents not an abstract bad piece removed from a board but a frightened adolescent losing access to the world he assumed would continue.

# 11. Sakayanagi: sovereign pruning and rule through informational asymmetry

If Horikita’s Class C is moving toward accountable visible leadership, Sakayanagi’s Class A demonstrates the opposite constitutional form: **sovereignty through controlled information**.

## 11.1 Yamauchi is a cross-class experiment

The Yamauchi operation is not simply an attempt to expel a weak enemy.

Sakayanagi wants several things at once:

- to test Yamauchi as a manipulable subject;
- to create pressure against Ayanokōji;
- to see whether Ayanokōji will resist;
- to guarantee that Ayanokōji survives through A-class external praise;
- and to generate another stage in their rivalry.

Her chess language becomes literal political practice. She says the king must remain to the end.

The important ethical point is that preserving Ayanokōji is not respect for his autonomy. It is preservation of a desired opponent.

## 11.2 Katsuragi is a decoy; Yahiko is the true target

Sakayanagi publicly announces Katsuragi as the student A class will expel. Katsuragi accepts because he knows the factional balance makes resistance futile. Yahiko protests.

The later result reveals that this entire public certainty was false. Yahiko is the real target.

The deception accomplishes several things:

- prevents outside actors from knowing whom to rescue;
- preserves Katsuragi because he still has strategic value;
- removes a lower-value loyalist;
- and demonstrates to the remnants of the Katsuragi faction that opposition carries costs borne by people they care about.

Yahiko’s expulsion therefore functions partly as **political terror by example**.

Sakayanagi is not merely ranking ability. She is governing allegiance.

## 11.3 Her polity is efficient because consent is not required

A class reaches apparent consensus in minutes because the decisive judgment has already been authored by one person. The formal vote remains, but its substantive uncertainty has largely disappeared.

That is why Sakayanagi’s class is useful as a comparative constitution. It is not simply “the strong class.” It is a regime in which:

> **collective procedure ratifies a sovereign decision whose informational basis is unequally distributed.**

That form can be extremely effective. It is also deeply vulnerable to the leader’s private motives.

# 12. Ichinose and Nagumo: when solidarity threatens to consume the self

Volume 9 restored Ichinose’s ability to face her past. Volume 10 immediately tests whether solidarity can become another mode of self-erasure.

Her class has nearly enough pooled private points to buy out an expulsion, but remains roughly four million short. Nagumo can supply the difference.

His condition is that Ichinose date him.

The scene is important because Ichinose herself articulates the ethical problem. She asks whether Nagumo actually likes her because she believes a romantic relationship should arise from reciprocal feeling. She experiences the proposed exchange as wrong even before she can fully theorize why.

Her internal formulation is explicit:

> `自分が自分でなくなってしまう`

She fears that accepting the condition would make her cease to be herself.

Yet she nearly accepts anyway:

> `私は───皆を守るんだ`

This is solidarity becoming dangerous. The same trait that gives Class B extraordinary trust makes its leader vulnerable to converting her own body and intimate future into class property.

Nagumo intensifies the logic by requiring that every B-class student surrender every private point before he helps. His stated principle is that they should not be rescued without sharing pain.

This is politically revealing. Nagumo does not merely sell points for romantic access. He authors a **morality of collective suffering** around the transaction.

The result is coercive on multiple levels:

- institutional scarcity creates the need;
- financial scarcity makes Nagumo uniquely powerful;
- leader identity makes Ichinose feel responsible for everyone;
- romantic asymmetry makes the condition personally costly;
- and the deadline compresses deliberation.

Her eventual refusal becomes possible because another feasible route appears—not because the original pressure was unreal.

# 13. Class B: egalitarian constitutional trust under forced scarcity

Class B’s solution is not simply “Ichinose saves everyone because she is nice.” It is a remarkably coherent constitutional response to the exam.

## 13.1 Capital as collective insurance

Ichinose asks every classmate to surrender all currently held private points. They do so almost immediately because their accumulated trust in her is high enough to support a transfer that would leave them economically vulnerable.

After the cross-class transaction supplies the remaining capital, Kanzaki accepts the role of deliberate criticism target. The class then spends exactly twenty million points to cancel his expulsion.

The material cost is extreme. For a period, B class becomes poorer than D class.

Ichinose’s answer is categorical:

> private points can be earned again; a lost classmate cannot be returned.

This is the most explicit rejection in Year 1 of reducing personhood to the school’s exchange system.

## 13.2 The lottery fallback matters more than the successful rescue

The most revealing element appears afterward when Ayanokōji asks what Class B would have done without Nagumo or him.

Ichinose had two possibilities:

1. expel herself;
2. use a lottery.

She rejects self-expulsion because she also wants to remain and fight. The fallback agreed by the entire class was therefore to draw lots and have the three unlucky students receive the coordinated criticism votes, while praise votes remained uncontrolled.

This is not an efficient ability-preservation strategy. That is precisely why it matters.

Class B’s principle is:

> if the institution forces one of us to bear a loss we do not regard as morally deserved, we will distribute that risk as equally as possible rather than pretend that differential school utility gives us rightful jurisdiction over a person’s future.

The plan could still produce tragedy. Equality of risk does not make forced expulsion good. But it distinguishes **fair procedure under unjust scarcity** from **meritocratic ranking under unjust scarcity**.

Ayanokōji is impressed not because the lottery is strategically optimal but because a class capable of actually accepting such a procedure is extraordinary. The key ability belongs to the social system:

> **Class B possesses enough trust to execute a rule that individually advantaged students have strong incentives to reject.**

This is a form of generative political ability.

## 13.3 Ichinose’s rescue by Ayanokōji is autonomy-enhancing and strategically instrumentalized

Ayanokōji creates a transaction:

- Ibuki transfers roughly four million of Ryūen’s accumulated private points to Ichinose;
- Ichinose promises all forty B-class external praise votes to Ryūen;
- Ayanokōji records the agreement and serves as witness.

Ichinose explicitly has a choice. She can reject the arrangement and return to Nagumo. She chooses the exchange.

After the exam she admits that she is profoundly relieved. She had tried to tell herself that unwanted dating was a small price for classmates, but believes she would eventually have regretted it.

Ayanokōji meanwhile tells Ryūen that the event was useful for building a relationship of trust with Ichinose.

Both facts must remain in view:

> **He treats her rescue as relationship capital. The route he creates nevertheless preserves her romantic autonomy.**

Instrumental motive does not automatically negate autonomy-enhancing consequence.

# 14. Ryūen’s class after Ryūen: authority, loyalty, and the politics of voluntary return

D class begins the Class Vote with a seemingly obvious answer: remove Ryūen.

That consensus is rational in one sense. Ryūen accumulated enormous reputational debt through violence, coercion, and fear. Unlike an ordinary low-performing student, he has actively generated reasons for classmates to want him gone.

Yet his absence reveals something his prior rule concealed: some students have come to regard his strategic capacity as structurally necessary.

## 14.1 Ryūen accepts expulsion

Ryūen himself does not mount an elaborate rescue. He transfers his accumulated private-point wealth to Ibuki and appears prepared to leave.

That resignation should not be mistaken for moral reform. It is more accurately a suspension of sovereign will after defeat. The person who previously imposed decisions on the class is no longer willing to fight for his own office.

## 14.2 Ishizaki and Ibuki generate the rescue demand

The immediate demand to save him comes from below.

Ishizaki and Ibuki visit Ayanokōji and ask for a method. Their motives are imperfect:

- they believe the class cannot compete at the highest level without someone like Ryūen;
- they have personal loyalty and unresolved debt;
- Ibuki remains openly irritated with him;
- Ishizaki’s devotion is emotionally more transparent.

This is important for `RYUEN_FEAR_LEGITIMACY`.

A ruler who once governed primarily through fear is beginning to possess a different kind of authority: **people choose to preserve him when they are finally free not to**.

That does not retroactively legitimate his earlier coercion. It is evidence that the relationships produced under his rule are no longer wholly reducible to coercion.

## 14.3 Ayanokōji forces them to confront substitution

Ayanokōji refuses the fantasy formulation “save Ryūen.”

He insists on the full sentence:

> save Ryūen **by causing someone else to be expelled**.

This is one of his strongest pedagogical moments in the volume because he does not allow loyalty to hide its externalized cost.

Ibuki ultimately chooses Manabe for a bluntly personal reason: she dislikes her. She also knows the redirection may expose Ibuki herself to retaliation and accepts that risk.

The decision is not ethically purified by strategic necessity. It is simply owned more honestly.

## 14.4 Hiyori’s quiet agency becomes class-preservation politics

Hiyori agrees to help coordinate the female side of the voting because she judges Ryūen’s continued presence beneficial to D class.

This extends her Volume 9 development. Her epistemic restraint does not mean political passivity. She can dislike coercion, value calm relationships, and still participate in a harsh collective decision when she judges the class’s future to require it.

The key distinction is that Hiyori’s agency remains **quiet rather than absent**.

# 15. Ayanokōji and Ryūen: utility does not exhaust desire

After the result, Ayanokōji tells Ryūen that if Ishizaki and Ibuki had never asked for help, he would likely have approached Hiyori and constructed a similar route anyway.

He gives two motives:

> `一之瀬への恩を売るため、そして、何となく龍園を退学させたくなかった。`

The first is straightforwardly strategic: establish debt/trust with Ichinose.

The second is unusually resistant to reduction. `何となく` does not describe a fully articulated rational objective. It is closer to:

> somehow; for some reason; I simply did not want him gone.

This matters because Ayanokōji’s Year 1 growth often appears in exactly these partially theorized preferences. Ordinary attachment does not arrive as a grand confession that overturns his strategic cognition. It appears as a desire that sits beside calculation without being completely explained by it.

Ryūen has become:

- a useful future rival;
- a strategically valuable class actor;
- a source of unpredictability;
- and a person whose absence Ayanokōji does not want.

The last category is relationally important even if the text does not yet name it friendship.

# 16. The four class constitutions revealed by one forced-choice mechanism

Volume 10 is especially valuable because every class receives essentially the same institutional demand and produces a different political response.

## 16.1 `POLITY-HORIKITA`: developmental pluralism becoming accountable triage

Class C is fragmented enough that anonymous coalition politics could determine the result. Horikita responds by trying to create a public deliberative center.

Her emerging constitution values:

- contribution;
- growth;
- loyalty;
- argument;
- and visible responsibility.

Its weakness is that it still depends partly on information Ayanokōji supplies from outside public deliberation.

## 16.2 `POLITY-ICHINOSE`: solidarity and egalitarian risk

Class B pools capital, designates a temporary technical loser for financial rescue, and keeps a lottery as the fallback.

Its constitution values:

- equal membership;
- reciprocal sacrifice;
- leader credibility;
- consensual burden sharing;
- and refusal to rank human entitlement by ability.

Its weakness is economic and personal: extraordinary solidarity can make the collective willing to consume the leader’s private self if the leader volunteers too much.

## 16.3 `POLITY-SAKAYANAGI-ORIGIN`: aristocratic sovereignty

Class A centralizes judgment in Sakayanagi.

Its constitution values:

- strategic usefulness;
- hierarchical obedience;
- information control;
- deterrence;
- and leader-authored future planning.

Its strength is decisiveness. Its weakness is obvious: the ruler’s private rivalry and grudges can become class policy.

## 16.4 `POLITY-RYUEN`: post-authoritarian vacuum and emergent loyalism

D class initially tries to remove the former tyrant. A subordinate network then acts voluntarily to preserve him.

The constitution is transitional:

- Ryūen’s old coercive sovereignty has weakened;
- no stable replacement leadership exists;
- peer loyalty becomes unexpectedly important;
- and Ayanokōji/Hiyori temporarily supply coordination from the margins.

The class is beginning to answer whether Ryūen can possess legitimacy after fear ceases to be sufficient.

# 17. Chabashira’s “real world” defense: institutional ideology, not automatic authorial truth

During Hirata’s collapse, Chabashira offers a recognizable defense of the exam.

Society, she argues, requires people in authority to make difficult personnel decisions. ANHS trains students who may become important adults. If they treat the Class Vote as meaningless harassment, they will fail to grow from it.

This position has real force. Leadership does sometimes require allocating scarce resources, dismissing employees, triaging missions, or choosing among harmful alternatives.

But the novel gives multiple reasons not to convert Chabashira’s speech into the work’s unqualified thesis.

First, the analogy is incomplete. A workplace termination is not identical to expulsion from an experimental school in which one’s entire educational trajectory and class-A objective are bound to continued enrollment.

Second, the school deliberately creates the scarcity rather than merely responding to an external scarcity.

Third, the exam’s outcomes are contaminated by:

- romantic coercion;
- factional deception;
- retaliatory grudges;
- wealth inequality;
- hidden cross-class deals;
- and possible adult targeting of one named student.

Fourth, Chabashira herself has already acknowledged that growth is difficult to forecast.

The stronger reading is therefore:

> **Chabashira articulates the institution’s self-justification: compelled judgment as developmental realism. Volume 10 then stress-tests that justification by showing how easily “realism” becomes a language for manufactured disposability and captured procedure.**

# 18. `実力 / jitsuryoku` after the Class Vote

Volume 10 makes the series’ title concept harder, not simpler.

Who is the “best” student to preserve?

A student can possess:

- high academic scores;
- athletic ability;
- strong discipline;
- social legitimacy;
- political loyalty;
- leadership capacity;
- hidden strategic skill;
- future developmental potential;
- capital;
- cross-class reputation;
- or the ability to make other people better.

The exam cannot reduce these cleanly to one scalar without importing a political theory.

### Yamauchi

Weak on several visible measures and politically culpable in the conspiracy. Yet he remains a person with an uncertain developmental future.

### Sudō

Previously one of the easiest “weak-link” candidates; now direct evidence that a student can convert physical excellence plus effort into growing academic and social usefulness.

### Hirata

High social value and strong current class contribution, but psychologically incapacitated by the very leadership problem the exam creates.

### Kōenji

Enormous latent capacity, low ordinary cooperative contribution.

### Ichinose

Extraordinary social legitimacy capable of generating financial coordination and equal-risk consent—an ability the OAA-like scalar systems of later institutional measurement would struggle to capture even conceptually.

### Ayanokōji

Possesses overwhelming hidden capacity but depends upon intentional under-display. He becomes top praise recipient partly because an external rival authors the praise environment.

The lesson is not that measurement is impossible.

It is:

> **every measurement of ability is also a choice about which human capacities an institution or polity wishes to reward.**

# 19. Protection, ownership, and the moral asymmetry of Ayanokōji’s care

The volume creates three revealing protection structures.

## Hirata: protection through self-erasure

He tries to substitute himself for every other possible victim.

## Ichinose: protection through self-commodification

She nearly trades an unwanted intimate relationship for the class’s safety.

## Ayanokōji: protection through environmental control

He protects selected people by changing who bears the surrounding costs.

The third is the most effective and the least publicly accountable.

Kei can feel safer if Manabe disappears. Ryūen can survive if another D-class student receives the redirected votes. Ichinose can preserve her romantic autonomy if D and B class exchange praise for points. Horikita can lead effectively if she receives hidden information through Manabu.

Ayanokōji repeatedly turns care into **architecture**.

That can be humane. It can also become possessive when the protected person does not know what costs were imposed on others in her name.

Volume 10 therefore strengthens a central project distinction:

> **protection is not identical to respect for autonomy.**

The more powerful the protector becomes, the more important it is to ask who was allowed to consent to the structure of rescue.

# 20. Ordinary life as counter-curriculum

The Class Vote is one of the year’s harshest institutional episodes, yet the volume continues to give small evidence that Ayanokōji’s school life is changing his temporal experience.

He thinks that the current year is passing with a speed comparable to many previous years:

> `明らかに、今までの何年にも匹敵するような速さで日々が過ぎ去っている。`

That is not merely a comment about being busy.

White Room life was highly structured, repetitive, externally authored, and oriented toward measurable development. ANHS life is increasingly dense with:

- relationships;
- uncertainty;
- rivalry;
- leisure;
- embarrassment;
- concern for others;
- and events he did not anticipate.

Subjective time becomes evidence of lived difference.

His quiet library relationship with Hiyori continues to matter for the same reason. Reading beside someone without needing to extract strategic value is a small counter-curriculum to the environment that trained him to treat every interaction as a performance problem.

Horikita’s conversation with Manabu mirrors this. She cannot straightforwardly say school is fun, but she can say it is not boring. Her perceptual world has widened beyond the brother-centered ladder that originally defined school value.

Ordinary life does not sit outside the series’ political themes. It is one of the main alternatives to instrumental human development.

# 21. Japanese narration, voice, and register

Volume 10 uses register collapse and register choice as direct characterization.

## 21.1 Hirata

Hirata’s ordinary voice is socially polished, calming, and inclusive. His prologue’s internal language is reflective and morally self-monitoring.

The phrase:

> `堀北……ちょっと黙れよ`

is therefore powerful not because it is extreme profanity—it is not—but because it violates the carefully maintained Hirata register. The roughness signals the return of a buried coercive self.

His later attempt to make the class vote against him uses performative hostility. He tries to speak an identity into existence that the class’s accumulated experience with him rejects.

## 21.2 Horikita

Horikita’s public speech retains her characteristic analytic directness, but the social function changes. Earlier bluntness often produced distance. Here she uses direct language while accepting responsibility for its consequences.

Her private register with Manabu is markedly less armored. `勇気をください` is a request rather than a competitive claim. The shift makes the scene an act of vulnerability, not merely strategic consultation.

## 21.3 Manabu

Manabu’s speech remains compressed and authoritative, but his key advice is unusually non-competitive. He dismantles comparison rather than intensifying it.

`おまえはおまえらしく成長すればいい` is important precisely because it comes from the person whose recognition Suzune spent years treating as the objective measure of worth.

## 21.4 Ichinose

Her speech with Nagumo is hesitant, polite, and full of self-correction when discussing romance. This is not generic shyness. She is trying to articulate an ethical intuition in a power-imbalanced negotiation.

Her later conversation with Ayanokōji becomes lighter once the unwanted condition is no longer necessary. The relief is linguistic as well as conceptual.

## 21.5 Sakayanagi

Sakayanagi’s polite, playful register makes domination sound civilized. Terms such as `実験体` and `調教中` become more disturbing because they are delivered without loss of composure.

Her rhetoric consistently turns people into pieces, subjects, and useful examples while preserving the surface of refined conversation.

## 21.6 Ayanokōji

His narration moves effortlessly between ordinary observation and severe utility reasoning. The most revealing transitions are often unmarked.

A thought about Kei’s peace of mind can become a thought about increasing her trust through Manabe’s removal. A rescue for Ryūen can be simultaneously a relationship-building event with Ichinose. A classmate’s public leadership can be described as a means he used to bury Yamauchi.

The absence of rhetorical alarm is itself characterization. Instrumental cognition remains ordinary to him even as relational motives become more visible.

# 24. Critical reservations and interpretive cautions

Several tempting conclusions should remain rejected at the Volume 10 boundary.

## 24.1 “Yamauchi is objectively the least valuable human in C class.”

Not established.

Horikita supplies a politically defensible class-retention argument. The text also establishes developmental uncertainty and the institutional artificiality of the scarcity.

## 24.2 “Hirata is simply too naive to lead.”

Too shallow.

His operational paralysis is real, but his refusal to treat peers as legitimately rankable sacrificial objects identifies a moral problem the successful strategists do not solve.

## 24.3 “Horikita is merely executing Ayanokōji’s plan.”

Incorrect as a complete description.

Ayanokōji supplies hidden information and uses her intervention strategically. Horikita independently seeks courage, formulates the public argument, chooses the criteria, names the target, challenges Kushida, and accepts visible liability.

## 24.4 “Ichinose’s willingness to date Nagumo proves romantic interest.”

Rejected.

Her internal narration explicitly treats the arrangement as wrong, identity-threatening, and something she later believes she would regret.

## 24.5 “Ayanokōji saves Ryūen purely because Ryūen is useful.”

Insufficient.

He states both strategic and partially untheorized relational motives.

## 24.6 “Sakayanagi’s class is meritocratic.”

Incomplete.

Her decisions incorporate ability, but also factional control, deterrence, personal rivalry, deception, and revenge.

## 24.7 “The Class Vote is simply the school teaching real-world layoffs.”

Rejected as an unqualified reading.

That is Chabashira’s institutional defense. The volume itself later introduces evidence that the exam architecture may have been influenced by an adult campaign against Ayanokōji.

# 28. Cumulative delta through Volume 10

## Ayanokōji Kiyotaka

**Strengthened:** environmental authorship is now operating at the level of expulsion allocation. He can shape who receives information, who occupies the visible office of judgment, which classes possess a feasible rescue route, and how another person’s continued presence becomes relationship capital.

**Strengthened:** ordinary affect continues to coexist with strategic cognition. His subjective sense that school time is passing unusually quickly and his partially untheorized wish to keep Ryūen both resist an “emotionless optimizer” reading.

**Darkened:** protection and dependency management converge in his private response to Manabe. He is willing to use another student’s expulsion as both relief for Kei and evidence of his own protective power.

**Qualified:** he does not author every meaningful choice. Suzune must approach Manabu herself; Horikita authors the public retention principle; Ibuki independently selects Manabe; Ichinose freely accepts the cross-class bargain; Class B independently possesses the social constitution that makes rescue possible.

## Horikita Suzune

**Major development:** she becomes a visible political leader rather than merely a high-performing solver.

Her achievement is not an objectively correct ranking of human worth. It is:

- choosing a rule;
- naming a target;
- exposing her evidence;
- challenging an influential ally in Kushida;
- forcing friendship-based objections into explicit substitution terms;
- and accepting that her own standing may be damaged by the judgment.

**Major development:** Manabu’s recognition no longer means imitation. His explicit instruction to grow as herself becomes the strongest anti-copying statement in her arc so far.

## Hirata Yōsuke

**Major revision:** his friendliness is now legible as a trauma-informed political project.

He has already experienced two failures:

1. passive class society allowed bullying to culminate in attempted suicide;
2. coercive suppression of class conflict created order without happiness.

His ANHS persona tries to escape both. The Class Vote makes that escape structurally impossible.

**New tension:** his care is reciprocal enough that classmates save him against his own attempted self-sacrifice, but he cannot emotionally survive the fact that the class is still forced to lose someone else.

## Ichinose Honami

**Strengthened:** solidarity is not merely affective popularity. It is capable of producing:

- complete point pooling;
- voluntary temporary targeting of Kanzaki;
- massive cross-class praise legitimacy;
- and a unanimous fallback lottery that high-performing students elsewhere would rationally resist.

**Darkened:** the same collective ethic creates a self-sacrificial vulnerability. Ichinose nearly treats her own intimate autonomy as an expendable class resource.

**Strengthened:** when an alternative exists, she chooses it and later names her relief, showing that class commitment does not erase a private self with legitimate preferences.

## Sakayanagi Arisu

**Strengthened:** her leadership is sovereign rather than merely strategic. She governs not only outcomes but information about which outcome is actually intended.

**Darkened:** Yamauchi and Yahiko show two forms of instrumental personhood:

- experimental subject outside her class;
- deterrent example inside her class.

**Complicated:** she protects Ayanokōji against an adult-directed expulsion attempt, but her protection is partly proprietary: the opponent she wants must remain available for her desired contest.

## Ryūen Kakeru

**Major development:** authority begins shifting from coercion toward voluntary preservation.

He is ready to leave. Ishizaki and Ibuki are not. Their effort to preserve him does not erase earlier fear politics, but it is evidence that his political significance has outlived his immediate capacity to command.

## Sudō Ken

**Strengthened:** growth is now longitudinal and multidimensional.

Academic improvement is joined by the ability to process a collective moral tradeoff involving a friend. His trajectory is direct counterevidence to static judgments based on earlier visible failure.

## Hiyori Shiina

**Strengthened:** quiet agency becomes political participation. She remains a low-pressure relational presence while also accepting coordinated action when she judges Ryūen’s survival beneficial to her class.

## Kōenji Rokusuke

**Strengthened:** his philosophy is revealed as latent-capacity elitism. He correctly refuses to equate present display with total ability but treats his own private assessment of potential as sufficient authority.

## Advanced Nurturing High School

**Major revision:** institutional legitimacy becomes an explicit open question.

The school is no longer only a harsh developmental environment. The ending creates local evidence that exam architecture and surveillance systems can be captured or redirected by adult actors with a personal target.

# 29. Open questions at the Volume 10 endpoint

3. **Does Manabu’s instruction to “grow as yourself” actually free Suzune from comparison, or merely create a new standard she must learn to inhabit?**

4. **Can Class B sustain its egalitarian constitution after spending nearly all of its accumulated financial security?**

5. **Will Ichinose learn to distinguish commitment to the class from the belief that her private self is always the cheapest thing to sacrifice?**

6. **Does Nagumo understand his dating condition as coercive, or does he interpret access created by institutional leverage as legitimate proof of leadership entitlement?**

7. **Can Ryūen return to political relevance through loyalty rather than fear, and would such a return actually change his governing methods?**

8. **What is the moral status of Ayanokōji’s partially untheorized wish to preserve Ryūen? Is this rivalry, attachment, curiosity, or something the current vocabulary cannot yet stabilize?**

9. **Will Ayanokōji’s increasing use of protection as relationship architecture make his attachments more reciprocal or more proprietary?**

10. **What limits exist on Sakayanagi’s willingness to use subordinate suffering as political demonstration?**

11. **Why was Chairman Sakayanagi removed, and how much of the Class Vote’s design was affected by the adult attempt to remove Ayanokōji?**

12. **How reliable is Sakayanagi’s account of the earlier outside-criticism version of the examination?** It is highly relevant testimony, but the volume does not give an independent administrative document confirming every design detail.

13. **What exact powers will Tsukishiro possess as acting chairman, and what procedural constraints, if any, remain capable of restraining him?**

14. **Can Ayanokōji defend himself institutionally without reproducing the same evidence control and environmental authorship used against him?**

15. **What does the Protection Point do to Ayanokōji’s future risk calculus now that he possesses a formal institutional shield against one expulsion?**

16. **Does the class remember Yamauchi primarily as a betrayer, a weak link, a manipulated victim, or some unstable combination?** The political story chosen after expulsion may matter as much as the vote itself.

17. **Is Kōenji’s behavior toward the expelled Yamauchi merely cruel spectatorship, or did it also serve a deliberate hatred-absorbing function?** The latter is plausible but not established by his own stated motive.

# 30. Final synthesis

Volume 10 takes the series’ title—`実力至上主義`, supremacy of ability—and subjects it to its most severe Year 1 test so far.

The school does not merely reward superior performance. It manufactures a situation in which ability, loyalty, popularity, money, reputation, social trust, factional control, private affection, and hidden manipulation all become competing currencies for the **right to remain**.

That exposes the impossibility of treating `実力` as a neutral scalar.

Yamauchi can be weak in several measurable respects and still be a person whose future cannot actually be known. Sudō can look like an obvious liability in earlier volumes and then become one of the clearest examples of growth. Hirata can be one of the class’s most socially valuable people and still collapse when the institution demands a form of judgment his moral identity cannot absorb. Ichinose can possess extraordinary political ability precisely because she refuses ability-based ranking as the foundation of membership. Sakayanagi can run the most efficient class polity while using that efficiency to conceal targets and convert human loss into deterrence. Ayanokōji can be the best hidden evaluator in the year while his evaluations remain private, selective, and ethically unaccountable.

The volume’s best move is therefore to refuse the comforting idea that successful strategy proves correct moral theory.

Horikita wins politically because she makes a judgment the class can coordinate around. Hirata loses operationally because he refuses to rank persons. That does **not** mean Horikita has solved the moral problem and Hirata has merely failed to understand reality.

Instead, they identify different truths:

> **Horikita:** leadership cannot evade responsibility when a decision is unavoidable.

> **Hirata:** being forced to choose does not automatically make the chooser morally entitled to declare another person disposable.

Both truths survive the exam.

Ichinose contributes a third:

> when the loss is unjustly imposed, a community can refuse to convert unequal ability into unequal human entitlement and instead share the burden as equally as possible.

Sakayanagi contributes a fourth, darker answer:

> a sovereign can make the system efficient by deciding in advance whose future matters and withholding enough information that collective procedure merely ratifies her private judgment.

Ayanokōji stands between these constitutions and learns from all of them. His own practice becomes increasingly powerful because he rarely needs to occupy the visible seat of authority. He can distribute information, alter feasible options, make exchanges possible, protect selected people, and let other actors own the final choice.

This is why Horikita’s development is so important to his own arc.

If she were merely a puppet, his authorship would remain total.

But Volume 10 gives her a principle he did not simply hand her: after years of measuring herself against Manabu and then against Ayanokōji, she is told to **grow as herself**, and she responds by taking public responsibility in a way Ayanokōji characteristically avoids.

Her visible accountability is something his hidden sovereignty lacks.

The closing adult confrontation then turns the entire volume outward. Tsukishiro enters as a person with institutional title, surveillance access, physical confidence, and an explicit mandate tied to Atsuomi. He demonstrates what happens when environmental authorship no longer belongs to unusually gifted students but to the administrator who can control the recording system and the rules themselves.

Ayanokōji’s restraint during the assault is telling. He does not fail to retaliate because he lacks power. He refuses because power has become evidentiary vulnerability. A selectively edited image of a student attacking the acting chairman could author an institutional reality more dangerous than the physical encounter.

Volume 9 argued that control over the information environment can determine what counts as truth.

Volume 10 advances the proposition:

> **control over institutional procedure can determine whose truth, ability, and future are allowed to remain actionable at all.**

That makes the Class Vote the appropriate bridge into late Year 1. The school is no longer merely the arena in which Ayanokōji studies other people.

The school itself has become an object whose authorship must be questioned.
