## Narrative structure

Volume 7.5 contains six substantive movements:

1. `初めての冬` — Ayanokōji prepares for his first Christmas date and asks whether he can learn how another person becomes irreplaceable.
2. `恋の矢` — Kei’s post-rooftop interiority, her emerging affection, and her attempt to help Satō while suppressing her own feelings.
3. `伊吹澪の災難な一日` — Sakayanagi’s ideological challenge, Ayanokōji’s growing interest in cinema, Ibuki’s rematch, and ordinary goals outside class rank.
4. `それぞれの過ごし方` — Ryūen’s post-defeat politics, the 800-million-point plan, Manabu’s warning about Nagumo, Horikita’s autonomy, and Airi’s gift.
5. `波乱のＷデート` — Satō’s ordinary romantic offer, Ayanokōji’s social and aesthetic preferences, Nagumo’s public test, Hirata’s model of protection, and Satō’s rejected confession.
6. `矢の行方` — Kiriyama’s covert opposition, Kei’s negotiated partnership, the end of the Hirata arrangement, private given names, the medicine, and the distinction between indispensable and irreplaceable.

The author’s afterword explicitly identifies the book as a supplementary continuation of Volume 7 and notes that only three in-story days pass (`Y1V07.5|spine:16|xhtml:text/part0015.html|p:0006-0008`). This is important formally. The volume is not a detached holiday anthology. It is the immediate psychological and political aftershock of the rooftop.

# 1. Executive thesis

Volume 7.5 asks whether Ayanokōji can enter ordinary intimacy without translating intimacy into strategic selection.

The book opens with a question of **irreplaceability**. Before meeting Satō, Ayanokōji wonders whether he can learn:

- what it means to like someone;
- what it means to value someone;
- how shared time can itself become happiness;
- and how another person becomes `かけがえのない存在`—an existence for which no substitute can perform the same personal meaning (`Y1V07.5|spine:10|xhtml:text/part0009.html|p:0002-0022`).

The book ends with a near-answer that is deliberately not the same word. Ayanokōji says Kei has become `必要不可欠な存在`—an indispensable existence—and imagines that after the Class A struggle and student-council conflict end, she might become a friend or something beyond friendship (`Y1V07.5|spine:15|xhtml:text/part0014.html|p:0354-0368`).

The lexical difference is the volume’s governing structure.

`かけがえのない` means irreplaceable in a personal, singular sense. The person cannot simply be substituted because the value belongs to who that person is.

`必要不可欠` means indispensable or absolutely necessary. It may describe a person without whom a system cannot function. The emphasis can remain operational.

Ayanokōji wants to know how someone becomes irreplaceable. By Christmas evening, he knows that Kei has become indispensable.

That is genuine movement. It is not yet the answer he asked for.

Satō offers the cleanest route toward ordinary romance available to him in Year 1. She is:

- attractive;
- sincere;
- socially competent;
- directly interested in him;
- and capable of giving him a conventional high-school relationship unconnected to blackmail, trauma, covert operations, or class politics.

Ayanokōji wants the things such a relationship could provide. He explicitly desires:

- a girlfriend;
- ordinary student life;
- romantic companionship;
- and eventually sexual experience.

He enjoys the date. He finds no major defect in Satō. He later wonders whether refusing her was foolish.

Yet he rejects her.

The rejection cannot responsibly be reduced to one explanation. Four explanations coexist.

First, he does not presently love her. He correctly judges that accepting a confession while withholding reciprocal feeling could be disrespectful.

Second, he is emotionally underdeveloped. He cannot yet imagine himself inhabiting the relationship she wants.

Third, the external environment has changed. His father, Sakayanagi, Nagumo, Chabashira, and the school’s expulsion mechanisms make ordinary transparency dangerous.

Fourth, and most explicitly in his private reasoning, choosing Satō would destabilize Kei. Kei’s fear of replacement would reduce her reliability as an operative and protected collaborator. Satō does not possess Kei’s:

- deception;
- crisis response;
- social reach;
- mental resilience;
- or ability to act inside Ayanokōji’s concealed world.

Ayanokōji therefore preserves the relationship most compatible with abnormal life rather than choosing the relationship that might teach him normal life.

This is the volume’s central contradiction:

> **He wants ordinary love, but when ordinary love becomes a real possibility, he protects the person who can survive his extraordinary system.**

The book is not content to make that choice purely instrumental. Ayanokōji buys medicine before he knows Kei is healthy. No examination requires it. No one sees the purchase. He worries that rooftop exposure may have made her ill. He observes her from a distance and abandons the delivery only after seeing her healthy (`Y1V07.5|spine:12|xhtml:text/part0011.html|p:0190-0197`).

That is care.

Its form remains characteristic:

- anticipate a physical risk;
- purchase a contingency;
- avoid direct emotional contact;
- observe the person without announcing the observation;
- and treat absence of visible illness as sufficient evidence.

The medicine protects against a cold. It does not protect Kei from spending two days believing he did not care.

The volume therefore distinguishes **practical empathy** from **relational communication**. Ayanokōji is already capable of acting because another person’s condition matters to him. He is not yet capable of making that concern reciprocal in the moment it is needed.

Kei’s own development is equally double-sided.

She does not say the rooftop erased her past. She corrects that fantasy immediately. The past remains. What has changed is that she possesses more strength not to be defeated by it (`Y1V07.5|spine:11|xhtml:text/part0010.html|p:0002-0024`).

She also begins behaving ethically toward people outside her protector relationship. Satō trusts her. Earlier Kei might have invented romantic expertise to preserve status. Now she hesitates because careless advice would betray someone who approached her sincerely. She helps Satō for mixed motives:

- genuine friendship;
- responsibility toward trust;
- curiosity about Ayanokōji;
- and the hope that a successful Satō relationship might suppress her own emerging attraction.

Mixed motive does not make the care false.

By the end, Kei objects that Ayanokōji publicly named her his partner without asking. She demands `態度と誠意`—appropriate attitude and sincerity. She then agrees to help him on an explicit condition: he must protect her with his full ability (`Y1V07.5|spine:15|xhtml:text/part0014.html|p:0157-0205`).

This is more reciprocal than the Volume 4 recruitment. It is still not equal.

Ayanokōji controls:

- more information;
- more physical power;
- the meaning of the threats;
- and the future boundaries of the relationship.

Kei consents to continued partnership without knowing the White Room, the full structure of his plans, or the extent to which he intentionally moved her dependence from Hirata to himself.

The volume’s most honest self-diagnosis belongs to Ayanokōji:

> even after leaving the White Room, he remains inside it.

He cannot paint the ordinary future. He automatically builds insurance. He judges relationships through replacement value. He continues believing that other people may suffer if he wins in the end (`Y1V07.5|spine:14|xhtml:text/part0013.html|p:1103-1110`).

This does not mean his feelings are counterfeit. It means the White Room persists as the grammar through which he interprets feelings he has not learned to name.

Volume 7.5 is therefore the bridge between coerced dependency and possible intimacy.

It does not cross the bridge.

It ends by making the bridge visible.

# 2. Position in the Year 1 architecture

The first seven volumes progressively expand Ayanokōji’s control over what other people can see, believe, and expect.

| Volume | Governing escalation |
|---|---|
| Volume 1 | authored visibility |
| Volume 2 | authored legibility |
| Volume 3 | authored environment |
| Volume 4 | authored dependency |
| Volume 4.5 | ordinary life as counter-curriculum |
| Volume 5 | controlled public exposure |
| Volume 6 | distributed integration under hidden contingency |
| Volume 7 | sovereignty over fear and futurity |
| Volume 7.5 | intimacy translated into strategic selection |

Volume 7.5 does not reduce the stakes after the rooftop. It changes the unit of analysis.

Volume 7 asks who can control another person’s future through:

- ownership;
- expulsion threats;
- violence;
- fear;
- and rescue.

Volume 7.5 asks what happens after those controls become relationships.

- What does Kei’s loyalty become once immediate torture ends?
- What does Ryūen’s authority become once fear no longer guarantees obedience?
- What does Horikita’s growth become once Ayanokōji claims he may withdraw?
- What does Sakayanagi’s interest become once she openly names her theory of genius?
- What does Manabu’s concern become once Nagumo holds formal power?
- What does Ayanokōji’s desire for ordinary life become when an ordinary girl asks him to choose it?

The volume also performs a crucial formal counterpoint to Volume 7.

The rooftop is an environment Ayanokōji has extensively secured in advance. The Christmas date is not. There is no formal winning condition. Conversation cannot be solved by force, evidence, or hidden witnesses. Ayanokōji can investigate Satō’s preferences, but the information does not tell him what he should feel.

The ordinary world therefore becomes a domain in which greater information does not necessarily produce a correct answer.

That is precisely why the date matters.

# 3. Kei after the rooftop — strength without erasure

Volume 7.5 opens Kei's viewpoint by correcting the most tempting but least credible version of recovery.

For a moment, she imagines that the terrible past has disappeared. She immediately revises the thought. The past remains. What has changed is that she now possesses more strength not to be defeated by it (`Y1V07.5-E004`).

That distinction should govern the entire post-rooftop reading.

The confrontation did not:

- remove the scar;
- undo middle-school bullying;
- make Ayanokōji's Volume 4 conduct consensual;
- eliminate her need for safety;
- or convert dependency into independence overnight.

It did give her evidence that she can choose under conditions of terror. The girl who had understood survival through attachment to a protector discovered that immediate survival did not exhaust the range of motives available to her.

## 3.1 Naming the harm

Kei calls Ryūen's conduct `虐め / bullying` and knows that Ayanokōji arranged the earlier Manabe assault (`Y1V07.5-E005`). The plainness of the term matters. The rooftop was not merely:

- a special examination;
- a strategy;
- or a contest between hidden leaders.

It was the deliberate recreation of a victim's earlier abuse.

The same passage asks whether an ordinary person could forgive Ayanokōji for using that history. Kei's internal answer is that she already has.

Forgiveness here should not be confused with moral exoneration. She does not decide that the conduct was harmless. Her emotional reality is that the person who injured her also became:

- the person who understood the concealed structure of her life;
- the person who did not react to the scar with disgust;
- the person who eventually came;
- and the person who can now prevent recurrence.

The volume depicts this as psychologically powerful, not ethically clean.

## 3.2 The scar and recognition

Kei repeatedly returns to Ayanokōji's reaction to her scar. He did not display disgust (`Y1V07.5-E007`). For a girl who has organized social life around preventing that mark from becoming visible, non-disgust carries enormous meaning.

But the reassurance cannot be separated from the way he acquired access to the scar:

- coercive isolation;
- physical restraint;
- sexualized threat;
- and the deliberate collapse of every protective role she believed she possessed.

This is a recurrent Year 1 structure: Ayanokōji can provide an unusually powerful form of recognition because he first enters a private area another person did not consent to expose.

The resulting comfort is real. The method remains violating.

## 3.3 What Kei now sees in him

Her private description of Ayanokōji is more complex than idealized rescue fantasy. She sees him as:

- intelligent;
- mature;
- calm;
- physically extraordinary;
- cold;
- cruel;
- and nevertheless the person who comes in the end (`Y1V07.5-E006`).

She is not falling for an imagined harmless boy. Her attraction incorporates knowledge of danger.

That makes the relationship psychologically credible and ethically more troubling. The cruelty has not disappeared from the object of affection. It has become part of the private self she believes only she can see.

## 3.4 The `恋する乙女` diagnosis

Kei recognizes the familiar symptoms of a `恋する乙女 / maiden in love` while resisting the classification (`Y1V07.5-E008`). Her denial has several sources:

- embarrassment;
- anger at what he did;
- fear that affection will intensify dependence;
- fear of replacement;
- and the impossibility of fitting the relationship into an ordinary romantic narrative.

The text therefore does not ask whether affection and resentment can coexist. It assumes they can.

The more difficult question is whether affection formed inside coercive protection can later become reciprocal without the coercive structure being confronted explicitly.

# 4. Sakayanagi, Ichinose, and the ideology of natural genius

Ayanokōji encounters Sakayanagi while buying medicine for Kei. The scene joins two forms of development in one physical location:

- private concern for the girl whose dependency he helped construct;
- and an ideological challenge concerning whether human ability can be constructed at all.

Sakayanagi announces that she intends to attack Ichinose and Class B (`Y1V07.5-E015`). Her declared target is not simply another class's points. She believes she has found a vulnerability whose exposure will allow her to demonstrate the instability beneath Ichinose's public moral authority.

## 4.1 `持たざる者`

Sakayanagi describes the White Room as an institution for `持たざる者`—those who do not possess innate genius (`Y1V07.5-E016`). Her theory is aristocratic:

- natural genius is qualitatively distinct;
- ordinary people may improve through effort;
- artificial education may produce high performance;
- but no constructed product can surpass genuine inborn superiority.

Defeating Ayanokōji would therefore prove more than personal strength. It would make him evidence against the educational theory that produced him.

At the Volume 7.5 boundary, this is Sakayanagi's philosophy, not an established scientific conclusion. The source does not isolate:

- inherited predisposition;
- selection effects;
- educational intensity;
- motivation;
- trauma;
- opportunity;
- or the interaction among them.

Indeed, Year 1 has repeatedly shown people developing abilities through relation and experience:

- Sudō's strength becomes socially usable;
- Horikita becomes capable of shared leadership;
- Airi becomes more socially active;
- Kei acts beyond pure survival;
- and Ryūen's followers generate loyalty his fear model cannot explain.

Sakayanagi's objection to a crude “education creates everything” thesis is serious. Her belief that birth fixes an unsurpassable hierarchy is much stronger than the evidence available.

## 4.2 Genuine goodness and hidden vulnerability

Sakayanagi explicitly distinguishes Ichinose from Kushida. Ichinose's goodness is genuine (`Y1V07.5-E018`). Her vulnerability therefore cannot be reduced to:

> the kind girl is secretly evil.

The attack will instead test whether real goodness can remain politically stable when the person carrying it possesses an unresolved private wound.

Sakayanagi claims an underlying connection between Ichinose and Kamuro and describes a cold/hot-reading process through which she encouraged Ichinose to disclose information (`Y1V07.5-E017`). The precise shared weakness remains unresolved at this boundary. It should not be filled in from later knowledge.

## 4.3 Ayanokōji's selective nonintervention

Ayanokōji recognizes that Sakayanagi may destroy Class B and considers allowing it because a weakened rival benefits his class (`Y1V07.5-E019`).

This is an important limit on the apparent humanization of the Christmas volume.

He can:

- worry about Kei's health;
- remember Satō's confession;
- enjoy films;
- and value low-pressure friendship

while still treating another class's psychological collapse as useful observational material.

Ordinary feeling and strategic instrumentality do not replace one another. They coexist.

# 5. Cinema, Ibuki, and self-authored goals

The Ibuki chapter is comic, but its analytical function is substantial. It places two highly competitive students inside leisure and asks whether they can possess goals not assigned by class conflict.

## 5.1 Cinema as chosen interest

Ayanokōji says cinema is becoming a genuine hobby (`Y1V07.5-E021`). He enjoys:

- trailers;
- genre experimentation;
- unfamiliar narratives;
- anticipation;
- and the possibility of learning through fiction.

This is more important than identifying a favorite film. The activity is chosen for pleasure rather than optimization.

When the screening fails shortly before the conclusion, Ayanokōji stays because he wants to experience the ending rather than replace experience with a review (`Y1V07.5-E023`). The preference is epistemic and aesthetic:

> another person's summary is not equivalent to seeing the work for himself.

That principle quietly parallels his rebellion against the White Room. An institution may tell him which knowledge matters. He increasingly wants direct experience before accepting another person's assigned meaning.

## 5.2 Ibuki cannot stop turning leisure into competition

Ibuki remains because leaving before Ayanokōji feels like losing (`Y1V07.5-E023`). Her pride colonizes a meaningless situation.

Yet she also possesses a genuinely self-authored goal: watch every film screened at the school (`Y1V07.5-E024`). The ambition has no class-point value. It is difficult only because she decided it matters.

Ayanokōji respects this kind of goal. It is one of the clearest ordinary-life forms of freedom in the volume:

- the person chooses the objective;
- the person determines why it matters;
- no institution defines the reward.

## 5.3 Ibuki's correct criticism and Ayanokōji's cruelty

Ibuki accuses Ayanokōji of inconsistency. He claims to want invisibility but repeatedly chooses to use ability. Her criticism is accurate.

His reply—that she was like a roadside stone and too insignificant even to mock—is unnecessarily cruel (`Y1V07.5-E022`). It reveals the indifference with which he once categorized her.

The line also shows why later respect matters. Ayanokōji does not begin with universal human recognition. People acquire salience through:

- relevance;
- resistance;
- interest;
- or relationship.

The project should not mistake increasing salience for moral equality.

## 5.4 The final rematch

Ibuki demands one conclusive rematch. Ayanokōji accepts, calibrates force, and makes the contest appear more serious than it is (`Y1V07.5-E026`–`E028`).

The fight differs from the rooftop:

- there is no extended psychological breaking;
- the stakes are limited;
- the opponent requests the contest;
- and a three-tap surrender ends it.

The scene is still unequal. Ibuki does not understand the full gap. But it provides a bounded closure she can recognize.

Ayanokōji's broad discussion of average sex-based physical difference should be treated cautiously. It supports the local conclusion that Ibuki cannot overcome his far greater training and strength through determination alone. Its more general language risks converting averages into absolute individual destiny (`Y1V07.5-E027`).

## 5.5 Improvised face-saving

When they become trapped in a staff-only warehouse, Ayanokōji uses an employee name he previously observed and implies that he and “Mio” entered for romantic privacy (`Y1V07.5-E029`).

The lie is effective because it creates:

- a plausible explanation;
- shared responsibility;
- and an embarrassment strong enough to discourage further investigation.

Even in comedy, his perception becomes environmental control.

# 6. Ordinary social ability outside formal leadership

Volume 7.5 repeatedly finds meaningful competence in characters who are easy to classify as comic, weak, or peripheral.

## 6.1 Ike protects Shinohara

During a public dispute with an upperclass girl, Ike creates a face-saving explanation rather than directly accusing the other student of lying (`Y1V07.5-E032`). He invokes:

- Shinohara's poor eyesight;
- nearby witnesses;
- the boyfriend's presence;
- and the risk of teacher involvement.

The solution de-escalates without requiring the other party to admit malicious intent publicly.

This is practical social intelligence. It differs from Horikita's early tendency to identify the correct factual judgment and expect correctness to settle the interaction.

Ike and Shinohara continue hiding concern beneath insult, yet arrange to spend Christmas together (`Y1V07.5-E033`). Their relationship develops through actions that contradict the contempt they perform.

## 6.2 Airi and the problem of narrow experience

Ayanokōji interprets Airi's attachment as formed inside an unusually small social world. Because he was among her first safe connections, she may classify gratitude, dependence, admiration, and romance before gaining broader comparative experience (`Y1V07.5-E034`).

The interpretation may be partly correct. It also risks paternalism: he can use “you need more experience” to avoid confronting what her feeling asks of him.

The important self-application is that he applies the same uncertainty to himself. He knows too little about:

- school life;
- friendship;
- and romantic feeling

to trust immediate classification.

## 6.3 The Ayanokōji Group's low-pressure constitution

Haruka defines the friendship group through voluntary participation (`Y1V07.5-E035`). Members may join or decline without punishment.

This is one of the healthiest political forms available to Ayanokōji in Year 1:

- no host;
- no parasite;
- no compulsory usefulness;
- no threat of expulsion from belonging;
- and no need to win the relationship.

The limitation is informational. The group knows Kiyopon, not the full White Room strategist.

## 6.4 Followers after Ryūen

Ibuki explains that Ishizaki's post-rooftop silence is not simple relief. Ryūen's followers feel abandoned (`Y1V07.5-E025`).

This prepares the political question developed later in the volume:

> If rule began through fear, why do the ruled try to preserve the ruler after his fear-based sovereignty collapses?

The answer cannot be given fully at this point. The volume establishes that coercion has generated relationships more complicated than coercion alone.

# 7. Kei advising Satō — counterfeit expertise becoming ethical responsibility

Satō approaches Kei because the public school world has classified Kei as romantically experienced.

Kei is:

- Hirata Yōsuke's girlfriend;
- socially dominant among the girls;
- fashionable;
- confident;
- and apparently successful in precisely the field where Satō feels uncertain.

The classification is almost entirely false.

Kei's relationship with Hirata is a protective fiction. She has little more genuine dating experience than Satō. The scene therefore begins as another test of public legibility: the girl whose status depends upon appearing romantically secure is asked to convert the appearance into useful knowledge.

The earlier Kei could have improvised.

Her social survival has long depended upon the capacity to:

- sound certain;
- conceal ignorance;
- present adaptation as natural superiority;
- and make other people accept the identity she needs them to see.

Volume 7.5 introduces a different concern. Satō trusts her.

Kei recognizes that a careless answer might alter:

- Satō's expectations;
- the structure of the date;
- the timing of the confession;
- and the emotional injury she may receive.

The issue is no longer whether Kei can preserve the role of romantic expert. It is whether another person's reliance creates an obligation to speak honestly about the limits of that expertise.

This is a quiet but substantial ethical development.

Kushida accumulates trust because being trusted gives her recognition and informational sovereignty. Kei begins learning that trust may instead impose restraint upon the trusted person.

She still gives useful advice. Her guidance about:

- choosing an activity that reduces conversational pressure;
- making reservations;
- controlling the date's rhythm;
- and not assuming that a legendary confession location can override another person's feeling

shows real social intelligence.

Her lack of dating history does not make her useless. It means she must distinguish:

- socially informed judgment;
- from fabricated personal authority.

The double date also demonstrates that ethical action need not emerge from pure motive.

Kei helps Satō because:

- Satō has approached her sincerely;
- she does not want to betray that trust;
- she is curious about Ayanokōji's response;
- she wants to monitor the date;
- and she hopes a clear outcome will reduce her own uncertainty.

The mixture does not make the help false. It shows that human action rarely divides cleanly into altruism and self-interest.

Kei's phrase about the current self is important. High-handed confidence was not proof of genuine strength. A real friend does not exploit the admiration contained in a request for help. That insight marks a movement from **status as armor** toward **status as responsibility**.

# 8. Investigating Satō — romantic knowledge becomes personnel evaluation

Ayanokōji asks Kei to investigate Satō's:

- personality;
- interests;
- habits;
- friendships;
- and general reputation.

The request has an innocent interpretation.

A socially inexperienced boy preparing for a first date may want to know what the girl enjoys.

The request also belongs to Ayanokōji's established intelligence method. Satō is not simply a possible romantic partner. She is a possible new confidante, vulnerability, operational participant, or replacement for Kei.

The difference between ordinary curiosity and personnel assessment remains deliberately unstable until the date itself.

For Kei, that instability is existential.

If Satō becomes Ayanokōji's girlfriend, then Kei may lose:

- emotional significance;
- privileged access;
- strategic necessity;
- and the protection attached to remaining necessary.

Her jealousy cannot be divided into a neat romantic component and a separate survival component. The relationship Ayanokōji created has made those questions structurally inseparable.

This is one reason the volume's romance cannot be evaluated through ordinary triangle language alone.

Satō is not merely another girl competing for the boy Kei likes. She represents the possibility that Ayanokōji's system can function without Kei.

Kei obeys the request despite resentment. The obedience demonstrates:

- operational reliability;
- emotional discipline;
- and continuing subordination.

It also gives her knowledge she can use independently. She understands Satō's sincerity and therefore cannot dismiss her as an inferior obstacle.

The date consequently becomes a test for all three participants:

- Satō tests whether ordinary attraction can become a relationship.
- Kei tests whether she can help another girl while confronting her own jealousy.
- Ayanokōji tests whether the offered relationship is emotionally desirable and strategically compatible.

Only the first participant fully understands the test she is taking.

# 9. Ayanokōji's emerging preference profile

Volume 7.5 provides the richest Year 1 inventory so far of Ayanokōji's ordinary preferences.

The source matters because the preferences are not abstract claims that he wants “normal life.” They are concrete pleasures and interests.

## 9.1 Cinema

Ayanokōji says that cinema is becoming a hobby.

He enjoys:

- trailers;
- anticipation;
- unfamiliar stories;
- learning through fictional worlds;
- low-budget action;
- and unexpectedly emotional 3D animation.

When the theater screening fails shortly before the ending, he does not simply read a review. He wants to experience the completed work for himself.

This is a small declaration of aesthetic autonomy.

The point is not whether the film has high artistic status. He wants the experience because he has chosen it.

Ibuki's parallel goal—to watch every film screened at the school—also attracts his respect. The goal is difficult, self-imposed, and unrelated to class points. He recognizes the value of an ambition whose purpose belongs wholly to the person pursuing it.

## 9.2 Music and sound

During the Satō date, he suppresses his genuine answers because he fears they sound unlike a contemporary high-school boy.

His actual tastes include:

- Beethoven;
- Mozart;
- healing music;
- rain;
- and birdsong.

These preferences are revealing.

They are not optimized for social currency. They are quiet, patterned, and sensory. Rain and birdsong are not even conventional “music” so much as atmospheres in which attention can settle.

The scene demonstrates that Ayanokōji's social mask does not merely hide dangerous ability. It also hides harmless personal taste.

He is unsure whether an authentic preference is acceptable unless it resembles the expected answer.

## 9.3 Animals

He says he once wanted a dog and was refused.

When the others imagine owning pets, his thought moves immediately toward:

- time;
- cost;
- feeding;
- cleaning;
- and constraint.

He recognizes his own answer as emotionally dull.

Hirata succeeds conversationally because he joins the imagined pleasure rather than auditing the practical burden. He validates the other person's fantasy.

Ayanokōji understands the structure after seeing it. He does not yet produce it instinctively.

## 9.4 Snow

He repeatedly enjoys snow as sensory novelty.

The boy capable of extraordinary physical and academic performance lacks ordinary seasonal memories. Snow accumulating on his head while he waits for a political meeting is not a strategic resource. He lets it remain because the sensation interests him.

## 9.5 Books and quiet companionship

The volume continues the Hiyori reading relationship as evidence that Ayanokōji can connect through a shared interest not founded upon rescue, class leadership, or coercion.

## 9.6 Social scale

He repeatedly appears most comfortable with:

- one-on-one conversation;
- small groups;
- predictable social spaces;
- and relationships that do not require theatrical public enthusiasm.

His preference is not isolation.

It is **low-pressure belonging**.

The Ayanokōji Group supplies this more cleanly than any strategic partnership. Haruka explicitly defines membership through voluntary presence: people may join when they want and decline when they want. No one must prove usefulness at every meeting.

## 9.7 Women and romantic type

When asked what kind of girl he prefers, Ayanokōji does not give a narrow physical type. He suggests someone energetic enough to draw his quiet self forward.

The answer is relational.

He imagines a partner who can initiate experiences he does not yet know how to enter.

Satō may appear to fit that description. Kei fits it more forcefully. So do other girls in different ways.

The preference should not be treated as a solved romantic ranking. It indicates that he recognizes his own social passivity and is attracted to the possibility of another person making the ordinary world easier to enter.

The cumulative preference profile matters because it counters two reductive readings.

Ayanokōji is not an empty machine who has no tastes.

Nor does the existence of tastes prove that his ethics have become ordinary.

He can genuinely enjoy:

- music;
- film;
- snow;
- books;
- friendship;
- sexual possibility;
- and another person's presence

while still evaluating people through replaceability and strategic usefulness.

# 10. The Satō date — ordinary romance offered in transparent form

Satō offers Ayanokōji the most socially legible romantic possibility available in Year 1 so far.

She does not approach him through:

- blackmail;
- covert operations;
- shared institutional danger;
- trauma;
- or a hidden ideological rivalry.

She likes him.

She arranges time together.

She prepares a gift.

She intends to confess.

The simplicity is precisely what makes the date difficult for him.

Ayanokōji can solve environments in which:

- objectives are hidden;
- opponents possess incentives;
- and information can be transformed into leverage.

Satō's objective is visible. The unanswered question is whether he wants the same thing.

## 10.1 He notices the staging but permits it

The “accidental” meeting with Hirata and Kei does not deceive him.

He sees:

- prepared seating;
- Satō's imperfect surprise;
- and Kei's more capable performance.

He does not expose them.

This is a benign form of concealment. He protects Satō from embarrassment and allows the social experiment she planned to proceed.

## 10.2 Conversation as reciprocal movement

Ayanokōji describes conversation as receiving and returning a ball.

The metaphor is important because it identifies the difference between:

- possessing information;
- and creating continuity with another person.

A strategically useful question extracts data. A relational question returns something the other person can use to remain present.

He understands the structure intellectually but struggles to generate it naturally.

When asked about music, he searches for the culturally expected answer rather than offering his own. When pets are discussed, he audits burden rather than entering imagination. Satō and Hirata expose not ignorance of facts but a lack of practiced reciprocal play.

## 10.3 Enjoyment is genuine

The date does not bore him.

He enjoys:

- the film;
- the social outing;
- the unusual structure of the day;
- and learning what a date feels like.

He also finds it exhausting.

Both judgments are true.

The experience requires continuous attention to tacit expectations that other adolescents have learned through ordinary repetition.

## 10.4 Satō as a person with a future

Satō discusses fashion and design.

Her future is ordinary in the best sense: a personally meaningful interest that does not exist to answer the White Room, class competition, or Ayanokōji's father.

This makes the ethical risk of accepting her particularly visible.

Ayanokōji would not merely acquire a girlfriend. He would enter the future of a person whose goals do not belong to his hidden conflicts.

# 11. Deliberate emotional restraint — the rejection begins before the confession

The second pass requires an important correction to any reading in which Ayanokōji simply attends the date sincerely and makes a clean decision only when Satō confesses.

He reaches the judgment earlier.

Satō notices that he has not smiled.

Ayanokōji says he enjoyed the day. That is true.

His narration then admits that he could have given better answers. He deliberately did not because he had judged Satō:

> `そうするほどの相手ではない`

She was not the person for whom he would intensify the interaction.

He does not want her attraction to deepen.

This is not merely social incompetence. It is controlled emotional signaling.

The method is less harmful than simulating love and accepting the confession for experience. It remains asymmetrical because Satō does not know that the boy she is evaluating as shy or difficult has already begun managing the intensity of her attachment.

The date therefore contains three layers of Ayanokōji behavior:

1. genuine curiosity and enjoyment;
2. authentic difficulty with ordinary reciprocal conversation;
3. deliberate withholding intended to reduce future emotional cost.

A binary interpretation misses the volume's complexity.

He is both socially inexperienced and strategically regulating the social experience.

# 12. Satō's private ugliness and Ayanokōji's proportional judgment

Satō worries that Ayanokōji knows she once participated in a group conversation proposing that Horikita be bullied.

She fears that the private thought proves she is morally unworthy.

Ayanokōji's response is one of his more humane Year 1 judgments.

He distinguishes:

- resentment;
- private venting;
- and an ugly unacted proposal

from actual harmful conduct.

He says that people speak badly about others in private and that such imperfection should not determine an entire evaluation, provided the speaker accepts that others may speak similarly about them.

The position is not unlimited tolerance.

It rests on two boundaries:

- the thought was not enacted;
- and the person accepts reciprocity rather than demanding immunity from the same judgment.

This contrasts sharply with the school world's recurrent transformation of private ugliness into total social identity.

Kushida's destructive potential depends upon the assumption that every confidential hostile thought can be turned into public proof of hypocrisy.

Ayanokōji does not apply that standard to Satō.

He recognizes a person can contain uncharitable thoughts without those thoughts exhausting the person.

The irony is that he extends proportional moral judgment to Satō while later reducing her romantic suitability through an operational comparison she cannot see.

# 13. Satō's confession — courage without reward

Satō asks Ayanokōji to date her.

His refusal is immediate.

The speed is ethically significant.

He does not:

- prolong hope;
- accept provisionally;
- use her to test sexual or romantic experience;
- or create an ambiguous holding pattern.

He says that he does not currently love her and that accepting without reciprocal feeling would disrespect the courage required to confess.

He also says he is not yet emotionally developed enough to become the partner she wants.

These statements are true.

They are not complete.

## 13.1 Four simultaneous reasons

A responsible interpretation must retain at least four layers.

### A. No present love

He does not experience a feeling he can honestly identify as love for Satō.

### B. Emotional unreadiness

He cannot clearly imagine the ordinary future she offers.

### C. Ethical restraint

He recognizes that accepting a person's sincere affection merely to study romance would use her.

### D. Strategic incompatibility

The unresolved conflicts involving:

- his father;
- Sakayanagi;
- Nagumo;
- Kushida;
- and school expulsion mechanisms

make an ordinary transparent girlfriend vulnerable and operationally difficult.

Kei's continued stability and usefulness also weigh heavily.

## 13.2 The counterfactual

Ayanokōji wonders whether he might have accepted under earlier conditions, before the new external threats became visible.

This is unresolved counterfactual reasoning, not proof.

It does establish that his refusal is situational rather than evidence of total incapacity for romance.

## 13.3 Satō matters to him

He says that she is the first girl to confess sincerely and that he expects to remember the moment for the rest of his life.

The memory has no obvious strategic value.

It matters because someone chose him openly.

That is a significant ordinary experience for a boy whose childhood contained no private courtship, no unsupervised affection, and no reason to imagine being desired as an ordinary classmate.

## 13.4 The cost remains real

Satō cries and runs away.

The ethical quality of his refusal does not make rejection painless.

The volume permits both judgments:

- he chooses the less exploitative answer;
- and a courageous girl is still hurt.

# 14. Kei's interrogation — the ordinary explanation is not enough

Kei has hidden near the confession site.

Her presence exposes the double-date arrangement and confirms how far her stated role as Cupid has become personal surveillance.

She is not satisfied with the explanation that Ayanokōji simply does not love Satō.

She asks for the deeper reason.

This is an important change in their relationship. The Volume 4 Kei had little power to demand internal disclosure from the person controlling her safety. The Volume 7.5 Kei can:

- accuse;
- question;
- refuse easy answers;
- and demand that the person who knows nearly everything about her reveal something in return.

Ayanokōji answers only partially.

He says Satō wants an ordinary romance and that he cannot imagine providing it. Kei challenges the assumption. A person who genuinely likes him might accept:

- his coldness;
- hidden history;
- and unusual life.

Her response is both self-relevant and ethically perceptive.

She rejects the idea that Ayanokōji may decide unilaterally what another person's affection could survive.

He then admits that dating Satō would provide entry into:

- romance;
- sexual intimacy;
- and a world he does not know.

Kei objects to converting a relationship into access to experience.

That objection anticipates the volume's deepest problem: Ayanokōji repeatedly translates emotional life into curriculum because curriculum is the category through which he understands unknown development.

# 15. Why Satō loses to Kei — the largest reason is operational preservation

Ayanokōji eventually identifies the largest practical reason for refusing Satō:

> choosing Satō might destabilize Kei.

This is not a romantic declaration in ordinary form.

It is a comparative evaluation.

He considers whether Satō could replace Kei and tests the possibility against observed behavior.

## 15.1 Deception

Kei sustains the double-date performance more effectively. She recognizes weak points in Satō's acting and adapts around them.

## 15.2 Response under pressure

When Nagumo grips Ayanokōji's arm and creates a threatening atmosphere, Satō freezes. Kei intervenes immediately and transforms the confrontation into a socially legible objection to Nagumo's expression.

Her action is not physically decisive. It is politically intelligent.

She creates a reason for Hirata to support her and gives Nagumo an exit that does not require acknowledging a failed dominance test.

## 15.3 Psychological resilience

The rooftop has demonstrated that Kei can preserve a secret under extreme pressure even when she believes Ayanokōji may not come.

Ayanokōji translates this into reliability.

## 15.4 Social reach

Kei controls access to a large portion of the girls' network.

Satō is socially competent, but she does not possess the same political position.

## 15.5 Knowledge of the hidden Ayanokōji

Kei already knows:

- his strategic capability;
- some of his violence;
- his relation to Ryūen;
- his use of blackmail;
- and enough of his inner difference to function around it.

Satō knows the relay runner and quiet classmate.

## 15.6 External threat environment

Ayanokōji identifies his father, Sakayanagi, Nagumo, and the school's expulsion mechanisms as reasons he requires a collaborator capable of operating in abnormal conditions.

The evaluation is brutal because it is explicit.

Satō is not rejected only because Ayanokōji feels less for her. She is rejected because the existing partner is more compatible with the hidden war governing his life.

The comparison clarifies what `indispensable` means at this stage.

Kei has become difficult to replace because she combines:

- social power;
- secrecy;
- knowledge;
- resilience;
- and emotional attachment.

The bond is personally meaningful precisely through capacities that remain operationally valuable.

The source does not allow those dimensions to be separated cleanly.

# 16. The honey-trap thought — sexuality as another deployable resource

Ayanokōji briefly imagines whether Kei's flexibility might extend to a sexual-compromise operation against an adult authority.

He then recognizes that she is unsuited to such a mission.

The thought is disturbing for a reason larger than feasibility.

It shows how rapidly his strategic mind converts another person's sexuality into a possible instrument.

This is especially severe because Kei's history includes:

- sexualized humiliation;
- bodily violation;
- and Ayanokōji's own use of sexual threat during recruitment.

He does not act on the idea.

The absence of action matters.

The readiness with which the possibility appears also matters.

The scene belongs in the relationship analysis because it reveals the asymmetry between what Kei believes partnership means and what Ayanokōji is still capable of imagining her body can be used for.

The text does not ask us to believe that every strategic thought becomes intention. It does establish that White Room instrumentality extends into domains ordinary intimacy would treat as requiring special consent and personal inviolability.

# 17. “Even after leaving the White Room, I am inside it”

After Kei accuses him of seeing people only as tools, Ayanokōji says that he does not intend such a thing.

The denial cannot govern interpretation by itself.

The surrounding narration has just:

- compared Satō and Kei as replacements;
- ranked deception and resilience;
- imagined sexual exploitation;
- and identified the effect of one girl's romantic selection on the utility of another.

The more important passage follows.

Ayanokōji wonders whether `恋心 / romantic feeling` exists in him at all.

He then recognizes that even after leaving the White Room, he remains inside it.

The White Room has become an internal architecture:

- forecast every threat;
- prepare insurance;
- avoid dependence;
- evaluate people by use;
- and allow others to suffer if final victory is preserved.

This is one of the most self-aware passages in Year 1.

It should not be read as a final metaphysical verdict that he cannot love.

His uncertainty is evidence of developmental deprivation.

The White Room made romance structurally impossible through:

- surveillance;
- absence of privacy;
- constant training;
- lack of leisure;
- and the treatment of attachment as irrelevant to the curriculum.

Because he has not experienced love, he risks treating absence of experience as absence of capacity.

The entire school project exists because capacities may emerge in new environments.

His speculation should therefore remain a `UA / unresolved ambiguity`, not be upgraded into proof of permanent emotional incapacity.

The scene does establish something harsher:

> **Ayanokōji knows that his old rule still governs him and does not yet believe he can discard it.**

He wants ordinary life while assuming that ordinary life must remain subordinate to a survival doctrine built in captivity.

# 18. The medicine — care expressed through logistics

The two cold medicines are the volume's clearest object of care.

Ayanokōji buys them on the morning after the rooftop because he expects repeated exposure to freezing water may make Kei ill. He buys two types because he lacks ordinary experience deciding which medicine is suitable.

The action contains no visible strategic reward.

It occurs before:

- the date;
- Satō's confession;
- Kei's jealousy;
- the Kiriyama meeting;
- and their later partnership negotiation.

It is therefore strong evidence that concern preceded the emotional and political developments of Christmas Day.

The receipt matters because it converts a hidden action into temporal proof.

Kei believed that Ayanokōji had ignored her for two days.

The object reveals that he had been thinking about her.

## 18.1 Why he did not contact her

His reasons are characteristic:

- visiting her dorm room risks exposure;
- calling may cause her to hide weakness;
- renewed contact may impose psychological burden after the harm he caused;
- distant observation shows her apparently healthy with Satō.

These are not absurd reasons.

They demonstrate accurate knowledge of her habits and the constraints of secrecy.

They also demonstrate the failure of care that refuses vulnerability.

A medicine purchased privately cannot tell Kei:

- that he is worried;
- that he recognizes the continuing emotional cost;
- or that he is available if she wants contact.

He protects against a possible physical illness while leaving the emotional interpretation entirely unmanaged.

Kei spends the interval believing that he does not care.

## 18.2 Practical empathy

The act is best described as **practical empathy**.

Ayanokōji can:

- anticipate bodily consequence;
- purchase a remedy;
- observe condition;
- minimize operational risk;
- and explain why direct contact may be counterproductive.

He finds it much harder to say directly:

> I was worried about you.

## 18.3 Misreading strength

He admits that he underestimated Kei.

He expected:

- retreat;
- concealed weakness;
- and slower recovery.

She approaches him instead.

This is another important challenge to his predictive model. Kei does not merely survive the test he designed. Her subsequent behavior exceeds the fragility he assumed.

The correct developmental lesson would be that another person possesses more agency than his model allowed.

Ayanokōji records the correction. Whether he will permit that agency greater structural freedom remains unresolved.

# 19. Hirata and Ayanokōji — two protection models

Volume 7.5 places Hirata's protection of Kei beside Ayanokōji's without declaring either perfect.

## 19.1 Hirata's protection

Hirata accepted a false relationship because Kei needed:

- public status;
- visible male affiliation;
- and a socially credible shield.

The arrangement was:

- nonsexual;
- publicly legible;
- limited by his commitment to broader harmony;
- and designed to end when she no longer required it.

Hirata interprets Kei's ability to walk without him as evidence that the protection may have succeeded.

He does not respond to her potential departure as betrayal or loss of property.

## 19.2 Ayanokōji's protection

Ayanokōji offers more complete security.

He can:

- expose attackers;
- blackmail them;
- defeat violence directly;
- control witnesses;
- and map institutional retaliation.

His protection is more effective because it crosses boundaries Hirata refuses to cross.

It also becomes more secure as Kei's dependence deepens.

## 19.3 Protection designed to end versus protection designed to remain central

The distinction is not that Hirata is purely moral and Ayanokōji purely abusive.

Hirata's universalism limits his ability to choose Kei over everyone else. Ayanokōji's partiality gives her absolute confidence that he will come.

The ethical difference lies partly in the desired endpoint.

Hirata's ideal end is:

> Kei no longer needs him.

Ayanokōji's internal description of the transition is:

> Kei becomes able to leave Hirata while making Ayanokōji the new host.

The first model treats departure as success.

The second treats retained centrality as success.

# 20. The Hirata relationship is not yet publicly over

The prior analysis overstated the chronology by describing Volume 7.5 as though Kei and Hirata had already publicly ended the relationship.

The source supports a more precise account.

Kei and Hirata have discussed:

- the artificial nature of the relationship;
- the diminishing need for it;
- and the problem of extending the fiction indefinitely.

Kei decides that she wants to release the role.

She withholds the Christmas gift from Hirata and redirects it to Ayanokōji.

But the breakup has not yet been publicly completed within this volume.

The distinction matters for both chronology and psychology.

Kei is in transition:

- no longer emotionally organized around Hirata's status;
- not yet publicly separated;
- and increasingly dependent upon the hidden protector who cannot be acknowledged publicly.

The public school still sees one relationship.

The private architecture has already changed.

# 21. Kei's “nine years” — trauma narrative as strategic authorship

Kei reveals that the claim of being bullied for nine years was a lie told to Hirata.

The actual abuse occurred during middle school.

The correction does not weaken the reality or severity of the abuse.

The source has already established:

- sustained humiliation;
- bodily violence;
- social exclusion;
- sexualized coercion;
- and a permanent scar.

What the correction reveals is Kei's agency as a narrator.

She lengthened the timeline because a more extreme story would make Hirata:

- understand the danger;
- accept the protective relationship;
- and remain committed to the role.

Kei is not merely the object of other people's authored narratives.

She can author a narrative strategically to secure protection.

This complicates the parasite label further.

She does not passively attach herself to the strongest available host. She identifies what the prospective protector needs to hear and shapes the information accordingly.

The action is manipulative.

It is also the behavior of a traumatized adolescent trying to make another person grasp that ordinary reassurance is not enough.

The scene should therefore be entered as a factual correction and a character refinement, not as a reason to doubt the underlying victimization.

# 22. From coercive collaboration to negotiated protected partnership

The relationship between Ayanokōji and Kei changes category in this volume.

It does not become equal.

It does become more explicit and more negotiated.

## 22.1 “Trusted partner”

Before Kiriyama, Ayanokōji calls her:

> `信頼できるパートナー`

The phrase has an immediate instrumental purpose. It justifies her presence during a politically sensitive meeting.

It also publicly names the relationship more positively than “tool,” “spy,” or “protected person.”

Kei is surprised.

## 22.2 She rejects unilateral definition

After Kiriyama leaves, Kei objects that Ayanokōji declared the partnership without asking her.

This is not refusal of the relationship.

It is a demand that the relationship be acknowledged through:

- consultation;
- attitude;
- and sincerity.

Her objection is a meaningful increase in agency.

## 22.3 Explicit bargain

Ayanokōji asks for continued assistance.

Kei says she will lend him her power if he protects her fully.

This spoken exchange is ethically different from Volume 4.

In Volume 4, refusal is constrained by:

- blackmail;
- exposure;
- and sexualized threat.

In Volume 7.5, Kei possesses more room to negotiate, and her assent includes a reciprocal demand.

The relationship is therefore better classified as:

> **negotiated protected partnership**

rather than the earlier:

> **coerced protected collaboration**.

The historical coercion remains active. The new negotiation does not retroactively cleanse the original terms.

## 22.4 She offers rescue in return

Kei says that if necessary she will save Ayanokōji.

The statement is not yet backed by equal power.

It matters because she refuses the identity of permanent recipient.

She wants to become someone capable of contributing to his survival.

Ayanokōji calls the offer reassuring and says he means it.

This is one of the first moments in which reciprocity becomes an explicit aspiration rather than only a reader's inference.

# 23. Ryūen after defeat — from visible ruler to suspended strategic possibility

Ayanokōji meets Ryūen privately on December 24. The encounter is neither reconciliation nor a continuation of rooftop hostility in the same form.

Ayanokōji calls Ryūen's surrender an `英断 / wise or courageous decision` (`Y1V07.5-E036`). The praise refers to a specific act of leadership. Ryūen could have continued fighting for personal pride, but doing so would have exposed:

- Ishizaki;
- Ibuki;
- Albert;
- and the class

to greater institutional danger.

Accepting the end of the fight protected people who had followed him.

That does not erase the violence through which he ruled them. It demonstrates that his political identity already contains more than domination for its own sake.

## 15.1 Adversarial transaction

Ayanokōji tells Ryūen that Sakayanagi appears ready to attack Ichinose (`Y1V07.5-E037`). The information can redirect Ryūen's attention away from immediate revenge and toward the wider first-year field.

He also states that he still intends to expel Kushida (`Y1V07.5-E038`). This preserves the central disagreement with Horikita's rehabilitation project.

The conversation is not founded on trust. Both understand that the other may exploit weakness. Their relation becomes:

- mutually informative;
- strategically useful;
- and openly adversarial.

Ryūen proposes that his class may avoid immediate conflict with Ayanokōji's class (`Y1V07.5-E040`). This is not peace. It is temporary alignment produced by the existence of other profitable targets.

## 15.2 The 800-million-point plan

Ayanokōji initially dismisses the plan as impossible (`Y1V07.5-E030`). Ryūen forces a broader calculation.

If one class transfer costs twenty million private points, moving approximately forty students requires roughly eight hundred million. An ordinary class cannot accumulate that amount through monthly income alone.

But the school-wide economy contains:

- hundreds of students;
- three school years;
- reward flows;
- transfers;
- contracts;
- and points that may be concentrated rather than merely saved.

Ayanokōji revises impossibility into a small but nonzero probability (`Y1V07.5-E039`). The exact mechanism remains undisclosed and must remain an open question locally.

The plan is politically revealing even before feasibility is known.

Ryūen publicly presents himself as an individualist who will use everyone else. The memorandum imagines bringing the entire class to A.

This does not prove hidden altruism. A ruler may understand the group as property to be delivered to the destination he chose. It does establish that his ambition is not exhausted by personal escape.

# 24. Manabu, Nagumo, and the expansion of political scale

Ayanokōji brings Ryūen into a meeting with Horikita Manabu (`Y1V07.5-E041`). The act itself is strategic reallocation.

A defeated first-year ruler may still be useful against:

- Sakayanagi;
- Ichinose;
- Nagumo;
- or the wider institutional system.

Preserving Ryūen means preserving an unpredictable source of pressure inside the school.

## 16.1 Manabu's concern

Manabu believes Nagumo's rule may produce unprecedented expulsions. He describes the student council as possessing meaningful influence over:

- school culture;
- disputes;
- special-examination design;
- and the practical enforcement of norms.

The council is not ceremonial. It is one of the institutions capable of changing what the school tests and rewards.

Nagumo's prior involvement in multiple expulsions establishes that his meritocracy is not abstract rhetoric. It already produces human removal.

## 16.2 Nagumo's mobility promise

Nagumo promises to create routes through which capable students can rise beyond the limitations of their classes (`Y1V07.5-E083`). The proposal has genuine meritocratic appeal.

A strong student may be trapped by:

- weak classmates;
- collective penalties;
- or a leader who cannot use the student's ability.

But opportunity becomes patronage if one political actor controls:

- who qualifies;
- when the route appears;
- and what loyalty is required in return.

Nagumo's “individual freedom” may therefore enlarge his own sovereignty over individual futures.

## 16.3 Kiriyama's opposition

Kiriyama claims to resist Nagumo from inside the council after his own class fell from A (`Y1V07.5-E082`). His explanations shift among:

- preserving school tradition;
- accepting responsibility;
- protecting juniors;
- and restoring what was lost.

Ayanokōji distrusts the saintliest version and finds self-interest potentially more credible (`Y1V07.5-E086`). The judgment is not proof that Kiriyama lies. It identifies unresolved motive.

Kiriyama refuses to reveal an earlier special examination because doing so would violate rules and risk expulsion (`Y1V07.5-E085`). His opposition remains procedurally bounded.

At the same time, he threatens mutual destruction if Ayanokōji exposes him (`Y1V07.5-E087`). The covert alliance begins through deterrence, not interpersonal trust.

## 16.4 Kei's independent judgment

Kei reaches a similar distrustful assessment of Kiriyama without simply repeating Ayanokōji's words (`Y1V07.5-E088`). This matters for the emerging partnership.

She is not only:

- an information channel;
- a protected dependent;
- or someone who follows orders.

She can produce independent social judgment relevant to Ayanokōji's political decisions.

# 25. Horikita, Kushida, and autonomy against both brother and strategist

Horikita meets Kushida privately in a public café. The setting requires Kushida to maintain her public persona, but Horikita's goal is not merely to exploit the mask. She continues trying to create a relationship in which dialogue can occur without immediate aggression.

When Ayanokōji wants to postpone discussion of the student council because Kushida is present, Horikita insists that class-related matters should not be hidden from her.

The principle is morally serious and strategically hazardous.

Horikita wants inclusion to become the method through which distrust is overcome. Ayanokōji sees a still-hostile actor receiving additional information.

The difference continues the Volume 6 conflict over integration versus removal.

## 17.1 The student-council proposal

Ayanokōji wants Horikita in the student council partly because:

- Manabu believes she may help contain Nagumo;
- the role would broaden her institutional experience;
- and she could become a visible political actor while Ayanokōji remains outside formal office.

Kushida helps establish direct contact with Manabu. Manabu confirms that joining may benefit Suzune.

Horikita still refuses.

This is one of her strongest acts of autonomy so far. Earlier, Manabu's approval would have been almost dispositive. Here she decides that the role does not yet fit the path she wants.

She chooses against:

- her brother's recommendation;
- Ayanokōji's strategy;
- and the emotional reward of long-desired recognition.

Ayanokōji intends to continue persuading her. The contradiction is exact:

> **He wants Horikita to become autonomous, while preferring that autonomy to move toward the institutional position he considers useful.**

# 26. Ending the Hirata relationship — independence and transfer

Kei considers ending the public relationship because:

- Ryūen's immediate threat has diminished;
- Manabe's leverage has been contained;
- the female social position appears more durable;
- and maintaining the fiction creates pressure.

This is genuine movement toward independence.

Ayanokōji's internal account reveals a simultaneous transfer. He expected this development and prepared himself as the new host (`Y1V07.5-E094`).

The relationship therefore moves from:

- visible, socially protective fiction;

to:

- private, strategically powerful attachment.

Kei asks whether losing Hirata makes her less valuable. Ayanokōji reassures her through the logic she trusts most. If Hirata were necessary to her value, he would already have ordered her to preserve the arrangement.

This is intimacy translated through utility.

He also advises that if she abandons a useful backup, she should build a strategy appropriate to her own capacity (`Y1V07.5-E095`). That statement preserves a route toward agency, even while he remains the most important protective structure.

# 27. Names, gifts, medicine, and the language of private attachment

## 23.1 `清隆` and `恵`

Kei already uses `清隆 / Kiyotaka` privately. Ayanokōji begins using `恵 / Kei` (`Y1V07.5-E096`).

The names create a hidden symmetry unavailable to the rest of the class.

Publicly:

- Ayanokōji;
- Karuizawa.

Privately:

- Kiyotaka;
- Kei.

Kei's intense reaction proves that the change is emotionally consequential, not merely linguistically tidy.

But symmetry of address does not eliminate asymmetry of power. Ayanokōji still knows more, controls more, and can define the relationship's strategic boundaries more completely.

## 23.2 Gifts

The volume uses gifts to distinguish relational languages.

### Satō's unoffered gift

Represents the ordinary romance that does not begin.

### Airi's gloves

Airi notices something Ayanokōji wanted and offers practical warmth without demanding immediate romantic response.

### Kei's redirected gift

The present was purchased to maintain the public Hirata fiction. Giving it to Ayanokōji redirects the object from performed relationship toward private attachment (`Y1V07.5-E093`).

### Ayanokōji's medicine

His gift is contingency planning.

He bought medicine before seeing Kei healthy. The receipt proves the concern was not invented later to placate her (`Y1V07.5-E097`).

He explains that he avoided direct contact because:

- secret communication created exposure risk;
- she might hide weakness;
- and renewed contact could increase emotional burden.

He also admits that he underestimated her strength (`Y1V07.5-E098`).

This is one of the cleanest cases of genuine practical empathy in Year 1.

It also shows the limitation of care delivered only through indirect control. Kei spent two days believing he did not care.

The medicine addressed a possible physical illness. Direct communication would have addressed the emotional injury.

# 28. Irreplaceable versus indispensable

The opening and ending form a deliberate near-answer.

At the beginning:

> `かけがえのない存在`

At the end:

> `必要不可欠な存在`

The phrases overlap but are not equivalent.

`かけがえのない` emphasizes singular personal non-substitutability. The relationship would lose its meaning if the person were replaced.

`必要不可欠` emphasizes necessity. A system, plan, or life structure cannot function in the same way without that person.

Kei clearly reaches the second category (`Y1V07.5-E100`).

She is:

- the strongest bridge to the class's girls;
- one of the few people who knows the hidden Ayanokōji;
- resilient under pressure;
- socially adaptive;
- willing to enter danger;
- and emotionally invested enough to remain close.

Evidence also pushes toward personal exception:

- he worries about her health when no one is watching;
- her replacement affects his romantic choice;
- he grants private naming;
- he calls her partner;
- and he imagines that she may become more than a friend.

But he makes deeper intimacy conditional upon strategic conflict ending.

He still imagines personal life as something that can begin after the environment has been brought under control.

The volume's precise endpoint is therefore:

> **Kei has become functionally indispensable and personally exceptional. Whether she has become emotionally irreplaceable remains unresolved.**

# 29. Relationship architecture at the Volume 7.5 endpoint

Volume 7.5 presents several models of relationship that differ in intensity, transparency, and coercion.

| Relationship | Governing structure | What it makes possible | Central limitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ayanokōji Group | voluntary low-pressure belonging | ordinary friendship without constant utility | members know only a partial Ayanokōji |
| Satō–Ayanokōji | direct ordinary romantic offer | transparent courtship and conventional adolescent development | feeling is not reciprocal; his hidden life cannot be shared safely |
| Hirata–Kei | protective public fiction | social safety without sexual claim | the intimacy is false and depends on status performance |
| Ayanokōji–Kei | protection, secrecy, utility, emerging affection | exceptional trust, operational resilience, private recognition | coercive origin, information asymmetry, and dependence |
| Ayanokōji–Ryūen | adversarial transaction | mutual recognition and strategic redirection | no moral trust; each remains exploitable to the other |
| Horikita–Kushida | attempted integration | refusal of immediate disposability | trust is one-sided and danger remains active |
| Ayanokōji–Kiriyama | mutual deterrence | limited political information exchange | both anticipate betrayal |
| Ike–Shinohara | hostile affection and situational protection | ordinary growth through reciprocal attention | direct feeling remains hidden behind insult |
| Ayanokōji–Sakayanagi | ideological rivalry | meaningful challenge and mutual recognition of ability | other people may become bait in the contest |

The healthiest relationship is not necessarily the most intense.

The Ayanokōji Group permits freedom. The Kei relationship offers greater knowledge and stronger protection, but those benefits are purchased through greater control.

The volume therefore refuses a simple equation:

> more intimacy = more reciprocity.

A relationship can become emotionally central while remaining structurally unequal.

# 30. Ability and legibility

Volume 7.5 expands the Year 1 ability taxonomy beyond formal examination.

## 26.1 Social ability

Kei's ability includes:

- reading atmosphere;
- giving practical dating advice;
- detecting danger;
- intervening against Nagumo;
- and judging Kiriyama independently.

Satō's hesitation during the Nagumo encounter does not make her weak in an absolute sense. It reveals that different social environments demand different forms of response.

## 26.2 Conversational ability

Ayanokōji's “ball” metaphor establishes conversation as a competence:

- receive;
- answer;
- return.

Hirata demonstrates that relational imagination is a form of intelligence. He can enter another person's hypothetical world instead of auditing it from outside.

## 26.3 Aesthetic ability and self-authored preference

Cinema, music, books, and Ibuki's film goal show that a person may develop competence or commitment outside the school's reward structure.

The ability to choose a difficult goal for no institutional reward is itself evidence of autonomy.

## 26.4 Political ability

Nagumo's political ability lies not only in personal scores but in:

- creating opportunity;
- allocating mobility;
- attracting loyalty;
- and making future examinations reflect his theory of merit.

Kiriyama's weaker position illustrates the difference between:

- possessing correct objections;
- and possessing enough institutional power to make those objections govern outcomes.

## 26.5 Measured versus usable ability

Ayanokōji evaluates Satō and Kei according to what his hidden life can use. This is not the same as total human value.

Satō may be better suited to:

- ordinary courtship;
- transparent social life;
- and a relationship in which neither person is a covert operator.

Kei is better suited to his present political environment.

The comparison is valid only for the system whose objective he has chosen.

# 31. Institutional and political development

Although Volume 7.5 contains no formal special examination, it substantially expands the institutional field.

## 27.1 The student council as rule-producing authority

Manabu and Kiriyama make clear that the student council can influence:

- school culture;
- conflict mediation;
- examination ideas;
- and the practical distribution of opportunity.

Nagumo's succession therefore changes more than ceremonial leadership.

## 27.2 Meritocracy and patronage

Nagumo's promise to elevate individually capable students has two interpretations.

### Meritocratic correction

Students should not remain trapped permanently by weaker class cohorts.

### Personal patronage

The person who controls access to mobility can demand loyalty from everyone who wants it.

At the Volume 7.5 boundary, both possibilities remain live.

## 27.3 Inter-year politics

Kiriyama warns that contact across school years will intensify through future special examinations (`Y1V07.5-E084`). The political scale is expanding from:

- four first-year classes;

to:

- year groups;
- student council;
- and the whole school.

Ayanokōji's attempt to withdraw from Class D politics is therefore immediately undermined by larger institutions becoming interested in him.

## 27.4 Class constitutions in transition

### Horikita's class

Ayanokōji imagines Horikita as visible leader supported by Hirata and Kei (`Y1V07.5-E090`). This remains a developmental design, not yet a completed succession.

### Ryūen's class

The visible ruler has withdrawn, but follower loyalty and the 800-million-point plan preserve him as a latent center.

### Ichinose's class

Its moral leader is becoming Sakayanagi's target.

### Sakayanagi's class

Its leader treats other classes as material for proving a theory of genius.

# 32. Ethics and autonomy

The volume's most important ethical question is not whether Ayanokōji cares.

He does.

It is whether care delivered through superior control enlarges or constrains the other person's authorship.

## 28.1 Satō

Ayanokōji refuses to use her as romantic practice. This is morally significant.

He also screens her through information she does not know is being collected and regulates the intensity of her hope before she understands the decision has been made.

The refusal is preferable to acceptance under false pretenses. It is not perfectly transparent.

## 28.2 Kei

Kei gains:

- explicit partnership;
- recognition of ability;
- private naming;
- permission to challenge him;
- and a spoken protection bargain.

She still lacks:

- full knowledge of danger;
- equal ability to define the relationship;
- and freedom from the protector whose continued interest stabilizes her school life.

## 28.3 Hirata

Hirata's model respects exit. Its protection is less effective against predatory actors but more compatible with the protected person's eventual independence.

## 28.4 Ichinose

Ayanokōji's willingness to observe her possible destruction for class advantage demonstrates that selective care remains highly particular.

## 28.5 Ryūen

Preserving a defeated rival can create future development. It can also preserve a dangerous actor because he remains useful to the observer.

## 28.6 Kiriyama

Ayanokōji's alliance with him is built through suspicion and leverage rather than shared consent to a common political project.

# 38. Critical reservations and strongest counterarguments

## 34.1 Kei's recovery is rapid

Only a short interval separates severe retraumatization from energetic involvement in Satō's romantic plans. The text establishes resilience, but the pace risks understating ongoing somatic and psychological effects.

### Strongest defense

Kei's public performance has always required rapid functional recovery. Continued activity does not prove absence of distress.

## 34.2 The relationship can romanticize coercive attachment

Medicine, gifts, private names, and partnership follow immediately after a crisis Ayanokōji helped intensify.

### Strongest defense

The volume does not hide his methods. Kei knows substantial parts of what happened and actively confronts him. Genuine attachment can emerge from morally compromised history without making that history acceptable.

## 34.3 Satō is evaluated as personnel

Ayanokōji's comparison reduces sincere romantic possibility to operational compatibility.

### Strongest defense

He ultimately refuses rather than exploiting her affection. Strategic danger is genuinely relevant to whether he can offer a safe relationship.

## 34.4 The medicine may be overstated as emotional proof

Buying medicine demonstrates concern. It does not prove love or selfless care.

### Strongest defense

No one witnesses the purchase and no immediate strategic reward follows. It is relatively clean evidence that Kei's physical condition matters to him.

## 34.5 Horikita's autonomy may be temporary

She refuses the council, but Ayanokōji plans continued persuasion.

### Strongest defense

The important development is that Manabu's approval no longer overrides her judgment. Later persuasion cannot erase the autonomy already exercised here.

## 34.6 Sakayanagi's nature/education binary is crude

The philosophy underestimates gene-environment interaction and selection effects.

### Strongest defense

Her challenge is not presented as settled science. It is a character ideology that gives her rivalry intellectual structure.

## 34.7 Ayanokōji's gender generalization is reductive

His analysis of Ibuki turns average physical differences into broad individual destiny.

### Strongest defense

The local conclusion—that Ibuki cannot bridge the enormous specific gap—is well supported. The problem lies in the generalization, not the outcome of the match.

# 42. Cumulative delta through Volume 7.5

## 44.1 Ayanokōji delta

Ayanokōji is now more clearly capable of:

- cultural preference;
- ordinary romantic desire;
- practical concern;
- memory investment;
- and enjoyment of experiences with no formal strategic score.

At the same time, he continues translating intimacy into:

- replacement value;
- risk management;
- education;
- and protected utility.

Volume 7.5 therefore strengthens the thesis that humanization and control are developing together rather than one replacing the other.

## 44.2 Kei delta

Kei moves from coerced collaborator toward negotiated protected partner.

She gains:

- greater explicit agency;
- ethical responsibility toward Satō;
- independent judgment of Kiriyama;
- private naming;
- and aspiration toward reciprocal protection.

She does not become independent of Ayanokōji. The relationship's coercive history and host structure remain active.

## 44.3 Horikita and class-leadership delta

Horikita continues trying to integrate Kushida and refuses the student council despite Manabu's approval.

Her visible leadership is becoming less reducible to either:

- imitation of her brother;
- or execution of Ayanokōji's preferred path.

Ayanokōji nevertheless continues imagining her development and role from above.

## 44.4 Ryūen and class-politics delta

Ryūen moves from defeated ruler toward suspended strategic possibility.

The 800-million-point calculation suggests a class-wide political horizon, while his followers' behavior confirms that coercive rule has generated voluntary attachment.

Neither development proves moral reform.

## 44.5 Institution delta

The student council becomes legible as an institution capable of influencing special examinations and school culture. Nagumo's power operates across an entire year group rather than one class.

Year 1 politics has expanded from class rivalry toward school-wide governance.

## 44.6 Ability/merit delta

The volume adds several undermeasured abilities:

- romantic honesty;
- social mediation;
- self-authored leisure goals;
- perception of motive;
- institutional networking;
- and the ability to make another person feel safe enough to disclose uncertainty.

It also shows that an indispensable operative is not automatically an irreplaceable person.

## 44.7 Ethics/autonomy delta

The strongest positive models are:

- Satō's direct confession;
- Hirata's protection designed to end;
- Haruka's noncompulsory group norm;
- Airi's attentive gift;
- Kei's negotiated consent to continued partnership;
- and Horikita's refusal of an externally preferred political path.

The central negative persistence is Ayanokōji's belief that another person's stability, sexuality, and social position may be evaluated as deployable resources.

## 44.8 Ordinary-life delta

Cinema, music, pets, fashion, gifts, winter, and conversation become genuine preference evidence.

Ordinary life is no longer only what Ayanokōji wishes to observe. He is beginning to participate and to discover that some experiences cannot be resolved through information alone.

# 43. Open questions at the Volume 7.5 endpoint

1. Can `必要不可欠` develop into `かけがえのない`, or will Ayanokōji continue defining personal importance through function?
2. Does Kei's negotiated partnership genuinely reduce dependency, or merely make dependency feel chosen?
3. Will Kei end the public relationship with Hirata, and what social consequences will follow?
4. Can she retain Ayanokōji's protection while building independent sources of safety and identity?
5. Will she ever receive the complete truth about why he arranged the Manabe incident and delayed rooftop rescue?
6. How would she judge the relationship if she knew his full internal model of host, guilt, and replacement?
7. Does Ayanokōji's practical concern for Kei become communicatively reciprocal care?
8. Is his inability to imagine ordinary romance a temporary developmental deprivation or a more durable structural limitation?
9. Would he have accepted Satō in a less threatening environment?
10. What lasting effect will the rejection have on Satō's relation to him and Kei?

# 44. Final synthesis

Volume 7.5 begins with Ayanokōji asking whether another person can become irreplaceable.

The volume does not give him that answer.

It gives him an ordinary girl who likes him, a date, a confession, and a transparent route into the adolescent life he says he wants.

Satō offers:

- direct affection;
- conventional dating;
- social openness;
- and a relationship whose meaning would be publicly intelligible.

Ayanokōji refuses.

The refusal is not evidence that he lacks desire. He enjoys the day, wants a girlfriend, recognizes sexual curiosity, and understands that he may be throwing away a real possibility.

Nor is the refusal only evidence of moral restraint. He does correctly judge that accepting without reciprocal feeling would use Satō unfairly. But his deepest practical comparison does not ask which girl he loves.

It asks which girl can survive and function inside his hidden world.

Kei can:

- lie;
- improvise;
- read atmosphere;
- keep secrets;
- recover under pressure;
- intervene around dangerous upperclassmen;
- and continue working after learning how severely he used her.

Satō cannot replace her.

The relationship Ayanokōji preserves is therefore the relationship most compatible with the White Room logic he wants to escape.

That is the volume's central irony.

Yet function does not explain everything.

Before Kei demands proof, Ayanokōji has already bought medicine.

No examination requires it. No audience observes the purchase. The act has no clear immediate payoff.

He worries that the cold water may have made her ill.

His concern is real.

Its form remains White Room-shaped:

- research the appropriate object;
- prepare contingencies;
- observe from a distance;
- minimize exposure;
- and reveal the care only when strategically useful in conversation.

Kei receives the medicine and the explanation. She also receives the knowledge that his concern can exist where she cannot see it.

That knowledge deepens attachment.

The volume's most hopeful development belongs partly to her.

Kei no longer merely accepts the role assigned to her. She objects when Ayanokōji calls her his partner without consultation. She demands sincerity. She negotiates continued cooperation. She offers to help save him. She considers abandoning Hirata's public protection because she believes she can walk without that specific structure.

These are genuine acts of agency.

They occur inside a transfer Ayanokōji anticipated and encouraged. He wanted her capable of leaving Hirata while remaining centered upon himself.

Independence and dependency therefore grow together.

Hirata clarifies the ethical difference.

He protects Kei so that she may eventually no longer need him.

Ayanokōji protects her so effectively that he becomes increasingly difficult to leave.

The first model accepts the end of necessity as success.

The second experiences necessity as security.

But the volume does not let Hirata's model exhaust intimacy either. His relationship with Kei is safe precisely because it is not romantic and because important parts of each person remain concealed. Ayanokōji and Kei enter a more dangerous form of recognition: each knows something socially disqualifying about the other.

- Kei knows his coldness and hidden power.
- Ayanokōji knows her scar, fear, false status, and dependence.

Their given names create a private world built around that asymmetrical knowledge.

Whether the world becomes reciprocal depends upon what happens to the asymmetry.

The broader political plot repeats the same problem at institutional scale.

Nagumo promises individually capable students escape from weak classes. The promise may produce freedom. It may also make their future dependent upon the person allocating escape.

Kiriyama claims to resist for tradition and younger students, but cooperation begins through mutual leverage.

Ryūen's followers choose to preserve the ruler who once controlled them through fear.

Horikita refuses the student council even after receiving the approval she once desired most.

Across these cases, the question is not merely whether a person chooses.

It is:

> **Who authored the field in which that choice became rational?**

Volume 7.5's ordinary-life scenes provide the counterexample.

Airi gives gloves because she noticed what Ayanokōji wanted.

Haruka permits friends to decline without penalty.

Ibuki chooses to watch every school film for no class advantage.

Ike protects Shinohara by reading a social situation more carefully than his academic reputation would predict.

Ayanokōji remains in a broken cinema because he wants to see the ending himself.

These actions do not become meaningful because an institution has optimized them.

They matter because the people chose what they were for.

That is the knowledge Ayanokōji still lacks in romance.

He knows how to make another person necessary.

He does not yet know how someone becomes irreplaceable.

The opening term belongs to love's singularity.

The ending term belongs to a system's dependence.

Kei stands between them.

She is now indispensable to his strategy, personally exceptional to his attention, and increasingly able to demand recognition inside the relationship.

She may become more.

The possibility is not trivial. Ayanokōji imagines it.

But he imagines it after the conflicts end, after danger is controlled, after ordinary life can safely begin.

Volume 7.5's deepest challenge is that ordinary life does not wait for perfect control.

Satō's confession arrives now.

Airi's gloves arrive now.

Kei's jealousy, gift, anger, and partnership arrive now.

Horikita's autonomous refusal arrives now.

The world outside the White Room is not a curriculum that begins when every threat has been eliminated.

It is the unpredictable accumulation of choices made while danger, uncertainty, and incomplete knowledge remain.

Ayanokōji has become capable of recognizing one person as necessary.

His next problem is whether he can value that person in a way that leaves her free to become unnecessary to his system without becoming unimportant to him.

That is the difference between protection and ownership.

It is also the distance between indispensability and love.
