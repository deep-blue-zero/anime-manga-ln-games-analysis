# 1. Executive thesis

Volume 5 is the first Year 1 book in which ability becomes difficult to hide behind institutional ambiguity.

Bodies run in public. Placements are scored. Cameras observe contact. Every class submits the exact order in which its students will compete. Athletic success can be converted into private points or points on a future written examination. Failure can do the reverse. The festival creates one of the school's most direct systems of **public embodied legibility**.

Yet the volume immediately demonstrates that visible excellence is not self-sufficient.

Sudō can outrun almost everyone and still fail as a leader. Horikita can outperform most classmates and still be unable to move efficiently while tied to a slower partner. Kōenji possesses extraordinary physical ability but withholds it from collective use. Ryūen can sacrifice local contests and still dominate the festival's political environment. Sakayanagi cannot physically participate at all and nevertheless commands a growing faction inside Class A. Hirata is less spectacular than Sudō but can convert other people's limitations into usable coordination. Ayanokōji has concealed much of his physical capacity for months, then chooses—briefly and dramatically—to become one of the most visible bodies on the field.

The governing question is therefore not:

> **Who is strongest?**

It is:

> **What must strength become before other people can actually live, move, and grow beside it?**

The sports festival makes that question literal through the three-legged race. Horikita initially treats coordination as a problem solved by making the weaker partner approach the stronger person's pace. Ayanokōji defeats that premise by deliberately running too fast for her. Only after being made the weaker partner can she understand that high ability creates a responsibility to perceive the person attached to it. His explicit lesson is short: look at the other person; sometimes give the other person initiative. The strong do not cease being strong. They make strength usable by adjusting its expression.

Sudō must learn the same lesson at a different scale. His athletic excellence wins events, but he becomes unable to teach because he assumes others can reproduce what is obvious to him. He then mistakes being the class's best athlete for being entitled to command it. When classmates fail to meet his standards, he insults them; when Hirata and Yukimura challenge him, he strikes Hirata and abandons the festival. The class's refusal to follow him materializes something the school does not formally score: **accumulated social trust**.

Horikita's development runs in parallel. She begins the volume with a more collective outlook than before—Volume 4 taught her that some problems cannot be solved alone—but she still understands weaker students as sacrificial placements in an optimization model. She eventually discovers that she and Sudō share the same defect. Both possess ability. Both crave recognition. Both have used superiority to justify contempt. Neither initially knows how to make another person stronger without first demanding that person become more like them.

Their conversation during the lunch break is therefore more important than any placement on the scoreboard. Horikita admits that she has not been as superior as she imagined. Sudō admits that his desire to lead was mixed with a desire to be praised. She rejects his belief that being born to `クズ` condemns him to be `クズ`, asks for his strength, and promises to lend him hers in return. He answers that someone has recognized his `存在意義 / reason for existing` outside basketball. The scene does not establish mature equality, friendship, or romance; Horikita herself explicitly refuses those labels. It establishes their first credible **reciprocal developmental commitment**.

Ayanokōji occupies the contradiction at the center of that growth.

He teaches Horikita a comparatively low-coercion lesson in partner adaptation: demonstrate, articulate the principle, then leave the next move to her. He praises Sakura's effort without exploiting it. He recognizes that the distributive logic of Horikita's sports-festival plan is not the only possible justice. At the same time, he privately commands Kei without explanation, deliberately permits the class to be beaten down because he believes suffering will become `後の力`, and tells Horikita that what she needs is `敗北と再生 / defeat and rebirth`. He wants her to acquire Sudō as a `武器 / weapon`, explicitly comparing that relationship to his own use of Hirata and Kei.

Volume 4.5 had already proved that Ayanokōji knows lower-coercion ways of affecting people. Volume 5 therefore makes his choice of method more ethically legible. He can scaffold. He can encourage. He can invite. He can also withhold rescue and author developmental crisis. The difference cannot be explained by inability to imagine alternatives.

The volume simultaneously complicates his relationship to visibility.

His participation in the final relay is planned. Hirata has already prepared the substitution. That matters. But the decision to turn the relay into a full-speed duel with Manabu is not adequately explained by strategy. Ayanokōji calls Manabu back, asks whether Manabu is interested in him as an individual, offers the race, tells him to run at full speed, and then describes the experience in language that strips away strategy and intellect. The open track is contrasted with the sterile room in which he once ran. Winning the class contest ceases to matter. For a few seconds, the only thing that matters is the person running beside him.

This is controlled exposure, but it is also **intrinsic desire overcoming concealment**.

The volume closes by moving that problem from the track to the White Room. Sakayanagi names the institution, calls Ayanokōji his father's `最高傑作 / masterpiece`, and declares herself suited to burying a `偽りの天才 / false genius`. The exact theory behind those terms remains unresolved at the Volume 5 endpoint. Ayanokōji's response, however, is unusually explicit. He wants a genuine defeat because his own defeat would mean defeating the man who made him. The contradiction is stark: the boy conditioned to value victory can imagine liberation only through an opponent strong enough to overcome that conditioning at full strength.

Volume 5 therefore links three forms of freedom:

- Sudō's freedom from inherited self-contempt;
- Horikita's freedom from the belief that superiority means isolation;
- Ayanokōji's freedom from a life in which ability only executes someone else's purpose.

The school ranks all three through performance. The volume is more interested in whether any of them can decide what their ability is **for**.

# 2. Second-pass findings at a glance

The fresh reread confirms the first-pass thesis that Volume 5 distinguishes **being strong** from **making other people stronger**, but the second pass sharpens several elements.

## 2.1 Strength is relational, not merely individual

The festival repeatedly tests whether high individual capacity can be converted into collective action. A fast runner who cannot coordinate with a partner, an athlete who cannot teach, or a genius whom no one trusts may have high possessed ability but low socially usable ability.

## 2.2 Public visibility is a new political burden

Previous volumes often rewarded concealed causality. The sports festival places bodies before classmates, teachers, cameras, and rival observers. Ayanokōji's strategic problem changes from **how to remain unseen** to **how to manage what a newly visible performance will be taken to mean**.

## 2.3 Horikita and Sudō are developmental mirrors

The most important relationship turn is not that Horikita reforms Sudō from above. Each sees an analogous failure in the other: pride, need for recognition, poor adaptation to weaker people, and inability to convert talent into legitimate leadership.

## 2.4 Hirata is the volume's clearest relational competence model

Hirata's leadership is embodied in small adjustments. He asks what another person is experiencing and changes his own position accordingly. This is a kind of practical intelligence that the festival's rankings do not directly measure.

## 2.5 Ayanokōji's developmental ethics become more difficult to excuse

He can produce low-coercion learning when he chooses. His decision to let Class D absorb avoidable harm for developmental purposes is therefore not merely an inevitable consequence of his conditioning; it is an exercise of judgment.

## 2.6 Ryūen attacks the relationship between truth and institutional action

His most effective tactics are not simply violent. They produce real injuries or suspicious effects while obscuring intent. He understands that a camera can record an event without recording the meaning needed to punish it.

## 2.7 The relay is not reducible to counterintelligence

The substitution is preplanned and the exposure can later be given a strategic cover story. But the full-speed duel itself is narrated as purposeless competition in the strongest sense: strategy and intellect recede, and Ayanokōji wants the contest because the contest exists.

## 2.8 Sakayanagi turns concealed history into a new axis of competition

Her closing recognition confirms that Ayanokōji's hidden background is known by at least one peer. What `偽りの天才` means remains locally unresolved; the second pass does not import later explanatory frameworks.

# 3. Position in the opening Year 1 architecture

The second-pass opening arc now reads as a progression in what can be authored or controlled.

| Volume | Dominant problem |
|---|---|
| V1 | **Authored visibility** — what ability and causality others are permitted to see |
| V2 | **Authored legibility** — what version of truth or identity becomes institutionally actionable |
| V3 | **Authored environment** — what conditions make other people contribute, fail, or receive public meaning |
| V4 | **Authored dependency** — what conditions make protection feel necessary and who controls access to it |
| V4.5 | **Counter-curriculum** — development through ordinary experiences whose purpose is not institutionally predetermined |
| V5 | **Controlled exposure** — what happens when hidden ability becomes public, relational, embodied, and politically interpretable |

Volume 5 does more than extend the escalation. It folds the sequence back on Ayanokōji. Until now, he has generally controlled other people's visibility while minimizing his own. The final relay places him inside the system of observation he has been managing.

That change matters because the volume has already established that public visibility is not neutral. Sudō's visible excellence invites leadership expectations. Horikita's visible competence makes her a target. Kōenji's visible power becomes politically frustrating because it cannot be commanded. Sakayanagi's visible bodily incapacity conceals political influence. Once Ayanokōji runs at full speed, his body becomes new social evidence. He cannot simply make the performance disappear. He can only attempt to author the explanation attached to it.

This is also the first volume in the second pass where `実力主義 / meritocracy based on ability` becomes an explicit competing political program. Nagumo's promise of an `究極の実力主義の学校` makes the title's ideology something characters can argue about rather than merely inhabit.

# 4. Narrative architecture

Volume 5 is structured around repeated movements from **capacity** to **coordination**.

## 4.1 Sudō's monologue: talent without stable self-worth

The opening gives the athlete the narrative authority Ayanokōji usually monopolizes. Sudō admits that he is not a well-formed person. His mother left; his father works quietly as a cleaner; he came to despise the smallness he associated with his family. He abandoned academics, tested individual sports, discovered his ceiling, then found basketball—a team sport that still allowed exceptional individual performance to remain visible. A recommendation from a strong basketball school offered a path outward. Violence destroyed it.

He then converts failure into identity:

> `クズの両親から生まれたクズ`

`Y1V05|spine:11|xhtml:text/part0009.html|p:0015`

This is crucial because later Horikita does not merely tell Sudō to behave better. She rejects the ontology behind that sentence. She insists that what he becomes is not settled by parentage.

## 4.2 Rules before bodies

The festival is introduced through an unusually detailed scoring architecture. The school first defines how every body will be converted into numbers: group points, class points, private points, written-test protection, written-test penalties, and individual awards.

The result is not a celebration of sport. It is a translation machine between domains of merit.

An athletic performance can protect a student who is academically weak. Conversely, poor athletic performance can make a future written examination more dangerous. The school deliberately makes one kind of ability compensable by another—but only through competition.

## 4.3 Class D debates what fairness means

The class must decide whether to optimize its strongest athletes or preserve more individual opportunity. Hirata gives the conflict procedural form. Horikita argues for an ability-first plan; Kei, following Ayanokōji's private order, supplies opposition and routes the discussion through Kushida; Kushida proposes compromise.

The vote ends 16–13 in favor of Horikita's plan, with abstentions. The important point is not only which policy wins. The scene separates several theories of fairness:

- maximize class outcome;
- preserve individual opportunity;
- compensate those intentionally sacrificed;
- defer to social legitimacy;
- or refuse to treat any one formula as the sole `正義 / justice`.

## 4.4 Training makes leadership visible before the festival

Sudō is chosen as athletic leader and immediately reveals the distinction between expert and teacher. Hirata's contrasting micro-adjustments establish the novel's answer before the main crisis occurs.

Horikita receives a literalized version of the same lesson during three-legged-race practice.

## 4.5 The festival becomes an adversarial information system

Once the event begins, Ryūen's possession of Class D's participation sheet lets him schedule favorable opponents, target Horikita, and provoke Sudō. The cameras increase evidence but do not solve interpretation. His tactics are carefully placed near the line where malicious intent is hard to prove.

## 4.6 Collapse creates the lunch-break developmental center

Sudō strikes Hirata and abandons the festival. Horikita is injured and focused on her own failures. Ayanokōji refuses rescue and instead pressures Horikita to retrieve him.

The narrative then temporarily shifts away from competition toward two students admitting what they lack.

## 4.7 The final relay converts hidden ability into public spectacle

Horikita gives up the anchor position she wanted for personal reasons. Sudō returns. Ayanokōji enters the relay through a planned substitution. The class appears more coherent precisely when its strongest individuals stop treating the event as an individual stage.

Ayanokōji then breaks that pattern personally by turning the race into a duel he wants for himself.

## 4.8 The epilogue expands the battlefield

Kushida's hostility becomes explicit. Ryūen's evidentiary trap is countered by an unknown source of stronger evidence. Sakayanagi reveals knowledge of Ayanokōji's hidden past. The sports festival ends, but the volume replaces bodily visibility with a more dangerous question: **who knows what the visible person really is?**

# 5. The sports festival as an institutional mechanism

The sports festival is not framed exactly like the hidden-rule special examinations of Volumes 3 and 4, so this artifact does not flatten the terminology by calling every school event the same kind of `特別試験`. It is nevertheless unmistakably an institutional evaluation system with class-point, private-point, and academic consequences.

## 5.1 Formal mechanism

The first-years are divided across the broader Red/White structure, with Class A and Class D on Red and Classes B/C on White. The event distributes points at several levels.

### Event scoring

Individual compulsory events award group points by placement; selected events award larger values, and the final relay is tripled. Team events can produce large fixed awards.

### Red/White penalty

The losing side costs every class on that side 100 class points.

`Y1V05|spine:13|xhtml:text/part0011.html|p:0092-0093`

### Year-class ranking

The year-level class ranking separately awards or subtracts class points:

- first: +50;
- second: no change;
- third: -50;
- fourth: -100.

`Y1V05|spine:13|xhtml:text/part0011.html|p:0094-0098`

The winning Red/White side receives no positive bonus merely for winning. It avoids the 100-point loss.

### Individual reward and academic exchange

Top-three individual placements may be exchanged for private points or future written-test points. Last place costs private points and may become an academic penalty if the student lacks sufficient balance.

`Y1V05|spine:13|xhtml:text/part0011.html|p:0109-0114`

### Aggregate bottom-ten penalty

The bottom ten students in the year receive a ten-point penalty on the next written examination.

`Y1V05|spine:13|xhtml:text/part0011.html|p:0130-0136`

### Participation-sheet lock

The most strategically important rule is the class's responsibility to submit the exact event and order assignments. After the deadline, the schedule cannot be changed even if circumstances change.

`Y1V05|spine:13|xhtml:text/part0011.html|p:0162-0167`

That rule turns information about bodies into targetable intelligence.

## 5.2 What the system makes commensurable

The school deliberately permits physical performance to modify academic risk. That is not a trivial bonus mechanic. It declares that one kind of excellence can function as currency in another domain.

For a student like Sudō, that can be emancipatory. Basketball-level athletic competence now has direct institutional power in a school system that earlier threatened him through academics. For a physically weak but academically strong student, the same mechanism can feel punitive. The school does not ask whether cross-domain conversion is intrinsically fair. It establishes a market in which bodies can buy academic insurance.

## 5.3 The event rewards contribution but not necessarily flourishing

The winning side's lack of a positive class-point bonus is important. At the end of the volume, every first-year class loses class points despite the event's enormous labor. Red wins, yet Class A loses 50 and Class D 100 because of their year ranking. White's Class B loses 50 after netting first-place compensation against the side penalty, and Class C loses 100.

The institution therefore teaches a bleak but useful lesson:

> **Victory can mean only losing less.**

This is a meritocracy organized not simply around rewards but around differential exposure to loss.

## 5.4 Participation is collective; cost remains uneven

Horikita's optimization plan correctly sees that the class result rewards concentrating its best athletes. Shinohara correctly sees that weak students then lose individual chances at rewards and expose themselves to academic penalties. Both are describing real features of the same design.

The school forces the class to decide who should bear the opportunity cost of maximizing collective rank.

## 5.5 Cameras do not produce justice automatically

The festival increases surveillance in order to avoid ambiguous adjudication. Ryūen responds not by abandoning misconduct but by shifting toward conduct whose intent is difficult to prove. The lesson continues Volume 2's epistemic problem: more observation does not erase the distance between event truth, evidence, interpretation, and enforceable record.

# 6. Ability after four volumes: from possession to conversion

Volume 5 is an unusually clean demonstration of why `実力` cannot be treated as a single power ranking.

## 6.1 Possessed ability

Sudō, Horikita, Kōenji, Manabu, Nagumo, Shibata, and Ayanokōji possess substantial athletic ability. The festival can expose some of this directly.

## 6.2 Displayed ability

Ayanokōji deliberately displays little until the final relay. Kōenji displays enough to make his strength undeniable while refusing sustained contribution. Sakayanagi's bodily limitation means physical display is unavailable to her, but other kinds of power remain visible through factional alignment.

## 6.3 Measured ability

The school's placements measure performance under particular event conditions. They do not measure:

- willingness to contribute;
- teaching ability;
- social legitimacy;
- capacity to regulate anger;
- ability to interpret evidence;
- strategic deception;
- or political authority.

## 6.4 Socially usable ability

This is where Hirata repeatedly outperforms more spectacular students. He is not simply kind. He converts difference into coordination. He can make a partner more effective without first demanding that the partner resemble him.

## 6.5 Developmental ability

Sudō's most important accomplishment is not a first-place finish. It is the capacity to hear criticism, return, apologize, and imagine a different relationship between his talent and the class.

Horikita's most important accomplishment is not outrunning Ibuki. It is recognizing that failure can teach and that a weaker or more volatile classmate may be someone to develop with rather than discard.

## 6.6 Political ability

Ryūen demonstrates that the ability to place opponents into bad decision environments can matter more than one's own athletic placement. Sakayanagi demonstrates that a person can accumulate followers even while contributing no physical score at all.

## 6.7 Moral ability

The volume also introduces a dimension the school does not score: whether a strong person can use power without converting everyone nearby into expendable means.

Ayanokōji's strategic brilliance remains extremely high. His moral use of that brilliance is considerably less stable.

# 7. Sudō Ken — from visible talent to accountable contribution

Sudō receives one of the strongest developmental arcs in early Year 1 because the volume refuses to make improvement identical with winning.

## 7.1 The shame beneath arrogance

The opening monologue reveals that Sudō's pride is partly defensive. He does not merely believe himself superior. He fears that one act of violence confirmed a hereditary verdict: that his family is worthless and he is therefore worthless too.

Basketball matters because it supplies the clearest counterevidence. It is the one field where he can be visibly excellent.

That explains why the sports festival is psychologically dangerous. It offers him a school-wide stage on which ability can finally produce admiration—and therefore tempts him to confuse recognition with leadership.

## 7.2 Why basketball fits him

Sudō says he disliked team play but accepted basketball. That is not contradictory. Basketball lets individual brilliance remain visible inside collective success. His problem is therefore not that he cannot function in groups at all. It is that he initially understands group membership through the visibility of his own contribution.

## 7.3 The failed instructor

Once classmates ask him for help, Sudō begins sincerely. The scene matters because his failure is not hypocrisy. He actually wants to teach.

The problem is cognitive and relational. What his trained body experiences as obvious cannot simply be copied by another body.

Ayanokōji's narration states the principle directly:

> `指導者は自分と相手は根本的に違う...`

`Y1V05|spine:21|xhtml:text/part0019.html|p:0115`

A leader must recognize that self and other are fundamentally different.

Hirata then demonstrates the positive model. He makes small positional adjustments, asks how the partner experiences them, and changes again. Expertise becomes teaching only when it can model another person's limitations rather than treating those limitations as disobedience.

## 7.4 The failed leader

Sudō's second collapse is political. He interprets “leader” as an entitlement generated by being the best athlete. Hirata refuses to deny his athletic authority but asks a different question: how many classmates presently recognize him as someone they want to follow?

The answer is visible around them. People keep their distance.

Yukimura then voices the class's hidden criticism: Sudō looks more invested in displaying himself than in winning together. Sudō strikes Hirata.

This is the moment raw capability becomes socially unusable.

## 7.5 Trust as accumulated capital

After Sudō leaves, almost nobody follows. Ayanokōji explicitly contrasts this with Hirata or Kushida. If either of them had fled, the class would search.

The point is not that Sudō deserves abandonment. It is that leadership legitimacy has a history. Social trust is accumulated through repeated conduct and cannot be demanded at the moment one needs it.

## 7.6 The Horikita conversation

Sudō's confession is striking because he names what he wanted: recognition. He wanted people to see his excellence and stop treating him as trash.

Horikita recognizes the same structure in herself. Her audience was narrower—primarily Manabu—but the logic is similar. Both treated the judgment of another as proof of self-worth.

She rejects his inherited fatalism:

> `自分自身が決めるもの`

`Y1V05|spine:37|xhtml:text/part0035.html|p:0162-0166`

This is not motivational fluff. It directly attacks the ontology established in Sudō's opening monologue.

## 7.7 `存在意義` beyond basketball

When Horikita asks him to lend her his strength and promises to lend him hers, Sudō says he feels his `存在意義` recognized outside basketball.

`Y1V05|spine:41|xhtml:text/part0039.html|p:0034`

That is the volume's most important Sudō development. The school rewards him for athletic output; Horikita recognizes him as a person whose strength can matter in a shared project.

## 7.8 Why the development remains provisional

The second pass should not call Sudō “reformed.” One apology and one return do not erase his violence. His romantic attachment to Horikita also makes their motivational structure asymmetrical.

What the volume establishes is narrower and stronger:

- he can hear criticism after emotional collapse;
- he can return voluntarily;
- he can apologize publicly;
- he can accept a behavioral constraint against unjustified violence;
- and he can imagine his ability as something lent to others rather than merely displayed before them.

That is genuine development. Its durability is an open question at the Volume 5 endpoint.

# 8. Horikita Suzune — superiority becomes responsibility

Horikita's Volume 5 development is not a sudden conversion from selfishness to kindness. It is the beginning of a more sophisticated theory of strength.

## 8.1 She already changed before the festival

Early in the volume she tells Ayanokōji that the previous special examination demonstrated something she can no longer deny: some problems cannot be solved by one person alone.

That is real progress. But her first sports-festival policy shows the limit of the lesson. She now accepts the necessity of other people without yet fully accepting their claims as individuals.

## 8.2 The ability-first plan

Horikita's proposal is rational at the class level. Put strong athletes where they can maximize the class result; use weaker students as sacrificial placements against superior opponents; compensate some costs afterward.

Shinohara's objection is equally rational at the individual level. The weaker student loses chances at private points, academic protection, and the meaningful experience of competing for a result.

Ayanokōji refuses to declare Horikita's plan the only `正義`.

`Y1V05|spine:19|xhtml:text/part0017.html|p:0115-0119`

The disagreement is important because the later volume does not simply vindicate optimization. It teaches Horikita that leadership must account for people whose value is not exhausted by the class aggregate.

## 8.3 The three-legged race as miniature leadership theory

Horikita initially expects partners to approach her pace. She is physically better; therefore her speed seems like the natural standard.

Ayanokōji reverses positions by running too fast for her. Once she becomes the limiting partner, he can articulate the principle:

> `相手を見ること`

and sometimes

> `相手に主導権を与える`

`Y1V05|spine:21|xhtml:text/part0019.html|p:0265-0266`

The lesson is not “the stronger person must always slow down.” It is that coordination requires the person with greater capacity to understand what the relationship can actually transmit.

Ayanokōji then stops:

> `あとは本人次第だ`

`Y1V05|spine:21|xhtml:text/part0019.html|p:0270`

This is an important counterexample to his harsher developmental interventions. He can create an instructive experience, state the lesson, and leave the subject with genuine room to act.

## 8.4 Personal motive beneath strategic language

Horikita's insistence on running anchor appears irrational until the class presses her for a reason. She finally admits that her brother is probably the anchor for the third-years.

`Y1V05|spine:25|xhtml:text/part0023.html|p:0111`

This is a small but important act of vulnerability. The class can accommodate her personal desire only after she stops disguising it as impersonal optimization.

Sudō voluntarily yields the anchor position once he understands why it matters to her. That sequence foreshadows the lunch conversation: cooperation becomes possible when motive is disclosed rather than hidden behind superiority.

## 8.5 Injury and inversion

Horikita's injury turns the earlier three-legged-race lesson into lived necessity. When paired with Kushida, the injured Horikita cannot set the pace. Kushida must lead.

The novel does not need to explain the symbolism. The person who began the volume treating weaker partners as the problem becomes the weaker partner whose continued participation depends on another person's adaptation.

## 8.6 Why Ayanokōji calls her useless

Ayanokōji's confrontation is deliberately harsh. He does not claim Horikita has no skills. He argues that her current skills are not the skills the class needs most.

If she insists she will pull others toward Class A, then individual placement ceases to be the only relevant metric. A leader who cannot retrieve the class's most important athlete because she is preoccupied with her own score is, in that institutional moment, less useful than the volatile athlete she looks down upon.

This is a legitimate leadership insight delivered through an ethically troubling pedagogy.

## 8.7 `武器` and the limit of Ayanokōji's educational language

Ayanokōji tells Horikita she has an opportunity to obtain a personal `武器`, explicitly comparing Sudō to his own use of Hirata and Kei.

`Y1V05|spine:31|xhtml:text/part0029.html|p:0320-0323`

This exposes the contradiction of his educational insight. He teaches Horikita to recognize another person's needs while privately conceptualizing that person as a resource.

The reader should not automatically assume Horikita adopts that ontology simply because the intervention helps her reach Sudō.

## 8.8 Horikita exceeds the framing

The lunch-break conversation is important precisely because Horikita does more than recruit an athlete.

She admits her own failure. She recognizes Sudō's need for self-definition. She rejects the claim that his parents determine his worth. She promises to help bring him back if he loses his way. She asks for his strength while offering her own.

That is closer to reciprocity than to weapon acquisition.

This is one of the clearest early examples of Ayanokōji setting conditions whose human result is **richer than his instrumental vocabulary for it**.

## 8.9 Giving up the relay

Horikita's most concrete leadership choice occurs later. The relay is the event she wanted most because it offered a chance to run beside Manabu. Once she recognizes that her injury threatens the class, she withdraws and bows to the class.

Her development is therefore not proven by what she says to Sudō alone. She gives up the very reward around which she had hidden a personal motive.

## 8.10 Failure as educational material

At the end of the festival she describes the experience as one that made her grow and uses a formulation that would have been difficult to imagine for early Horikita:

> `失敗を糧に`

`Y1V05|spine:45|xhtml:text/part0043.html|p:0178`

This does not mean she embraces failure sentimentally. She now sees failure as information that can alter future action rather than as proof that an inferior person should be discarded.

# 9. Hirata Yōsuke — relational ability as invisible infrastructure

Hirata is not the volume's dramatic center, but he is its clearest positive model of socially usable ability.

## 9.1 Procedure before authority

He gives the class's policy disagreement a form people can inhabit: competing proposals, discussion, and vote. He does not simply impose the efficient answer.

That procedural instinct matters because Class D's problem is increasingly not lack of good ideas but lack of legitimate mechanisms for deciding among them.

## 9.2 Teaching by adaptation

Where Sudō grows frustrated that classmates cannot reproduce his motions, Hirata adjusts his own position by centimeters and asks how it feels to the other person.

This is a small scene with major theoretical value. It demonstrates that empathy is not merely emotional softness. It can be a **technical method of coordination**.

## 9.3 Leadership without denial of another's talent

When Sudō explodes, Hirata does not claim he is the true athletic leader. He explicitly acknowledges Sudō's leadership within the sports festival while separating that from legitimacy.

This distinction is sophisticated:

> capacity can justify a role without justifying every use of authority inside that role.

## 9.4 The lie that prevents immediate punishment

After Sudō strikes him, Hirata tells the teacher he fell. This is ethically ambiguous. It prevents institutional escalation and gives Sudō space to return voluntarily, but it also shields violence from formal accountability.

The school can only punish what becomes an enforceable record. Hirata uses the victim's control over testimony to change that record.

The incident therefore belongs not only to the leadership thread but to the series' recurring distinction among event truth, proof, credibility, and institutional action.

## 9.5 Hirata and Ayanokōji

Ayanokōji increasingly relies on Hirata as social infrastructure. The final relay substitution is not spontaneous; Hirata has already helped prepare the conditions for Ayanokōji to enter.

Yet the public story continues to make Horikita the visible author of the class's improvement.

Hirata therefore occupies an intermediate position: he knows more about Ayanokōji's competence than most classmates, participates in hidden coordination, and still functions through consent and visible social trust rather than fear.

# 10. Ryūen Kakeru — from violence to evidentiary warfare

Ryūen's Volume 5 strategy is important because it demonstrates development in an antagonist without moral rehabilitation.

He becomes better at making violence **institutionally deniable**.

## 10.1 The leaked participation sheet

Once Class D's schedule is fixed, leaked information lets Ryūen place strong athletes against weak ones, sacrifice his own weaker students against Sudō and other strong opponents, and target Horikita repeatedly.

The strategy converts advance knowledge into a structural advantage rather than a single trick.

## 10.2 Provocation as an attack on usable ability

Ryūen understands that Sudō's strongest physical capacity is paired with poor emotional regulation. He therefore does not need to defeat Sudō athletically. He needs to turn Sudō into a reason Class D defeats itself.

The pole-toppling and other confrontations repeatedly press that weakness.

## 10.3 Local losses can serve political victory

The sudden release during tug-of-war is exemplary. Ryūen accepts the local consequence in order to destabilize the opponent physically and emotionally.

He therefore operates on a different time horizon from students who interpret every event as an isolated contest.

## 10.4 The sticky headband and disappearing proof

After Hirata nearly succeeds against Ryūen's cavalry, a sticky transparent substance is found on Hirata's hand. Ryūen says it is merely hair wax. By the time the accusation is made, the headband no longer preserves decisive evidence.

`Y1V05|spine:31|xhtml:text/part0029.html|p:0121-0132`

The tactic is effective because suspicious physical evidence is not identical with proof of malicious intent.

## 10.5 Kinoshita: making a false causal story from real injury

The Kinoshita operation is a more serious escalation.

Ryūen later explains the construction in hypothetical form: obtain Class D's schedule through Kushida, train Kinoshita to produce a plausible collision, then ensure that the resulting injury is severe enough to make Horikita's alleged misconduct credible. He explicitly says he inflicted the injury himself after the staged event and that Kinoshita agreed in exchange for a 500,000-point share.

`Y1V05|spine:47|xhtml:text/part0045.html|p:0045`

This deserves careful ethical classification. Kinoshita's agreement does not transform the act into harmless gamesmanship. Ryūen intentionally injures a subordinate to manufacture evidence in a coercive competitive environment. Payment explains consent to participation; it does not erase bodily harm or the political power relationship surrounding the choice.

## 10.6 Real evidence, false causation

The accusation against Horikita is dangerous because nearly every visible component is true:

- a collision occurred;
- Horikita looked back;
- Kinoshita is injured;
- cameras exist;
- complaint timing can be made plausible;
- the injury has material consequences.

The falsehood lies in the causal attribution of intention.

This is the mature form of the problem Volume 2 introduced: truth can be defeated not by inventing every fact but by arranging true facts around a false explanation.

## 10.7 Ryūen's own recording

Horikita attempts to use a recording of Ryūen's explanation against him. Ryūen reveals that he has recorded the wider conversation too, preserving context sufficient to neutralize a selective excerpt.

He is therefore no longer merely the actor who exploits weak evidence. He actively manages the evidentiary record.

## 10.8 The recording that turns the system back on him

Ryūen then receives audio apparently capturing the real pre-festival planning meeting. The new recording is more damaging because it contains direct planning rather than a hypothetical reconstruction.

At the Volume 5 endpoint, the source of that recording remains unresolved. Ryūen infers an internal Class C betrayal or penetration point. Ayanokōji has earlier sent prepared communications, and the reader has reason to connect him to the counteroperation, but the exact recorder, acquisition mechanism, and chain of custody are not fully established locally.

The second pass therefore records the hidden-source mechanism as **unresolved/strong inference**, not textual fact.

# 11. Ayanokōji Kiyotaka — visibility becomes something he wants

Volume 5 is one of the most important early books for Ayanokōji because it places ordinary desire, strategic authorship, and White Room conditioning into unusually direct conflict.

## 11.1 He resents externally authored purpose

Chabashira pressures him to produce results worth the institutional cost of protecting him. Ayanokōji answers that what he does is his choice and privately resents the erosion of his ordinary days.

`Y1V05|spine:17|xhtml:text/part0015.html|p:0006-0008`

The objection is not only to risk. It is to someone else assigning a purpose to his life.

That makes his later behavior toward Horikita ethically significant. The boy who resents being developed for another person's objective repeatedly decides what developmental pressure other people require.

## 11.2 He can recognize distributive pluralism

During the class-policy vote he tells Horikita that her plan is not necessarily the sole justice. He recognizes that weaker students experience opportunity costs even when the class aggregate benefits.

This matters because Ayanokōji is not incapable of seeing individual claims against collective optimization.

## 11.3 Kei remains under private command

The same scene reveals a sharper contradiction. Kei opposed Horikita because Ayanokōji ordered her to do so. When she asks why, he tells her that continually demanding explanations will get her nowhere. She correctly interprets the relation: obey without reasons.

`Y1V05|spine:19|xhtml:text/part0017.html|p:0170-0177`

Volume 4.5 showed signs of ordinary personal concern between them. Volume 5 does not erase that. It shows that concern and hierarchy coexist.

## 11.4 The class becomes a developmental project

Ayanokōji tells Kei that he does not care about personally rising to Class A, but has begun to think he might make the present class capable of doing so.

`Y1V05|spine:19|xhtml:text/part0017.html|p:0186`

This is a major shift in object. The class is no longer merely an environment he survives. It becomes something he may cultivate.

The ethical question is immediately visible: does cultivation create people capable of independent action, or people whose development remains dependent upon his hidden design?

## 11.5 Low-coercion teaching exists in the same volume

The three-legged-race lesson is crucial because it disproves any claim that Ayanokōji can teach only through crisis. He creates a demonstration, explains the principle, and stops.

The subject retains ownership of the next action.

This is why the harsher Horikita intervention cannot be excused as the only educational language he possesses.

## 11.6 Deliberately allowing Class D to lose

When Kei asks whether he can help, he says the most important thing is to do nothing and let the class be beaten down because it will become later strength.

`Y1V05|spine:31|xhtml:text/part0029.html|p:0193-0198`

This is one of the clearest early statements of developmental environmental authorship.

The defense is real: immediate rescue might preserve the class's existing weaknesses. Horikita might not recognize her dependence on Sudō; Sudō might not confront his legitimacy problem; Kushida's betrayal might remain hidden; Ryūen's methods might remain less exposed.

The criticism is equally real: Ayanokōji claims the authority to decide that classmates should absorb avoidable injury, humiliation, point loss, and anxiety because he predicts future developmental benefit.

## 11.7 The harsh Horikita intervention

He calls Horikita useless, attacks her self-conception, tells her she is less useful than Sudō in the current context, and refuses to give her the answer after pushing her to the edge of it.

His stated goal is `敗北と再生`.

`Y1V05|spine:31|xhtml:text/part0029.html|p:0305-0327`

This is not identical with the Volume 4 operation against Kei. The threat and harm are much lower, and Horikita has greater exit capacity. But the underlying authorship logic is continuous: he determines the subject's developmental deficit, creates or permits pressure, withholds direct rescue, then waits for self-reconstruction.

## 11.8 He preserves Horikita as public author

At lunch, when Hirata asks about the leaked information and broader plan, Ayanokōji continues to attribute the visible strategy to Horikita.

`Y1V05|spine:31|xhtml:text/part0029.html|p:0372-0376`

The pattern from earlier volumes remains: hidden authorship develops another person's visible legitimacy.

The ethical ambiguity is permanent. This may give Horikita room to become a genuine leader; it also means the class cannot fully evaluate the person shaping its environment.

## 11.9 Ordinary social weakness remains real

The borrowed-item race gives the volume a comic but analytically important counterpoint. Ayanokōji draws a task requiring ten friends and cannot satisfy it. He draws `好きな人 / someone you like` and reacts with adolescent panic rather than strategic mastery.

`Y1V05|spine:37|xhtml:text/part0035.html|p:0046-0060`

The text refuses to let his strategic superiority become total human competence.

## 11.10 The relay was planned—but the duel is not reducible to the plan

Ayanokōji had already arranged through Hirata to enter the final relay. That is textual fact. The public appearance itself is therefore not spontaneous.

But the decisive escalation occurs after Manabu asks about Ayanokōji's lack of `熱量`. Ayanokōji calls him back, asks whether Manabu is interested in what kind of person he is, and offers a race.

The causality matters.

Manabu does not force disclosure. Ayanokōji actively solicits recognition from someone he respects.

## 11.11 `戦略も、知略も関係ない`

The relay narration is unusually explicit:

- he has never run seriously in such an open world;
- the sterile room of his earlier running is different;
- catching other runners does not matter;
- strategy and intellect do not matter;
- the person beside him is the entire contest.

`Y1V05|spine:45|xhtml:text/part0043.html|p:0057-0068`

This is among the strongest Year 1 evidence for **intrinsic preference**.

The race matters because he wants it.

## 11.12 Controlled exposure after desire

Afterward, he immediately thinks about interpretive containment. The class can claim that Horikita and Hirata kept him hidden as a reserve, particularly to complicate Ryūen's model of Class D.

`Y1V05|spine:45|xhtml:text/part0043.html|p:0092-0096`

The right synthesis is therefore neither:

- “he lost control and exposed himself,” nor
- “the entire race was only counterintelligence.”

It is:

> **planned participation → personally motivated full-speed escalation → strategic management of the exposure's meaning.**

## 11.13 Desire for defeat

Sakayanagi's closing challenge produces another unusually direct admission. If someone genuinely defeats him, that would symbolically defeat the father who created him. He wants the contradiction broken.

`Y1V05|spine:47|xhtml:text/part0045.html|p:0181-0183`

This is not a normal competitive desire to lose. He does not want to throw a match. A deliberately manufactured defeat would prove nothing. The liberating defeat must overcome the very excellence imposed upon him.

His condition is therefore paradoxical:

> **Only the ability that imprisons him can validate the defeat that might free him from what that ability means.**

---# 12. Horikita and Sudō — reciprocal development without false equality

The Horikita–Sudō relationship is the volume's clearest test of whether Ayanokōji's instrumental developmental model necessarily determines the kind of relationship that emerges from it.

His language is blunt: Sudō can become Horikita's `武器`. Horikita's actual conversation with Sudō produces something less possessive.

## 12.1 Shared defect

Both begin from a form of superiority organized around recognition.

Sudō wants broad recognition. He wants classmates to acknowledge what basketball and athletic performance prove about him.

Horikita wants narrow but absolute recognition from Manabu. Her insistence on anchor position makes that private motive temporarily override the impersonal rationality she normally claims.

The difference in audience should not obscure the structural similarity.

## 12.2 Sudō sees himself as inherited failure

His monologue treats family background as destiny. Losing the basketball recommendation after violence appears to confirm that he can never become more than the people he despises.

Horikita's answer is unusually direct because she attacks the premise rather than merely the behavior. A child born to people judged worthless is not therefore worthless. What a person becomes remains, at least partly, a matter of self-authorship.

This is one reason her intervention succeeds where ordinary scolding has not. She does not merely tell Sudō not to be violent. She gives him a different ontology of the self.

## 12.3 Horikita confesses her own deficit

The conversation is reciprocal because she does not speak from a position of complete superiority. She admits that she also wanted recognition and that her earlier treatment of weaker classmates was wrong.

That confession is important. It converts advice into shared diagnosis.

## 12.4 The promise is bilateral

Horikita does not simply say “help the class.” She says that if Sudō begins to lose his way, she will pull him back, asks him to lend her his strength, and offers hers in return.

`Y1V05|spine:41|xhtml:text/part0039.html|p:0029-0034`

The exchange is not fully equal. Sudō's romantic attachment remains stronger, Horikita has more academic and strategic authority, and she still sees his athletic capacity as valuable class infrastructure. But the form is genuinely bilateral.

## 12.5 Why Horikita refuses romance/friendship labels

Her internal reaction after Sudō agrees is deliberately difficult to classify. She knows the feeling is not simply friendship or romance.

`Y1V05|spine:41|xhtml:text/part0039.html|p:0036-0037`

The second pass should resist forcing a familiar category onto the scene. What has emerged is political and personal at once: the experience of discovering that another person may become part of one's self-authored path without being reducible to either intimacy or utility.

## 12.6 The wager becomes a behavioral covenant

After the festival, Horikita uses Sudō's failure to become overall top athlete not as an excuse to humiliate him but to impose a behavioral obligation: no unjustified violence from now on. She also says that she herself deserves a penalty for failing to answer the class's needs and eventually permits him to call her `鈴音`.

The relation remains asymmetric, but it is no longer organized simply around admiration flowing upward.

## 12.7 What the volume does not establish

Volume 5 does not prove:

- that Sudō's violence problem is solved;
- that Horikita has become a mature leader;
- that their relationship is egalitarian;
- that Sudō's romantic interest is reciprocated;
- or that Ayanokōji's developmental design owns the result.

It establishes a credible first transition from **recognition-seeking superiority** toward **mutual developmental obligation**.

# 13. Kushida Kikyō — social indispensability and deliberate betrayal

Volume 5 resolves one important local ambiguity while creating a much larger political problem.

## 13.1 Ayanokōji's hypothesis

Before the festival, Ayanokōji raises the possibility that Kushida herself exposed her preferential-student identity during the Volume 4 examination. Horikita initially resists because betrayal seems irrational from the perspective of class interest.

Ayanokōji's answer is methodologically important: class interest is not necessarily Kushida's governing interest.

`Y1V05|spine:23|xhtml:text/part0021.html|p:0063-0084`

The volume therefore asks Horikita to stop treating the class as the default unit of everyone's motivation.

## 13.2 Horikita's initial response is reintegrative

When she later confronts Kushida, Horikita does not initially demand a confession about the past. She asks a prospective question: can Kushida be trusted as a classmate from now on?

This matters for Horikita's developing political philosophy. Her first instinct is not immediate expulsion or revenge. She wants to know whether future cooperation remains possible.

## 13.3 Kushida's social value is not fake merely because loyalty is false

Kushida remains one of the class's most socially capable students. She can gather people, communicate across groups, support morale, and make coordination easier. Those abilities do not vanish when the reader learns she is willing to betray the class.

The volume therefore produces a difficult category:

> **a highly cooperative person who is not politically loyal.**

Cooperation and loyalty are different abilities.

## 13.4 Her priority is Horikita's removal

Kushida eventually states the objective plainly. She wants Horikita expelled and is willing to let Class D fall if necessary. She also adds Ayanokōji as a target because he knows too much.

Her willingness to damage the class for private identity protection confirms the earlier leak as betrayal rather than tactical sacrifice for Class D.

## 13.5 Why the middle-school past remains unresolved

The volume establishes that Kushida and Horikita attended the same middle school and that Kushida fears people who know her past. It does not yet give the reader the complete event history necessary to judge that past.

This artifact therefore preserves the motive at the correct level:

- she wants people with dangerous knowledge removed;
- the exact content and moral meaning of that knowledge remain locally incomplete.

# 14. Sakayanagi Arisu — bodily limitation, political power, and the problem of manufactured genius

Sakayanagi's role in Volume 5 is deliberately paradoxical.

The sports festival is the book's most direct test of the body, and the character who cannot participate becomes one of its most important political actors.

## 14.1 Disability as aggregate competitive deficit

Sakayanagi apologizes because she will be unable to compete. Her classmates do not blame her. The prose explicitly presents her limitation as something outside voluntary control.

`Y1V05|spine:15|xhtml:text/part0013.html|p:0013-0021`

The second pass therefore avoids the stronger claim that the school simply treats disability as misconduct. What the text does establish is more precise: the event structure still converts her bodily absence into an aggregate disadvantage for the team.

The institution can refrain from moral blame while still making a physical difference competitively costly.

## 14.2 Political ability exceeds bodily measurement

Ayanokōji notices that Sakayanagi's faction has grown despite her nonparticipation in previous major examinations. Her influence is therefore evidence against the sports festival's apparent equation of visible physical contribution with meaningful ability.

She cannot score on the track. She can reorganize Class A.

## 14.3 Katsuragi's internal opposition

During the festival, Class A experiences factional friction severe enough to undermine its team efficiency. Ayanokōji strongly infers that Sakayanagi is behind the disruption because she watches the conflict with visible amusement and because the behavior fits the broader factional pattern.

At the Volume 5 endpoint this remains **strong inference**, not a directly confessed plan.

## 14.4 `偽りの天才`

The closing phrase `偽りの天才 / false genius` strongly suggests that Sakayanagi understands Ayanokōji through an opposition involving natural and manufactured excellence. But Volume 5 does not fully define her theory.

The correct local status is therefore:

- Sakayanagi knows the White Room;
- she knows enough about Ayanokōji to identify him as his father's manufactured `最高傑作`;
- she regards defeating him as personally meaningful;
- the exact philosophical content of `偽りの天才` remains unresolved.

## 14.5 Recognition after concealment

The number `８年と２４３日` is psychologically destabilizing because it proves an asymmetry Ayanokōji did not know existed. He has spent the school year managing other people's knowledge of him; Sakayanagi reveals that she has possessed a hidden history of him since childhood.

The encounter therefore reverses the volume's visibility structure. The person who has managed exposure discovers that he has long been an object of someone else's observation.

# 15. Manabu and Nagumo — two emerging politics of merit

Volume 5 gives student-council politics a new ideological clarity.

## 15.1 Nagumo names the project

Nagumo criticizes the outgoing order for preserving rescue mechanisms and producing too few expulsions. He promises an:

> `究極の実力主義の学校`

`Y1V05|spine:45|xhtml:text/part0043.html|p:0006`

The phrase matters because it converts the title's `実力主義` from ambient institutional ideology into a contested program that can become more or less radical.

## 15.2 What Volume 5 allows us to say about Manabu

Nagumo's criticism implies that the existing student-council order combines strict competition with some forms of institutional rescue. It would be premature to construct Manabu's complete political philosophy from this exchange alone.

The local contrast is sufficient:

- Nagumo regards rescue as dilution of meritocratic truth;
- the preceding order has tolerated mechanisms that prevent every failure from becoming irreversible elimination.

## 15.3 Manabu sees class change

Before the relay, Manabu tells Ayanokōji that Class D had looked hopeless but now appears different.

Ayanokōji attributes that change to Suzune:

> `あんたの妹が変えたんだ`

`Y1V05|spine:45|xhtml:text/part0043.html|p:0022`

# 16. Kōenji and the political problem of unusable excellence

Kōenji continues to function as a standing objection to any theory that possessed ability automatically becomes class power.

He does not participate meaningfully in the festival and offers an illness rationale the class cannot effectively contest. Yet when Sudō tries to assert physical dominance, Kōenji effortlessly catches the punch and tells him he cannot win.

The contrast is almost schematic:

- Sudō contributes massively but lacks self-regulation;
- Kōenji possesses even greater capacity but refuses collective obligation.

From the class's perspective, the more useful athlete is not necessarily the stronger athlete.

This matters for `実力`. A capacity that cannot be governed, recruited, persuaded, or voluntarily offered may remain personally real while being politically unavailable.

Horikita begins to recognize this when she wonders whether a person more socially liked—someone like Kushida—might be capable of moving Kōenji where her own authority fails.

That question marks social legitimacy itself becoming legible to Horikita as a form of ability.

# 17. The wider ensemble — class infrastructure becomes visible

Volume 5 is unusually good at showing how a class is made from more than its top strategists.

## 17.1 Yukimura

Yukimura's confrontation with Sudō matters because criticism no longer comes only from Horikita or Hirata. A less athletic student asserts a legitimate claim against a leader whose behavior is damaging the group.

His willingness to speak despite Sudō's intimidation is a form of civic courage.

## 17.2 Ike

Ike remains physically limited, but the narrative does not treat that as equivalent to moral irrelevance. He participates, struggles, and later apologizes for what he perceives as insufficient contribution when Sudō apologizes.

The moment reinforces the volume's interest in self-evaluation rather than static ranking.

## 17.3 Sakura

Sakura proudly reports that she did not finish last. Ayanokōji did not see the performance, but his response is straightforward encouragement rather than manipulation.

This is another low-coercion relationship moment that matters precisely because nothing strategic is being extracted from it.

## 17.4 Kei

Kei remains under Ayanokōji's private direction, but she also produces independent social information. Her empathy toward injured and targeted Horikita partly comes from recognizing an image of her own bullied past.

That emotional comparison is not something Ayanokōji commands into existence.

## 17.5 Hashimoto and Class A

Hashimoto's willingness to challenge Katsuragi indicates that Class A's visible unity is already politically fractured. The festival exposes not only bodies but factional loyalties.

# 18. Relationship architecture through Volume 5

## 18.1 Ayanokōji–Horikita

The relationship remains asymmetrical in knowledge and hidden authority, but Horikita increasingly produces meaningful action from principles rather than instructions.

Ayanokōji teaches the three-legged-race lesson directly, then later uses much harsher pressure around Sudō. Horikita's actual promise to Sudō exceeds his `weapon` framing.

**Current state:** developmental partnership with major information asymmetry; Horikita's independent authorship is increasing but not secure.

## 18.2 Horikita–Sudō

The relation changes most sharply this volume.

It moves from one-sided admiration and irritation toward a bilateral commitment structured around ability, behavioral accountability, and mutual aid.

**Current state:** emerging reciprocal developmental bond; romantic asymmetry remains; durability unresolved.

## 18.3 Ayanokōji–Kei

Kei is more perceptive and conversationally comfortable with Ayanokōji than the Volume 4 relation alone suggested, but the power imbalance remains severe. He still issues instructions without explanation and expects compliance.

**Current state:** protected covert collaboration with strong leverage asymmetry and growing interpersonal familiarity; not equal partnership.

## 18.4 Horikita–Kushida

Horikita initially seeks future trust rather than retroactive punishment. By the end of the volume, Kushida's expulsion objective is explicit.

**Current state:** attempted reintegration collapses into open but still partially concealed antagonism.

## 18.5 Ayanokōji–Manabu

This relationship acquires genuine competitive desire. Manabu is not simply a useful senior or test of strength. Ayanokōji asks to be seen by him and chooses a contest with no direct class benefit.

**Current state:** respect and curiosity expressed through challenge; one of the few relationships where Ayanokōji voluntarily increases his own visibility.

## 18.6 Ayanokōji–Ryūen

They remain opponents operating through hidden causality. Volume 5 makes the comparison ethically sharper because both are willing to let other people absorb harm for strategic ends, though the methods and objectives are not morally identical.

Ryūen directly injures a subordinate and engineers false accusations. Ayanokōji permits preventable class suffering and injury to continue because he values later developmental and strategic benefit.

The similarity is **instrumental authority over others' costs**; it should not be inflated into complete moral equivalence.

## 18.7 Ayanokōji–Sakayanagi

The relation begins in radical information asymmetry. She knows him; he does not know her history with him. Her challenge is the first peer-level encounter explicitly tied to the hidden institution that formed him.

**Current state:** one-sided historical recognition becomes declared rivalry; exact ideological content unresolved.

# 19. Class politics and the four constitutions

Volume 5 develops all four class polities through their relationship to visible performance.

## `POLITY-HORIKITA`

Class D's leadership remains distributed and unstable.

- Horikita supplies analytic policy.
- Hirata supplies procedure and social legitimacy.
- Kei supplies a major female social bloc and, secretly, Ayanokōji-directed opposition.
- Kushida supplies broad trust while secretly betraying the class.
- Sudō supplies indispensable athletic capital but initially lacks legitimate authority.
- Ayanokōji authors part of the environment while publicly attributing strategy elsewhere.

The class is therefore not yet Horikita's in a simple sense. Its growing strength comes from **functional differentiation**.

## `POLITY-RYUEN`

Ryūen's class displays centralized command with strong information control. It can accept local sacrifices because one actor controls the larger plan.

Its strength is coherence under asymmetric strategy.

Its danger is that subordinates' bodies and reputations can become instruments of the center.

## `POLITY-ICHINOSE`

Class B wins the first-year ranking. The volume gives it less internal focus than other classes, but its athletic depth and coordination are visible. Ichinose also obeys White-side obligations even where cross-class sympathy might point elsewhere.

The class continues to represent social integration rather than centralized coercion.

## `POLITY-SAKAYANAGI-ORIGIN`

Class A's formal status conceals constitutional fracture. Katsuragi tries to operate as stable visible authority while Sakayanagi's faction grows and may be willing to sacrifice short-term class efficiency for internal political victory.

The class's high rank therefore does not imply political unity.

## Ayanokōji's shadow constitution

Volume 5 gives the clearest early statement that Ayanokōji may be willing to make Class D capable of reaching Class A while remaining indifferent to personally occupying the visible center.

His authority is environmental rather than constitutional:

- choose what information circulates;
- decide when intervention is withheld;
- preserve public leaders;
- recruit private networks;
- and allow crisis to reveal developmental deficits.

The class can grow under this structure while remaining unable to consent to the person shaping it.

# 20. Institutional analysis — meritocracy as conversion, exposure, and loss

The sports festival reveals a school less interested in pure athletic ability than the surface premise suggests.

## 20.1 Cross-domain compensation

Athletic performance can offset academic weakness. This is one of the school's more defensible forms of plural evaluation: a student like Sudō is not reduced to mathematics or English.

Yet compensation is earned competitively. The institution recognizes plural ability by converting it into scarce advantage rather than by simply broadening the definition of competence.

## 20.2 The bottom-ten penalty

The ten-point future academic penalty creates a feedback mechanism in which weakness in one domain can intensify risk in another.

The same system that lets athletic excellence rescue academic weakness can make athletic weakness worsen academic vulnerability.

## 20.3 Fixed schedules transform transparency into attack surface

The class must publish its own internal allocation to the school and cannot alter it after the deadline. Once Kushida leaks the participation sheet, organizational transparency becomes exploitable intelligence.

## 20.4 Cameras increase record density, not moral certainty

The school attempts procedural clarity through surveillance. Ryūen demonstrates that malicious actors can adapt by designing events where the record contains true facts but ambiguous intent.

## 20.5 Student council as rule-making power

Nagumo's entrance shifts the institutional horizon. The most powerful students may not merely optimize within the rules. They may acquire authority to redesign what kinds of failure become survivable.

The difference between playing an examination and designing the examination is becoming politically central.

# 21. Ethics and autonomy

Volume 5 is less overtly abusive than Volume 4, but ethically it may be more revealing because Ayanokōji demonstrates that he can choose among different pedagogical modes.

## 21.1 The three-legged-race lesson

**Goal selected by:** Ayanokōji, but aligned with Horikita's stated desire to improve.

**Information:** he openly explains the principle after demonstrating it.

**Refusal capacity:** high.

**Engineered suffering:** minimal; frustration is temporary and directly connected to the lesson.

**Agency afterward:** increased.

**Assessment:** comparatively legitimate developmental scaffolding.

## 21.2 Allowing Class D to be beaten down

**Goal selected by:** Ayanokōji.

**Information:** classmates do not know he has anticipated the betrayal and intentionally withholds intervention.

**Refusal capacity:** low because they do not know the relevant alternatives exist.

**Foreseeable harm:** class-point loss, humiliation, injury pressure, Sudō destabilization.

**Benefit:** exposes Kushida, forces leadership development, reveals Ryūen's methods, changes Horikita/Sudō.

**Assessment:** strategically intelligible environmental authorship with serious autonomy deficit. Benefit does not retroactively create consent.

## 21.3 Harsh confrontation with Horikita

His language is designed to destabilize her self-conception. Unlike the Kei operation, there is no bodily threat or sexual coercion, and Horikita retains substantial ability to reject him.

The method is still manipulative in the ordinary sense: he selects the emotional pressure necessary to produce a developmental crisis.

## 21.4 Horikita's sports policy

Horikita is not ethically wrong merely because she optimizes. The class faces genuine shared penalties, and her compensation mechanism acknowledges individual cost.

The problem is that she initially treats weaker students' lost opportunities as sufficiently priced once the aggregate is improved. The debate makes distributive justice part of leadership rather than a solved equation.

## 21.5 Ryūen and Kinoshita

This is the clearest serious wrongdoing in the volume. A leader intentionally inflicts bodily injury on a subordinate to create evidence for a false accusation. Payment and agreement do not erase the harm.

## 21.6 Hirata shielding Sudō

Hirata's lie to the teacher protects Sudō from immediate punishment. The strongest defense is rehabilitative: formal punishment at that moment may have hardened the rupture and eliminated the possibility of voluntary return.

The strongest criticism is institutional: concealing assault means the victim privately decides whether violence should enter the record, potentially normalizing unaccountable harm.

## 21.7 Horikita's behavioral demand

Her post-festival ban on unjustified violence is more legitimate than simple punishment because Sudō accepts it as part of an ongoing relationship and because she binds herself to accountability too.

# 23. Japanese narration, voice, and register

## 23.1 Sudō's `俺`

Sudō's monologue uses rough, compressed masculine self-description. The most revealing feature is not slang itself but the ease with which moral verdict becomes identity: `クズ` moves from parents to self.

His later speech with Horikita becomes less performatively aggressive when he admits the need for recognition.

## 23.2 Horikita's polished feminine register under pressure

Horikita retains controlled feminine sentence endings even when angry or injured. Her changes are therefore often visible less as a total register switch than as what she becomes willing to say within the same surface discipline.

The admission about Manabu, the confession of her own inadequacy to Sudō, and the request `私にあなたの力を貸して` are important because vulnerability enters a voice that normally protects itself through complete, rationalized statements.

## 23.3 Ayanokōji's register split

The relay intensifies the contrast between ordinary/embodied narration and strategic narration.

Before and after the run he thinks about plans, cover stories, opponents, and the cost of exposure. During the duel the syntax becomes immediate and sensory. The narration explicitly dismisses strategic and intellectual frames.

The difference is evidence. It should not be flattened into the claim that the hidden strategist remains uniformly in control of his motives.

## 23.4 Kei's private register

Kei's private speech with Ayanokōji is increasingly direct and familiar, but linguistic familiarity does not by itself prove relational equality. The private command structure remains intact.

## 23.5 Ryūen's theatrical threat language

Ryūen repeatedly makes accusation, provocation, and mockery public performances. His rough register is not merely temper. It is a political technology designed to make the target respond emotionally in front of witnesses.

## 23.6 Sakayanagi's refined challenge

Sakayanagi's politeness makes the final threat more distinctive. She does not need Ryūen's vulgarity to assert superiority. `お久しぶりです` precedes information capable of destabilizing Ayanokōji more effectively than public aggression has.

# 24. Appearance, illustration, and embodied characterization

The visual layer is unusually important in a volume about public bodies.

## 24.1 Sakayanagi seated with cane

The early monochrome illustration visually emphasizes the contradiction already present in prose: a girl physically excluded from the festival's central metric nevertheless commands visual and political presence.

The cane and seated posture establish bodily limitation without making her visually negligible.

## 24.2 Injury as public legibility

The Horikita illustrations during the festival emphasize strain and bodily cost. Her competence is not abstract; the body that normally supports her superiority becomes the site through which Ryūen makes that superiority costly.

## 24.3 Sudō and Horikita in shared framing

The illustration around their confrontation places them as a relational pair rather than simply teacher and problem student. That supports the chapter's own structure: each is discovering a version of the same deficit.

It should not be used as proof of romantic reciprocity.

## 24.4 Ayanokōji and Manabu

The relay illustration makes hidden physical capacity visually public. Ayanokōji is no longer represented primarily as an observer at the edge of other people's tests. His body occupies the same competitive frame as the senior figure he has chosen to challenge.

## 24.5 Horikita and Kushida split framing

The late split portrait makes the contradiction between their public surfaces visually explicit. They remain members of the same class while their political relationship is becoming openly adversarial.

## 24.6 Character-database inserts

The recurring student-database pages remain part of the series' institutional visual grammar: people are turned into profiles and measurable categories. Their presence in Volume 5 is particularly apt because the narrative repeatedly demonstrates capacities the obvious metrics miss—legitimacy, teaching, betrayal, factional influence, and the desire to compete.

---# 25. Motifs and recurring language

## 25.1 Pace

The volume repeatedly turns pace into moral and political language.

Sudō cannot understand why others cannot keep up with his instruction. Horikita expects partners to approach her pace. Ayanokōji runs beyond Horikita's capacity to teach her that coordination requires adaptation. Manabu and Ayanokōji later choose to match themselves against one another at full speed.

The question is not simply how fast someone can move. It is **who gets to set the pace and who bears the cost of mismatch**.

## 25.2 Heat

Manabu's `熱量` formulation captures something institutional measurement misses. Ayanokōji possesses enormous capability but often appears detached from the purposes around which that capability is organized.

The relay briefly gives the reader a different answer: heat can emerge when he chooses the purpose himself.

## 25.3 Weapons

Ayanokōji's `武器` vocabulary remains one of the clearest signs that his developmental intelligence is ethically compromised. He can correctly identify another person's role in a social system while reducing that person's subjectivity to a function.

Horikita's later bilateral promise with Sudō complicates that frame.

## 25.4 Defeat and rebirth

`敗北と再生` applies first to Horikita, but the volume ends with Ayanokōji wanting a defeat capable of breaking his own contradiction. Failure therefore becomes more than loss. It may be the condition under which a fixed theory of self can change.

## 25.5 Evidence

Cameras, wax, injuries, audio, video, and recorded planning continue the series' epistemic motif. Evidence is never simply “the truth.” It is material that someone must frame, authenticate, contextualize, and make institutionally actionable.

## 25.6 Recognition

Sudō wants recognition from everyone. Horikita wants recognition from Manabu. Ayanokōji unexpectedly asks Manabu to become interested in him. Sakayanagi arrives already recognizing him in a way he did not authorize.

The volume's characters repeatedly discover that being seen is both desired and dangerous.

# 26. Formal and literary strengths

## 26.1 Sudō's monologue creates causal symmetry

Opening with Sudō gives the later Horikita conversation structural force. His self-condemnation is not introduced only when she needs to refute it. The reader has already inhabited the logic from inside him.

## 26.2 The sports festival literalizes abstract leadership

The three-legged race may be the volume's cleanest formal device. An abstract theory of relational leadership becomes a problem of two bodies tied together. The metaphor works because it is also literally the event being trained for.

## 26.3 Public spectacle changes the protagonist's narrative position

Most early Ayanokōji competence is revealed after the fact or kept inside private causal chains. The relay stages competence before the entire school. This makes visibility itself part of the dramatic action.

## 26.4 Ryūen's tactics continue the evidence motif without repeating Volume 2

Volume 2 focused on the absence of proof. Volume 5 is more sophisticated: proof exists, but the causal meaning of the proof is manipulated. Real injury can serve a false story.

## 26.5 The book interleaves growth and danger

Horikita and Sudō's developmental turn occurs in the same volume that Kushida's betrayal becomes explicit and Sakayanagi exposes White Room knowledge. The class becomes more capable at exactly the moment the strategic world around it becomes more dangerous.

## 26.6 The relay is formally overdetermined in a productive way

The scene works simultaneously as:

- sports climax;
- Ayanokōji–Manabu relationship scene;
- embodiment of freedom;
- controlled exposure;
- school-wide reputation event;
- and White Room contrast.

No one reading exhausts it.

# 27. Critical reservations and strongest counterarguments

## 27.1 The sports-festival scoring system is dramatically useful but heavily engineered

The cross-domain conversion between athletic placement and future written-test performance is thematically productive, but also transparently designed to force the exact conflicts the novel wants to study. Institutional realism is secondary to laboratory function.

## 27.2 Ayanokōji's competence risks absorbing too much causal credit

The narrative repeatedly makes him the person who sees the class's developmental deficits before everyone else. A weaker reading could therefore turn Horikita and Sudō into demonstrations of his genius.

The strongest evidence against that flattening is the content of their actual conversation. Horikita's language of bilateral support and self-determination is not simply the `weapon` language Ayanokōji gives her.

## 27.3 Horikita's leadership turn may be overstated

The strongest counterargument to describing Volume 5 as “Horikita becomes a leader” is obvious: she still depends on Ayanokōji to diagnose the class crisis, remains deeply oriented toward Manabu's recognition, misreads Kushida, and has not yet demonstrated durable class-wide legitimacy.

The second-pass formulation is therefore intentionally narrower:

> **Volume 5 produces Horikita's first credible self-authored leadership turn, not completed leadership.**

## 27.4 Sudō's improvement is still dependent on romantic motivation

His responsiveness to Horikita partly reflects attraction. That does not make the growth false, but it complicates claims that he has already internalized a general ethic of collective responsibility.

## 27.5 Sakayanagi's disability and school design require precision

It would be easy to overread the sports festival as the school “punishing” disability. The actual text is subtler. Classmates recognize her physical limitation as involuntary and do not blame her, while the event's aggregate scoring still makes her absence a competitive handicap. The analytical criticism should target **structural consequence**, not invent a formal moral sanction the source does not establish.

## 27.6 The legal analogy in Ayanokōji's narration is character evidence, not a legal treatise

His emergency-survival analogy asserts a radical individual right to survival even against a more vulnerable person. The important evidence is what the analogy reveals about his moral reasoning. This artifact does not treat his summary of legal doctrine as authoritative jurisprudence.

## 27.7 Strongest counterargument to the “intrinsic relay” reading

The relay substitution was planned; Ayanokōji knows exposure can serve strategic ends; afterward he immediately constructs a cover story. One could therefore argue the entire full-speed performance remains optimization.

The source weighs against reducing it that far. He himself initiates the personal contest, asks Manabu to be interested in him, tells him to run fully, disregards the other runners, contrasts the moment with the sterile room of his past, and explicitly says strategy and intellect do not matter. Strategy remains around the experience. It does not fully explain the experience.

## 27.8 Strongest counterargument to the “developmental authorship” critique

Ayanokōji does not create Ryūen's aggression or Kushida's betrayal. Allowing the attack to unfold may be read as prudent observation rather than authorship: acting too early could reveal him, preserve the traitor, or fail to produce durable learning.

The criticism survives because he explicitly frames nonintervention as desirable developmental pressure and claims authority over the classmates' exposure to its cost. He is not merely unable to help. He chooses not to because he prefers what the suffering may produce.

# 31. Cumulative delta through Volume 5

Volume 5 materially changes the Year 1 model established through the first reconciliation checkpoint.

## 31.1 Ayanokōji delta

### Before Volume 5

The reconciled opening arc had established that Ayanokōji could:

- conceal ability and author visibility;
- manipulate institutional legibility;
- design environments;
- manufacture dependency;
- and also participate in lower-pressure ordinary relationships that did not require domination.

### Volume 5 adds

1. **The class as a developmental object.** He explicitly distinguishes personally wanting Class A from wanting to make the class capable of reaching it.
2. **A direct comparison between low- and high-coercion pedagogy.** The three-legged-race lesson and the later `敗北と再生` intervention occur in the same volume.
3. **Deliberate developmental nonintervention.** He says the class should be allowed to suffer because the suffering will become later strength.
4. **Intrinsic competitive desire.** The Manabu duel matters independently of class outcome.
5. **Chosen visibility.** He voluntarily increases the amount of evidence the school possesses about him.
6. **Explicit desire for a true defeat.** Defeat becomes imagined as a way to negate his father's authorship.

The strongest new formulation is:

> **Ayanokōji's central Year 1 problem is no longer merely whether he can escape being used. It is whether he can decide what his own ability is for without making other people the material through which that freedom is exercised.**

## 31.2 Horikita / class-leadership delta

Horikita moves from accepting that relationships are necessary to understanding that leadership requires adaptation to unequal people.

The important changes are:

- recognizes social legitimacy as a real form of ability;
- admits a personal motive to classmates;
- sees herself as developmentally analogous to Sudō;
- accepts responsibility for retrieving a valuable classmate rather than simply judging him;
- asks for reciprocal support;
- gives up a personally important relay opportunity for class viability;
- begins treating failure as educational information.

This is the first strong evidence that her leadership can become more than Ayanokōji's public mask. It is not yet proof that it has.

## 31.3 Sudō delta

Sudō's previous arc involved flashes of effort, loyalty, and athletic value. Volume 5 provides the first sustained transformation of how he understands that value.

He moves from:

> **talent as proof that he is not worthless**

through:

> **talent as entitlement to command**

and toward:

> **talent as strength that can be lent to a shared project.**

The difference is politically significant.

## 31.4 Relationship delta

### Ayanokōji–Kei

Private familiarity grows, but obedience without explanation remains active. The relation is still far from reciprocal equality.

### Horikita–Sudō

A new bilateral developmental bond becomes one of Class D's possible leadership resources.

### Horikita–Kushida

The possibility of ordinary future trust is directly tested and fails. Kushida's hostility is now explicit.

### Ayanokōji–Manabu

Respect becomes voluntarily embodied competition. Ayanokōji asks to be seen rather than merely inferred.

### Ayanokōji–Sakayanagi

A new rivalry begins with Sakayanagi possessing historical knowledge of him he did not know she had.

## 31.5 Institutional delta

The school now demonstrates that:

- ability in one domain can purchase survival in another;
- class victory can merely reduce loss;
- fixed administrative transparency can become an attack surface;
- surveillance increases evidence without eliminating interpretive conflict;
- student government may possess power to radicalize the institution's meritocratic rules.

The title's ideology is becoming explicitly political.

## 31.6 Ethics/autonomy delta

Volume 4 produced the most severe early example of coerced dependency. Volume 4.5 showed that lower-coercion relationships are possible. Volume 5 puts those findings into direct comparison inside Ayanokōji's own pedagogy.

The ethical ledger must therefore track not only **whether an intervention works**, but **why he selects a particular level of coercion when alternatives exist**.

## 31.7 Ability/merit delta

The volume substantially strengthens the multidimensional ability model:

- Sudō: high possessed/displayed physical ability; initially low instructional and political usability; rising developmental capacity.
- Horikita: high possessed academic/physical ability; growing socially usable and political ability.
- Hirata: moderate-to-high displayed competence but exceptional socially usable ability.
- Kōenji: extraordinary possessed ability with low collective availability.
- Sakayanagi: low/absent athletic participation but high political ability.
- Ryūen: ability to convert information, provocation, and evidence into political control.
- Ayanokōji: extraordinary hidden capacity whose public display now creates strategic and relational consequences.

## 31.8 Truth/proof/record delta

The project should now distinguish at least four recurring evidentiary operations:

1. **insufficient proof of a true event** — Volume 2;
2. **manipulation of what can be seen or inferred** — Volumes 2–3;
3. **real facts organized around false causation** — Volume 5 Kinoshita;
4. **counter-recording and context control** — Volume 5 Ryūen/Horikita.

The series' epistemology is becoming more sophisticated than simple “lies versus truth.”

## 31.9 Open-question delta

New live questions include:

- Can Horikita reproduce this leadership insight without Ayanokōji forcing the crisis?
- Can Sudō maintain behavioral change when Horikita is not immediately watching?
- What exactly does Kushida's past contain?
- Who supplied the recording of Ryūen's real planning meeting, and through what chain?
- What does Sakayanagi mean by `偽りの天才`?
- How does she know the White Room and Ayanokōji?
- What does Nagumo's `究極の実力主義` become in practice?
- Will Ayanokōji's public athletic exposure produce costs he cannot fully contain?
- Is his desire for defeat compatible with his conditioned refusal to lose?

# 33. Final synthesis

Volume 5 begins with a boy who thinks talent failed to save him from being the kind of person he was born to be.

It ends with another boy wondering whether being defeated might save him from the kind of person someone else deliberately made him.

Between those two poles, the sports festival subjects ability to the most public form of measurement Year 1 has yet used. Bodies are ranked, class points move, written-test protection is awarded, cameras record contact, and the strongest students become impossible to ignore.

The result is not a clean vindication of meritocracy.

Instead, every important character demonstrates a way in which ability exceeds or fails its measurement.

Sudō is physically exceptional and politically unstable. Horikita is academically and athletically exceptional but initially unable to coordinate with weaker partners. Hirata converts moderate personal capacity into broad class usability. Kōenji possesses power the class cannot reliably access. Sakayanagi cannot run but can reorganize a class. Ryūen uses information and deniability to make stronger athletes perform worse than they should. Kushida's extraordinary social cooperation coexists with betrayal. Ayanokōji's greatest capacities remain deliberately hidden until he chooses to reveal one of them.

The three-legged race supplies the simplest model for the entire volume:

> **When two unequal people are tied together, the stronger person cannot solve the problem merely by being stronger.**

Strength must learn the other person's pace.

That lesson changes Horikita and Sudō because each discovers that superiority without adaptation produces isolation rather than leadership. Horikita's promise to lend strength in both directions is the volume's most hopeful political statement. It does not abolish hierarchy. It imagines unequal people becoming mutually necessary without making weakness identical with worthlessness.

Ayanokōji understands this principle intellectually before either of them does. That is why his position remains ethically difficult.

He can teach through demonstration and leave the choice to the learner. He can encourage effort without extracting utility. He can recognize distributive unfairness. He can also decide that classmates should be allowed to suffer because he expects suffering to improve them, and he can describe people as weapons even while helping them form relationships richer than that description.

The volume therefore does not let developmental effectiveness settle the ethical question.

It makes the question harder:

> **What right follows from understanding another person well enough to know how they might grow?**

Ryūen offers one answer: superior understanding entitles the ruler to use bodies, fear, evidence, and injury as resources.

Horikita is beginning to discover another: ability creates responsibility to account for the person beside you.

Hirata already practices a softer version: leadership is the repeated adjustment that lets unlike people remain inside the same project.

Nagumo introduces a political answer still mostly undefined: merit should become more radical and rescue less available.

Sakayanagi introduces an ontological answer: not all excellence may count as the same kind of genius, and Ayanokōji's manufactured ability itself can become the object of judgment.

Ayanokōji has not settled on an answer.

His final relay is important because, for once, ability is not serving a class, teacher, father, examination, or developmental plan. He tells Manabu to run, feels the open air, and reduces the world to the person beside him. Strategy returns immediately afterward, because he is still Ayanokōji and exposure must be managed. But for the duration of the race, capability acquires a purpose he selected himself.

That makes Sakayanagi's closing challenge more than a cliffhanger.

The sports festival has spent an entire volume asking how strength becomes public. Sakayanagi asks what that strength **is**.

Ayanokōji's answer is not pride in being the masterpiece. It is a wish that somebody might defeat it.

The contradiction at the end of Volume 5 is therefore sharper than the one at its beginning:

> **He wants freedom from the person who authored his ability, yet he increasingly exercises freedom by authoring the developmental environments of other people.**

At the same time, the people he develops are beginning to exceed the categories through which he understands them. Horikita does not merely acquire Sudō as a weapon; she promises him reciprocal support. Sudō does not merely become useful; he discovers a value outside basketball. Class D does not merely score; it begins to acquire a political culture in which different abilities can become mutually usable.

The strongest possibility Volume 5 opens is therefore not that Ayanokōji will become a better controller.

It is that the people around him may become strong enough—in ways he did not wholly design—to change what strength means to him.
