# 1. Executive thesis

Volume 4.5 asks a deceptively simple question that the first four volumes could not answer inside their special examinations:

> **What does development look like when nobody has assigned the purpose of the experience?**

The first four volumes repeatedly force students into designed environments. The school hides criteria, manufactures scarcity, prices information, rewards betrayal, and turns social relationships into tools of evaluation. Ayanokōji increasingly learns to operate in the same grammar. By Volume 4 he does not merely solve an environment. He manufactures one around Karuizawa Kei, deliberately destabilizing her existing protection and then offering himself as the stronger alternative.

Volume 4.5 does not reverse that development. Ayanokōji still:

- withholds information;
- pressures people into actions they initially refuse;
- thinks about favors as future leverage;
- hides his ability;
- assigns roles to Kei without full reciprocity;
- and contemplates expulsion as a tool of class development.

But summer vacation changes what the narrative is able to observe. The same capacities that dominate special examinations—social reading, risk management, concealment, bargaining, information networks, adaptive problem solving—are placed inside situations in which the meaningful result is not a class-point victory.

A sick younger sister receives a birthday gift.

A socially anxious girl is permitted to reject a confession in her own words.

Horikita asks another person for help and says `ありがとう`.

Ibuki and Ayanokōji cooperate because an elevator has stopped.

Kei laughs in a swimming pool.

Ayanokōji discovers that soft-serve ice cream tastes extraordinarily good.

Friends shop for a birthday present whose strategic value is negligible.

These events form an **ordinary-life counter-curriculum**. They are not outside the institution—points, surveillance, class identity, student-council politics, and the threat of expulsion remain everywhere—but their value is not exhausted by institutional measurement.

The second pass therefore sharpens the earlier thesis. Ordinary life is not simply an “unscored exam.” Calling it an exam still grants evaluation too much conceptual priority. The stronger formulation is:

> **Ordinary life is the domain in which students can acquire experiences whose purposes have not already been fixed by the school or by a strategic designer.**

That distinction matters most for Ayanokōji. Volume 4.5 supplies unusually strong evidence that his social personality is not merely a disguise covering a complete hidden self. He tells Kei that the human being called Ayanokōji Kiyotaka is in one sense `生まれたばかり / newly born`, still `液状 / liquid`, not yet solidified. The language is developmental. His ordinary self is unfinished because ordinary life itself is unfamiliar.

The volume also provides a more demanding ethical test than “does he care?” He clearly can care. He protects Airi's privacy, helps Katsuragi's sister, refuses to exploit one moment of potentially useful evidence, supports Airi's right to reject Yamauchi, destroys voyeuristic footage rather than weaponizing it, and derives genuine pleasure from friendship and leisure.

Yet care does not reliably restrain control. He pressures Horikita into participation, orders Kei to expose the competition swimsuit she uses to conceal her scar, physically pushes her into the pool, and manages Operation Delta through covert complicity rather than direct accountability.

The most important development is therefore not that Ayanokōji becomes “good.” It is that Volume 4.5 proves he possesses **choices between different modes of intervention**.

He can manipulate.

He can coerce.

He can scaffold.

He can protect privacy.

He can preserve refusal.

He can decline to turn information into leverage.

That makes his ethics more consequential, not less. Once alternative modes are demonstrably available to him, future coercion cannot be explained simply as the only relational grammar he possesses.

# 2. Position in the Year 1 architecture

## 2.1 The cumulative movement through Volume 4.5

The second pass now supports a clear five-volume escalation.

### Volume 1 — hidden evaluation

The school withholds evaluative criteria and reveals that Class D's problem is not simply low talent but fragmented, context-dependent, socially unusable ability. Ayanokōji begins by controlling how much of himself the institution can measure.

### Volume 2 — legibility

Truth becomes politically effective only when it can be made credible, visible, and actionable. Sudō has truth without proof; Sakura has a hidden self that can exist only through controlled visibility; Ayanokōji learns to manage what others are able to verify.

### Volume 3 — ecology and environmental authorship

The island exam demonstrates that ability changes value with environment. Ayanokōji escalates from controlling legibility to controlling the conditions in which other people act, including Horikita's vulnerability, isolation, and public attribution.

### Volume 4 — trust and dependency

The zodiac examination converts trust into political capital. Ayanokōji extends environmental authorship into private dependency by using Kei's trauma, delayed rescue, coercion, and promised protection to create a hidden social-information relationship.

### Volume 4.5 — counter-curriculum

The institution loosens its formal grip without disappearing. Students encounter ordinary leisure, embarrassment, friendship, gifts, attraction, bodily inconvenience, boredom, and purposeless conversation. The central analytical question changes from:

> What can a person do under designed pressure?

into:

> **What kind of person takes shape when the environment does not already dictate what success means?**

That is why the .5 volume is structurally essential. It does not pause the “real” plot. It tests whether the identities produced or revealed by the examinations survive in ordinary time.

## 2.2 Ordinary life is not institution-free

The strongest counterargument to the counter-curriculum thesis is visible everywhere in the text.

Summer vacation remains saturated by institutional structures:

- Ayanokōji transfers private points to Sudō;
- clubs pay point rewards;
- the school restricts contact with the outside world;
- surveillance cameras shape behavior;
- students assess future class utility;
- student-council succession matters;
- leisure can become a class contest with lunch as a wager;
- and Operation Delta is evaluated partly through its potential to cause expulsions and destroy Class D.

So the volume does not create a pure private sphere beyond power.

Its achievement is subtler. It creates **partial spaces of underdetermination** inside the school. In those spaces, characters can discover values that are not reducible to the official incentive system. Horikita's enjoyment of the pool does not increase class points. Airi's successful refusal produces no ranking advantage. Ayanokōji's first birthday-present shopping trip has no hidden exam solution waiting behind it. The institution remains the horizon, but it does not fully author the meaning of the moment.

# 3. Narrative and vignette architecture

Volume 4.5 is built as a chain of loosely connected summer stories rather than a single examination plot. That formal difference is itself analytical evidence.

## 3.1 `それでも夏休みは終わりへと向かっていく`

The short opening invokes `サザエさん症候群 / Sazae-san syndrome`, the melancholy produced by the approaching end of leisure. Ayanokōji immediately broadens the idea into a reflection on adulthood: most people will spend decades constrained by work and society, while student life contains a comparatively unusual quantity of freedom. He concludes by introducing the volume as small stories about students wavering in the space between child and adult.

The framing is explicit enough to matter. The volume announces that summer is not narratively empty. It is a developmental interval in which adolescents negotiate:

- independence and dependence;
- irresponsibility and accountability;
- attraction and respect;
- freedom and obligation;
- play and institutional consequence.

`Y1V04.5|spine:11|xhtml:text/part0009.html|p:0002-0013`

## 3.2 `意外と伊吹澪は常識人である`

Ayanokōji's attempt to attend a fortune-teller's event becomes an encounter with Ibuki. The story begins with loneliness and awkward social invitation, passes through a discussion of conversational ability, introduces the fortune-teller's language of childhood misfortune and self-determined fate, and culminates in the pair being trapped in an elevator.

The sequence moves from abstract prediction to practical interdependence. The fortune-teller claims the future is not fixed; the broken elevator immediately forces Ayanokōji and Ibuki to act in an uncertain present.

## 3.3 `意外と葛城康平は悩んでいる`

A mundane birthday-shopping outing expands into three different forms of social knowledge:

1. the boys' inability to choose a suitable present;
2. Kushida's encyclopedic relationship knowledge;
3. Katsuragi's hidden familial burden.

Ayanokōji ultimately helps Katsuragi circumvent the school's isolation rules so a gift can reach his younger sister. The story makes a serious institutional point through ordinary affection: a rule designed for security can also make legitimate family care impossible.

## 3.4 `さりとて日常に潜む危険性`

Horikita becomes trapped by an absurdly mundane object: her hand is stuck in a water bottle during a dormitory water outage. Her competence becomes practically useless because pride and social isolation leave her with very few people she is willing to call.

The joke is structurally exact. A character who wants self-sufficiency is defeated by a problem too trivial to solve through excellence alone.

## 3.5 `女難、災難の１日。天使のような悪魔の笑顔`

Yamauchi asks Ayanokōji to mediate a confession to Sakura Airi. The story could easily become another manipulation exercise. Instead, Ayanokōji explicitly preserves Airi's right to refuse, refuses to distribute her contact information without consent, stays with her because she asks not to be alone, and encourages Yamauchi to speak directly.

It is one of the cleanest examples so far of Ayanokōji using social insight **without privately predetermining another person's emotional answer**.

## 3.6 `他クラスとの交流会`

The pool outing gathers several earlier threads:

- Kei's post-Volume-4 relationship with Ayanokōji;
- Airi's developing friendship;
- Sudō's increasingly legible athletic value;
- Ichinose's student-council ambition;
- Horikita's fixation on class evaluation;
- Ayanokōji's concealment;
- and cross-class leisure.

The chapter contains both the volume's warmest ordinary-life material and some of its sharpest ethical contradictions.

## 3.7 `池寛治と山内春樹と須藤健の夏休み（番外編）`

The bonus story reveals the hidden machinery behind Operation Delta. Its placement after the main story matters. What initially appears to be a successful protective sabotage becomes ethically more complicated once the reader sees that Ayanokōji knowingly joins the voyeuristic operation, allows the camera to reach the women's changing room, and sabotages the footage from inside.

The bonus story therefore performs retrospective recontextualization **within the same volume**.

# 4. Ayanokōji Kiyotaka
## An unfinished social self with more than one available ethic

## 4.1 Freedom and loneliness are already in tension

Ayanokōji begins the Ibuki story by describing his holidays as simple because nobody calls him and he calls nobody. He names the condition directly:

> `つまるところ孤独なのだ。`

He immediately insists that freedom is enough and that he should not ask for excess happiness. Yet the narration undermines the defense almost at once. When Sudō appears to invite him out, Ayanokōji admits that he has not been invited to play for some time and cannot suppress his happiness.

`Y1V04.5|spine:12|xhtml:text/part0010.html|p:0004-0018`

The contradiction is important because neither pole is fake.

He genuinely values freedom from obligation.

He also genuinely wants to be wanted.

He recognizes that more friendships create more demands, but the absence of those demands is not emotionally neutral. This is the beginnings of a distinction the earlier volumes have not yet articulated well: **freedom from people is not the same as freedom with people**.

## 4.2 His social self is being trained through ordinary reflection

The discussion with Ibuki about conversational anxiety produces one of the volume's best ability arguments. Ayanokōji distinguishes possession of a capacity from usable performance. If a person can speak fluently while acting under another role, the capacity exists; anxiety prevents access to it in the ordinary situation.

He compares communication to athletic ability:

> talent and cultivated ability are tested, but possession is not identical to successful use.

His conclusion about Ibuki is explicit:

> she has the ability to speak, but cannot use it well.

`Y1V04.5|spine:14|xhtml:text/part0012.html|p:0059-0075`

The analysis is not merely about Ibuki. Ayanokōji admits that prolonged solitude led him to think seriously about why he could not make friends. The comic exaggeration that follows does not erase the sincerity of the inquiry. He is deliberately studying sociality because it does not come automatically.

This materially strengthens the Year 1 ability framework:

> **Possessed social ability can exist while remaining psychologically inaccessible or relationally unusable.**

## 4.3 `生まれたばかり` and `液状`: the social self is not a completed mask

Kei notices that Ayanokōji's speech and manner with her differ from his public demeanor. His response is one of the most important self-descriptions in early Year 1.

He says that the individual and human being called Ayanokōji Kiyotaka is, in a sense, `生まれたばかり / newly born`. He remains `液状 / liquid`; becoming solid will take time.

`Y1V04.5|spine:27|xhtml:text/part0025.html|p:0121-0135`

This is stronger than a simple “multiple masks” interpretation.

A mask presupposes a stable face underneath it.

Liquid presupposes **unfinished form**.

The novel therefore offers a model in which several Ayanokōjis can all be real without being equally mature:

- the quiet public student;
- the sharp strategic observer;
- the sexually curious adolescent;
- the lonely boy who wants friends;
- the person who treats people as assets;
- the person who is learning to enjoy purposeless companionship.

The task of the series is not necessarily to reveal which one is “the true Ayanokōji.” Volume 4.5 suggests that ordinary life is participating in the creation of the answer.

## 4.4 Blankness is autobiographical, not emotional

Later at the pool, Kei tells him there is nothing ordinary about him and that he looks capable of killing without hesitation. He responds that there really is “nothing,” that there is so little to talk about that he is simply `真っ白 / pure blankness`.

`Y1V04.5|spine:29|xhtml:text/part0027.html|p:0373-0382`

At the local Volume-4.5 boundary, this should not be converted into later backstory. Its present function is psychological and autobiographical. Ayanokōji lacks ordinary material through which adolescents narrate themselves:

- family stories;
- childhood friendships;
- hobbies accumulated over years;
- birthdays and gifts;
- ordinary firsts;
- social mistakes remembered as anecdotes.

The volume fills that blankness with first experiences.

He goes shopping with male classmates for pleasure.

He tries to choose a birthday gift.

He spends pointless time at the pool.

He eats an ordinary soft-serve ice cream and reacts with visible astonishment.

These events are small precisely because the missing life they begin to supply is small.

## 4.5 He can decline optimization

The strongest ethical development in the volume is not an act of heroism. It is a refusal to exploit an available advantage.

When Ayanokōji notices Katsuragi buying a feminine birthday item, his strategic reflex immediately identifies the possibility of capturing evidence of shoplifting or another weakness that could later become leverage. He then stops himself.

The moment does not prove that he has abandoned instrumental reasoning. His mind still produces the exploit automatically. What changes is that **the existence of leverage does not compel its use**.

That distinction becomes even clearer in the memory-card sequence. Ayanokōji later possesses material that could potentially be used to expel students. He openly discusses the strategic value of expulsion and ranks Sudō as worth preserving because of physical potential. Yet he ultimately destroys the card and tells Kei that Class D will not lose an expelled student through this opportunity.

`Y1V04.5|spine:29|xhtml:text/part0027.html|p:0331-0390`

The text therefore refuses both easy readings:

- he is not suddenly post-instrumental;
- he is not an automaton that always converts information into maximal leverage.

He has a strategic pruning model **and** the capacity to decline an efficient weapon.

## 4.6 Ordinary pleasure reaches the body before theory

The soft-serve scene is analytically disproportionate to its plot importance.

Ayanokōji chooses the most standard milk soft serve, tastes it, and responds:

> `これは……美味しいな……`

Ichinose notices that she has rarely or never seen him make that expression.

`Y1V04.5|spine:31|xhtml:text/part0029.html|p:0058-0065`

There is no strategic benefit.

No relationship is being manipulated.

No test is being solved.

The body simply encounters pleasure.

The scene is important because it demonstrates preference formation without theory. Ayanokōji does not decide that ordinary food will humanize him and then execute the lesson. He tastes something and likes it.

That is what the counter-curriculum can do that authored development cannot: produce an answer the learner did not select in advance.

# 5. Ibuki Mio
## Social capacity, skepticism, and cross-class cooperation without trust

Ibuki is one of the volume's most successful cases of ordinary life broadening a character who had previously been read primarily through class conflict.

## 5.1 She is not socially incapable

Ayanokōji's conversational analysis sharply revises a superficial reading of Ibuki as simply abrasive or antisocial. Her problem is not absence of communicative capacity. She can perform convincingly when she has a defined role, as the island infiltration demonstrated. In ordinary interaction she becomes self-conscious about how she will be evaluated and allows anticipated judgment to degrade performance.

The distinction matters because it resembles the series' larger account of `実力`:

> **An ability can be real while remaining unusable under a person's habitual social conditions.**

Ibuki's isolation is therefore partly psychological architecture. She is not condemned to it by temperament alone.

## 5.2 Her relationship to belief is more nuanced than “tough skeptic”

Ibuki is interested in fortune-telling. Ayanokōji approaches the subject through cold-reading theory and statistical skepticism, while Ibuki objects to the assumption that complete rational certainty is the only respectable posture. The novel does not establish supernatural truth. It does allow Ibuki to articulate a more plural attitude toward uncertainty than her physical/direct persona might suggest.

The fortune-teller's statement that Ayanokōji experienced an unusually harsh childhood, lacks ordinary family benefit, and possesses `宿命天中殺` is **not** reliable exposition merely because parts sound suggestive. Ayanokōji himself repeatedly questions the method. What matters thematically is the fortune-teller's final qualification:

> even an unusual fate is a characteristic, not a command; what one becomes is decided by oneself.

`Y1V04.5|spine:14|xhtml:text/part0012.html|p:0205-0230`

Within a series increasingly concerned with classification, the scene offers a useful counter-image. A label may describe conditions without possessing sovereignty over the future.

## 5.3 The elevator produces practical equality

When the elevator stops, class identity becomes irrelevant. Ayanokōji and Ibuki do not need trust in the political sense; they need coordination.

Ayanokōji initially uses ordinary safety knowledge and attempts to contact help. As heat and anxiety rise, he gives Ibuki the elevator-command theory partly because he wants to redirect her panic and buy time. He privately doubts the trick will actually work.

`Y1V04.5|spine:14|xhtml:text/part0012.html|p:0385-0405`

This is still psychological management, but it is importantly different from Volume 4. He is not creating Ibuki's distress in order to make himself necessary. He is responding to an independently occurring problem and trying to preserve her usable agency until rescue arrives.

When the elevator moves, staff later indicate that external remote control—not their secret command—caused the result. Ayanokōji's confidence therefore does not become magical competence. The scene allows him to be useful while also being wrong about causation.

Katsuragi's discreet involvement in the rescue further matters because it produces a cross-class moral space. He says that friendly relations between students of different classes outside the examination setting are welcome. Rivalry has not abolished the possibility of ordinary mutual aid.

# 6. Katsuragi Kōhei
## Family obligation beneath the class-leader exterior

Katsuragi's chapter is one of the strongest demonstrations that a student's political role is not the whole person.

## 6.1 Birthday shopping exposes Ayanokōji's missing ordinary knowledge

Ayanokōji joins Ike, Yamauchi, and Sudō to buy a birthday gift. He explicitly notes that meeting male classmates for pure leisure, rather than school or examination reasons, is new to him.

`Y1V04.5|spine:15|xhtml:text/part0013.html|p:0040-0059`

His gift instincts are comically malformed. Without ordinary experience, he considers roses and a ring—objects whose romantic meaning wildly exceeds the situation. The joke makes the same point as his social-self metaphor: ordinary competence is learned through ordinary participation.

The episode also resists treating low-stakes consumption as meaningless. Birthday spending creates social memory. Choosing an object requires knowing another person's preferences. The boys' difficulty is therefore a small exercise in thinking about somebody who is not present.

## 6.2 Kushida's social database is a form of extraordinary ability

When the boys try to determine who might have a birthday on August 29, Kushida consults a list containing birthdays across the first-year cohort. She has already gathered data that no formal exam would reward but that gives her enormous social reach.

She also stops the boys from mocking Katsuragi's baldness by explaining his alopecia rather than allowing his body to remain a joke.

The sequence should be entered into the cumulative ability model as evidence that Kushida possesses **distributed relationship memory**:

- names;
- dates;
- personal circumstances;
- social boundaries;
- and the tone required to correct others without destroying the interaction.

The ethical danger of such knowledge remains live after Volume 1, but its utility is undeniable.

## 6.3 Katsuragi's sister changes the meaning of rule-breaking

The birthday item is for Katsuragi's twin younger sister outside the school. Their parents and grandparents are dead; relatives care for her, and Katsuragi sees himself as carrying a quasi-parental responsibility toward a physically frailer sibling.

The school forbids ordinary external contact and delivery. The rule is understandable in light of the institution's isolation design, but the family case exposes its moral bluntness. A system can be consistently administered and still produce unreasonable burdens when it refuses to distinguish among motives.

Ayanokōji helps construct a delivery method through Sudō's exceptional off-campus basketball access, a disguised container, cash-on-delivery logistics, and proof of transfer.

His motives are mixed. He sincerely helps Katsuragi, but he also thinks in terms of acquiring a favor and retaining leverage if Katsuragi later betrays him.

This is characteristic of Volume 4.5:

> **The presence of strategic accounting does not make the benevolent result unreal; the benevolent result does not erase strategic accounting.**

Sudō's agreement is also revealing. The story of a sick younger sister makes refusal emotionally difficult for him. His rough exterior does not prevent particularistic empathy.

The suggestion that Manabu may have quietly facilitated some part of the off-campus delivery remains only a local inference. The text does not permit it to be promoted to fact.

# 7. Horikita Suzune
## Independence encounters the practical necessity of other people

## 7.1 The bottle is a miniature political theory

Horikita becomes physically trapped by a bottle during a temporary water outage. The comedy works because the object is beneath the scale at which she expects competence to matter.

She tries to solve the problem herself until exhaustion makes self-sufficiency irrational. Her available social network is tiny. She does not want to ask Kushida. She eventually calls Ayanokōji.

`Y1V04.5|spine:21|xhtml:text/part0019.html|p:0073-0104`

This is not a dramatic conversion to sociability. It is more analytically useful than that.

Horikita discovers a limit condition:

> **Autonomy requires the capacity to obtain help without experiencing dependence as humiliation.**

A person who has nobody to call is not maximally free. In some circumstances, that person is practically fragile.

Airi's contribution reinforces the point. She provides water without demanding a humiliating explanation and communicates more comfortably through chat than face-to-face. The help is low-pressure because it respects the recipient's embarrassment.

Horikita eventually says:

> `……ありがとう`

`Y1V04.5|spine:23|xhtml:text/part0021.html|p:0256-0278`

The word is ordinary. The developmental significance is not. She accepts assistance without immediately converting the debt into contempt or denial.

The chapter then punctures any temptation to treat Ayanokōji as omnipotent: he puts his own hand in the bottle and becomes stuck as well. The comedy equalizes bodies in a way the strategic narrative rarely can.

## 7.2 Surveillance can be used for privacy as well as control

Ayanokōji notices the elevator surveillance camera and positions himself to shield Horikita's embarrassing condition from view.

`Y1V04.5|spine:23|xhtml:text/part0021.html|p:0150-0158;0224-0228`

Earlier volumes repeatedly use surveillance to produce legibility. Here concealment serves dignity rather than deception. That is a useful correction to any interpretation that treats visibility as inherently liberating.

A person can need to be seen in order to obtain justice.

A person can also need **not** to be seen in order to preserve privacy.

The ethical question is who controls the exposure.

## 7.3 Kushida remains an unresolved leadership failure

Horikita believes Kushida manipulated the zodiac result and experiences the outcome as personal defeat. Ayanokōji argues that a leader must account even for betrayal originating within the group.

The practical point is strong: leadership cannot model only cooperative members.

The normative claim requires qualification. A leader bears responsibility for designing systems resilient to betrayal, but that does not mean the leader becomes morally responsible for every deliberate act of another person. Ayanokōji's formulation risks turning leadership into total liability—the same logic by which superior perception can become a claim to superior jurisdiction.

## 7.4 Horikita still interprets leisure through competition

At the pool, Horikita quickly accepts Ichinose's playful framing of water volleyball as a miniature class confrontation and a wager over lunch. She still understands social play most comfortably when it resembles a contest with visible stakes.

Yet by the end of the day she voluntarily remains with Ayanokōji, suggests walking together, and says:

> `今日は意外と楽しかったわ。たまにはこんな休みがあるのも悪くないわね`

`Y1V04.5|spine:31|xhtml:text/part0029.html|p:0086-0099`

The word `意外と / unexpectedly` is crucial. She has acquired evidence against one of her own assumptions. Leisure possesses value even when it cannot be converted into Class A progress.

That is development through experience rather than persuasion.

# 8. Sakura Airi and Yamauchi Haruki
## Refusal, responsibility, and a low-coercion model of social intervention

The confession story provides one of the clearest ethical counterpoints to Volume 4.

## 8.1 Ayanokōji protects informational consent

Yamauchi wants Airi's contact information. Ayanokōji does not simply provide it. He recognizes that contact information belongs to Airi's private sphere and that disclosure without permission could expose her to pressure she did not choose.

That is significant because Volume 3 had shown Ayanokōji willing to trade Sakura's email address as leverage in the Horikita mud operation. The current volume does not erase the earlier violation. It demonstrates that he is capable of a more autonomy-preserving decision.

The contrast should remain visible rather than resolved into linear moral progress.

## 8.2 He refuses to commodify romance

Yamauchi's fantasy that he can offer all of his private points is treated as misguided. Ayanokōji recognizes that romantic acceptance cannot legitimately be purchased like an exam point or a school service.

This is another important limit to the political economy of the school. The institution can assign prices to many things. Intimacy does not become reciprocal merely because one party offers a valuable resource.

## 8.3 Airi is given the right to reject

Airi contacts Ayanokōji, asks about his dating experience, and appears relieved that he has none. When the confession approaches, she asks:

> `お願い……１人にしないで`

Ayanokōji stays.

More importantly, he tells her that she has the right to refuse.

`Y1V04.5|spine:26|xhtml:text/part0024.html|p:0158-0173`

This is not an environment designed to force the “correct” romantic result. Ayanokōji does not decide that Airi ought to date Yamauchi, nor does he decide that she ought to reject him. He creates enough safety for her to articulate her own answer.

That makes the scene analytically valuable for the Year 1 governing question:

> **Ayanokōji can support development without claiming authorship over the destination.**

## 8.4 Yamauchi's rejection produces modest growth

Airi rejects him directly. Yamauchi is embarrassed and disappointed, but he eventually recognizes that his “love” was built mostly from her attractiveness and body rather than deep knowledge of her as a person. He also recognizes that confessing creates responsibility: once feelings are placed before another person, their response has to be endured.

`Y1V04.5|spine:26|xhtml:text/part0024.html|p:0220-0234`

This is a small but genuine moral maturation. The novel does not need to transform Yamauchi into a sophisticated romantic subject. It only needs him to understand that desire does not create entitlement.

## 8.5 `友達……だからな`

When Ayanokōji remains with Yamauchi after the rejection, he explains the action through friendship:

> `友達……だからな`

`Y1V04.5|spine:26|xhtml:text/part0024.html|p:0235`

The pause gives the category weight. “Friend” remains new enough that Ayanokōji has to say it almost experimentally.

Later, when Airi thanks him for inviting her to the pool, he answers much more naturally:

> `別に普通のことだろ友達なんだから`

`Y1V04.5|spine:29|xhtml:text/part0027.html|p:0033-0046`

That variation is development at the level of language. Friendship moves from a consciously adopted term toward an ordinary relational assumption.

# 9. Karuizawa Kei
## From survival identity to glimpses of voluntary enjoyment

Volume 4.5 cannot be read ethically without carrying forward the coercive origin of Ayanokōji and Kei's current relationship from Volume 4.

## 9.1 `偽りの強者`: Kei revises her own survival performance

Kei explains that her middle-school bullying was effectively denied by multiple parties: the school, the aggressors, and Kei herself. That collective suppression allowed her to arrive at Advanced Nurturing High School without an official identity as victim and create a new social position.

Ayanokōji tells her to stop bullying Airi. His wording is unusually direct:

> becoming an aggressor to avoid becoming a victim is still becoming an aggressor.

Kei calls herself a `偽りの強者 / false strong person` and concedes that she may have gone too far.

`Y1V04.5|spine:27|xhtml:text/part0025.html|p:0069-0116`

This should not be read as proof that her public dominance is entirely false. The better distinction is:

- the status is real;
- the ability to mobilize others is real;
- the security is contingent;
- the identity remains organized around terror of falling back into victimhood.

Ayanokōji's intervention has a legitimate ethical object—stop Kei from reproducing harm—but the scene is also hypocritical. He condemns her use of coercion shortly after having used psychological and sexual coercion against her.

The novel does not have him acknowledge the symmetry.

## 9.2 She can speak about trauma with him, but asymmetry remains

Kei remarks that she can talk about her past with Ayanokōji more easily than expected. This is meaningful evidence of trust or at least reduced defensive arousal. It does not erase the fact that he acquired access to that past through coercion.

The relationship has therefore become psychologically usable before it becomes ethically reciprocal.

## 9.3 Ayanokōji still claims jurisdiction through command

He orders Kei into the pool operation despite her resistance. He explicitly keeps the arrangement secret from Horikita and admits that he does not yet trust Horikita enough to reveal this independent network; Horikita functions partly as a convenient public cover.

That establishes two layers of Class D governance:

- visible Horikita-centered leadership;
- hidden Ayanokōji-centered information infrastructure.

Kei is becoming one of the principal components of the latter.

## 9.4 Scar, swimsuit, and the social meaning of the body

Kei's scar continues to organize bodily behavior. A competition swimsuit can conceal it, but wearing one in leisure risks violating the fashionable image through which she maintains female social status. She therefore experiences the pool as a conflict between:

- bodily safety;
- social legibility;
- personal style;
- and the desire to participate.

The body is not merely a private object. In Kei's social world it is an element of political performance.

## 9.5 `命令だ`: genuine enjoyment after genuine coercion

Ayanokōji tells Kei to remove the rash guard and swim. She refuses. He answers:

> `命令だ`

She calls him terrible and says she hates him. He then grabs her arm and pushes her into the pool.

`Y1V04.5|spine:31|xhtml:text/part0029.html|p:0005-0027`

Kei retaliates by pulling him in. She laughs. Ayanokōji reflects that seeing her happy expression may have made the result worthwhile.

Both facts are true:

1. Kei's later enjoyment is genuine.
2. Kei's initial refusal is genuine.

The first does not retroactively cancel the second.

This is a compact example of the series' recurring developmental danger:

> **A successful outcome can tempt both character and reader to treat coercion as validated by the happiness it eventually produces.**

A rigorous reading must refuse that shortcut.

# 10. Sudō Ken, Ichinose Honami, and socially usable ability

The pool chapter quietly extends the volume's argument about what the school does and does not measure.

## 10.1 Sudō's basketball begins converting talent into institutional value

Sudō reports receiving roughly 3,000 private points as recognition for club contribution and says stronger upperclassmen receive much larger sums. This is significant because basketball ceases to be merely the personal ambition Horikita dismissed in Volume 1. It is now legible inside the school's political economy.

`Y1V04.5|spine:27|xhtml:text/part0025.html|p:0313-0322`

Horikita responds with genuine respect and suggests Sudō's day of making a major contribution to the class may be approaching.

That is a substantial change from the Volume 1 study-group conflict. She is beginning to see a capacity that cannot be reduced to academic ranking as class-relevant `実力`.

Ayanokōji's own later calculus makes the same point more coldly. When Kei challenges his willingness to contemplate expulsion despite having saved Sudō, he says Sudō's physical potential matters because the class is relatively weak in that dimension and Kōenji cannot be counted as reliably usable power.

The distinction is precise:

> **Possessed ability becomes collective power only when the group can reasonably expect access to it.**

Sudō's development therefore contains two movements:

- greater discipline makes his athletic talent more institutionally usable;
- the class becomes better at recognizing that talent as value.

## 10.2 Ichinose understands institutional sorting more broadly than Horikita

The student-council conversation exposes a difference in their theories of class placement.

Ichinose suggests the school may evaluate students through a broad composite involving intelligence, maturity, cooperation, and total human capacity. Horikita still treats her Class D placement as an obvious institutional mistake.

`Y1V04.5|spine:29|xhtml:text/part0027.html|p:0116-0134`

This is important negative evidence against an overly smooth Horikita-growth narrative. She has changed in practice faster than she has changed in self-conception. She can appreciate Sudō's value and enjoy leisure while still believing that her own placement in D must simply be erroneous.

Development is uneven.

## 10.3 Ichinose's frugality is political, not merely personal

After losing the water-volleyball wager, Ichinose covers lunch and notes the importance of preserving points for more consequential needs. Her ordinary spending behavior already reflects class-level responsibility.

This reinforces the contrast between the four emerging political cultures. Ichinose's leadership is not based on centralized fear or aloof preservation. It appears through distributed sociability, generosity, and careful resource management.

## 10.4 Ayanokōji still suppresses visible performance during play

During water volleyball, Ayanokōji recognizes that Ichinose is watching him and deliberately moderates his performance.

Even in leisure, concealment remains habitual.

That matters because Volume 4.5 does not create an entirely unguarded protagonist. The ordinary student and the hidden strategist coexist in the same scene. The counter-curriculum provides opportunities for spontaneous experience; it does not automatically dismantle defensive conditioning.

# 11. Student-council politics enter ordinary space

Volume 4.5 does not contain a special examination, but it quietly widens the institutional horizon.

## 11.1 Manabu: office does not create ability

In conversation with Ayanokōji, Manabu makes an important distinction about political authority: the student council itself is not inherently powerful. Its significance depends on the competence of the people using the office.

He also indicates that class placement is not a simple A-to-D ranking of raw individual ability and that Suzune's placement in D has reasons she has not understood.

Manabu then attempts to recruit Ayanokōji as vice president. Ayanokōji refuses.

This is consistent with his current preference for hidden or low-visibility agency. Formal office would increase both responsibility and legibility—two things he is actively avoiding.

Manabu warns that Nagumo's coming order may transform the school in ways he dislikes. Volume 4.5 therefore introduces institutional succession not through a formal political chapter but through the same summer environment in which students are shopping and swimming.

The contrast matters:

> ordinary life is not apolitical; it is where political commitments become personal.

## 11.2 Ayanokōji distinguishes institutional mission from personal concern

When Manabu's appeal concerns the abstract welfare of the school, Ayanokōji declines involvement. When he asks whether the request is really personal concern for Suzune, he indicates that such a framing might affect his willingness.

That distinction is significant for his emerging relational ethics.

He is more responsive to a particular person than to a generalized institutional mission.

This does not automatically make him morally better. Particularism can also produce favoritism. But it demonstrates that individual attachment already has motivational force independent of class optimization.

## 11.3 Nagumo and Ichinose: mobility becomes aspirational

At the pool, Ichinose identifies Nagumo as a model partly because he began outside Class A yet appears positioned to become student-council president. She imagines herself eventually following the same path.

Her ambition is institutionally important. Ichinose is not merely a kind class representative. She wants formal school-wide authority.

Horikita's response is defensive on Manabu's behalf, revealing that her relationship to student-council politics remains inseparable from her brother.

The scene therefore establishes two very different routes into institutional ambition:

- Ichinose sees the council as a route of mobility and service;
- Horikita sees it through the emotional shadow of Manabu.

Neither trajectory is resolved at this volume boundary.

# 12. Class and political development without an examination

The absence of a formal test lets the volume reveal which class structures persist when nobody is explicitly competing.

## 12.1 Class identity loosens but does not disappear

Ibuki can cooperate with Ayanokōji.

Katsuragi can discreetly assist students from another class.

Ichinose can socialize and play with D-class students.

The pool can briefly become a shared social environment rather than a battlefield.

These moments demonstrate that the school has not fully transformed peer identity into class identity. Cross-class friendship and ordinary contact remain possible.

That matters politically because any system premised on permanent zero-sum hostility is operating against the social tendencies of the students themselves.

## 12.2 Horikita remains visible; Ayanokōji builds parallel infrastructure

From the outside, Horikita remains the obvious strategic center of Class D. Ayanokōji explicitly values that arrangement because she functions as a `隠れ蓑 / cover` for his own activity.

At the same time, his private relationship with Kei is developing into a separate channel for:

- information;
- covert action;
- social influence;
- and operational secrecy.

He specifically says that he does not yet trust Horikita enough to reveal everything.

Thus the class already has two forms of governance:

1. **public developmental leadership**, increasingly associated with Horikita;
2. **hidden environmental authorship**, increasingly associated with Ayanokōji.

The long-term compatibility of these structures remains an open problem.

## 12.3 Kushida's power remains infrastructural

Kushida is not the central dramatic subject of Volume 4.5, but her birthday knowledge and cross-class social reach demonstrate why simple moral judgment cannot exhaust her political role.

Her network produces usable knowledge that neither Horikita nor Ayanokōji can reproduce through ordinary friendship at this stage.

The question is not merely whether Kushida is trustworthy.

It is whether a class can safely depend upon social infrastructure concentrated in a person whose private hostility remains unresolved.

# 13. Institutional analysis
## The school loosens pressure without ceasing to govern

## 13.1 Campus isolation structures even private affection

Katsuragi's sister story demonstrates the breadth of the school's isolation. The institution is not only controlling examination information. It restricts ordinary family exchange so aggressively that a birthday gift requires covert logistics.

The security rationale is intelligible: uncontrolled contact with the outside world could compromise the school's closed competitive environment.

The human cost is also real.

A rule that treats all external exchange as suspect cannot distinguish between cheating and care.

This is a recurring institutional problem in miniature:

> **A system optimized for clean control acquires moral blind spots because the human reasons for behavior are more varied than the rule's categories.**

## 13.2 Surveillance is pervasive but normatively ambiguous

The dormitory/elevator camera can protect safety, provide institutional oversight, or threaten privacy depending on who controls access and purpose. Ayanokōji shielding Horikita from the camera shows that reducing legibility can be humane.

Operation Delta reverses the ethical valence. Students create unauthorized surveillance for sexual access.

Volume 4.5 therefore broadens the surveillance theme beyond school versus student. The relevant question becomes:

> **Who has the right to observe whom, under what conditions, and for what purpose?**

Visibility is neither intrinsically emancipatory nor intrinsically oppressive. Consent and jurisdiction matter.

## 13.3 Private points colonize leisure but do not define it completely

Points appear everywhere:

- fortune-telling costs money;
- birthday gifts cost money;
- Sudō receives club rewards;
- Ichinose wagers lunch;
- Katsuragi offers compensation;
- Ayanokōji thinks about favors and future exchange.

This confirms that the school has successfully created a universal economic language.

Yet several of the volume's most important outcomes resist price:

- Horikita's gratitude;
- Airi's refusal;
- Yamauchi's acceptance;
- Kei's laughter;
- Ayanokōji's first-taste pleasure;
- Ibuki's practical cooperation.

The point system can facilitate these encounters. It does not fully explain their value.

# 14. Ability and legibility
## From possessed capacity to socially available capacity

Volume 4.5 may be the strongest non-examination evidence so far for the claim that `実力` is relational rather than merely possessed.

## 14.1 Ibuki: capacity blocked by self-consciousness

Ibuki can communicate but cannot reliably access the capacity when she imagines herself being judged. Her problem is performance availability, not absence of talent.

## 14.2 Sudō: capacity becomes collective only through discipline

Sudō's body has always been powerful. Basketball training, point rewards, and growing emotional regulation are beginning to make the capacity socially and institutionally useful.

## 14.3 Kushida: relational memory as political ability

Birthday lists and personal knowledge demonstrate a form of data management embedded in friendship rather than bureaucracy.

## 14.4 Airi: digital communication as context-sensitive ability

Airi communicates more comfortably through chat than face-to-face. Her social capacity therefore changes with medium. The school could easily misread one register as the whole person.

## 14.5 Kōenji as the absent comparison

Ayanokōji's expulsion discussion treats Kōenji's ability as strategically unavailable because the class cannot count on his participation. This is a crucial distinction:

> **Ability that cannot be coordinated may remain individually impressive while contributing little to collective security.**

## 14.6 Moral ability appears in low-stakes choices

The volume also gives increasing evidence for a category not captured by academic or physical performance: the capacity to exercise judgment when no formal reward demands it.

Examples include:

- respecting Airi's right to refuse;
- deciding not to exploit potential evidence against Katsuragi;
- destroying the memory card;
- Airi facing Yamauchi directly rather than hiding;
- Yamauchi accepting the rejection without retaliation.

These are forms of competence in living with other people.

# 15. Ethics and autonomy
## Volume 4.5 proves that Ayanokōji has more than one intervention model

The most useful ethical insight of the second pass is comparative. The volume contains several Ayanokōji interventions whose methods differ sharply.

| Case | Goal | Subject knowledge | Refusal capacity | Method | Result | Ethical character |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Ibuki elevator | prevent panic / escape | high | meaningful | reassurance, shared problem solving | cooperation until rescue | low coercion |
| Katsuragi gift | get gift to sister | high | full | bargaining, logistical circumvention | successful delivery plan | consensual but rule-breaking |
| Horikita bottle | protect privacy / help | high | high | practical aid, concealment from camera | assistance accepted | autonomy-preserving |
| Airi confession | let Airi answer safely | high | high | support, privacy, explicit right to refuse | direct rejection | strongly autonomy-preserving |
| Kei anti-bullying | stop harm to Airi | partial | limited due dependency | command + promised protection | behavioral correction | substantively justified goal, asymmetrical method |
| Kei swimming | produce leisure/enjoyment | high | **low** | `命令だ`, physical push | later enjoyment | coercive paternalism |
| Operation Delta | stop permanent voyeuristic footage / class catastrophe | partial and distributed | girls have none | covert complicity + sabotage | footage destroyed | protective outcome, ethically compromised means |

The table matters because it eliminates a convenient excuse.

Ayanokōji is not forced to choose between total nonintervention and coercive authorship. He demonstrably knows how to:

- preserve refusal;
- offer support;
- allow direct speech;
- share a problem;
- and walk away from leverage.

When he chooses coercion, it is increasingly a choice among available methods.

## 15.1 Interdependence is the healthier alternative to both isolation and parasitism

The volume repeatedly stages two bad extremes:

- Horikita's self-sufficient isolation;
- Kei's trauma-shaped conviction that survival requires attachment to a stronger host.

Neither is genuine autonomy.

Horikita cannot solve every mundane problem alone.

Kei cannot feel safe if safety depends upon unconditional submission to the strongest protector.

The stories point toward a third form:

> **interdependence—relationships in which assistance does not erase the recipient's capacity to refuse or act independently.**

Airi's confession scene comes closest to this model. She needs Ayanokōji present. Needing him does not determine her answer. Support enlarges her capacity to choose rather than narrowing it.

## 15.2 Benevolent coercion remains coercion

The pool scene is the clearest test. Ayanokōji correctly intuits that Kei may enjoy swimming once the social and bodily barriers are crossed. His prediction is accurate.

That accuracy does not create jurisdiction over her body.

The ethical defense is obvious: he wants her to experience ordinary enjoyment and knows her trauma/social status may prevent her from choosing it spontaneously.

The criticism is stronger:

- she explicitly refuses;
- no urgent harm requires overriding her;
- the benefit is leisure, not rescue;
- and he possesses a relationship in which his commands carry security implications.

This is precisely the kind of case where beneficial outcome cannot settle legitimacy.

# 16. Operation Delta
## Protection through complicity, surveillance, and hidden sabotage

The second pass materially corrects the earlier reading of this episode.

## 16.1 The plan is explicitly criminal

Ike proposes sending a radio-controlled camera through the ventilation system into the girls' changing room and recording them undressing. Ayanokōji repeatedly identifies the plan as criminal and warns that discovery could produce expulsion.

`Y1V04.5|spine:33|xhtml:text/part0031.html|p:0082-0123`

The boys' rationalization that voyeurism is simply “youth” does not receive serious moral validation from Ayanokōji's narration. He understands both the privacy violation and the institutional risk.

## 16.2 Ayanokōji joins rather than directly stopping the plan

He concludes that if he simply refuses, Ike and the others may attempt the operation secretly. He therefore agrees to cooperate on the condition that it happen only once, while privately intending to sabotage it.

`Y1V04.5|spine:33|xhtml:text/part0031.html|p:0124-0135`

This is a familiar Ayanokōji structure:

> enter the system → preserve insider access → control the outcome from within.

The strategic logic is coherent.

The ethical problem is that insider control makes him a participant in the immediate violation.

## 16.3 The camera really reaches the changing room

This is the key correction.

The camera enters the women's changing-room space. The boys see students on the live monitor. The device is actively recording.

`Y1V04.5|spine:33|xhtml:text/part0031.html|p:0145-0155`

Ayanokōji uses the risk of detection to discourage prolonged live viewing, but he does not prevent the initial unauthorized visual capture.

The earlier formulation that he simply “prevents a sexual violation” is therefore too clean.

A more accurate statement is:

> **Ayanokōji limits and ultimately destroys the durable product of a privacy violation that he knowingly allows to begin as part of his sabotage strategy.**

## 16.4 Kei executes the decisive covert intervention

Kei has been briefed in advance. She enters the changing room with selected friends, finds the camera, removes the active memory card, replaces it with a blank card, and restores the setup without revealing the sabotage.

`Y1V04.5|spine:35|xhtml:text/part0033.html|p:0070-0087`

The scene is also strong evidence of her operational competence. She:

- prepares the vent in advance;
- recruits compliant social cover;
- controls sightlines;
- removes and replaces the storage medium;
- returns locks and environmental details;
- and keeps her heart rate and presentation controlled.

Calling Kei a “parasite” is increasingly inadequate as a total ability description. She is an effective covert operator precisely because of social awareness the formal school has not measured.

## 16.5 The boys learn nothing

The genuine recording is destroyed. The boys later discover only a blank card and conclude that the technology failed.

`Y1V04.5|spine:35|xhtml:text/part0033.html|p:0092-0118`

This protects the girls from permanent possession, copying, and later viewing.

It also means the perpetrators avoid accountability.

They do not learn:

- that the girls' privacy matters;
- that their conduct crossed a serious boundary;
- that another student deliberately stopped them;
- or that the class could have been damaged by their conduct.

Ayanokōji solves the immediate problem while preserving the boys' ignorance.

This is strategically elegant and educationally incomplete.

## 16.6 Formal contradiction: anti-voyeurism plot inside eroticized fanservice

The novel's own visual and prose framing complicates its ethical message. The narrative condemns covert nude recording while devoting substantial attention to swimsuits, breasts, changing-room bodies, and eroticized observation.

The contradiction does not make the privacy argument meaningless. It does mean the work participates in a form of spectator pleasure adjacent to the conduct it condemns.

The distinction is consent and publication context, not the absence of objectifying gaze.

A strong final synthesis should therefore preserve both:

- the narrative's explicit opposition to unauthorized permanent recording;
- the commercial/fanservice structure through which female bodies are still made available to the reader.

# 17. Japanese narration, voice, and register

Volume 4.5 is unusually useful linguistically because the characters spend so much time outside formal examination speech.

## 17.1 Ayanokōji: ordinary interiority is not a strategic afterthought

His internal narration remains casual `オレ` speech, but the subject matter becomes markedly mundane:

- loneliness;
- invitations;
- gift etiquette;
- awkward attraction;
- food;
- pool clothing;
- embarrassment;
- and the novelty of being a friend.

The humor is important evidence. When he considers roses and a ring as birthday gifts and immediately recognizes that he has drifted into proposal territory, the joke is not a cold strategic performance for another person. It is interior self-correction.

Likewise, his visible discomfort around swimsuit-clad Ichinose is distinctly adolescent. He notices her body, becomes unable to tolerate the situation comfortably, and retreats. Volume 4.5 therefore strengthens the distinction between **sexual noticing** and **sexual violation**. Ayanokōji is clearly heterosexual and attracted to women; that does not require participation in voyeuristic recording, and his refusal to assign the footage recreational value is meaningful even though his sabotage method remains ethically compromised.

## 17.2 Kei: public gyaru fluency and private directness

Kei's private register with Ayanokōji is blunt, impatient, teasing, and frequently challenging. She can tell him that he is strange, inconsistent, or cruel in ways that her public caste-management persona would not permit with most classmates.

Her phrase `偽りの強者` is particularly revealing because it is an act of self-theorization. She can distinguish between the outwardly dominant position she has built and the secure strength she believes she lacks.

At the same time, Operation Delta shows her public-social competence at full power. She selects friends who will follow her instructions without demanding explanation, uses ordinary social intimacy as visual cover, and performs the covert task without visible panic. The private “false strong person” is therefore also a genuinely skilled social actor.

## 17.3 Horikita: gratitude remains linguistically difficult

Horikita's `……ありがとう` is not remarkable grammar. The hesitation is the characterization. She still treats help as something that threatens her self-image, so even ordinary thanks requires crossing an internal boundary.

By contrast, `今日は意外と楽しかったわ` retains her polished feminine register while admitting that experience has contradicted expectation. She does not become a slangy, socially relaxed girl. Development occurs inside her established voice.

## 17.4 Airi: digitally mediated fluency

Airi's speech repeatedly shows a difference between mediated and direct communication. Text/chat lowers the pressure of being watched and allows her greater fluency. In person, pauses, repetition, and self-devaluation become more visible.

Her confession-rejection scene is important because the answer has to become audible in face-to-face social space. She is not merely able to decide privately; she learns to voice the decision while another person can hear it.

## 17.5 Ibuki: roughness without total social incompetence

Ibuki's clipped and confrontational style remains recognizable, but the fortune-teller story gives her polite behavior, curiosity, and capacity to cooperate. Her respectful thanks to the fortune-teller is a useful reminder that rough fictional voice should not be collapsed into absence of manners or emotional range.

## 17.6 Manabu and institutional speech

Manabu remains terse and authoritative. His speech about the student council is especially revealing because he treats office as instrumental rather than sacred. Authority acquires value through the person capable of using it. That position parallels the series' broader suspicion of institutional labels: class rank, office, and scores do not automatically exhaust actual capacity.

# 18. Appearance, illustration, and embodied characterization

## 18.1 The visual corpus foregrounds the body more aggressively than the prose thesis requires

The volume's color front matter and pool illustrations devote extensive attention to swimsuits and female bodies. This is commercially legible fanservice, but it also intersects with several narrative questions:

- Airi's attempts to reduce bodily visibility;
- Kei's scar and competition swimsuit;
- fashion as caste maintenance;
- Ayanokōji's own concealed trained body;
- ordinary adolescent attraction;
- and Operation Delta's violation of bodily privacy.

The overlap is productive and uncomfortable. The work wants the reader to enjoy visual access to bodies while condemning unauthorized access inside the story.

That tension should remain part of the formal analysis rather than being solved by declaring one mode legitimate and the other irrelevant.

## 18.2 Kei's swimsuit is both concealment and status problem

The competition swimsuit protects the scar from view but threatens the fashionable presentation through which Kei maintains social legitimacy. Embodiment therefore becomes political. Her body carries trauma that must be physically hidden while her clothing carries the social identity that must remain visibly current.

The pool scene's coercion is more serious because the request is not simply “join a game.” Ayanokōji is asking her to expose a mode of bodily presentation she has strong reasons to avoid.

## 18.3 Airi and the politics of concealment

Airi uses the rash guard to reduce exposure. The prose repeatedly notices her body, and the illustrations participate in the same visual economy. The text is therefore self-contradictory in a productive way: Airi's discomfort with visibility is characterized sympathetically even while the publication makes her body an object of reader attention.

## 18.4 Ayanokōji's rash guard

Ayanokōji wears a rash guard because he does not want to expose his body. Ichinose's accidental/proximate contact lets her notice that his musculature does not match the ordinary-student presentation. He lies that he does not train.

This is one of the rare moments when bodily evidence threatens the concealment his speech maintains.

The contrast with Kei is revealing. Both use clothing to manage what their bodies reveal. The reasons differ, but each demonstrates that public identity is partly a problem of controlled embodiment.

## 18.5 Comic bodily helplessness

The bottle episode and elevator episode repeatedly reduce competence to the body:

- Horikita cannot simply reason her hand out of the bottle;
- Ayanokōji later repeats the mistake;
- Ibuki and Ayanokōji cannot intellectually command a broken elevator into submission;
- heat and fatigue place hard limits on composure.

The volume thereby gives physical contingency a democratizing role. No amount of status or strategic reputation abolishes the ordinary vulnerability of having a body.

# 19. Motifs and recurring language

## 19.1 `自由 / freedom`

Freedom opens the volume as a privilege of student life and appears immediately beside loneliness. The text therefore begins to detach freedom from mere absence of control.

Freedom can mean:

- having no calls to answer;
- having enough time to waste;
- being outside direct adult supervision;
- being able to choose friendships;
- being able to reject romance;
- or being able to enter relationships without surrendering the self.

The volume does not settle which form is highest.

## 19.2 `友達 / friend`

Friendship progresses from concept to habit.

`友達……だからな` is hesitant and newly claimed.

`普通のことだろ友達なんだから` is already more natural.

The language supplies a small-scale chronology of relational learning.

## 19.3 `生まれたばかり / newly born`, `液状 / liquid`, `真っ白 / blank`

These three images form the volume's central selfhood cluster.

They do not describe emotional nonexistence.

They describe **lack of settled autobiographical form**.

Ayanokōji can possess strong strategic habits, appetites, attraction, preferences, and perceptiveness while still lacking an ordinary narrative identity.

## 19.4 Containers and entrapment

The volume repeatedly uses objects that hold or trap:

- an elevator;
- a water bottle;
- a gift container;
- a swimsuit concealing a scar;
- a memory card holding images;
- the school campus itself.

What matters is often who controls the boundary.

A sealed container can protect.

It can imprison.

It can conceal.

It can preserve evidence.

It can make a person dependent on someone outside it.

## 19.5 Cameras, cards, and controlled memory

The memory card is an especially strong object because it turns an ephemeral violation into durable, reproducible data. Ayanokōji's destruction of it is therefore not merely destruction of evidence. It is destruction of the **future power of the image**.

This extends Volume 2's concern with cameras and proof. Visual evidence can produce justice; visual evidence can also become a weapon of sexual domination.

## 19.6 Food and unassigned pleasure

Soft serve, junk food, birthday chocolate, and ordinary shopping repeatedly appear without needing to become symbols of class advancement. Food is one of the simplest places where preference can emerge without explanation.

For Ayanokōji, this matters because preference itself is part of personhood.

# 20. Formal and literary strengths

## 20.1 Vignette form reveals what exam plotting compresses

Special examinations are powerful narrative engines, but they tend to make every action legible through strategy. Volume 4.5 can spend time on events whose significance is delayed or noninstrumental.

The result is richer ensemble characterization:

- Ibuki becomes awkward, curious, and practically cooperative;
- Katsuragi becomes a brother carrying family responsibility;
- Horikita becomes ridiculous and grateful;
- Airi becomes an agent capable of refusal;
- Yamauchi becomes briefly self-reflective;
- Kei becomes both covert operator and girl enjoying a pool;
- Ayanokōji becomes someone who likes ice cream.

These are not decorative details. They establish the human material upon which later political structures act.

## 20.2 Comic objects reveal political philosophy

A water bottle exposes the limits of isolation.

A birthday gift exposes institutional overreach.

A swimsuit exposes the intersection of trauma and status.

A memory card exposes the political durability of images.

Soft serve exposes the possibility of unassigned preference.

The novel repeatedly allows mundane objects to do philosophical work without announcing that they are metaphors.

## 20.3 Retrospective revelation inside the volume

Operation Delta is initially encountered through Ayanokōji and Kei's mysterious behavior in the pool chapter. The bonus chapter later reveals the boys' plan and Ayanokōji's covert sabotage.

That structure reproduces one of the series' favorite formal strategies in miniature:

> event first → unexplained behavior → later causal reconstruction.

The later reconstruction does not merely answer “what happened?” It changes the ethical interpretation of the main chapter.

## 20.4 Development through repetition with variation

The volume repeatedly presents a person in difficulty and another person helping:

- Ibuki in the elevator;
- Katsuragi with his sister's gift;
- Horikita with the bottle;
- Airi with the confession;
- Kei with swimming;
- the girls with Operation Delta.

The forms of help differ dramatically.

Some preserve autonomy.

Some bargain.

Some deceive.

Some coerce.

Some protect without educating.

The repetition therefore turns “help” into an analytical category rather than an automatic virtue.

# 21. Critical reservations and strongest counterarguments

## 21.1 The counter-curriculum thesis can be overstated

Ordinary life is not outside the school.

Points, surveillance, class rivalry, social status, club rewards, formal office, and expulsion risk penetrate summer leisure. Even the pool outing becomes a wager and a site of strategic concealment.

The better claim is therefore not that ordinary life is free of institutional design, but that it contains **more underdetermined meaning** than a special examination.

## 21.2 The volume sometimes rewards coercive paternalism

Kei's pool scene is staged as comic and ultimately joyful. Horikita's attendance and other social participation are also sometimes produced through pressure or leverage.

The narrative's warmth can make the successful emotional result feel like justification.

It is not.

A later laugh does not erase an earlier refusal.

## 21.3 Operation Delta is ethically worse than the earlier summary allowed

The earlier analysis correctly emphasized that Ayanokōji destroys the recording and prevents long-term possession of the girls' images. It understated his complicity in allowing the camera to reach the changing room and begin recording.

The source supports a harsher formulation:

> **He chooses controlled participation in a violation because he believes direct opposition may fail.**

That is strategically plausible and morally compromised.

## 21.4 The perpetrators avoid meaningful accountability

Ike, Yamauchi, and Sudō learn that their technical plan “failed,” not that their sexual entitlement was wrong. The covert solution protects the class from scandal but preserves the moral ignorance that produced the problem.

This resembles a broader Ayanokōji tendency: solving the dangerous outcome can take priority over creating transparent understanding.

## 21.5 Fanservice complicates the privacy ethic

The narrative condemns unauthorized observation while the publication openly invites reader observation of the same bodies. The distinction between fictional nonconsensual surveillance and commercial illustration is real, but the coexistence creates an unresolved formal tension.

## 21.6 The gift-smuggling plan violates rules for a sympathetic reason

Katsuragi's motive is compelling. The school's security restriction is also intelligible. Ayanokōji's solution again prefers effective circumvention to transparent challenge or reform.

The story therefore risks making clever rule-evasion look morally self-validating whenever the motive is sympathetic.

## 21.7 Ayanokōji's anti-bullying instruction is hypocritical

He correctly tells Kei that avoiding victimhood does not justify becoming an aggressor. He does not apply the same principle to his own treatment of her in Volume 4.

That contradiction should remain central to any later ethics synthesis.

# 25. Cumulative delta after Volume 4.5

Volume 4.5 changes the Year 1 model less by introducing a new formal institution than by exposing the inadequacy of interpreting the school only through its formal examinations. The volume repeatedly places abilities, vulnerabilities, and relationships already visible under competitive pressure into situations where no class point is awarded and no official evaluator has specified what success means.

That shift matters because the previous four volumes could encourage a deterministic reading of Ayanokōji and of Advanced Nurturing High School alike. If every consequential scene is an exam, crisis, disciplinary case, or strategic contest, then human development can appear synonymous with adaptation to pressure. Volume 4.5 supplies a second developmental environment: ordinary life.

The result is not an ethically purified summer. Strategy, leverage, concealment, coercion, and surveillance all remain present. The difference is that the volume repeatedly shows characters producing values that the school did not assign in advance.

## 25.1 Ayanokōji delta

The most important development is the increased precision with which the text separates Ayanokōji's **unfinished ordinary self** from both a simple performance and a completed hidden personality.

His language matters:

> `オレという個体、人間は正直言って『生まれたばかり』だ。`
>
> `まだ液状。固形化するには時間がかかる。`

At this endpoint, the most responsible local reading is not that a normal boy exists underneath the strategist and is finally emerging. Nor is it that the normal boy is wholly false.

Instead:

> **Ayanokōji possesses an already-developed strategic architecture and an incompletely developed ordinary social identity.**

That distinction explains several apparently contradictory behaviors within the same volume.

He can:

- be genuinely happy when he believes Sudō wants to spend time with him;
- feel disappointed when that invitation turns out to be instrumental;
- study his own inability to make friends;
- enjoy purposeless conversation with Airi;
- call Yamauchi his friend;
- be surprised by the taste of ordinary soft serve;
- care that Kei gets an opportunity to enjoy herself;
- and decline an available opportunity to collect leverage on Katsuragi.

At the same time, he can:

- contemplate which D-class student might eventually be unnecessary enough to expel;
- preserve information asymmetry with Horikita and Kei;
- order Kei into a swimsuit situation she initially refuses;
- use embarrassment as leverage against Horikita;
- and knowingly participate in Operation Delta long enough for unauthorized surveillance and recording to begin before he destroys the resulting data.

The strongest cumulative revision is therefore:

> **Humanization and ethical improvement are not synonyms.**

Volume 4.5 gives much stronger evidence that Ayanokōji is developing ordinary preferences, attachments, and autobiographical self-awareness. It gives much weaker evidence that those developments have yet reorganized the ethics of his strategic conduct.

A second important delta is that Ayanokōji demonstrates more than one mode of intervention.

Across the volume he can:

1. **redirect without dominating** — giving Ibuki something to do in the elevator while privately doubting the method;
2. **support autonomous choice** — telling Airi that the romantic decision is hers and remaining nearby when she asks him not to leave;
3. **circumvent institutions for another person's private good** — helping Katsuragi send the birthday gift;
4. **decline available leverage** — refusing to turn Katsuragi's suspicious behavior into a future weapon;
5. **coordinate covert protection through command** — using Kei to sabotage Operation Delta;
6. **coerce toward a beneficial experience** — ordering and physically pushing Kei into the pool despite her refusal.

This is analytically important. Ayanokōji is not incapable of low-coercion assistance. He already possesses alternatives. The ethical question is increasingly why he selects one mode rather than another.

## 25.2 Horikita and class-leadership delta

Horikita's development in Volume 4.5 is deliberately small-scale and therefore valuable.

No special examination forces her to cooperate. No class-point emergency compels her to reconsider a strategy. Instead, mundane incapacity exposes the practical limits of her preferred self-sufficiency.

The bottle episode produces a sequence that would have been difficult for early Volume 1 Horikita:

> private problem → failed solitary solution → indirect request for help → assistance from more than one person → explicit gratitude.

Her `……ありがとう` is ordinary language. Precisely for that reason it matters. It is one of the first moments in which dependence is not immediately redescribed as weakness, debt, or strategic necessity.

The pool outing adds a second revision. Horikita admits:

> `今日は意外と楽しかったわ。たまにはこんな休みがあるのも悪くないわね`

The important word is `意外と`—unexpectedly. She did not begin with an argument that leisure would be useful. Experience changes her evaluation.

This strengthens the developing distinction between:

- independence;
- isolation;
- dependence;
- and interdependence.

Horikita is not yet a mature collaborative leader. She remains highly competitive, continues to interpret class placement through her own self-evaluation, and is still inclined to treat internal betrayal primarily as someone else's moral failure. Ayanokōji's criticism that a leader must account for betrayal as part of the environment is strategically sound even if it risks assigning impossible total responsibility.

The Volume 4.5 endpoint is therefore modest:

> **Horikita has begun discovering that accepting help and enjoying company need not invalidate autonomy.**

That is a more durable foundation for leadership than being temporarily forced to cooperate by an exam.

## 25.3 Relationship delta

The volume clarifies at least three distinct relational structures.

### Ayanokōji and Airi: low-pressure reciprocity

Airi's romantic dilemma provides one of the cleanest examples so far of Ayanokōji refusing authorship over another person's intimate decision.

He offers interpretation.
He rejects the idea that Airi lacks the right to refuse.
He does not select the answer for her.
When she asks him not to leave her alone, he stays.

This is dependence in a limited sense—Airi wants emotional support—but it is not the same architecture as Kei's Volume 4 dependency. Her safety and permission to decide are not made conditional on obedience.

That distinction should remain available for later ethical comparison.

### Ayanokōji and Kei: usefulness becomes familiarity, but asymmetry persists

Kei is increasingly useful as a covert operator because she can mobilize girls without revealing Ayanokōji's role. Her successful sabotage of Operation Delta demonstrates genuine competence rather than passive obedience.

Her private conversation with Ayanokōji also becomes more psychologically reciprocal. She challenges him about who he is, hears the `生まれたばかり` / `液状` account, and exposes her own theory of the `偽りの強者`.

Yet the relationship remains structurally unequal.

Ayanokōji can:

- assign tasks;
- conceal motives;
- invoke the language of command;
- and determine what information Kei receives.

Her operational agency therefore exists **inside** a relationship whose terms he still controls to a much greater degree.

### Cross-class contact: rivalry does not exhaust personhood

Ibuki and Katsuragi receive ordinary-life characterization outside their competitive class functions.

Ibuki can be:

- anxious;
- curious;
- polite to a fortune-teller;
- embarrassed by social difficulty;
- and capable of accepting cross-class aid.

Katsuragi can be:

- worried about his sister;
- willing to violate school rules for family care;
- grateful;
- and open to limited friendship across formal class lines.

This matters politically because the class system is not total. Students can form relationships that do not map perfectly onto institutional allegiance.

## 25.4 Institutional delta

Volume 4.5 exposes the school through its absences.

There is no major official examination, yet institutional design remains everywhere:

- outside contact is tightly restricted;
- family gifts require covert circumvention;
- elevators are monitored;
- the student council possesses confidential information;
- private points continue shaping decisions;
- club participation becomes materially valuable;
- class placement remains a disputed institutional judgment;
- and student-council succession threatens to alter the school's political order.

The result is important for the larger ANHS analysis:

> **Ordinary life at Advanced Nurturing High School is not outside the institution. It occurs inside an unusually enclosed environment whose rules continue shaping which ordinary relationships are easy, difficult, or illicit.**

Katsuragi's birthday gift is the clearest example. The act is mundane and humane. The school makes it a security problem.

Ayanokōji's opposite reaction to the same isolation is equally important. What Katsuragi experiences as deprivation can feel like protection to someone who values being unreachable from the outside.

The institution therefore does not impose one meaning on isolation. It creates a condition whose value depends heavily on biography.

## 25.5 Ethics and autonomy delta

The volume strengthens **interdependence** as a third category between isolation and dependency.

Several earlier relationships risked presenting the choice as:

> stand alone, or attach yourself to someone stronger.

Volume 4.5 supplies other possibilities.

Horikita can ask for help without surrendering herself.
Airi can ask Ayanokōji to stay without giving him authority over her romantic answer.
Katsuragi can accept assistance across class lines without becoming subordinate to Class D.
Ibuki can cooperate in an emergency without changing allegiance.

These scenes are ethically significant because they show that vulnerability need not automatically generate ownership.

At the same time, two major cases prevent the volume from becoming a simple celebration of benign ordinary life.

### Kei at the pool

Ayanokōji has a plausible benevolent motive: he wants Kei to enjoy herself and prevent her from retreating into a purely defensive social posture.

But she refuses.
He answers `命令だ`.
He physically pushes her into the water.
She later laughs and participates genuinely.

The correct ethical reading must preserve both facts:

> **the enjoyment is genuine; the initial coercion is also genuine.**

A positive result does not retroactively create consent.

### Operation Delta

The second-pass correction is more substantial.

The earlier analysis sometimes described Ayanokōji as simply preventing covert sexual recording. That formulation is too clean.

He correctly identifies the plan as criminal and recognizes the risk of expulsion. He also predicts the boys may attempt it secretly if he openly refuses. He therefore joins the operation and sabotages it from within through Kei.

But the camera does reach the women's changing room.
Live images appear.
Recording begins.

Only afterward does Kei remove the active memory card and substitute a blank one, allowing the permanent data to be destroyed.

The most precise ethical formulation is:

> **Ayanokōji prevents durable possession and distribution of the footage, but he knowingly permits the immediate privacy violation to occur as part of the sabotage plan.**

This is protective in outcome and complicit in method.

The boys also evade accountability. They believe the device simply failed. They do not have to confront the violation they attempted.

The episode therefore becomes a useful early example of one of Ayanokōji's recurrent ethical tendencies:

> prefer covert control of the outcome over transparent confrontation with the people producing the harm.

## 25.6 Ability and merit delta

Volume 4.5 broadens the developing ability framework precisely because formal evaluation recedes.

### Ibuki

Ayanokōji's distinction between possessing communication ability and being able to use it under social pressure is one of the volume's clearest statements of **socially usable ability**.

A person may understand conversation and still become functionally impaired by anticipated judgment.

### Airi

Her ease in text chat compared with face-to-face conversation demonstrates that ability can be **medium-dependent**.

The person has not changed.
The environment has.

### Sudō

Basketball is becoming more than an abstract future dream. Club participation begins generating institutional and social value. His athletic capacity can be translated into recognizable performance.

### Kei

Her conventional academic or physical profile would not explain her value in Operation Delta. Her important capacity is social deployment:

- timing;
- secrecy;
- mobilization;
- group cover;
- and understanding how girls will move through the space.

### Kushida

The birthday list and her management of Ike's mockery demonstrate relational memory and conflict mediation as forms of political ability.

### Kōenji

His presence continues to raise the inverse problem: enormous possessed ability may remain unavailable as class power if the individual refuses collective claims on it.

The cumulative conclusion is becoming stronger:

> **`実力` is not merely what a person can do. It is also what can become available, legible, and usable within a particular human environment.**

## 25.7 Open-question delta

Several earlier questions are now sharpened rather than answered.

Ayanokōji's ordinary desires are increasingly difficult to dismiss as camouflage, but it remains unclear whether they can become strong enough to constrain his strategic habits.

Horikita can accept help privately, but it remains unclear whether she can transform that experience into distributed public leadership.

Kei is already more than the `寄生虫` model through which she explains herself, but her safety remains attached to an asymmetrical protector.

The student council is becoming more important, but the incoming political order remains undefined.

Most importantly, the volume demonstrates that Ayanokōji already knows how to help someone without completely authoring their choice.

That changes the future ethical question from:

> Can he learn a less controlling method?

to:

> **When he already possesses less controlling methods, under what conditions does he choose them?**

---

# 26. Open questions at the Volume 4.5 endpoint

These questions are intentionally restricted to what remains unresolved after Volume 4.5.

1. **Can Ayanokōji's emerging social self become more stable without his strategic self determining what that stability is allowed to look like?**

2. **Can Horikita move beyond accepting emergency assistance and develop a model of leadership in which other people's independent strengths are deliberately incorporated rather than merely tolerated?**

3. **Can Kei become stronger in a way that reduces rather than deepens the structural necessity of attaching herself to Ayanokōji's protection?**

4. **How durable is Ayanokōji's statement that D class will not lose a student through expulsion, given that he simultaneously contemplates which students might be unnecessary enough to remove?**

5. **Why is Hirata, whose visible academic, social, and leadership abilities appear strong, assigned to Class D?**

6. **What changes if Ayanokōji's concealed physical, academic, or strategic ability becomes publicly legible rather than privately suspected by a few students?**

7. **What political changes will follow the coming student-council succession that Manabu warns Ayanokōji about?**

8. **Can Horikita revise her belief that her own Class D placement is effectively a mistake, or will she continue to treat institutional judgment as accurate for others but mistaken in her own case?**

9. **Can Kushida's extraordinary social infrastructure be integrated into Class D without allowing the private hostility beneath it to become an existential threat to the class?**

10. **Can ordinary cross-class relationships such as Ayanokōji–Ibuki or the Katsuragi collaboration survive when formal class competition again creates direct conflicts of interest?**

11. **Will Ayanokōji increasingly choose low-coercion support like the form he provides Airi, or will crisis continue pushing him toward manufactured dependency and command?**

12. **When Ayanokōji destroys leverage he could have retained, as with the memory card, is that best understood as moral restraint, strategic preference, protection of class stability, or an unstable combination of all three?**

13. **What does Ayanokōji's description of himself as `真っ白` ultimately mean if he is simultaneously accumulating preferences, friendships, memories, and forms of self-description that make him progressively less blank?**

14. **Does Manabu's claim that class assignment is not a simple descending ranking of raw ability imply that Class D is specifically organized around defects of social conversion, character, cooperation, or some broader institutional criterion?**

15. **Can ordinary experience change a character more durably than an examination does precisely because the result is not immediately translated into points, rank, or victory?**

---

# 27. Final synthesis

Volume 4.5 is easy to underestimate because almost nothing in it resembles the conventional machinery of *Classroom of the Elite* at maximum intensity.

There is no major special examination.
No class is expelled from contention.
No single tactical victory reorganizes the ranking.
No central antagonist is defeated.

Instead, the volume gives us:

- a fortune-telling booth;
- a stalled elevator;
- a birthday present;
- a hand trapped in a bottle;
- an awkward confession;
- a pool;
- soft serve;
- and a deeply questionable changing-room plot.

Those small objects and incidents are precisely why the volume matters.

The first four volumes repeatedly ask what people do when someone else defines the stakes.

The school defines academic survival.
The disciplinary process defines credible truth.
The island exam defines resource scarcity.
The zodiac exam defines trust as a competitive commodity.
Ayanokōji increasingly learns to define other people's decision environments himself.

Volume 4.5 asks a different question:

> **What happens when no official examiner has told the students what the experience is for?**

The answer is not that strategy disappears.

Ayanokōji still reads incentives.
He still conceals ability.
He still accumulates information.
He still thinks about future expulsion.
He still pressures Kei and Horikita.
He still prefers covert control to open confrontation in Operation Delta.

But ordinary life creates spaces in which strategy is no longer the only available criterion of value.

A birthday gift matters because Katsuragi loves his sister.

A conversation matters because Airi does not want to face a frightening decision alone.

Friendship matters because Ayanokōji wants someone to invite him out for himself rather than as an intermediary.

Soft serve matters because it tastes good.

A pool outing matters because Horikita discovers that an afternoon can be enjoyable without increasing class points.

These are not trivial exceptions to the serious story.

They identify what the serious story is supposedly trying to preserve.

This gives Volume 4.5 a crucial place in Ayanokōji's development.

After Volume 4, it would be easy to treat his ordinary adolescent narration as merely camouflage surrounding the true strategic machine. Volume 4.5 makes that reduction increasingly untenable.

He is lonely.
He wants friendship.
He is inexperienced with gifts.
He studies his own social failures.
He experiences attraction.
He enjoys food.
He notices when someone has become more confident.
He can care about whether another person enjoys herself.
He can refuse to collect leverage.
He can support Airi's right to say no.

And yet the person possessing all of those traits can still command, manipulate, conceal, and participate in ethically compromised methods.

The volume therefore does not reveal a warm “real Ayanokōji” beneath a cold false one.

Its more challenging proposition is the one Ayanokōji himself supplies:

> `生まれたばかり`
>
> `まだ液状`

His ordinary social self is unfinished.

That unfinished state explains why contradictory relational models coexist around him.

With Airi, he can help without deciding.

With Horikita, he can protect dignity while also using embarrassment as leverage.

With Katsuragi, he can assist while consciously declining a potential weapon.

With Kei, he can recognize trauma and create genuine enjoyment while still relying on command.

With Operation Delta, he can protect the girls from permanent exploitation while allowing an immediate violation to occur because covert sabotage seems strategically cleaner than transparent prevention.

Ayanokōji is not choosing between humanity and strategy.

He is learning what forms of relationship are possible while still possessing a strategic grammar that often treats superior understanding as permission to control the environment.

That is why the volume's strongest positive model is not independence and not dependency.

It is **interdependence**.

Horikita asks for help and remains Horikita.
Airi asks someone to stay and keeps ownership of her answer.
Ibuki accepts cross-class aid without surrendering allegiance.
Katsuragi collaborates with rivals for a family purpose no class system can fully absorb.

These relationships suggest that vulnerability does not logically require ownership.

They therefore provide an implicit alternative to both:

- Horikita's early isolation;
- and Kei's trauma-shaped belief that survival requires attachment to a stronger protector.

The school itself remains ambivalent.

Advanced Nurturing High School gives Ayanokōji something the life he hints at before enrollment apparently lacked: time, peers, leisure, privacy, tastes, friendship, embarrassment, and the possibility of acquiring memories that have no obvious instrumental purpose.

At the same time, it encloses those experiences inside surveillance, point economies, class hierarchy, outside-contact restrictions, and an emerging student-council power struggle.

Ordinary life is therefore not outside the experiment.

It is the part of the environment whose outcomes are least completely specified in advance.

That difference may be enough to matter.

The second-pass correction to Operation Delta makes the volume's ethical architecture especially clear. Ayanokōji is capable of producing a protective outcome without choosing the cleanest protective method. His competence lets him control the end state. It does not automatically give him moral authority over what others must endure on the way there.

That same distinction will remain necessary whenever the series presents developmental success as evidence of strategic brilliance.

A person may become happier.
A class may become stronger.
A threat may be neutralized.
A recording may be destroyed.

None of those outcomes alone answers whether the route respected the people whose lives supplied the variables.

Volume 4.5 therefore deepens rather than interrupts the series' central problem.

Special examinations show what students can do when someone else defines success.

> **Volume 4.5 shows who they can begin to become when success is not predefined.**

For Ayanokōji, that distinction may be more important than any exam result so far.

The strategic boy already knows how to make other people move.

The unfinished ordinary boy is beginning to discover why some experiences matter even when nobody wins them from anyone else.

The next question is whether those experiences can eventually teach him something strategy alone cannot:

> **how to enter a relationship in which the other person's freedom is not merely a condition to model, but a source of change he permits to act upon himself.**
