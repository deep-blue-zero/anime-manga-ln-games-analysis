# 『ようこそ実力至上主義の教室へ』
## Year 1 — Institutions, Surveillance, Adult Power, and the White Room
### A plural experimental school under capture: enclosure, oversight, and the contest over who may author a student

> **CANONICAL YEAR-1 SPECIALIST SNAPSHOT**
>
> This document is the primary analytical home for Advanced Nurturing High School as an institution, its surveillance and record systems, teachers and student-council actors, adult administrative power, Atsuomi’s ownership claim, and the Year 1 evidence concerning the White Room. It synthesizes the fourteen canonical Japanese novel deep readings, the *First File* paratext audit, the final Year 1 evidence lock, the canonical institution/examination and class-polity ledgers, the Japanese terminology index, and the frozen `Y1_01–Y1_06` specialist layer.
>
> It does **not** import Year 2, Volume 0, *Second List*, Year 3, adaptation material, wikis, or later-franchise answers. The detailed White Room statements made by Tsukishiro remain testimony where Year 1 itself withholds independent verification.

---

# 0. Scope, primary home, and interpretive controls

Year 1 repeatedly invites the reader to speak about “the school” as if Advanced Nurturing High School possessed one coherent will.

The novels ultimately make that description inadequate.

ANHS is simultaneously:

- a physically enclosed campus;
- a prestigious educational institution;
- a market organized through private and class points;
- a surveillance environment;
- a laboratory of special examinations;
- a bureaucracy of teachers, administrators, and records;
- a political arena partially governed by students;
- a refuge from external family power;
- a producer of friendships, romance, leisure, and ordinary adolescent time;
- and an institution whose rules and records can be captured by a hostile acting chairman.

The institution can protect and endanger the same student.

Different actors can use the same infrastructure for incompatible purposes.

The analytical task is therefore not to decide whether ANHS is simply “good,” “evil,” “fair,” “corrupt,” “meritocratic,” or “another White Room.” It is to identify **which layer of the institution is acting, what authority it possesses, what evidence supports the claim, and whether that authority is answerable to anyone else**.

This document applies eight controls.

## 0.1 ANHS is not one actor

The institution contains competing authorities.

Chairman Sakayanagi admits Ayanokōji and protects his choice to remain. Chabashira uses real information to manufacture a false expulsion threat. Mashima challenges deliberately engineered expulsion and later accepts personal cost to protect students. Hoshinomiya supplies information that enters Nagumo’s leverage structure. Manabu and Nagumo propose different student-council orders. Tsukishiro uses acting-chairman authority to falsify a communication record. Teachers later organize an oversight network against that interference (`Y1V02-E016`; `Y1V07-E030–E033`; `Y1V11-E016–E025`; `Y1V11.5-E024; Y1V11.5-E034–E036`).

Claims about “the school” must therefore be decomposed into:

- architecture;
- office;
- officeholder;
- implementation;
- infrastructure;
- and contestation.

## 0.2 Institutional capacity is not institutional legitimacy

The power to observe, rank, punish, expel, rescue, or alter a record establishes capacity.

It does not establish moral or procedural right.

Tsukishiro’s intervention is effective because the communication system obeys him. Atsuomi’s retaliatory power is frightening because it may reach people outside the immediate conflict. Chabashira’s lie is persuasive because she possesses authentic background information. Nagumo’s council power is consequential because trust, money, access, and rule knowledge are concentrated around him.

None of these facts answers whether the use is legitimate.

## 0.3 Institutional design and institutional capture must remain distinct

Year 1 contains harsh rules that appear to be part of ordinary ANHS design:

- concealed early evaluation;
- class hierarchy;
- expulsion thresholds;
- priced rescue;
- anonymous voting;
- concentrated leader liability;
- and point-based mobility.

It also contains evidence of **capture**:

- an adult mandate to remove one student;
- manipulated surveillance;
- intercepted communication;
- and replacement of an intended chess command (`Y1V10-E109–E111`; `Y1V11-E138–E145`).

The ordinary system may be ethically objectionable without every objection depending on capture. Conversely, Tsukishiro’s corruption cannot be excused as merely another difficult examination.

## 0.4 Surveillance is not one practice

Year 1 uses cameras, phones, tails, rumors, recordings, testimony, anonymous boards, and official files for different purposes.

Surveillance can:

- deter violence;
- create admissible evidence;
- invade privacy;
- preserve privacy when selectively blocked;
- help a predator or political actor map institutional blind spots;
- convert another person’s attention into usable counter-surveillance;
- or allow an administrator to decide which recording becomes official reality.

The analysis must distinguish **observation**, **documentation**, **interpretation**, and **record control**.

## 0.5 White Room evidence requires explicit tiers

Year 1 contains several different evidentiary forms:

1. Ayanokōji’s direct first-person account of his experience.
2. Sakayanagi’s childhood memory of visiting the facility.
3. statements made by her father during that visit.
4. Atsuomi’s self-interested account of his project and retaliation.
5. Tsukishiro’s adversarial exposition.
6. *First File* data and editorial framing.

These sources do not have identical authority.

The detailed generation history, failure structure, and incoming-student claim supplied by Tsukishiro remain unresolved testimony because Ayanokōji explicitly warns that Tsukishiro can make complete fiction sound true (`Y1V11.5-E111–E116`). *First File* may strengthen the franchise’s broad official framing without transforming every fine-grained statement into omniscient fact (`Y1FF-E008`, `Y1FF-E055`).

## 0.6 Effectiveness does not retroactively justify deprivation

The White Room is described as extraordinarily effective at producing competence. Ayanokōji does not deny that it taught him academic, martial, and survival capacities (`Y1V07-E027`, `Y1V07-E040`).

That effectiveness does not settle whether:

- the child consented;
- failure was survivable;
- ordinary life could legitimately be removed;
- the creator acquired ownership;
- or the institution was entitled to define the whole purpose of development.

The same control applies to ANHS. A harsh examination may generate real growth without every imposed risk becoming justified by the growth.

## 0.7 Ordinary life is institutional evidence

Meals, dates, gifts, friendship groups, books, private calls, holidays, sports, embarrassment, and boredom are not analytically irrelevant because they are unscored.

They show what ANHS makes possible that the White Room did not. They also show that the meaning of the school exceeds its official educational design.

This ordinary-life surplus is one of the strongest reasons ANHS cannot be reduced to a White Room duplicate.

## 0.8 The Year 1 boundary is binding

The document may state what Year 1 establishes, what it strongly implies, and what it leaves unresolved.

It may not use later works to settle:

- the full White Room chronology;
- the exact structure of its generations;
- the identity or reliability of later White Room students;
- national political relationships behind the project;
- later limits on Tsukishiro;
- or ANHS’s mature institutional purpose.

Those questions belong to later retrospective documents.

# 1. Executive thesis

The strongest Year 1 institutional reading is:

> **Advanced Nurturing High School is neither the White Room’s humane opposite nor merely the same prison with classmates. It is a pluralized, marketized, surveilled developmental institution that gives students genuine space for ordinary life and reciprocal formation while retaining a dangerous experimental grammar: enclosure, hidden criteria, authored scarcity, ranking, removal, and adult control over the conditions and records of performance. Its ethical advantage lies less in benevolence than in incompleteness—no single actor fully controls the student, relationships exceed official design, teachers and students can resist, and power can sometimes be made answerable. Tsukishiro demonstrates how contingent those safeguards are.**

The White Room begins from concentrated authorship.

An adult project defines:

- the child’s environment;
- the curriculum;
- the relevant capacities;
- the pace of development;
- the meaning of success;
- and the future for which the developed person supposedly exists.

Atsuomi then converts causal authorship into a claim of ownership:

> `おまえは俺の所有物だ。`

The sentence is the institutional ideology in its most explicit form. The creator of the developmental environment claims jurisdiction over the developed person (`Y1V07-E023`).

ANHS distributes authorship far more widely.

Teachers, examinations, class leaders, student-council actors, peers, rivals, friends, and the students themselves all shape development. The school still encloses and observes them, but it cannot completely determine what they become. Ike’s practical knowledge, Sudō’s discipline, Horikita’s leadership, Kei’s agency, Hirata’s care, Ryūen’s post-defeat loyalty network, Ichinose’s solidarity, and Ayanokōji’s ordinary-life desire all emerge through relations that no single administrator wholly controls.

This pluralism is morally important.

It is not secure.

The school’s ordinary rules already contain severe coercion. It hides the first evaluative criteria, converts class life into a point economy, makes expulsion a recurring possibility, and eventually creates a vote because no first-year student has yet been removed (`Y1V01-E005`, `Y1V01-E011`; `Y1V10-E013–E019`). Chabashira’s defense of the Class Vote translates manufactured scarcity into “real-world” leadership training. Tsukishiro goes further. He treats the first-year cohort as experimental material, naturalizes designed exclusion, and then alters the channel through which the chess result enters the institutional record (`Y1V10-E065`; `Y1V11-E016–E025`, `Y1V11-E140–E145`).

Year 1 therefore progresses through three scales of institutional authorship:

1. **environmental authorship** — rules decide which capacities and behaviors become relevant;
2. **political authorship** — teachers, councils, classes, and strategists shape how those rules are inhabited;
3. **record authorship** — administrators can decide what officially happened.

The third scale is the most dangerous because it can make the first two politically unreal.

A sophisticated examination, authentic effort, and superior decision do not establish meritocratic legitimacy if the record layer is corrupt.

The school’s strongest internal answer appears in Volume 11.5. Ayanokōji does not demand private exemption. He asks for adults willing to observe, recognize interference, and preserve legitimate defense. Chairman Sakayanagi recommends Chabashira and Mashima. Mashima’s earlier judo decision demonstrates a teacher accepting punishment rather than permit formal procedure to expose a student to serious avoidable injury (`Y1V11.5-E017–E024; Y1V11.5-E034–E036`).

That coalition does not solve the problem.

It establishes the Year 1 institutional ideal:

> **power should be divided enough that no officeholder can convert a private objective into unquestionable educational reality; consequential judgments should be visible, contestable, correctable, proportionate, and honestly recorded.**

The final Year 1 question is therefore not simply whether ANHS is better than the White Room.

It is:

> **Can an institution cultivate exceptional ability without claiming ownership of the people it cultivates—and what checks are required when the same institution can define the test, observe the subject, punish failure, and write the official record?**

# 2. The Year 1 institutional map

ANHS is best understood as a layered order rather than one vertical chain.

| Layer | Principal actors or mechanisms | Form of power | Year 1 vulnerability |
|---|---|---|---|
| **External developmental sovereignty** | Atsuomi; White Room | authors childhood environment and claims the resulting person | concentrated purpose becomes ownership |
| **Formal school administration** | Chairman Sakayanagi; Tsukishiro; school rules and infrastructure | admission, policy, surveillance access, record authority | office can protect autonomy or capture procedure |
| **Teaching and implementation** | Chabashira, Mashima, Hoshinomiya, other homeroom teachers | rule explanation, discretion, testimony, discipline, advocacy | private motives and incentives contaminate pedagogy |
| **Student quasi-government** | Manabu, Nagumo, student council, Tachibana, Kiriyama | school culture, council access, rule pressure, exam influence | public office can become personal patronage or rivalry apparatus |
| **Class constitutions** | Horikita, Ryūen, Ichinose, Sakayanagi and peers | local coordination, discipline, information, rescue, sacrifice | students reproduce coercive or proprietary institutions internally |
| **Surveillance and record layer** | cameras, phones, recordings, anonymous boards, witnesses, official result systems | makes conduct legible and actionable | observation can be manipulated, incomplete, or selectively preserved |
| **Counter-curriculum** | friendship, leisure, romance, books, meals, ordinary time | generates ends and identities outside official scoring | remains vulnerable to intrusion and instrumentalization |

The layers interact.

Chabashira can exploit background information obtained through the adult institution to coerce a student for class advancement. Nagumo can use council access, private-point networks, and upper-year trust to design political operations inside an examination. Ayanokōji can exploit peer surveillance to author a false causal story. Tsukishiro can bypass all of them by controlling infrastructure.

The map also shows why “school versus student” is too simple.

Students repeatedly become institutional actors.

Teachers sometimes become dissidents.

A chairman can act as refuge.

A student can reproduce White Room logic inside a nominally freer environment.

The institutional problem is not located only in age or office. It appears wherever one actor’s superior knowledge and control cease to be answerable to the person being shaped.

# 3. Entry into ANHS: freedom inside enclosure

Ayanokōji experiences ANHS first as release.

The dormitory separates him from his father. The campus prevents ordinary outside contact. The same enclosure Katsuragi later names as a major disadvantage is precisely what makes the institution attractive to Ayanokōji (`Y1V01-E006–E007`; `Y1V04.5-E023`).

This creates the first institutional paradox:

> **A boundary can imprison one student and protect another.**

The school’s isolation is not neutral. Students cannot freely contact the outside world. Goods, information, and mobility are mediated through the campus economy and school-approved channels. Yet the boundary also limits Atsuomi’s immediate reach and creates a jurisdiction within which Chairman Sakayanagi can defend Ayanokōji’s expressed choice to remain (`Y1V07-E028–E032`).

The campus therefore functions as both:

- **enclosure** — reducing ordinary exit and external contact;
- **asylum** — obstructing an external claimant who denies the student’s autonomy.

This dual character matters for every later comparison with the White Room.

The White Room’s enclosure serves the institution’s total developmental purpose.

ANHS’s enclosure also creates space for purposes the institution did not assign:

- making friends;
- reading for pleasure;
- eating preferred food;
- dating;
- giving gifts;
- joining clubs;
- forming rivalries;
- or simply wasting time.

Ayanokōji’s later description of ordinary school life as his first self-chosen project is possible only because the enclosure does not exhaustively control the meaning of life within it (`Y1V11.5-E006; Y1V11.5-E021–E026`; `Y1FF-E059–E061; Y1FF-E066–E073`).

## 3.1 Freedom is initially marketized

The opening allocation of 100,000 private points presents the school as radically permissive. Students can purchase comfort and pleasure with little immediate adult interference (`Y1V01-E005`).

The zero-point reveal retroactively transforms that freedom into data.

The students were not simply free.

They were being judged while ignorant of the criteria (`Y1V01-E011`).

This is not a minor orientation trick. It establishes the school’s foundational paternalism:

> the institution withholds the meaning of the environment so that unguarded behavior becomes more diagnostically useful.

The resulting data may be real. The procedure remains ethically asymmetric because only the evaluator understands that the period is an examination.

## 3.2 Ayanokōji authors himself inside the enclosure

The school’s observation regime is not omniscient.

Ayanokōji deliberately produces mediocre entrance scores and a mediocre swim time. His official record accurately preserves the output he supplied and radically misrepresents his latent capacity (`Y1V01-E008`, `Y1V01-E013`; `Y1FF-E004–E005`).

This is an important institutional limit.

A surveillance system can record conduct faithfully and still be deceived about the person when the subject understands the incentives and controls expression.

The school does not merely inspect students.

Students inspect the school, infer what is being measured, and adapt the self made visible.

# 4. Hidden evaluation and the pedagogy of surprise

ANHS repeatedly uses incomplete disclosure as an educational technology.

The institution appears to assume that students reveal more when they do not know the full evaluative object. Year 1 examples include:

- the concealed opening-month criteria;
- delayed or strategically timed rule disclosure;
- examination structures whose most consequential interaction is discovered only after students inhabit them;
- and special exams that force classes to infer what the school is actually testing.

The strongest defense of this method is practical.

If students receive every criterion in advance, they may perform compliance rather than reveal ordinary judgment. Surprise can expose:

- irresponsibility;
- adaptability;
- ability to infer unstated incentives;
- and the consequences of collective behavior.

The criticism is not that surprise produces no information.

It is that the institution can convert its own opacity into evidence of student deficiency.

A student who fails to infer an unstated rule is judged within an environment whose asymmetry the institution itself created.

That problem intensifies when the consequence is not merely feedback but:

- class-point loss;
- financial deprivation;
- exposure to violence;
- or expulsion.

## 4.1 Hidden criteria teach institutional suspicion

The students rapidly learn that every apparent freedom may conceal another test.

This produces competence.

It also produces a culture in which:

- rules are mined for loopholes;
- neutral gestures are treated as possible traps;
- friendship may be evaluated as information risk;
- and administrators are assumed to be withholding decisive conditions.

The school therefore teaches more than adaptability.

It teaches **epistemic distrust**.

That distrust is sometimes rational. Chabashira’s deception, Nagumo’s engineered examination field, and Tsukishiro’s record manipulation reward students who refuse to accept the official surface (`Y1V03-E029–E031; Y1V03-E050`; `Y1V08-E130–E142`; `Y1V11-E140–E145`).

## 4.2 The educational object remains unstable

A special examination may officially test one set of capacities while actually rewarding another.

The zodiac examination is called a test of `シンキング`, yet success depends on distrust management, identity inference, class loyalty, and willingness to betray (`Y1V04-E010–E015`). The Mixed Training Camp officially evaluates morality, discipline, order, initiative, academics, and physical ability while Nagumo’s real political victory comes from trust expenditure, financing, testimony, and depletion of another class’s future insurance (`Y1V08-E121–E128; Y1V08-E130–E142`).

The institution’s stated lesson and the environment’s operative lesson need not coincide.

That divergence is one reason Year 1 never permits “the exam result” to become a transparent statement of total merit.

# 5. Points and marketized government

`Y1_06` is the primary home for the political economy of private and class points. The institutional question here is narrower:

> **What kind of authority is created when rules, mobility, rescue, and continued membership can be purchased?**

The point system decentralizes some power.

Students can use resources to:

- buy past examinations;
- purchase the single point needed to prevent Sudō’s expulsion;
- negotiate information;
- pool insurance;
- finance rescue;
- or theoretically purchase class transfer (`Y1V01-E022–E029`; `Y1V02-E009–E010`).

This can protect autonomy against arbitrary authority.

Ayanokōji and Horikita do not need a teacher’s mercy to preserve Sudō. They find a price inside the rules. Ichinose’s class can convert solidarity into a collective rescue fund. A class with sufficient capital can preserve a member whom the institution has marked for removal.

The same structure creates inequality before the rule is applied.

A formally uniform rescue price has radically different meaning for:

- a wealthy individual;
- a class with pooled savings;
- a class with no liquidity;
- or a student whom another leader wants to acquire through a conditional loan.

The rule says everyone may buy rescue.

The economy decides who actually can.

## 5.1 Rule flexibility can become rule opacity

Chabashira confirms that points may alter school rules, but the full menu of purchasable exceptions is not transparently published (`Y1V04-E034`). This makes institutional literacy itself a form of power.

Students who know or infer that rules are negotiable possess options invisible to classmates who treat the written rule as final.

The result resembles a market governed partly by precedent and privileged information rather than an equal public code.

## 5.2 Mobility can liberate or create patronage

The twenty-million-point class-transfer mechanism offers a theoretical route beyond inherited class fate. Nagumo’s political project also promises stronger individuals greater mobility (`Y1V02-E009`; `Y1V07.5-E074–E078`; `Y1V11.5-E039–E042`).

The liberating argument is strong:

> a capable person should not be imprisoned by weaker classmates or an initial placement.

The patronage danger is equally strong:

> if mobility depends on resources, council access, or recognition by one powerful leader, individual merit becomes dependent upon proximity to the person authorized to certify and finance it.

Marketized freedom is therefore not automatically self-authorship.

It can become another route through which a gatekeeper converts opportunity into obligation.

# 6. The surveillance ecology

ANHS does not possess one total surveillance system comparable to a simple panopticon.

It contains overlapping practices whose coverage, reliability, ownership, and purpose vary.

A useful Year 1 taxonomy is:

| Surveillance form | Examples | Primary function | Central risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| **ambient institutional observation** | campus cameras, island monitoring, phones and location services | safety, rule enforcement, exam data | students may not know what is observed or how it will be used |
| **evidentiary capture** | photographs, recordings, witness testimony | turn events into actionable proof | partial footage may distort causal meaning |
| **peer surveillance** | Ryūen’s phones, Hashimoto’s tail, Kushida’s recorders | intelligence, deterrence, leverage | private actors create shadow archives without consent |
| **counter-surveillance** | staged meetings, detected tails, false causal stories | control what the observer believes | observation becomes a channel for manipulation |
| **privacy protection** | shielding Horikita from elevator camera, destroying voyeur footage | prevent humiliating or sexualized permanent record | depends on private discretion rather than stable rights |
| **administrative record capture** | Tsukishiro’s dummy footage and chess interception | author official reality | officeholder can make authentic conduct institutionally disappear |
| **internalized surveillance** | Ayanokōji’s White Room risk logic | anticipatory self-control | the institution persists after physical exit |

## 6.1 Cameras do not guarantee truth

Volume 2 introduces the distinction with unusual clarity.

The threat of surveillance works even when the devices are dummy cameras. The accused students react not to confirmed footage but to the possibility that a record exists (`Y1V02-E027–E030`). Later, actual photographs supply a stronger evidentiary basis (`Y1V02-E034`).

The sequence separates:

- belief that one was observed;
- existence of a record;
- evidentiary capacity;
- and probative force.

Manabu’s distinction between `証拠能力` and `証明力` makes that separation explicit (`Y1V02-E021`). A record can qualify as evidence without proving every disputed causal proposition.

## 6.2 Surveillance coverage is an object of strategic study

Ryūen experiments with camera locations, blind spots, punishment thresholds, and school response before the rooftop assault. He disables the relevant camera after mapping the institutional environment (`Y1V07-E050–E057`).

This produces a disturbing inversion.

Safety infrastructure becomes useful to the aggressor because predictable enforcement reveals where violence can be made less legible.

A rule-governed institution therefore needs more than devices. It needs:

- trustworthy reporting;
- protection for complainants;
- contextual investigation;
- and actors willing to intervene before the official record is complete.

## 6.3 Peer observation can become a controlled channel

Hashimoto’s tailing of Ayanokōji appears to give Class A information. Ayanokōji instead uses the observer to transmit a curated explanation of his own contacts. The watched subject authors the watcher’s conclusion (`Y1V09-E027`, `Y1V09-E051–E053`, `Y1V09-E085`).

This is institutionally important because it shows that more observation does not necessarily produce more knowledge.

A sophisticated subject can treat surveillance as an audience.

## 6.4 Privacy appears as practice more often than right

Horikita avoids an elevator camera during an embarrassing bodily crisis, and Ayanokōji shields the visible object when camera exposure becomes unavoidable (`Y1V04.5-E031`). In the pool incident, the destruction of the data prevents covert sexual recording from becoming a permanent, copyable archive (`Y1V04.5-E063–E068`).

These scenes demonstrate moral concern for privacy.

They do not establish a robust institutional privacy regime.

Protection depends upon:

- individual ingenuity;
- private restraint;
- or destruction of the record after intrusion has already occurred.

The school’s pervasive cameras coexist with substantial blind spots in rights, procedure, and enforcement.

# 7. Evidence, documentation, and record sovereignty

Year 1 progressively enlarges the politics of truth.

The sequence is:

> **event → observation → evidence → interpretation → actionable decision → official record**

A failure at any stage can separate what happened from what the institution treats as real.

Volume 2 already establishes that event truth is insufficient. Sudō may tell the truth, but the school must act through evidence that is admissible, credible, and strong enough to support the relevant proposition. Injury photographs prove injury more readily than they prove who initiated the fight. Sakura’s witness status matters, but her social credibility and lack of decisive material proof limit the institutional force of what she knows (`Y1V02-E021; Y1V02-E027–E034`).

The settlement does not reveal a pure judicial truth. It produces an actionable record through mutual withdrawal and point consequences (`Y1V02-E039`).

Volume 5 develops the same problem through edited or partial recordings. Horikita’s audio countermeasure is neutralized when Ryūen supplies a fuller video context. Another recording of actual planning then changes the evidentiary balance and creates a new suspicion of betrayal within Class C (`Y1V05-E040–E044`, `Y1V05-E067–E069`).

The institutional lesson is not “video tells the truth.”

It is:

> **the meaning of a record depends on scope, custody, context, and the competing records available.**

## 7.1 Rumor as an unofficial distributed file

Volume 9 shows that an institution can classify people even without a formal database.

Rumor converts:

- selected truths;
- false allegations;
- moral panic;
- prior reputation;
- and repeated circulation

into a social profile that constrains future action.

The school initially remains passive because Ichinose does not report the harassment and the damage appears local. Intervention arrives only when cross-class scale, confrontation risk, and reputational exposure make nonintervention institutionally costly (`Y1V09-E021–E025; Y1V09-E039–E041`, `Y1V09-E058–E063; Y1V09-E072–E079`).

This threshold response reveals a limit in the school’s person-protection logic.

Private suffering becomes actionable less because the institution recognizes the victim’s dignity than because the harm becomes large enough to threaten institutional order.

The strongest counterargument is administrative practicality. A school cannot formally investigate every unreported rumor. Intervention without a complainant may itself become paternalistic or escalate attention.

That defense does not erase the structural asymmetry. The target bears the cost until the harm becomes legible in the institution’s preferred category.

## 7.2 Records can be authored without being fabricated

Ayanokōji’s preferred method is often to shape what authentic observers see.

He does not need to forge Hashimoto’s report. He gives the tail a plausible sequence from which Hashimoto will draw the desired conclusion. He does not need to prove every rumor true. He saturates the information environment until school regulation becomes rational.

This is **causal authorship of evidence** rather than simple falsification.

Tsukishiro’s chess intervention crosses a different line.

Ayanokōji issues a command.

The transmission is intercepted, delayed, and replaced by automated voice. The substituted move enters the official match record (`Y1V11-E138–E145`).

The distinction matters:

- Ayanokōji often manipulates the environment from which another actor forms a conclusion.
- Tsukishiro substitutes the action through which the result becomes official.

The first can be coercive and deceptive.

The second attacks the integrity of the adjudicative record itself.

## 7.3 Record power outranks performance power

Volume 11 produces one of Year 1’s strongest institutional propositions:

> **The actor who controls the record may possess more practical sovereignty than the actor who performs best.**

Horikita can create the board state.

Ayanokōji can generate the superior command.

Sakayanagi can recognize that the official move does not fit the opponent she is facing.

None of them controls the published result.

This is why record integrity becomes a constituent part of meritocracy rather than an administrative detail. Without it, measurement does not merely contain error. It becomes a channel for the officeholder’s private purpose.

# 8. Examinations as institutional authorship

`Y1_06` is the primary home for examination theory. The institutional contribution is to identify the school’s power to create **educational worlds**.

A special examination decides:

- which scarcity exists;
- what cooperation costs;
- whether betrayal pays;
- which body or skill becomes useful;
- who bears collective liability;
- what information is distributed;
- whether rescue is available;
- and what result becomes permanent.

The examination therefore does not stand outside student development and merely inspect it.

It participates in development.

## 8.1 Authored environments produce real behavior

The deserted-island exam makes camping knowledge valuable, leadership identity dangerous, and resource allocation politically central (`Y1V03-E004–E016`). The zodiac exam makes distrust, social inference, and betrayal financially rational (`Y1V04-E010–E015`). Paper Shuffle turns teaching and pair compatibility into academic survival (`Y1V06-E012–E017; Y1V06-E029–E031`). The Mixed Training Camp links reward to leader liability and converts cross-year trust into exploitable capital (`Y1V08-E006–E021; Y1V08-E023–E026`, `Y1V08-E130–E142`).

The behavior produced in these environments is not fake.

A student who betrays under an incentive has truly betrayed.

A student who protects another at real cost has truly protected them.

But the institution remains responsible for choosing the conditions under which those actions become rational or necessary.

## 8.2 Development does not erase authorship

ANHS often succeeds at producing growth.

Sudō becomes more disciplined.

Horikita becomes more relational and publicly accountable.

Mixed groups form genuine temporary communities.

Students discover capacities that ordinary classroom measurement missed.

The institution may therefore defend itself as developmental rather than merely punitive.

The strongest criticism is not that the growth is illusory.

It is:

> **an institution cannot use a beneficial developmental outcome as retroactive consent to every method that produced it.**

This is the same problem that surrounds Ayanokōji’s interpersonal interventions. Effective cultivation creates a temptation to treat results as proof of jurisdiction.

## 8.3 Student actors can approach rule authorship

Year 1 gradually reveals that special examinations are not simply delivered by anonymous adult bureaucracy.

Teachers’ histories and rivalries influence group placement. Student-council actors can influence exam concepts and school culture. Nagumo’s network is capable of designing a strategy first and helping produce the examination field in which it operates (`Y1V04-E025`; `Y1V07.5-E042–E043`; `Y1V08-E130–E142`).

This blurs the line between student and institution.

A gifted student is not only a subject of evaluation.

He may become a partial author of the environment evaluating everyone else.

That is why the student-council conflict belongs in this document rather than only in class politics. It concerns who acquires access to institutional design.

# 9. Expulsion and manufactured disposability

Expulsion appears early as a consequence of failing an academic threshold. The ability to purchase one point prevents Sudō’s removal and immediately establishes that continued enrollment can be converted into a priced institutional option (`Y1V01-E026–E030`).

Later volumes change the meaning of expulsion.

It becomes:

- a threat used by Chabashira;
- a strategic possibility contemplated by Ayanokōji;
- a liability attached to group leadership;
- an object of Nagumo’s political theory;
- a bargaining tool;
- a protection commodity;
- and eventually an output the school deliberately wants to produce.

The Class Vote is the decisive escalation.

The extra examination exists because the first year produced no expellees. The institution treats that absence as a defect requiring correction (`Y1V10-E013–E019`; terminology anchor `退学者の不在`).

This is not simply failure carrying a known consequence.

The school begins with a desired outcome—someone must leave—and designs a mechanism to select the person.

## 9.1 The institution creates the scarcity it calls realism

Chabashira defends the vote by comparing it to adult leadership and painful personnel decisions (`Y1V10-E065`). The analogy identifies something real. Leaders sometimes must choose under scarcity.

But the school is not merely responding to external scarcity.

It authors the scarcity.

That difference matters because the institution can then present the resulting cruelty as education about “the real world,” hiding its own causal role.

The volume itself supplies the counterevidence:

- student growth is difficult to predict;
- anonymous coalitions can manufacture a target;
- wealth can purchase rescue;
- romantic leverage can finance survival;
- outside votes can become future immunity;
- and adult actors may already be targeting a named student (`Y1V10-E006–E007; Y1V10-E013–E019`, `Y1V10-E035–E036; Y1V10-E072–E073; Y1V10-E088; Y1V10-E094–E104; Y1V10-E109–E111`, `Y1V10-E109–E111`).

The stronger formulation is:

> **The Class Vote trains students to allocate an institutionally manufactured harm. It may reveal leadership, solidarity, fear, loyalty, and judgment. It does not prove the institution was entitled to require a victim.**

## 9.2 Protection Points formalize unequal futurity

The `プロテクトポイント` converts present reputation into a one-use future shield. Sakayanagi’s direction of A-class praise toward Ayanokōji shows that the protection is not a neutral reward for broadly visible merit; it can be politically authored (`Y1V10-E088`; `Y1FF-E056`).

Formal protection can increase autonomy.

It can also make future survival depend on:

- popularity;
- patronage;
- cross-class strategy;
- and the ability to attract a sovereign’s interest.

The institution thereby creates a tiered right to continue becoming.

## 9.3 Expulsion is also an internalized institutional language

By summer, Ayanokōji can casually model which students are “unnecessary” and potentially removable even outside an active exam (`Y1V04.5-E056`). Nagumo defines repeated expulsion as the proper competitive mode of the school (`Y1V08-E136`). Sakayanagi uses removal as sovereign pruning. Ryūen uses threat and fear to enforce utility.

The school’s expulsion architecture therefore migrates into student thought.

The most important institutional effects are not always the official punishments.

They include the categories through which students learn to imagine one another.

# 10. Ordinary life as counter-curriculum and institutional surplus

ANHS’s strongest distinction from the White Room is not a formal statement of educational philosophy.

It is the abundance of experiences that formal evaluation does not fully control.

The school creates the physical and temporal conditions for:

- friendship groups;
- sports participation;
- books and shared hobbies;
- dates and confession;
- gifts;
- movies;
- snow;
- holidays;
- food preferences;
- private phone calls;
- and purposeless conversation.

These experiences may later become strategically useful. That does not exhaust their meaning.

Ayanokōji calls ordinary study and exercise the daily life that gives him the greatest fulfillment. He enjoys a conversation with Kei that has no informational or tactical relevance. He wants tomorrow to arrive because he wants to see her (`Y1FF-E059–E061; Y1FF-E066–E073`).

This is institutional evidence because it demonstrates that ANHS does not assign one total purpose to every minute of the student’s existence.

## 10.1 Counter-curriculum is not outside the institution

Ordinary life occurs inside the school’s enclosure.

The institution provides:

- the dormitory;
- the campus;
- the peer cohort;
- the holiday calendar;
- the point economy;
- and the separation from family

that make those experiences possible.

ANHS therefore deserves causal credit for conditions it did not fully design as outcomes.

But the meaning is co-authored by students.

The school can create a pool outing. It does not determine whether that outing becomes friendship, embarrassment, criminal voyeurism, privacy protection, or all four at once (`Y1V04.5-E049–E062; Y1V04.5-E063–E068`).

## 10.2 The institution’s incompleteness is productive

A total institution attempts to close the gap between official purpose and lived meaning.

ANHS repeatedly fails to close it.

That failure is one of its virtues.

Students can become:

- friends across class lines;
- romantic partners whose relationship is not reducible to points;
- readers together;
- temporary communities inside coerced group assignments;
- or rivals whose private encounter matters more than the official result.

Ayanokōji’s desire for the `俗世間` and for knowledge of freedom finds material here (`Y1V07-E027`; terminology anchors `俗世間`, `自由とは何か`).

The counter-curriculum does not abolish surveillance or coercion.

It creates human ends that can challenge the institution’s claim to define what development is for.

# 11. Teachers as political actors

Teachers in Year 1 are not neutral delivery mechanisms for school rules.

They possess:

- discretion;
- access to confidential information;
- personal histories;
- class-based incentives;
- authority over evidence and procedure;
- and unequal willingness to resist administrative pressure.

The school’s class structure may even reward teachers materially when their class reaches A, giving adult staff a direct stake in student competition (`Y1V02-E016`).

This creates a structural conflict of interest.

A homeroom teacher is expected to:

- educate;
- protect;
- evaluate;
- enforce rules;
- and advocate for a class whose success may affect the teacher personally.

Year 1 shows several possible responses to that conflict.

## 11.1 Teachers can author the local field

The zodiac examination reveals that former classmates and rivals among the teachers deliberately place strategically important students into particular groups (`Y1V04-E025`). Horikita later uses Chabashira’s administrative role as part of the Paper Shuffle defense, securing exclusive submission authority and instructing the teacher to pretend that unauthorized submissions remain possible (`Y1V06-E056`).

The teacher is therefore not outside student strategy.

Administrative discretion can become an operational resource.

## 11.2 Teachers vary in their conception of duty

Chabashira initially instrumentalizes Ayanokōji for her unresolved Class A obsession.

Hoshinomiya’s information about Ichinose enters Nagumo’s leverage structure.

Mashima accepts punishment to prevent avoidable student danger and later insists that teachers cannot tolerate dishonest alteration of exams or results.

Chairman Sakayanagi recommends teachers as a monitoring network against the acting chairman.

The adult institution contains:

- compromised educators;
- competitive participants;
- conscientious professionals;
- and officeholders willing to corrupt procedure.

That plurality is the basis of possible resistance and the source of inconsistency.

# 12. Chabashira Sae — institutional protection reproduced as coercion

Chabashira is Year 1’s clearest example of an adult who occupies a legitimate educational role and uses it through a compromised private purpose.

Her trajectory should not be flattened into either:

- villainous manipulator;
- or secretly benevolent teacher.

She is both institutionally useful and ethically unreliable.

## 12.1 Unresolved student history becomes adult policy

Chabashira’s own failure as a Class D student destroyed a late opportunity to reach A (`Y1V03-E029`). She enters Year 1 carrying that history as unfinished identity.

The current class becomes a vehicle through which she may retroactively repair the earlier loss.

That is why Ayanokōji’s concealed ability becomes so important to her. She does not initially encounter him simply as a student to educate. She encounters a resource capable of fulfilling the adult future she still wants.

## 12.2 Authentic information supports a false threat

Chabashira knows enough about Atsuomi, the school’s protective role, and Ayanokōji’s abnormal background to make her story plausible.

She then falsely claims that failure to help Class D will allow his father to remove him (`Y1V03-E030`, `Y1V03-E050`; `Y1V04-E006–E007`).

The method reproduces White Room logic in miniature:

> continued access to the future you value is conditional on serving my developmental project.

Her authority as teacher, possession of confidential information, and control over institutional interpretation create the leverage.

## 12.3 Protective attachment becomes manufactured dependency

Chairman Sakayanagi and Chabashira wanted Ayanokōji to become attached to school life so he would choose to remain.

The original goal can be described as protective.

Chabashira’s method converts it into coercion. Rather than allow ordinary life to become valuable and then respect the choice generated by that value, she threatens the loss of ordinary life to obtain obedience (`Y1V07-E033`, `Y1V07-E075`).

This is a central Year 1 pattern:

> a beneficial end does not legitimate an imposed route to the end.

## 12.4 Exposure does not erase her usefulness

Once Ayanokōji reveals that her paternal mandate was fabricated, Chabashira loses the strongest leverage. He tells her to act as a teacher and guide the class rather than use him as substitute for unresolved failure (`Y1V07-E071–E075`).

The later relationship becomes pragmatic.

He still dislikes her earlier coercion. He nevertheless accepts that her office, knowledge, and partial commitment to the class can become useful in a defensive adult network (`Y1V11.5-E024; Y1V11.5-E034–E035`).

This is not forgiveness.

It is institutional differentiation.

A compromised actor can still perform a legitimate function when:

- the function is bounded;
- the coercive claim is exposed;
- other adults provide oversight;
- and the student no longer treats her account as unquestionable.

## 12.5 Her “real world” argument remains a stress-tested ideology

Chabashira’s defense of the Class Vote articulates ANHS’s harshest educational self-conception: future leaders must learn to make painful personnel decisions (`Y1V10-E065`).

The source does not require us to dismiss that proposition.

It does require us to notice who is speaking.

A teacher whose own past remains organized around one decisive class loss argues that manufactured exclusion will prepare students for adulthood. Her biography does not automatically falsify the argument. It makes the institutional ideology inseparable from adult wound.

# 13. Hoshinomiya Chie — intimacy, competition, and compromised informational boundaries

Year 1 gives less sustained interior access to Hoshinomiya than to Chabashira or Mashima. The responsible synthesis must therefore remain narrower.

Two facts are institutionally important.

First, Hoshinomiya states that the Class A homeroom teacher receives a special bonus (`Y1V02-E016`). This confirms that teachers are not wholly disinterested evaluators. Class competition can affect adult incentives.

Second, Nagumo uses information supplied by Hoshinomiya to infer that Ichinose has a concealed reason for B-class placement. He then conditions student-council access on disclosure of her secret (`Y1V09-E004–E008`).

The evidence supports a consequential informational failure:

> **private or sensitive student information moves from a teacher into a senior student’s patronage system.**

The local source does not establish every detail of Hoshinomiya’s intent, what she knew Nagumo would do, or whether the disclosure violated an explicit written confidentiality rule.

It does establish why adult informality can be dangerous in a closed school.

A teacher may experience herself as socially close, competitive, playful, or helpful while still transferring information that alters a younger student’s practical freedom.

Ichinose’s later trust in Nagumo is formed inside that adult-enabled asymmetry.

Hoshinomiya therefore represents a softer institutional risk than Tsukishiro:

- not direct record falsification;
- but porous boundaries through which personal knowledge becomes political leverage.

# 14. Mashima Tomonari — institutional conscience as costly action

Mashima is the strongest Year 1 example of adult professionalism becoming resistance.

His defining contribution is not that he possesses perfect knowledge.

It is that he accepts the possibility that formal procedure may need to be challenged when it ceases to serve a defensible educational purpose.

## 14.1 He distinguishes failure risk from designed expulsion

Before the Event Selection Exam, Mashima objects to the first-year policy trajectory. He does not argue that students should be protected from every loss. He distinguishes ordinary risk from a mechanism deliberately designed to force removal (`Y1V11-E016–E017`).

That distinction is institutionally mature.

Education may expose students to consequence without making human disappearance the proof that the system is serious.

## 14.2 The judo decision embodies teacher responsibility

In Volume 11.5, Mashima’s prior intervention is reconstructed. When random selection could have forced a student into a physically dangerous judo mismatch after the intervention window closed, he declared the event lost rather than allow the risk. He accepted punishment for violating the formal procedure (`Y1V11.5-E034`).

The scene creates a demanding professional standard:

> **A teacher cannot teach responsibility, safety, and honesty while hiding behind procedure when procedure creates avoidable serious harm.**

Mashima does not treat office as immunity.

He treats office as liability for what the system does to students.

## 14.3 Conscience requires strategy, not only purity

Mashima agrees to monitor Tsukishiro because teachers cannot permit authorities to alter exams or results dishonestly (`Y1V11.5-E035`).

Ayanokōji and Sakayanagi also recognize that open righteous confrontation could remove Mashima and destroy the check he provides.

The document should resist romanticizing conscience as spontaneous denunciation.

Institutional resistance must survive long enough to matter.

Mashima therefore represents **accountable institutional courage**, not simple moral exhibition.

# 15. Chairman Sakayanagi — refuge, bounded protection, and the weakness of office

Chairman Sakayanagi provides the strongest Year 1 alternative to proprietary protection.

He personally admits Ayanokōji despite the school’s opaque preselection process. When Atsuomi demands removal, the chairman refuses to expel the student against his expressed will. He also refuses to promise immunity from legitimate school rules (`Y1V07-E030–E032`).

The structure of his protection is important:

1. **Choice governs the refuge.** Ayanokōji may remain unless he chooses to leave.
2. **Protection is bounded by general rules.** The student is not placed above ordinary legitimate consequence.
3. **No personal submission is demanded.** The chairman does not exchange safety for service.
4. **The institution, not private ownership, supplies the shelter.**

This is protection that increases the protected person’s jurisdiction over his own future.

## 15.1 Admission reveals pre-meritocratic curation

The chairman also reveals that candidates are selected before the formal entrance examination. The visible test is partly camouflage (`Y1V07-E030–E032`).

ANHS’s meritocracy therefore begins after an opaque act of institutional curation.

The school decides who may enter the field before presenting later competition as a measure of those inside it.

This does not prove the selection is arbitrary.

It proves that no class ranking can be the complete origin of institutional value.

## 15.2 Protective office is vulnerable to political displacement

The chairman’s later removal or suspension creates the opening for Tsukishiro. Year 1 does not fully establish the external mechanism behind that transition. It does establish the consequence: a refuge dependent upon one officeholder’s integrity becomes fragile when the office changes hands.

This reveals a limitation in benevolent personal authority.

A good chairman is not a substitute for durable institutional checks.

## 15.3 From protector to oversight architect

In Volume 11.5, Chairman Sakayanagi helps build a monitoring network through Chabashira and Mashima and identifies an opportunity with reduced surveillance (`Y1V11.5-E017–E024`).

He does not promise omnipotent rescue.

He distributes awareness.

That move is the institutional analogue of reciprocal authorship:

> protection becomes more legitimate when it creates multiple witnesses and preserves the protected person’s right to defend himself rather than making one superior protector indispensable.

# 16. The student council as quasi-government

The student council begins as a familiar school office and develops into a constitutional institution.

Manabu initially insists:

> `生徒会そのものには何の力もない`

The statement does not mean the council is irrelevant. It means office does not mechanically generate capacity. Authority depends on what the holder can do with:

- confidential information;
- rule access;
- reputation;
- inter-year networks;
- formal visibility;
- and proximity to school administration (`Y1V04.5-E019–E021`).

By Volume 7.5, Manabu states that the council can influence school culture and special-exam concepts. The council therefore approaches **rule authorship**, not merely student representation (`Y1V07.5-E042–E043`).

This creates a blurred constitutional order.

Students who are themselves subject to examination may help shape the examinations governing other students.

The arrangement can produce:

- informed peer administration;
- leadership development;
- responsiveness to student conditions;
- and ambitious reform.

It can also produce:

- conflict of interest;
- personal patronage;
- upper-year domination of younger cohorts;
- private use of confidential information;
- and rule fields designed around the officeholder’s political project.

## 16.1 Council access is a scarce political resource

Ichinose’s desire to join the council becomes leverage. Nagumo presents himself as the route around Manabu’s alleged prejudice, uses Hoshinomiya-derived information to infer a secret, and conditions entry on disclosure (`Y1V09-E004–E008`).

The office that should symbolize public service becomes a gate through which a powerful senior acquires private knowledge.

Kushida later seeks council entry while pursuing Horikita’s and Ayanokōji’s removal (`Y1V09-E017`, `Y1V09-E047`). Horikita’s own possible council membership becomes an object of recommendation and later wager (`Y1V07.5-E045–E046`; `Y1V11.5-E100`).

Council access is therefore never merely extracurricular.

It offers:

- information;
- status;
- cross-year reach;
- proximity to rule formation;
- and a platform from which private conflicts can acquire institutional scale.

## 16.2 Student government reveals the school’s experimental ambition

ANHS does not reserve all meaningful authority for adults.

It gives unusually capable adolescents opportunities to affect school order.

This can be read as trust in student agency.

It can also be read as another experiment: the institution allows emerging elites to govern peers, then observes the resulting political systems.

Manabu and Nagumo do not merely display personalities.

They test two different designs of student rule.

# 17. Horikita Manabu — exemplary authority and the limits of distance

Manabu represents the strongest Year 1 model of restrained, impersonal student authority.

His public order values:

- rule compliance;
- competence;
- confidentiality;
- credibility;
- stable standards;
- and the hope that students graduate rather than being removed (`Y1V06-E039–E045`; `Y1V07.5-E042–E043`).

He distinguishes evidence from proof with unusual precision. His reputation can enforce private wagers because the parties believe he will preserve the agreed conditions. He resists turning Ayanokōji into the permanent guardian of Suzune’s future. At graduation, he refuses to prescribe the exact answer Ayanokōji should leave behind (`Y1V02-E021`; `Y1V06-E039–E045`; `Y1V09-E047`; `Y1V11.5-E087–E089`).

These are important forms of nonproprietary authority.

## 17.1 His institutional ideal shelters development

Manabu’s defense of class fate is not simple traditionalism.

He believes collective structure gives weaker individuals:

- time;
- shared resources;
- distributed roles;
- and a chance to recover before being converted into isolated competitors (`Y1V11.5-E084–E085`).

This is consistent with Year 1’s evidence that Sudō’s current value cannot be inferred from his entrance state.

Manabu’s meritocracy therefore retains a developmental buffer.

## 17.2 Exemplary conduct can create relational blindness

Volume 8 reveals the weakness of authority built through distance.

Manabu deliberately avoids deeply trusted friendship. Tachibana withholds danger because she refuses to become his burden. Nagumo attacks him through the very person and field Manabu tried not to involve (`Y1V08-E001–E004`, `Y1V08-E107–E108; Y1V08-E130–E137`).

The resulting rescue is morally serious and politically expensive.

Manabu’s class spends immense collective resources to preserve Tachibana. That choice demonstrates loyalty and exposes the cost of a leadership culture in which subordinates protect the leader from knowledge of their vulnerability.

The institutional lesson is:

> **Impartial authority can become less correctable when people who care about the leader believe that disclosing need would violate the leader’s ideal.**

## 17.3 Office cannot replace personal recognition

Nagumo’s final admission—that Manabu’s recognition was a central desired object—reveals that the council rivalry was partly relational (`Y1V11.5-E039–E042`).

Manabu’s late handshake does not validate Nagumo’s whole system.

It performs succession without humiliation.

The office changes hands, but the outgoing leader recognizes the successor as a person rather than merely a defective continuation of his own order.

That is one of Year 1’s most constructive institutional gestures.

# 18. Nagumo Miyabi — personalized meritocracy, patronage, and institutional entrepreneurship

Nagumo’s political intelligence should not be underestimated because his methods are coercive.

He understands that institutional power belongs to the actor who can:

- identify rules before others;
- publicize or close loopholes;
- build cross-class networks;
- accumulate trust;
- finance allies;
- distribute opportunity;
- and shape the examination environment (`Y1V06-E005–E006`; `Y1V08-E019–E021; Y1V08-E043–E049; Y1V08-E067–E075`; `Y1V09-E009–E016`).

He is not merely a talented student adapting to school.

He is an **institutional entrepreneur**.

## 18.1 `本当の実力主義` is a political claim

Nagumo promises a `本当の実力主義`—a true meritocracy—and defines the project through stronger individual mobility, clearer result orientation, and more interaction across years (`Y1V06-E005–E006`; `Y1V11.5-E042`, `Y1V11.5-E085`).

The appeal is real.

The fixed class system can trap exceptional students inside collective outcomes they did not choose. A more mobile structure may recognize individuals whom initial placement or weaker classmates suppress.

The difficulty is that Nagumo often becomes the person who:

- recognizes the talent;
- grants the opportunity;
- finances the movement;
- and defines the result.

A meritocracy mediated through one leader’s attention can become patronage.

The student is liberated from the class only by entering a more personalized dependency upon the allocator.

## 18.2 The council room becomes the ruler’s room

The physical remaking of the council space around Nagumo’s possessions visualizes public office as personalized sovereignty (`Y1V09-E009`).

His speech about Sakayanagi as acceptable “council property” based first on appearance reveals the same tendency at the level of persons (`Y1V09-E010`).

The council is not only administered by him.

It is symbolically absorbed into him.

## 18.3 Trust is accumulated for expenditure

Nagumo’s most mature political insight is that trust functions as capital.

He does not treat betrayal as the absence of trust. He builds trust so that it can be spent at the decisive moment (`Y1V08-E133–E134`; `Y1V09-E016`).

This method allows him to:

- arrange the Tachibana trap;
- use a sacrificial leader financed by a wider network;
- create actionable testimony;
- and inflict a larger strategic loss than the visible score suggests (`Y1V08-E130–E142`).

The method is effective because moral credibility becomes operational infrastructure.

Its constitutional danger is profound:

> if every accumulated relationship is potentially a one-use weapon, public trust becomes impossible to distinguish from deferred coercion.

## 18.4 Selective nonintervention is an exercise of power

Nagumo allows Sakayanagi’s attack on Ichinose after being told it may reach expulsion. He deliberately leaves room for non-council actors to continue the campaign (`Y1V09-E011–E013`).

The council does not need to order harm directly.

It can decide when not to stop it.

This makes tolerance itself a political instrument.

## 18.5 Romantic and financial leverage reveal entitlement

During the Class Vote, Nagumo conditions the missing rescue capital on Ichinose entering a romantic relationship with him. He can make the offer because his position and network give him liquidity that her class lacks (`Y1V10-E035–E036; Y1V10-E072–E073`).

The offer is formally rejectable.

The context makes refusal costly enough to threaten a classmate’s continued enrollment and the class’s solidaristic identity.

This is a recurring institutional pattern:

> **nominal choice can conceal practical coercion when one actor controls the scarce route to an urgent good.**

Nagumo’s meritocratic order is therefore not simply too harsh.

It risks translating superior institutional position into personal access to other people.

## 18.6 Nagumo remains more than a villainous officeholder

He keeps explicit promises. He notices overlooked initiative. He understands flaws in existing mobility mechanisms. He produces results. He ultimately wants recognition from Manabu rather than only his destruction (`Y1V08-E067–E075`; `Y1V11.5-E039–E042`).

The correct Year 1 judgment is not that his theory is empty.

It is that his reform project lacks sufficient separation between:

- merit;
- the leader’s judgment of merit;
- and the leader’s private desires.

# 19. Tsukishiro — procedural capture and the sovereignty of the acting record

Tsukishiro changes the institutional scale because he enters with adult office, surveillance access, physical confidence, and an explicit demand connected to Atsuomi.

Unlike student strategists, he does not need to persuade a class, win a vote, or accumulate ordinary peer trust.

He can intervene through administration.

## 19.1 The Volume 10 entrance is a demonstration of office as leverage

Tsukishiro introduces himself as the incoming acting chairman, kicks Sakayanagi’s cane, restrains Ayanokōji, and relays the demand that the student stop “playing” and return home (`Y1V10-E109–E110`).

The violence matters.

So does the evidentiary environment.

Tsukishiro says the surveillance feed has been replaced with dummy footage. Ayanokōji does not retaliate because selectively recorded violence by a student against an acting chairman could be used to destroy him institutionally (`Y1V10-E111`).

The acting chairman therefore controls two forms of power simultaneously:

- immediate physical coercion;
- future authorship of the event’s official meaning.

Ayanokōji’s superior physical ability becomes less relevant because using it would produce an administratively exploitable record.

## 19.2 Tsukishiro’s ideology naturalizes authored exclusion

In the teacher debate, Tsukishiro treats the first-year cohort as experimental material for future school design. He calls removal of non-adapting students a social mechanism and then `自然の摂理`—the law or order of nature (`Y1V11-E016–E025`; terminology anchor `自然の摂理`).

The rhetoric conceals authorship.

The institution:

- chooses the adaptation target;
- designs the test;
- sets the consequence;
- and removes the student.

Tsukishiro then redescribes the outcome as nature.

This is one of Year 1’s clearest ideological warnings:

> **A selection result can be real while the selection environment remains artificial and politically authored.**

## 19.3 Chess turns capture into record substitution

The Event Selection Exam should permit competition among student polities.

Tsukishiro’s interception makes the administrator a hidden participant. The officeholder can:

- monitor the transmission;
- replace the command;
- preserve the substituted action as official;
- and publicly retain the appearance of a valid result (`Y1V11-E138–E145`).

This is not merely bias.

It is **procedural capture**.

The institution’s formal machinery remains intact while serving a private objective contrary to the announced test.

## 19.4 Ayanokōji calls him `不純物`

In Volume 11.5, Ayanokōji describes Tsukishiro as an impurity contaminating a field that should be decided by student ability (`Y1V11.5-E019–E023`).

The phrase expresses a genuine fairness claim.

It should not romanticize the ordinary school as perfectly neutral. Ayanokōji himself arranged the likely Sakayanagi match by shaping surrounding choices. Teachers and council actors influence exam design. Class resources are unequal.

The narrower claim survives:

> student strategic authorship still operates inside the announced competitive field; Tsukishiro substitutes administrative control for the field itself.

## 19.5 His White Room exposition is part of the contest

Tsukishiro later supplies detailed claims about:

- institutional age;
- generations;
- failure;
- the fourth generation;
- and an incoming White Room student (`Y1V11.5-E111–E116`).

The scene itself denies the reader a clean lore channel.

Ayanokōji states that Tsukishiro can make total fiction sound true.

The acting chairman may possess privileged knowledge. He is also an adversarial speaker attempting to shape expectation and behavior.

The correct Year 1 classification is:

- **possible high-access testimony**;
- **strategically motivated**;
- **partly compatible with other evidence**;
- **not independently verified in full**.

## 19.6 Tsukishiro is an officeholder, not the whole institution

Volume 11.5 introduces internal resistance through Chairman Sakayanagi, Mashima, and Chabashira. Sakayanagi also acknowledges that without Tsukishiro’s interference she would have lost the chess contest (`Y1V11.5-E024; Y1V11.5-E034–E036`).

This prevents two opposite errors.

It would be wrong to treat Tsukishiro as a random external villain whose power says nothing about ANHS. His office and access make the capture possible.

It would also be wrong to treat his private objective as the uncontested will of every school actor.

His presence reveals **institutional vulnerability**, not total institutional unity.

# 20. Ayanokōji Atsuomi — paternal authority as proprietary sovereignty

Atsuomi’s Year 1 function is not limited to abusive fatherhood.

He embodies a political theory of development.

The theory is:

1. I created the environment that produced your ability.
2. Your ability exists for the purpose I assigned.
3. Departure from that purpose is theft or defect.
4. Anyone enabling departure may be punished.
5. Therefore I retain jurisdiction over your future.

The theory culminates in:

> `おまえは俺の所有物だ。`

The word `所有物` does not describe strict parenting.

It describes property (`Y1V07-E023`; terminology anchor `所有物`).

## 20.1 Parent, creator, and sovereign collapse into one role

Ordinary parent-child authority is already asymmetrical. Atsuomi adds developmental authorship and political coercion, then treats the combination as total jurisdiction.

He denies meaningful parent-child reciprocity. He claims authority over departure and even life and death. The child’s exceptional competence becomes evidence of the creator’s entitlement rather than of the child’s independent personhood.

## 20.2 Retaliation extends ownership through third parties

Atsuomi’s account of Matsuo and his son is partly testimony rather than independently verified fact. Ayanokōji recognizes the intended message regardless: anyone who assists his autonomy may suffer, including relatives who did not make the original choice (`Y1V07-E024–E025`).

The threat restructures freedom.

Departure is made morally expensive because the sovereign promises to externalize punishment onto helpers.

Ayanokōji rejects the guilt transfer. If Matsuo knowingly acted to secure his school placement, returning to the perpetrator would not honor the victim.

## 20.3 External power meets institutional jurisdiction

Atsuomi’s lack of immediate force inside the school matters. Ayanokōji infers that the father’s bodyguard absence, reliance on dialogue, and political vulnerability limit direct action within ANHS (`Y1V07-E028`).

Chairman Sakayanagi’s school jurisdiction therefore creates a real boundary against proprietary family power.

The boundary is later weakened when Tsukishiro occupies the acting chairmanship and relays Atsuomi’s demand (`Y1V10-E109–E111`).

This shows why refuge depends on institutional control of office, not merely walls.

# 22. What Year 1 directly establishes about the White Room

Despite the caution, Year 1 establishes a substantial core.

## 22.1 It is an artificial developmental project

Sakayanagi’s memory describes the stated object as:

> `人工的に天才を作る`

The White Room is designed to create genius artificially (`Y1V11-E002`; terminology anchor `人工的に天才を作る`).

This is stronger than inference from Ayanokōji’s competence. The project’s purpose is directly named within the Year 1 source.

## 22.2 It standardizes environment while sorting outcomes

Children receive controlled education in a common facility. Sakayanagi observes unequal survival within the program and understands the boy as an exceptional case. Her father fears normalization of such institutions if the model proves effective (`Y1V11-E002–E010`).

The novel thus connects educational production to removal of those who fail the selected standard.

## 22.3 It is extraordinarily effective in Ayanokōji’s own account

Ayanokōji does not dismiss the education as fraudulent.

He credits it with academic knowledge, martial training, self-defense, and broad survival competence. He says being called its greatest masterpiece produces boredom because the hierarchy appears closed above him (`Y1V07-E027`, `Y1V07-E040`).

The critique is not incompetence.

It is totality.

## 22.4 It excludes ordinary human experience structurally

The White Room did not need to issue a rule saying “romance is forbidden.” Constant surveillance, lack of leisure, lack of privacy, and the absence of ordinary social rhythm made romance structurally impossible (`Y1V07.5-E059`).

Ayanokōji’s rebellion centers on the excluded `俗世間` and the desire to learn `自由とは何か`—what freedom is (`Y1V07-E027`).

The institution defines entire domains of human experience as useless before the child can judge them.

## 22.5 It persists after physical exit

Ayanokōji says:

> `ホワイトルームを出ても尚、やはりホワイトルームの中`

Even after leaving the White Room, he remains inside it (`Y1V07.5-E077`; terminology anchor of the same phrase).

The statement identifies institutional afterlife.

White Room logic persists as:

- risk calculation;
- concealment;
- final-victory reasoning;
- expectation of human replaceability;
- emotional underdevelopment;
- and the tendency to turn relationships into curricula.

## 22.6 Its failures include relational incapacity, not only lower performance

Ayanokōji compares Ichinose’s suppressed suffering to White Room children who could not externalize pain and collapsed because they lacked someone to whom they could speak (`Y1V09-E090`).

This provides direct first-person evidence that the institution’s human cost includes more than academic failure.

It failed to create social structures through which distress could become shared and survivable.

## 22.7 Atsuomi treats its product as property

The facility’s political meaning is completed by Atsuomi’s ownership claim. The institution does not merely train a child. Its creator asserts that the trained person exists as his possession and future resource (`Y1V07-E023`).

The White Room is therefore an institution of development and an institution of proprietary personhood.

# 23. What Year 1 does not finally establish

The following claims should remain open or tiered at the Year 1 boundary.

## 23.1 Exact generation history

*First File* and Tsukishiro supply fourth-generation and broader generation framing (`Y1V11.5-E113–E116`; `Y1FF-E008`, `Y1FF-E055`).

The franchise clearly wants the generation structure in the reader’s prospective field.

Yet Tsukishiro’s fine-grained account remains adversarial testimony, and *First File* compresses rather than reproduces a fully audited institutional history.

The responsible formulation is:

> Year 1 officially frames Ayanokōji through a fourth-generation masterpiece model, while the complete historical mechanics remain outside independently verified scene evidence.

## 23.2 Exact failure rates and consequences

The novels establish severe attrition and psychological collapse in broad terms. They do not provide a complete independently verified statistical record of every cohort or every failed student.

## 23.3 The identity and number of incoming White Room students

Tsukishiro claims one such student is hidden among 160 incoming first-years and turns identification into a game (`Y1V11.5-E116`).

Ayanokōji trusts neither the number nor the promise.

The claim is therefore an active future threat, not a settled Year 1 fact about a named person.

## 23.4 The full national political structure behind the project

Year 1 establishes Atsuomi’s power, his vulnerability inside ANHS, and a conflict between school jurisdiction and external authority. It does not complete the larger state-political map.

## 23.5 Ayanokōji’s forecast of return

In Volume 11.5, Ayanokōji believes his post-graduation return and possible instructor role are effectively fixed (`Y1V11.5-E025`).

That belief is evidence of his current horizon and internalized institutional future.

It is not proof of destiny.

# 24. The White Room as a total developmental institution

The White Room’s defining feature is not simply severity.

It is **total purpose**.

The institution attempts to align:

- place;
- time;
- curriculum;
- observation;
- bodily training;
- emotional formation;
- standards of success;
- and future use

under one developmental objective.

The child is not merely educated inside the project.

The child’s world is made coextensive with the project.

## 24.1 Totalization removes the child’s authority over relevance

Every education selects.

No school can teach every possible subject or experience.

The White Room’s distinctive violence lies in treating the institution’s selection as exhaustive authority over what counts as humanly necessary.

Ordinary social life, leisure, romance, privacy, unsupervised curiosity, and the right to discover a useless preference are excluded before the child can assess their value (`Y1V07-E027`; `Y1V07.5-E059`).

This is why Ayanokōji’s interest in the `俗世間` is intellectually significant.

He is not merely asking for entertainment after difficult training.

He is disputing the institution’s right to close the question of what knowledge is worth having.

## 24.2 Success increases the ownership temptation

If the White Room had produced only failure, its moral illegitimacy would be easier to see.

Its effectiveness makes the institutional problem sharper.

Atsuomi can point to Ayanokōji’s exceptional capacity as evidence that the project works. He then treats effectiveness as title to the result.

This is a general danger of developmental institutions:

> **The more visibly an institution improves a person, the more tempting it becomes to treat the person’s later life as proof, property, or debt owed to the developer.**

Year 1 rejects that inference.

Causal contribution does not create ownership.

## 24.3 Failure becomes disappearance from the model

The project’s success criterion is narrow enough that students who cannot sustain the curriculum cease to function as participants in its visible achievement. Sakayanagi’s father fears what happens if society normalizes the model because the institution’s efficiency depends partly on treating unsuccessful children as costs of production (`Y1V11-E002–E010`).

This resembles the rhetoric Tsukishiro later uses at ANHS:

> those who do not adapt are excluded; that is nature.

The shared grammar is not proof that the institutions are identical.

It is evidence that experimental education can erase its own authorship by naming the discarded person naturally unfit.

## 24.4 The White Room lacks an internal public

Year 1 gives no evidence of:

- student government capable of altering the project;
- teachers publicly dissenting from the governing purpose;
- transparent appeal;
- ordinary family advocacy;
- or rival developmental philosophies operating inside the institution.

Ayanokōji’s account instead emphasizes constant surveillance, absence of privacy, and a closed hierarchy.

This is the institutional difference between being governed and being **wholly authored**.

# 25. ANHS and the White Room — comparative matrix

| Dimension | White Room | Advanced Nurturing High School in Year 1 |
|---|---|---|
| **Entry and consent** | child placed into project before meaningful self-authorship | students seek admission, but selection and full conditions are opaque |
| **Enclosure** | total developmental environment | closed campus that restricts outside contact but permits broad internal ordinary life |
| **Purpose** | artificial production of exceptional ability for creator-defined ends | cultivation of elite personnel, class competition, and adaptive capacity; purpose is plural and partly contested |
| **Curriculum** | centrally controlled and totalizing | academics, sports, clubs, special exams, leisure, self-directed relations |
| **Surveillance** | constant enough to make privacy and romance structurally impossible | pervasive but partial, exploitable, and sometimes contestable |
| **Social world** | relational deprivation is a defining defect | dense peer life, friendship, rivalry, romance, class polity, cross-class contact |
| **Failure** | attrition/disappearance within project; details remain partly tiered | points, class decline, penalties, and expulsion; some rescue and appeal routes exist |
| **Mobility** | creator defines progression and future | class transfer and points create formal but unequal mobility |
| **Institutional plurality** | one governing project dominates | chairman, acting chairman, teachers, council, classes, and peer networks can conflict |
| **Record** | Year 1 gives limited internal adjudicative detail | records, evidence, votes, points, and exam results are central—and vulnerable to capture |
| **Ordinary life** | treated as unnecessary or structurally excluded | becomes a genuine counter-curriculum and self-chosen end |
| **Ownership claim** | Atsuomi explicitly claims the developed child as property | legitimate school protection is formally bounded and can preserve choice, though students are still instrumentally classified |
| **Correctability** | little Year 1 evidence of internal challenge | teachers, students, and administrators can contest one another, though checks are fragile |

The matrix supports neither identity nor simple opposition.

ANHS shares enough grammar with the White Room to make comparison necessary.

It preserves enough plurality to make equivalence false.

# 26. The shared experimental grammar

Several similarities are substantial.

## 26.1 Enclosure and controlled environment

Both institutions separate subjects from ordinary external life and make the environment part of the developmental process.

ANHS is far less total, but its campus, point economy, and communication restrictions still create a bounded world whose rules differ from ordinary society (`Y1V01-E005–E007`; `Y1V04.5-E023`).

## 26.2 Students as data

The White Room explicitly observes whether controlled education can create genius.

ANHS repeatedly turns behavior into evaluative information:

- spending;
- attendance;
- cooperation;
- physical performance;
- rumor response;
- group leadership;
- and class survival.

*First File* later materializes this logic as profiles, ratings, calendars, class summaries, and hypothetical OAA numbers (`Y1FF-E001–E005; Y1FF-E012; Y1FF-E025; Y1FF-E033; Y1FF-E037; Y1FF-E040–E048; Y1FF-E051–E058`).

## 26.3 Authored scarcity

Both institutions define a demanding success environment and permit failure to remove the subject from the active path.

At ANHS, the school can create scarcity of:

- points;
- class position;
- rescue capital;
- and continued enrollment.

The Class Vote demonstrates the most direct authored disposability.

## 26.4 Developmental value can overshadow personhood

The White Room treats children through productive capacity.

ANHS repeatedly encourages students to evaluate peers through usefulness, risk, and future contribution. The institution does not require students to deny personhood explicitly. Its incentives make instrumental classification rational.

## 26.5 Observation and record support authority

Both systems depend on making performance legible. The White Room’s constant observation supports total curriculum. ANHS relies on cameras, teacher reports, points, testimony, and official records.

The difference is that ANHS’s record layer is more visible and therefore becomes a site of political struggle.

## 26.6 Success can be mistaken for legitimacy

Both institutions tempt observers to reason backward:

> the system produced excellence, therefore the system was justified.

Year 1 consistently blocks that inference.

# 27. The decisive differences

The differences are not sentimental details. They alter the institutional structure.

## 27.1 ANHS permits plural purposes

Students may want:

- Class A;
- peaceful school life;
- friendship;
- romance;
- leadership;
- revenge;
- sport;
- books;
- or simply survival.

The school’s rules shape those projects but do not reduce them to one adult-defined end.

Ayanokōji can prioritize school life over Class A. Horikita can reject the student council despite approval from Manabu and Ayanokōji. Kōenji can refuse to make his talent collective property. Kei can decide when the romance remains secret (`Y1V03-E017`; `Y1V07.5-E045–E046`; `Y1V11-E070–E073`; `Y1FF-E072`).

## 27.2 Peer relations generate development horizontally

The White Room’s dominant relation is institution-to-child and competitor-to-competitor within a controlled project.

ANHS allows development through:

- friendship;
- reciprocal rivalry;
- peer teaching;
- chosen loyalty;
- care;
- ordinary embarrassment;
- and correction from people without formal superiority.

The institution creates the setting. It does not monopolize the causal authorship.

## 27.3 Authority is divided

Chairman Sakayanagi can resist Atsuomi.

Mashima can challenge Tsukishiro.

Students can use rules against teachers.

The student council can pressure or revise school practices.

Classes create different political cultures.

This division generates inconsistency and vulnerability, but it also makes total ownership harder.

## 27.4 Refusal remains possible in meaningful domains

ANHS refusal is not always costless. Students can still:

- refuse council membership;
- decline cooperation;
- preserve private class loyalty;
- reject a romantic bargain;
- or choose a different developmental purpose.

Kōenji’s refusal is especially important. The school can measure or punish his noncooperation, but Year 1 does not establish that it can simply requisition his capacity.

## 27.5 Ordinary life has intrinsic standing

The White Room’s structure makes romance and purposeless time structurally impossible.

ANHS becomes valuable to Ayanokōji precisely because ordinary life can be pursued for itself.

That difference changes the institution’s relation to self-authorship.

## 27.6 Internal conscience exists

Mashima’s conduct, Chairman Sakayanagi’s protection, and even Manabu’s developmental defense of the class system show institutional actors attempting to limit pure selection logic.

The safeguards are fragile.

Their existence remains decisive.

# 28. Is ANHS an alternative to the White Room?

The Year 1 answer is:

> **ANHS is a genuine but unstable alternative because it decentralizes developmental authorship without abandoning experimental control. It is also a socially distributed version of some White Room logic because it continues to rank, surveil, pressure, and remove students through environments adults design.**

Both halves are necessary.

Calling ANHS only a White Room analogue obscures:

- Ayanokōji’s genuine freedom from Atsuomi;
- ordinary peer life;
- multiple political constitutions;
- teacher dissent;
- student refusal;
- and developmental outcomes not controlled by one project.

Calling it simply liberation obscures:

- hidden criteria;
- enclosure;
- point-mediated inequality;
- manufactured scarcity;
- adult incentives;
- expulsion as intended output;
- and the vulnerability of surveillance and records to capture.

The institution’s moral promise lies in **distributed authorship**.

Its danger lies in mistaking distribution for accountability.

Several powerful actors may shape a student without the student gaining meaningful control. Plural manipulation is not the same thing as freedom.

ANHS becomes a stronger alternative when:

- purposes can be chosen by students;
- development survives refusal;
- information asymmetry is contestable;
- protection does not require submission;
- and no one actor controls both test and record.

It approaches White Room logic when:

- the institution treats students as material;
- leaders naturalize removal;
- private motives capture official mechanisms;
- and a person’s future is converted into an output of someone else’s experiment.

# 29. The institution inside Ayanokōji

Physical exit does not end institutional formation.

Ayanokōji’s statement that he remains inside the White Room after leaving identifies one of Year 1’s most important forms of surveillance:

> the subject becomes his own monitor.

He anticipates:

- danger;
- betrayal;
- replacement;
- utility;
- and final outcome

before permitting ordinary emotion to govern action.

## 29.1 Internalized surveillance regulates intimacy

The White Room did not formally ban romance. It removed the privacy, leisure, and unstructured time through which romantic experience could emerge (`Y1V07.5-E059`).

At ANHS, Ayanokōji acquires the missing environment. He initially approaches intimacy through:

- probability;
- curriculum;
- dependency diagnosis;
- information control;
- and risk management.

The new experience is therefore filtered through the old institution.

## 29.2 He reproduces institutional authorship in miniature

Ayanokōji often:

- diagnoses another person;
- designs the environment;
- withholds decisive information;
- predicts response;
- and judges the developmental outcome.

The method resembles an individual White Room:

> a superior observer creates the conditions under which another person is expected to change.

Year 1 also supplies counterevidence.

He can preserve privacy, withhold intervention, invite rather than order, and value outcomes he did not fully author. Kei’s rooftop refusal, Horikita’s autonomous strategies, Hiyori’s low-leverage friendship, and ordinary reciprocal romance all exceed his simplest control model.

## 29.3 Institutional recovery requires more than self-authorship

Ayanokōji’s decision to remain at ANHS and pursue ordinary life is genuinely self-chosen.

His later desire to develop students across the four classes is also self-chosen (`Y1V11.5-E104`).

That is progress beyond Atsuomi’s assigned purpose.

It is not sufficient ethical progress.

A self-authored experiment can still treat other people as subjects.

The institutional lesson of Year 1 therefore reaches inward:

> **Freedom from one author does not legitimate becoming an unanswerable author over others.**

# 30. Institutional defense after capture

Volume 11.5 offers a provisional architecture for defending institutional integrity.

Ayanokōji identifies several realities:

- direct confrontation with the acting chairman is strategically dangerous;
- private physical superiority cannot solve record power;
- personal favoritism would reproduce the wrong structure;
- and student-level information is insufficient against adult administration.

He therefore seeks:

- witnesses;
- monitoring;
- adults willing to recognize interference;
- and a legitimate account of self-defense (`Y1V11.5-E017–E024; Y1V11.5-E034–E035`).

## 30.1 The defense separates protection from favoritism

Ayanokōji does not ask Chairman Sakayanagi to guarantee that he cannot lose.

He asks for a field in which Tsukishiro cannot secretly decide the outcome.

The distinction is fundamental:

- **favoritism** changes the result for the protected person;
- **integrity protection** prevents another actor from falsifying the procedure.

## 30.2 Oversight distributes epistemic authority

Chairman Sakayanagi recommends Chabashira and Mashima. No single protector is expected to know everything or solve everything.

This creates a minimal separation of powers:

- the student detects threat;
- the chairman supplies institutional context;
- teachers monitor implementation;
- records can later be challenged through multiple witnesses.

The structure remains informal and vulnerable. It is still superior to private rescue based on one patron’s unquestionable authority.

## 30.3 Adult courage remains personally costly

Mashima’s judo intervention and willingness to monitor Tsukishiro show that institutional conscience requires accepting professional risk (`Y1V11.5-E034–E036`).

Rules do not enforce themselves.

A written safeguard becomes real only when an actor is willing to bear the cost of invoking it against power.

## 30.4 Student agency remains necessary

The adult network does not replace Ayanokōji’s own defense.

He continues to analyze time limits, likely methods, and information channels (`Y1V11.5-E022–E023`). Legitimate protection preserves his participation rather than turning him into a passive beneficiary.

This is the strongest Year 1 institutional model of protection:

> **distributed oversight + truthful recognition + preserved self-defense + bounded adult authority.**

# 31. Strong counterarguments

A serious institutional synthesis must test its thesis against the strongest rival readings.

## 31.1 Counterargument: ANHS is plainly not comparable to the White Room

ANHS contains ordinary classes, clubs, friends, romance, holidays, and meaningful choice. Students voluntarily seek admission. It would therefore be sensationalistic to compare it to an institution that deprived children of ordinary life from early childhood.

### Response

The objection is correct against **equivalence**.

The comparison remains analytically necessary because Year 1 itself places the institutions together through:

- artificial development;
- experimental cohorts;
- adaptation language;
- surveillance;
- designed exclusion;
- and the danger that effectiveness legitimates control (`Y1V11-E002–E010; Y1V11-E016–E025`).

The responsible claim is structural rhyme plus decisive difference, not identity.

## 31.2 Counterargument: hidden criteria are necessary to evaluate authentic conduct

If students know every criterion, they will perform for the test. The opening month’s secrecy is a defensible diagnostic design.

### Response

The method can produce useful information.

Its legitimacy depends on stakes, transparency after the fact, opportunity for correction, and proportional consequence. Hidden observation used for low-stakes feedback differs from hidden observation that can erase resources or trigger expulsion.

Year 1’s criticism concerns conversion from useful diagnosis into total judgment.

## 31.3 Counterargument: surveillance is necessary for safety in a high-risk school

Cameras and records deter violence, resolve disputes, and protect students.

### Response

Year 1 supports the safety function. It also shows:

- blind-spot mapping;
- nonreporting;
- dummy cameras;
- selective footage;
- partial context;
- sexualized intrusion;
- and administrative substitution.

The issue is not surveillance versus no surveillance. It is governance of collection, access, context, custody, correction, and deletion.

## 31.4 Counterargument: the Class Vote teaches unavoidable adult responsibility

Leaders must sometimes dismiss personnel, allocate scarce resources, and choose among bad outcomes.

### Response

The analogy is strongest when scarcity is external and the decisionmaker has a legitimate role.

ANHS manufactures the scarcity and attaches an unusually irreversible consequence to a process shaped by popularity, capital, retaliation, secrecy, and adult interference. The exam may teach judgment while remaining unjust as designed.

## 31.5 Counterargument: Tsukishiro is an aberration, not evidence about ANHS

He arrives with an external private mission. The school’s ordinary legitimacy should not be judged by a hostile infiltrator.

### Response

His motive may be external.

His capacity is institutional.

He can manipulate surveillance, policy, communication, and results because the acting chairmanship concentrates access without sufficient automatic checks. Tsukishiro is evidence about **capture vulnerability**, not proof that every school actor shares his aim.

## 31.6 Counterargument: Chairman Sakayanagi proves the institution is fundamentally protective

The chairman admits Ayanokōji and resists Atsuomi.

### Response

His conduct is genuinely protective. His displacement also shows why personal virtue is insufficient. If refuge depends on one benevolent officeholder, the protected person remains vulnerable to succession.

## 31.7 Counterargument: the White Room’s efficiency may justify severity under existential national needs

Year 1 does not reveal the full political context. Perhaps extreme training serves an overriding social purpose.

### Response

The local source does not provide enough evidence to adjudicate every possible necessity claim.

It does establish that Atsuomi treats the child as property, ordinary human domains were excluded without the child’s authority, failure carried severe costs, and the developed person rejects the assigned purpose. Even a compelling public need would require a separate argument for consent, proportionality, protection, and nonownership.

## 31.8 Counterargument: Ayanokōji is uniquely biased against institutions that constrain him

His narration may interpret ordinary rules through trauma and self-interest.

### Response

The method treats his first-person account as evidence rather than omniscience. Independent Year 1 evidence includes:

- Sakayanagi’s memory;
- her father’s warning;
- Atsuomi’s ownership statement;
- Mashima’s objections;
- Tsukishiro’s intervention;
- and the official record mismatch.

The institutional thesis does not rest on Ayanokōji’s judgment alone.

# 32. A Year 1 standard for legitimate developmental institutions

Year 1 does not reject demanding education.

It suggests conditions under which developmental power becomes more defensible.

## 32.1 Bounded purpose

The institution may pursue academic, physical, social, or leadership development without claiming authority over every dimension of the person’s life.

## 32.2 Transparent object

Students should know what a consequential evaluation purports to measure, even if every operational detail cannot be disclosed in advance.

## 32.3 Proportionate consequence

The more incomplete the measurement, the less legitimate an irreversible consequence becomes.

## 32.4 Developmental humility

Current performance should not be treated as a final ceiling. Sudō’s trajectory makes this requirement concrete (`Y1V10-E006–E007`).

## 32.5 Record integrity

Authentic performance must reach the official record without covert substitution. Correction must remain possible.

## 32.6 Contestable authority

Teachers, administrators, councils, and student leaders need channels through which their models can be challenged.

## 32.7 Protected refusal

A student’s ability should not automatically become institutional property. Refusal may carry disclosed collective consequences, but it must remain morally and practically intelligible.

## 32.8 Privacy and data governance

Collection should be tied to purpose. Access, retention, context, and deletion require rules rather than private discretion alone.

## 32.9 Protection without submission

The Chairman Sakayanagi model is stronger than the Chabashira or Atsuomi models because refuge does not demand service and remains bounded by general rules.

## 32.10 Plural developmental routes

Students should be able to become useful, excellent, or fulfilled through more than one institutional template.

## 32.11 Ordinary life as an end

Leisure, friendship, romance, and private meaning should not need to justify themselves through future performance.

## 32.12 Separated powers

The actor who designs the test should not possess unchecked authority to alter the evidence and final record for a private purpose.

No Year 1 institution fully satisfies this standard.

ANHS contains partial approximations.

The White Room, as directly established, violates its core anti-ownership principles.

# 36. Final synthesis

Year 1 begins by asking students to believe that freedom and merit naturally align.

They enter a prestigious enclosed campus, receive abundant points, and are told that conduct will shape opportunity.

The first lesson is that the institution had already converted freedom into observation.

The criteria were hidden.

The consequences were collective.

The students’ unguarded behavior became evidence because only the school knew that the opening month was an examination.

From there, institutional power becomes progressively more visible.

The school:

- sets prices on rescue;
- defines class hierarchy;
- creates scarcity;
- shapes which capacities matter;
- permits student government to approach rule authorship;
- makes expulsion part of competitive policy;
- and records outcomes that determine future options.

Students do not remain passive subjects.

They learn to:

- buy exceptions;
- exploit rules;
- stage evidence;
- use surveillance as an audience;
- build class constitutions;
- pressure the school through reputational risk;
- and even help author examination environments.

This reciprocal intelligence makes ANHS dynamic.

It does not make it just.

The institution’s most serious danger appears when developmental authority becomes proprietary.

Atsuomi provides the explicit form:

> I built the environment that made you; therefore you belong to me.

Chabashira reproduces a smaller version when she makes continued refuge conditional upon serving her Class A ambition.

Nagumo risks reproducing it when opportunity, capital, council access, and intimate leverage flow through his personal recognition.

Sakayanagi risks it when superior knowledge of classmates becomes sovereign allocation.

Ayanokōji risks it when psychological insight becomes a private right to design another person’s development.

Tsukishiro produces the administrative form when he converts office into control over surveillance, communication, and the official result.

The White Room is the clearest total institution because it attempts to make environment, purpose, evaluation, and future one continuous act of authorship.

ANHS differs because authorship is divided.

The student is shaped by:

- teachers;
- peers;
- enemies;
- friends;
- lovers;
- classes;
- councils;
- rules;
- and self-chosen goals.

No single actor can fully predict the outcome.

That incompleteness allows human development to exceed the model.

It also creates political struggle over who controls the gaps.

Year 1’s institutional achievement is therefore not the discovery of a perfectly fair school.

It is the discovery that **plurality can limit ownership**.

Chairman Sakayanagi can defend a student against a father.

Mashima can challenge an acting chairman.

Horikita can refuse a council path recommended by both brother and strategist.

Kōenji can insist that knowledge of talent does not make talent collective property.

Kei can answer rather than merely receive a relationship role.

Students can make ordinary life valuable for reasons no examination assigned.

These are not complete freedoms.

They are spaces in which authorship becomes contestable.

Tsukishiro’s intervention demonstrates why contestability must be institutionalized rather than hoped for.

A field can contain real effort and real development while the office controlling the record makes those achievements politically unreal.

The strongest Year 1 standard is consequently procedural and anti-proprietary:

> **A developmental institution may challenge, measure, and cultivate people only if it acknowledges the limits of its model; preserves purposes the subject can choose; makes consequential power visible and correctable; separates protection from submission; protects privacy and truthful records; and refuses to treat causal authorship over development as ownership of the developed person.**

ANHS partially satisfies that standard and repeatedly violates it.

The White Room, as Year 1 directly establishes it, violates its center.

That is why ANHS can remain Ayanokōji’s chosen refuge without becoming innocent.

He is free there not because nobody shapes him.

He is freer because many people shape one another, none is entitled to become the whole author, and ordinary life gives him reasons to resist any institution that claims his future is already written.
