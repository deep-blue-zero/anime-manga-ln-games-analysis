# 『ようこそ実力至上主義の教室へ』
## Year 1 - Class Politics, Leadership, and Constitutional Development
### Four constitutions under pressure: authority, information, dissent, weakness, and collective power

> **CANONICAL YEAR-1 SPECIALIST SNAPSHOT**
>
> This document is the primary analytical home for the political and constitutional structure of the four first-year classes at the end of Year 1. It synthesizes the fourteen canonical Japanese novel deep readings, the *First File* paratext audit, the final Year 1 evidence lock, the canonical class-polity and institution/exam ledgers, and the frozen `Y1_01-Y1_04` specialist layer.
>
> It does not import Year 2, Volume 0, *Second List*, Year 3, adaptation material, wikis, or later-franchise outcomes. Questions Year 1 leaves unresolved - including succession, class transfer, later expulsions, and the long-term viability of each polity - remain unresolved here.

---

# 0. Scope, primary home, and interpretive controls

The four first-year classes are not simply four teams with four strong personalities at the top.

By the end of Year 1 they have become four distinct political orders.

Each polity develops characteristic answers to recurring constitutional questions:

- Who is entitled to decide?
- What makes leadership legitimate?
- Who possesses strategically relevant information?
- Can information be challenged or corrected?
- How is dissent treated?
- What happens to weak or inconvenient members?
- What counts as useful ability?
- Can ability be developed, or only selected?
- Who bears risk when the institution imposes scarcity?
- What holds the group together when the leader fails?
- Can the polity survive succession?
- How does it treat rival classes?
- And what happens when administrative power above the students changes the rules or the official record?

The central claim of this document is that Year 1 uses the class competition to stage competing theories of collective action. The classes are not allegorical states in a one-to-one sense, and no modern political label should be treated as a perfect translation. But they are political enough to support constitutional analysis.

## 0.1 Stable polity IDs matter more than class letters

Class letters are institutional ranks, not permanent identities.

A class can rise or fall while preserving much of its internal political culture. The stable identifiers therefore remain:

- `POLITY-HORIKITA`;
- `POLITY-RYUEN`;
- `POLITY-ICHINOSE`;
- `POLITY-SAKAYANAGI-ORIGIN`.

At the *First File* year-end checkpoint their official positions are 347, 508, 550, and 1131 class points respectively (`Y1FF-E058`). Those totals are real institutional outcomes. They are not a scalar ordering of total human worth, moral legitimacy, or even every kind of future competitive capacity.

## 0.2 Effectiveness, legitimacy, and morality are separate variables

A polity can be strategically effective while morally objectionable.

A leader can be voluntarily followed while remaining coercive.

A class can be humane toward members while strategically vulnerable.

A highly legitimate decision can still be substantively mistaken.

This distinction is indispensable for Ryuuen. His post-defeat followers increasingly choose him, which strengthens sociological legitimacy, while his willingness to use coercion, intimidation, and `勝つための悪逆` - wrongdoing for the sake of victory - remains ethically serious.

It is equally indispensable for Ichinose. Her class's solidarity is not invalidated because it loses points. Strategic failure does not prove that humane membership rules were fake.

## 0.3 A class polity is not reducible to its leader

Horikita's class contains Hirata's social legitimacy, Kushida's network, Kei's social authority, Sudo's physical and later domain expertise, Ike's practical knowledge, Koenji's unavailable excellence, and Ayanokoji's hidden authorship.

Ryuuen's polity contains Ibuki's antagonistic dissent, Hiyori's quiet correction, Ishizaki and Albert's voluntary loyalty, Kaneda's planning capacity, and members whose willingness to remain becomes politically significant after the ruler loses.

Ichinose's polity contains Kanzaki as a possible internal counterweight, broad peer trust, distributed contribution, shared capital, and norms that exist beyond one person's charisma.

Sakayanagi's class contains Katsuragi's rival governing philosophy, Kamuro's coerced service, Hashimoto's independent insurance politics, a deep conventional talent pool, and internal factional history.

Leadership matters. The polity exceeds the leader.

## 0.4 Information governance is constitutional governance

Year 1 repeatedly makes information political.

The classes differ not only in who knows the most, but in how knowledge moves:

- Horikita increasingly depends on distributed domain knowledge while Ayanokoji privately controls a hidden information layer.
- Ryuuen centralizes phones, fears, incentives, vulnerabilities, and operational intelligence.
- Ichinose builds unusually high trust and relatively open internal information circulation.
- Sakayanagi centralizes an elite map of talent and strategically allocates people from above.

The governing question is therefore not merely which leader has the best model of classmates.

It is:

> **What political arrangement can use knowledge about people without turning knowledge into ownership of them?**

## 0.5 Development and selection must remain distinct

Every class can select among existing abilities.

A stronger constitution can also make future abilities possible.

The Year 1 corpus repeatedly distinguishes:

- possessed ability;
- developed ability;
- expressed ability;
- legible ability;
- socially usable ability;
- distributed ability;
- generative ability;
- and recorded ability.

A polity that merely finds the best current pieces differs from a polity that can make members more capable over time.

## 0.6 The institution is a fifth constitutional layer

The four classes do not possess final sovereignty.

Special examinations define scarcity, incentives, visibility, liability, and rescue costs. Student-council actors can approach rule authorship. Teachers can enforce, resist, or selectively interpret institutional demands. Administrators can control the infrastructure through which a result becomes official.

Tsukishiro's interference in the Event Selection Exam establishes the strongest local example: Ayanokoji's intended chess command is delayed and replaced, while the substituted move remains part of the official event result (`Y1V11-E140–E145`).

A class can therefore govern itself competently and still lose control of the institutional meaning of its own performance.

## 0.7 Year 1 remains an epistemic boundary

The analysis ends at Volume 11.5 plus *First File*.

Later developments may eventually answer whether:

- Horikita's class survives without hidden correction;
- Ryuuen's authority becomes less coercive;
- Ichinose builds stronger adversarial defenses;
- Sakayanagi's class develops succession capacity;
- or the school changes the relative value of their constitutions.

Those answers are not imported here.

# 1. Executive thesis

The four first-year classes become four competing solutions to the same problem:

> **How can unequal, partially known, self-interested people be turned into durable collective power without destroying the persons whose abilities make the collective possible?**

`POLITY-HORIKITA` begins with the weakest integration and perhaps the most heterogeneous latent portfolio. It develops toward **developmental pluralism**: leadership increasingly learns to identify, cultivate, coordinate, and defer to different capacities. Its strength is generative. Its danger is that the public constitution remains shadowed by Ayanokoji, who can alter information, protection, incentives, and feasible outcomes without appearing in the chain of accountability.

`POLITY-RYUEN` begins with the clearest answer to coordination failure: **centralized authoritarian mobilization**. Ryuuen turns fear, retaliation, information monopoly, and asymmetric tactics into speed and unity. The model is strategically powerful but creates a succession bottleneck and weakens distributed development. His defeat does not destroy the polity. Followers voluntarily preserve him, revealing that fear never fully explained his authority. Year 1 therefore ends with more sociological legitimacy than he began with, not with democratic or moral reform.

`POLITY-ICHINOSE` builds the strongest **solidaristic trust order**. Membership is protected, resources are pooled, information moves through personal trust, and collective identity survives truthful disclosure of the leader's wrongdoing. Its weakness is not fake kindness. It is the conversion problem: norms designed for reciprocity can be exploited by actors who do not reciprocate, and extraordinary social capacity does not automatically become enough class points or strategic leverage (`Y1FF-E034–E037`).

`POLITY-SAKAYANAGI-ORIGIN` develops into **aristocratic optimization**. Sakayanagi sees ability unusually well, allocates people to roles, controls strategic information, and treats unequal capacity as a fact to be governed rather than concealed. The class has the deepest conventional talent pool and finishes far ahead. Its constitutional vulnerability is centralization. A sovereign who possesses the best model of everyone can make the polity highly efficient while depriving it of independent model-builders, robust dissent, and succession. Her private rivalry with Ayanokoji further creates fiduciary risk because classmates bear consequences generated by a contest they did not author (`Y1FF-E038–E040`).

The year does not identify a single winner among these constitutions.

The point table says Sakayanagi's class wins Year 1 institutionally (`Y1FF-E058`).

The literary architecture asks a different question.

Which polity can:

- develop people rather than merely classify them;
- use information without monopolizing it;
- coordinate without converting members into property;
- preserve dissent without dissolving into paralysis;
- survive the leader's failure;
- protect members without making protection economically or emotionally impossible;
- and remain governable when the institution itself can author scarcity and even capture the record?

No class fully satisfies that standard.

The Year 1 political thesis is therefore:

> **Classroom of the Elite does not oppose good leadership to bad leadership. It stages four incomplete constitutions whose virtues become dangerous when absolutized: Horikita's development can become hidden paternalism, Ryuuen's decisiveness can become coercive sovereignty, Ichinose's solidarity can become strategically exploitable dependence, and Sakayanagi's accurate hierarchy can become proprietary rule. Political maturity lies not in eliminating asymmetry, competition, or judgment, but in making power correctable, distributed enough to survive failure, and limited by the continued personhood of those being governed.**

# 2. A constitutional comparison framework

The four polities should be compared across common dimensions rather than by one overall score.

## 2.1 Membership

What makes a person a member worth preserving?

- present usefulness;
- developmental potential;
- equal membership;
- loyalty;
- strategic value;
- or the sovereign's judgment?

The Class Vote makes this question explicit because the institution requires each class to translate a theory of membership into an expulsion outcome (`Y1V10-E013–E019`).

## 2.2 Source of authority

Authority can arise from:

- formal office;
- voluntary trust;
- expertise;
- fear;
- reputation;
- control of information;
- ability to protect;
- successful outcomes;
- or the capacity to impose costs.

Year 1 repeatedly shows that these sources need not align.

## 2.3 Information architecture

Who can know:

- classmates' abilities;
- vulnerabilities;
- secrets;
- resources;
- preferences;
- plans;
- and external relationships?

More importantly, who can correct the dominant model when it is wrong?

## 2.4 Dissent and corrigibility

A constitution is more robust when disagreement can become information rather than automatically becoming treason.

Keisei's Year 1 endpoint - support Horikita when right, challenge her when wrong - gives the clearest explicit local statement of this principle (`Y1V11.5-E011–E012`).

Hiyori's bounded refusal toward Ryuuen and Kanzaki's loyal criticism of Ichinose provide parallel cases.

## 2.5 Treatment of weakness

Does weakness imply:

- expulsion;
- discipline;
- development;
- redistribution;
- insurance;
- temporary protection;
- or equal membership regardless of output?

The correct answer may differ by kind of weakness. Year 1 resists treating academic weakness, social volatility, deliberate sabotage, physical disability, and autonomous noncooperation as one problem.

## 2.6 Developmental capacity

Can the class make a member more capable?

Sudo is the clearest case of a member whose political value changes over time. Chabashira explicitly acknowledges that growth can overturn expectations (`Y1V10-E006–E007`). That fact destabilizes any constitution that treats present utility as destiny.

## 2.7 Coercion

Every polity operates inside institutional coercion.

The important distinction is how much additional coercion the polity itself creates, who controls it, and whether it is visible.

## 2.8 Protection and rescue

Who is entitled to rescue?

How is rescue financed?

Does protection create more autonomy or greater dependence?

The point economy turns mercy into a budgetary problem, especially during the Mixed Training Camp and Class Vote (`Y1V08-E020–E026`; `Y1V10-E013–E019`).

## 2.9 Succession

What remains if the central leader is absent, defeated, expelled, emotionally incapacitated, or no longer willing to govern?

Ryuuen's rooftop defeat makes this question visible first. Ayanokoji's hidden role makes it equally serious for Horikita's class. Sakayanagi's centralization makes it structurally important even while her polity is winning.

## 2.10 External relations

Classes do not exist in isolation.

They form alliances, bargains, rescue markets, information exchanges, rivalries, and temporary communities. A constitution that works internally may fail when facing an opponent with radically different norms.

## 2.11 Record sovereignty

Finally, who controls the official story of performance?

A class can win a causal contest and lose the record. Volume 11 makes this a constitutional problem rather than a technical footnote.

# 3. The political progression of Year 1

Year 1's class politics develop in five broad movements.

## 3.1 Volumes 1-2: latent ability without a constitution

Horikita's class begins as a collection of poorly coordinated individuals. Hirata possesses social legitimacy, Horikita possesses visible academic competence, Kushida possesses accessibility, and Ayanokoji begins influencing outcomes from concealment.

The early crisis establishes a principle that remains political throughout the year: an individually correct answer is not enough. The class needs someone who can translate ability into socially usable form.

The Sudo disciplinary case adds a second principle. Truth requires credibility, proof, advocacy, and institutional legibility. A class cannot protect a member merely by believing him.

Ichinose's cross-class assistance already supplies a countermodel: procedural knowledge and social trust can be political resources without requiring domination.

## 3.2 Volumes 3-4.5: four information regimes become visible

The island exam turns classes into strategic organizations.

Horikita becomes formal visible leader while Ayanokoji authors decisive hidden conditions. Ryuuen develops espionage and centralized operational control. Ichinose's class distributes tasks through trust. The future Sakayanagi polity remains internally factional and comparatively guarded.

The zodiac examination then makes trust itself a resource. Ichinose uses bounded openness. Ryuuen centralizes phones and information. Ayanokoji constructs a private intelligence channel through Kei (`Y1V04-E019–E027`).

Volume 4.5 matters because ordinary summer life reveals that political organization is not the whole of human relation. Students create attachments and preferences outside formal scoring. A constitution that claims to measure every valuable relation through points would therefore already be incomplete.

## 3.3 Volumes 5-7.5: leadership becomes relational and succession becomes visible

The sports festival exposes the limits of individual excellence.

Horikita and Sudo learn that coordination requires mutual adaptation. Ryuuen demonstrates how authoritarian intelligence can exploit injury, schedule information, and deniable pressure. Sakayanagi emerges more clearly as a competing theory of elite ability.

The Paper Shuffle then distinguishes integration from removal. Horikita attempts to convert Kushida from existential threat into governable member, while Ayanokoji prefers efficient pruning. The dispute is constitutional because it asks whether dangerous members remain candidates for development.

Volume 7 destroys Ryuuen's undefeated sovereignty. Yet his polity does not dissolve. Ibuki, Ishizaki, and Albert choose to preserve him (`Y1V07-E101–E105`). The first succession crisis therefore reveals a paradox: a coercive ruler can produce or uncover attachments that outlive the coercive foundation.

Volume 7.5 broadens the question upward. Student-council power can influence the design of future examinations (`Y1V07.5-E042–E043`). Political authority is beginning to approach rule authorship rather than mere rule adaptation.

## 3.4 Volumes 8-10: temporary polities, information warfare, and manufactured scarcity

The Mixed Training Camp creates temporary cross-class groups and prices leadership risk. Compulsory proximity produces real cooperation, while Nagumo demonstrates that actors with prior networks can weaponize the rule field (`Y1V08-E006–E026`; `Y1V08-E093–E095`; `Y1V08-E130–E142`).

Volume 9 moves from formal examinations to reputation. Sakayanagi authors an attack on Ichinose's public identity. Ichinose's class survives because membership does not depend on moral purity (`Y1V09-E087–E100`). Ayanokoji demonstrates that anonymous information can be used to alter the institution's cost calculation (`Y1V09-E058–E061`; `Y1V09-E078–E086`).

The Class Vote is the decisive constitutional stress test. The same forced scarcity produces four different responses:

- accountable triage in Horikita's polity;
- collective rescue and equal-risk fallback in Ichinose's;
- sovereign pruning in Sakayanagi's;
- and voluntary subordinate rescue of a fallen ruler in Ryuuen's.

The exam does not merely identify a loser. It reveals what each class believes membership permits it to do.

## 3.5 Volumes 11-11.5 and First File: portfolio government, reciprocal correction, and the limits of the file

The Event Selection Exam rewards class-wide portfolio knowledge. A commander cannot personally perform every event. Leadership therefore becomes the capacity to know who can do what, when to intervene, when to delegate, and how to make distributed skill usable (`Y1V11-E027–E045`).

Horikita's class improves because Sudo has developed beyond stale information, Hirata has returned, and Horikita increasingly treats classmates as sources of domain knowledge rather than merely objects of evaluation. Koenji simultaneously establishes the autonomy limit: his talent is not automatically collective property (`Y1V11-E062–E066`).

Volume 11.5 then introduces more explicit internal correction. Keisei defines loyalty as support plus dissent (`Y1V11.5-E011–E012`). Hiyori corrects Ryuuen (`Y1V11.5-E053–E056`). Horikita and Ichinose end an alliance without turning separation into hostility (`Y1V11.5-E059–E063`).

*First File* finally converts the four polities into institutional summaries. Its classifications are useful precisely because they are bounded. It records:

- Horikita's class as explosive individual potential with inadequate cohesion (`Y1FF-E025`);
- Ryuuen's as coordinated dictatorship overdependent on one leader (`Y1FF-E033`);
- Ichinose's as highly cohesive and protective but weak at result conversion (`Y1FF-E037`);
- Sakayanagi's as deep talent with strong allocation but monopolized strategy and incomplete unity (`Y1FF-E040`).

The guidebook does not replace the novels. It retrospectively sharpens the constitutional comparison.

# 4. `POLITY-HORIKITA` - developmental pluralism under a shadow constitution

`POLITY-HORIKITA` is the most dynamically changing class polity in Year 1 because its defining problem is not lack of individual talent. It is failure of aggregation.

*First File* states the end-of-year problem unusually clearly: the class contains distinctive students with explosive potential, but cohesion remains inadequate and leadership must aggregate individualists (`Y1FF-E025`).

That description is consistent with the year-long evidence. The polity's comparative advantage is heterogeneity. Its comparative weakness is that heterogeneity does not naturally become common action.

## 4.1 Initial constitution: overlapping authorities without unified government

The class does not begin with one ruler.

It begins with several partially overlapping forms of authority:

- Hirata has voluntary social legitimacy and emotional accessibility.
- Horikita has visible academic competence and increasingly public strategic credit.
- Kushida possesses broad social reach and trust access.
- Kei controls a substantial part of the girls' social atmosphere.
- Sudo possesses obvious physical capacity but initially weak self-regulation.
- Koenji possesses exceptional ability that the class cannot reliably mobilize.
- Ayanokoji possesses the strongest integrative capacity while deliberately withholding it from public office.

This is not liberal pluralism in a mature constitutional sense. It is fragmentation.

The class's early problem is that the different authorities do not form a shared decision architecture. Hirata cannot solve technical strategy. Horikita cannot initially generate broad voluntary cooperation. Kushida's public accessibility conceals private hostility. Kei's social authority is partly defensive. Ayanokoji coordinates without accountability.

The first-year story is therefore the conversion of overlapping social powers into something closer to government.

## 4.2 Horikita's first error: current weakness as political verdict

Early Horikita treats ability as relatively individual, visible, and stable.

Students who create collective costs appear as defects. That reasoning is not irrational inside ANHS. A failing student can impose point losses or expulsion risk on everyone else.

The problem is the inference from present liability to total future worth.

Sudo becomes the decisive counterexample. His academic weakness and volatility create real danger, but his athletic ability later becomes class value, and his capacity for discipline, dissent, and leadership changes substantially. Horikita eventually explicitly attributes his improvement to `彼自身の努力の結果` - the result of his own effort - rather than claiming him as her creation (`Y1V10-E006–E007`).

That formulation is constitutionally important. Developmental leadership does not imply ownership of the person developed.

## 4.3 Pedagogy becomes a prototype for politics

Horikita's first serious teaching failure reveals the same problem that later appears at class scale.

Knowing the answer does not mean another person can use the answer.

She has to research basketball, translate material into Sudo's conceptual world, and adjust her own method. That movement from judgment to translation becomes a political model: a leader must understand not only what a member lacks, but how that member can actually receive instruction.

The later class constitution repeatedly applies the same logic.

A leader does not have to become the best athlete, social mediator, or practical field specialist. She has to know when another person's domain competence should govern the local decision.

## 4.4 The island victory creates office through partly false attribution

Volume 3 is constitutionally double-edged.

Horikita performs genuine labor, bears visible responsibility, and grows under pressure. Ayanokoji also authors decisive hidden conditions and allows the public result to consolidate around her.

The outcome creates real office through incomplete causal history.

That is the origin of the **shadow constitution**.

The visible polity increasingly organizes around Horikita, Hirata, and other public actors. The hidden polity includes Ayanokoji's control over decisive information, private networks, rescue contingencies, and opponent modeling.

The problem is not that Horikita's leadership is fake. It is that the class cannot fully audit the conditions under which that leadership succeeds.

## 4.5 The shadow constitution expands through Kei

Volume 4 makes the hidden layer more durable.

Ayanokoji identifies Kei's social authority and constructs a private relationship through which he gains eyes, ears, and influence inside a part of the class Horikita cannot easily reach (`Y1V04-E019–E027`).

From a narrow strategic perspective this solves a coordination problem.

From a constitutional perspective it creates an executive network outside the public chain of accountability.

Horikita can govern visible class decisions while another actor privately controls information she does not know exists.

This is the polity's central Year 1 contradiction:

> **public developmental leadership grows inside an informational environment that is not fully public.**

## 4.6 Volume 5: coordination replaces self-sufficiency

The sports festival is the first major class-wide demonstration that leadership requires relational coordination.

Horikita's individual competence cannot compensate for weak group integration. The three-legged race literalizes the principle: one person's superior pace can produce failure when bodies cannot coordinate.

Her developing relation with Sudo supplies the correction. He becomes governable not because fear forces him into line but because recognition, discipline, attraction, pride, and reciprocal commitment begin to interact.

This is a politically healthier form of authority than simple command because the follower's later agency increases rather than contracts.

## 4.7 Paper Shuffle: integration versus removal

The Kushida problem forces Horikita to distinguish developmental pluralism from indiscriminate preservation.

She resists Ayanokoji's preferred expulsion solution and attempts to create enforceable noninterference through a wager. Importantly, she refuses to stake Ayanokoji without his consent; he must join voluntarily (`Y1V06-E039–E040`).

The result is not reconciliation. Kushida refuses the handshake.

The political achievement is narrower: conflict becomes bounded by covenant.

The political risk remains severe because Kushida's knowledge and willingness to sabotage classmates expose people who did not consent to Horikita's rehabilitation project.

Developmental leadership therefore reaches its first constitutional limit:

> a leader's belief in another person's future cannot automatically authorize imposing the risk of that experiment on everyone else.

## 4.8 Volume 7: apparent succession readiness, incomplete causal independence

By Volume 7 Ayanokoji can imagine the visible class functioning without his constant intervention.

Horikita has improved perception. Sudo has internalized restraint. Hirata remains a social institution. Kei and Kushida provide different forms of connectivity. The class has more differentiated functions than it possessed at entrance.

Yet the claim that the class can now stand without him is underinformed from the class's own perspective.

Its members do not know that their recent environment includes:

- a coerced/private Kei network;
- Ayanokoji's handling of Ryuuen;
- adult sanctuary;
- rooftop violence;
- evidence management;
- and multiple hidden interfaces with Manabu and teachers.

A polity cannot be declared constitutionally independent merely because its hidden sovereign believes he could withdraw.

## 4.9 Volumes 8-9: distributed social infrastructure and externalized information power

The Mixed Training Camp shows that Horikita's class members can operate across temporary polities. The exam forces students to depend on people outside ordinary class lines, demonstrating that political competence is partly portable.

Volume 9 adds a different lesson. Kushida's broad social network becomes a resource inside Ayanokoji's information campaign. The network is real class capacity, but its use is not publicly authorized.

This sharpens the distinction between **possessing infrastructure** and **governing infrastructure**.

A class may own a powerful social network in the descriptive sense while lacking collective control over how its information is deployed.

## 4.10 The Class Vote: accountable triage

The Class Vote forces `POLITY-HORIKITA` to confront manufactured scarcity.

Horikita condemns the exam's political quality - `こんなもの、試験とは呼べないわ` - because anonymous bloc politics can generate an expulsion without producing a public reason (`Y1V10-E050`).

She responds by making a criterion and candidate public.

That move is constitutionally important because it converts hidden coalition power into answerable judgment.

It does not prove that Yamauchi is objectively the least valuable human being. The evidence includes current contribution, betrayal, and future-value judgments, but the same volume explicitly acknowledges developmental uncertainty (`Y1V10-E006–E007`; `Y1V10-E051–E055`).

Sudo challenges her through friendship and forces the substitution problem into the open: saving one candidate exposes another (`Y1V10-E058–E059`). Hirata objects more radically to the class's moral jurisdiction over disposable membership.

Horikita's achievement is therefore **public liability**, not infallibility.

She owns the judgment rather than hiding behind anonymous procedure.

## 4.11 Hirata as an internal constitutional counterweight

Hirata matters because he represents a different theory of membership.

Horikita can accept selective sacrifice under forced scarcity if the class must act. Hirata resists the premise that the class should convert peers into ranked lives at all.

His position becomes politically paralyzing because refusal to choose cannot itself cancel the institution's mechanism.

Yet his objection remains morally necessary. Without it, developmental pluralism can become technocratic pruning whenever the institution creates enough pressure.

The class's stronger future constitution would need both capacities:

- Horikita's ability to make accountable decisions under scarcity;
- Hirata's insistence that institutional scarcity does not magically make people disposable.

## 4.12 Volume 11: portfolio government

The Event Selection Exam is the strongest Year 1 test of distributed operational leadership.

Horikita argues that `全員が協力し合う必要` - everyone needs to cooperate - because the class cannot win through one exceptional performer (`Y1V11-E050–E053`).

The exam rewards:

- knowledge of classmates;
- event design;
- substitution depth;
- delegation;
- motivation;
- and intervention timing (`Y1V11-E027–E045`).

Sudo's development becomes strategic surprise because opponents rely on stale data. Leadership has generated new capacity rather than merely revealing an old one.

Horikita also relies on Sudo's athletic judgment. This is a mature political movement: she governs a domain partly by deferring to someone with better local knowledge.

## 4.13 Koenji: the autonomy boundary

Koenji supplies the hardest limit to collectivist claims over ability.

He tells Horikita that whether his talent is used for her purposes is his own decision (`Y1V11-E062–E066`).

The statement is politically inconvenient and constitutionally clarifying.

A class may benefit from a member's talent. It does not follow that the class owns the talent.

This produces a tension no Year 1 polity fully solves:

- ANHS imposes collective consequences based on class membership;
- but individual capacities remain partly voluntary.

Horikita's developing strength is that she increasingly plans around the capacities actually available rather than assuming every talented person can be requisitioned.

## 4.14 Horikita becomes able to read the hidden reader

By Volume 11 she can identify that Ayanokoji predicted how she would act (`Y1V11-E085–E087`).

This is politically significant because invisible authorship becomes weaker once it can be named.

She cannot yet audit the full shadow constitution.

But she can increasingly detect that the environment has been authored.

The Volume 11.5 academic wager takes the next step. She confronts hidden matchmaking and contribution, then constructs a single-subject test whose outcome will impose future obligations (`Y1V11.5-E064–E068`).

A severe information asymmetry becomes partially contestable.

## 4.15 Volume 11.5: corrigibility becomes explicit

Keisei's pledge is the cleanest Year 1 constitutional statement for the mature Horikita polity:

- support when the leader is right;
- challenge when she is wrong.

Ayanokoji explicitly treats this as the proper class structure (`Y1V11.5-E011–E012`).

This is stronger than obedience and stronger than leaderless consensus.

It assumes:

- leadership is necessary;
- leaders can be wrong;
- followers possess independent judgment;
- and dissent can be loyal.

Horikita's leadership also becomes more self-authored. She stops chasing Manabu as specification and describes a path in which she will learn with and lead classmates (`Y1V11.5-E090–E093`).

The political significance is not psychological independence alone. A leader who no longer needs to prove worth by imitating a superior is better positioned to revise rather than defend inherited answers.

## 4.16 Ending the Ichinose alliance: separation as sovereign capacity

Horikita and Ichinose end their alliance because continued cooperation would constrain each class's pursuit of rank (`Y1V11.5-E059–E063`).

Neither treats separation as betrayal.

That is a sign of political maturation. A polity must be able to terminate a bargain when interests change without requiring demonization of the former partner.

The scene demonstrates an important difference between **relation** and **alignment**.

Respect can survive the end of coalition.

## 4.17 Final Year 1 constitutional form

The Year 1 endpoint is:

> **developmental pluralism with growing public accountability and corrigibility, operating under an unresolved shadow constitution.**

Its strengths are:

- heterogeneous talent;
- ability to generate new capacity;
- increasing delegation;
- increasing tolerance of loyal dissent;
- public ownership of difficult judgments;
- and growing recognition that ability remains partly autonomous.

Its vulnerabilities are:

- incomplete cohesion;
- Koenji's nonmobilizable excellence;
- Kushida's unresolved security threat;
- dependency on Ayanokoji's hidden information and contingency systems;
- and the possibility that development itself becomes paternalistic jurisdiction.

The class's final Year 1 question is not whether Horikita can lead.

She can.

It is:

> **Can the polity make its real constitution visible enough that the people governed by it can correct the powers on which their survival actually depends?**

# 5. `POLITY-RYUEN` - authoritarian mobilization after fear stops being enough

`POLITY-RYUEN` begins as the clearest Year 1 example of centralized coercive government.

Its strength is not simply that Ryuuen is violent.

His real political talent is the ability to combine:

- fear;
- incentive;
- information;
- retaliation;
- deception;
- and rapid operational coordination.

*First File* captures the contradiction. Ryuuen is rated highly in judgment and very poorly in cooperation, while the editorial summary describes overwhelming leadership alongside a form of class-directed obligation (`Y1FF-E026–E029`). The class summary then identifies coordinated dictatorship, overdependence on the ruler, and weak individual development (`Y1FF-E033`).

## 5.1 Fear as constitutional shortcut

Ryuuen's early polity solves a real collective-action problem.

If members disagree, conceal information, or pursue private interests, strategy slows.

Fear shortens the negotiation.

The leader makes defiance expensive enough that operational unity becomes rational.

This produces speed.

It also produces silence.

The difference matters. A silent subordinate may look coordinated while withholding information the ruler needs.

## 5.2 Information monopoly is the core of the regime

Ryuuen does not rely on physical intimidation alone.

He centralizes strategically useful information and tries to identify the hidden decision-maker behind Horikita's class. During the zodiac exam, phones and favored-person information become objects of control (`Y1V04-E019–E028`).

The regime's theory is approximately:

> people are predictable because fear, desire, money, pride, and vulnerability can be mapped and exploited.

This makes Ryuuen an effective adversarial model-builder.

The danger is epistemic as well as moral. If everyone is interpreted principally through leverage, voluntary motives become easy to underread.

That is exactly what happens after his defeat.

## 5.3 Strategic strength: asymmetric politics

Ryuuen repeatedly attacks where formal rules and social norms diverge.

His polity is willing to use:

- espionage;
- deniable pressure;
- strategic injury;
- evidentiary traps;
- intimidation;
- and attacks on opponents' expectations.

This makes the class dangerous even when its conventional academic portfolio is weaker than Sakayanagi's.

The political advantage is adaptability under conflict.

The moral cost is that other people are treated as environments to be manipulated rather than co-authors of the rules of interaction.

## 5.4 Authoritarianism contains a protection function

Ryuuen is not merely extracting from followers.

Volume 7 shows that he is willing to absorb responsibility for the rooftop and attempt to keep subordinates outside punishment (`Y1V07-E098`, `Y1V07-E104`).

That matters because coercive leaders can possess genuine fiduciary impulses toward their in-group.

It helps explain why later loyalty is not reducible to terror.

Protection, however, does not erase domination. A ruler may both protect followers and deny them meaningful influence over the methods used in their name.

## 5.5 The rooftop destroys the myth of irresistible sovereignty

Ayanokoji's victory matters politically because Ryuuen's regime relies partly on the credibility of personal supremacy.

Once the ruler is decisively defeated, fear no longer explains why the class should remain organized around him.

This creates Year 1's first serious succession crisis.

Ryuuen initially withdraws.

If the polity were nothing but fear, the political order should simply dissolve or transfer to whoever can impose the next strongest coercion.

That is not what happens.

## 5.6 Ibuki: dissent from inside the order

Ibuki's relation to Ryuuen is constitutionally valuable because it is never pure obedience.

She refuses active participation in parts of the rooftop torture and objects to escalation (`Y1V07-E058`; `Y1V07-E081`). Her refusal is incomplete; she remains present and implicated.

After the defeat she nevertheless waits for him, returns the phone, and uses his own doctrine of repeated defeat against his attempt to quit (`Y1V07-E101`).

Her loyalty therefore takes the form of antagonistic correction.

The follower does not simply preserve the sovereign.

She demands that he remain answerable to the standards he imposed.

## 5.7 Ishizaki and Albert: voluntary preservation after the ruler loses

Ishizaki and Albert threaten to leave if Ryuuen leaves (`Y1V07-E104–E105`).

That choice is among the most important political facts in the class's Year 1 arc.

The leader has lost the aura of invincibility.

The followers still choose continuation.

Possible motives include:

- belief in strategic competence;
- gratitude;
- shared identity;
- excitement;
- protection history;
- personal attachment;
- or absence of a preferable alternative.

The novels do not reduce the choice to one motive.

What changes is the authority structure.

Fear becomes insufficient as a total explanation.

## 5.8 Voluntary loyalty increases sociological legitimacy, not moral legitimacy

This distinction must remain explicit.

A leader can become more legitimate because people choose him after an opportunity to leave.

That does not prove the methods he uses against enemies are ethical.

By the Year 1 boundary Ryuuen remains willing to endorse severe, underhanded, and potentially criminal tactics so long as they are effective and better concealed. Ayanokoji's later coaching toward cleaner operational security is development in strategic sophistication, not conversion to humane politics.

The polity becomes more voluntary without becoming liberal.

## 5.9 Hiyori: the constitution's quiet corrective channel

Hiyori is the strongest evidence that the class can contain loyal refusal.

She questions whether the hunt for X is necessary, refuses participation, and still offers help if a genuine need arises (`Y1V07-E051`).

By Volume 11.5 she questions Ryuuen's dangerous methods and insists that a person he respects should correct him (`Y1V11.5-E049–E057`).

She does not challenge him by imitating his aggression.

That matters.

A constitution becomes more robust when dissent does not require the dissenter to become another version of the ruler.

## 5.10 Decentralized competence begins to appear

Volumes 8-9 show class members acting while Ryuuen is less formally visible. Hiyori, Ishizaki, and others exercise local judgment.

This does not establish a stable successor government.

It does show that the polity contains capacities its original fear model underdescribed.

The class is therefore not simply one mastermind plus interchangeable subordinates.

## 5.11 The Class Vote: the ruler becomes the object of preservation

The Class Vote reverses the normal direction of authoritarian protection.

Ryuuen is prepared to accept removal.

Followers construct routes to save him.

The polity therefore reveals a form of political attachment in which members preserve the leader even after his original legitimacy has been broken.

This is neither democracy nor mere brainwashing.

It is **emergent voluntary loyalism** inside a coercive political tradition.

The constitutional question becomes whether loyalty can be institutionalized into something that survives him, or whether it merely reconstitutes personal rule.

## 5.12 Year-end form

The Year 1 endpoint is:

> **authoritarian mobilization whose legitimacy has become more voluntary while strategic authorship remains highly centralized and coercive ethics persist.**

Its strengths are:

- speed;
- offensive imagination;
- willingness to exploit asymmetry;
- a leader skilled at modeling incentives;
- and followers whose attachment proves more durable than the original fear theory predicted.

Its vulnerabilities are:

- succession dependence;
- weak distributed development;
- informational bottlenecks;
- a tendency to confuse leverage with total motivation;
- and ethical exposure created by tactics that depend on intimidation or deniability.

The final question is:

> **Can Ryuuen convert people who choose him into a constitution that can correct him - or does voluntary loyalty merely make personal sovereignty more durable?**

# 6. `POLITY-ICHINOSE` - solidarity as infrastructure, not innocence

`POLITY-ICHINOSE` offers the strongest Year 1 counterexample to the assumption that politics becomes serious only when it becomes cynical.

The class's warmth is not the absence of structure.

It is a structure.

Trust lowers transaction costs. Reputation travels quickly. Members pool resources. Information can move without every exchange requiring threat or proof. The class can coordinate sacrifice because people expect others to reciprocate.

*First File* preserves this distinction. Ichinose's personal profile is conventionally strong and the class summary records high cohesion, collective savings, no expulsions, and insufficient conversion of those capacities into results (`Y1FF-E034–E037`).

The constitutional problem is therefore not:

> Is kindness real?

It is:

> **Can a polity whose strongest resource is reciprocal trust survive actors and institutions that do not reciprocate?**

## 6.1 Early strength: trust as low-cost coordination

Ichinose's early political value appears in practical form.

She can help another class understand procedure without immediately treating information as a weapon. Her own class distributes work through voluntary cooperation. In the zodiac exam she can use bounded openness as a strategic resource rather than assuming that all secrecy is strength.

This does not make Class B naive.

It means the polity possesses unusually high **relational capital**.

Members can coordinate because they do not need to price every interaction through fear, contractual enforcement, or leader surveillance.

## 6.2 Collective private points are a political institution

The class's point pooling is one of Year 1's clearest examples of economic infrastructure becoming constitutional infrastructure.

What outsiders may interpret as suspicious collectivism becomes, during the Class Vote, a rescue reserve.

The point pool matters for three reasons.

First, it converts trust into material capacity.

Second, it allows the class to socialize risk that ANHS otherwise individualizes.

Third, it reveals a political cost: the more the collective can mobilize private resources, the more members must trust whoever coordinates them.

The institution therefore exposes a general principle:

> **solidarity becomes politically consequential when it can move resources, not merely feelings.**

## 6.3 Ichinose's leadership is highly legitimate because people voluntarily expose themselves

During the Class Vote Ichinose asks classmates to surrender all private points. They comply rapidly enough to make the rescue plan viable (`Y1V10-E071`).

That is extraordinary authority.

It is not authoritarian authority.

The leader cannot simply seize the points. The transaction works because members believe:

- she will not privately steal the fund;
- she is acting for collective preservation;
- others will also contribute;
- and their vulnerability will not be exploited from inside the class.

This is one of the strongest examples in Year 1 of legitimacy as voluntary exposure to shared risk.

## 6.4 Solidarity can consume the leader

The same constitution creates danger for Ichinose.

When the class remains several million points short, Nagumo can exploit the gap. His condition is intimate rather than merely financial: Ichinose must date him (`Y1V10-E072–E075`).

Ichinose recognizes that the arrangement would violate something important about reciprocal romance and even fears `自分が自分でなくなってしまう` - becoming no longer herself.

She nearly accepts anyway because she experiences the leader's body and future as potentially available for class rescue.

This exposes a constitutional pathology inside otherwise humane solidarity:

> **if the leader embodies the whole group's promise of mutual protection, the leader may begin treating herself as collective property.**

A polity can therefore be non-disposable toward ordinary members while becoming exploitatively disposable toward its protector.

## 6.5 The Class Vote: universal rescue as constitutional principle

The class's first response is categorical preservation.

It attempts to save everyone.

After Ayanokoji's cross-class transaction supplies the remaining capital, Kanzaki accepts the technical criticism target, the class spends twenty million private points, and the expulsion is cancelled (`Y1V10-E087`; `Y1V10-E098–E104`).

Ichinose's reasoning is explicit: points can be earned again; a lost classmate cannot be restored (`Y1V10-E103`).

This is not simply sentiment.

It is a theory of membership:

> class membership is not a commodity whose loss is equivalent to a sufficiently large point gain.

The institution prices expulsion.

Ichinose's class refuses to interpret the price as moral equivalence.

## 6.6 The lottery fallback is the strongest egalitarian statement

The rescue succeeds, but the fallback reveals even more.

If no financial route existed, the class had agreed to use a lottery rather than rank members by present ability (`Y1V10-E105`).

This is not strategically optimal if the objective is maximizing measurable class strength.

That is precisely why it matters.

The class's principle is:

> if an unjust or morally arbitrary scarcity must fall on someone, current school utility does not automatically give us rightful jurisdiction to declare which person's future is worth less.

Equalized risk does not make forced expulsion just.

It refuses to convert ability ranking into a moral theory of continued membership.

## 6.7 The successful rescue does not make every feature of the constitution safe

Universal rescue can create several vulnerabilities.

It can:

- consume enormous resources;
- make adversaries confident that the class will pay almost any price to prevent loss;
- concentrate pressure on Ichinose as symbolic center;
- and delay hard judgments about members who knowingly harm the group.

The point is not that the class should become harsher.

The point is that unconditional membership requires **security architecture** if opponents know the commitment is unconditional.

## 6.8 Volume 9: legitimacy survives truthful wrongdoing

The rumor crisis tests whether solidarity depends on leader purity.

It does not.

Ichinose's past theft is true. The class does not preserve her by pretending it never happened. Members accept the act without reducing the entire person to the act (`Y1V09-E087–E100`).

This is politically mature recognition.

A leader can lose innocence without losing all legitimacy.

That distinguishes Class B from a charismatic cult in which the leader must remain morally immaculate.

The class's trust is longitudinal. It is based on accumulated experience of the person rather than one purified myth.

## 6.9 Kanzaki: loyal opposition is underdeveloped but indispensable

Kanzaki matters because high-cohesion polities need internal dissent more than they may initially realize.

His role is not to prove Ichinose wrong on every issue.

It is to preserve the possibility that loyalty can contain disagreement.

During the Class Vote he participates in the rescue without requiring a purity proof. His willingness to serve as the technical target also demonstrates trust in the collective mechanism.

Year 1 does not yet show a fully institutionalized opposition role.

But the need is visible.

A class whose members are highly reluctant to wound one another may need a person willing to say that protection and strategic adaptation are not always the same objective.

## 6.10 The Event Selection Exam: reciprocity meets adversarial nonreciprocity

Volume 11 exposes the polity's central strategic weakness.

Ryuuen's return as commander destabilizes Ichinose because her planning assumed his political absence (`Y1V11-E109–E111`).

His tactics then exploit B class's expectation that competition will remain inside a more conventional normative field (`Y1V11-E123–E130`).

The class's cohesion remains operationally real (`Y1V11-E126`).

The problem is that internal trust does not itself generate a defense against an opponent who regards norm violation as another instrument.

This is the strongest evidence for the year-end correction:

> **solidarity is political capacity, but solidarity is not adversarial security.**

## 6.11 The correct reform is not to become Ryuuen

A weak reading of the defeat would say:

> Ichinose must become ruthless.

The corpus does not require that conclusion.

The class's trust generates real advantages that would be lost if it adopted pervasive suspicion.

The more precise requirement is:

- preserve internal trust;
- improve threat modeling;
- distinguish reciprocal partners from nonreciprocal adversaries;
- develop contingency plans that do not require moral imitation of the opponent;
- and distribute strategic judgment so Ichinose's emotional state is not the sole center of class direction.

This is institutional adaptation rather than ideological conversion.

## 6.12 Ending the Horikita alliance: solidarity does not imply permanent coalition

Volume 11.5 gives an important positive sign.

Horikita and Ichinose recognize that continued alliance now conflicts with their classes' competitive interests. They end it openly and preserve personal respect (`Y1V11.5-E059–E063`).

Ichinose therefore demonstrates that warmth does not require permanent alignment.

A solidaristic polity can set boundaries.

This is politically important because critics of Class B often assume kindness makes separation impossible.

Year 1 provides contrary evidence.

## 6.13 Private attachment does not erase public obligation

*First File* adds a final political clarification.

Ichinose spends part of spring break thinking alone about the class and Year 2 (`Y1FF-E074–E076`). Ayanokoji's support restores warmth and forward motion, and her feeling toward him exceeds the easy category of `大切な友達` - precious friend - without fully resolving into a named relationship (`Y1FF-E077–E080`).

She nevertheless returns herself to the class contest.

The point is constitutional, not merely romantic.

Cross-class attachment does not absorb her public purpose.

## 6.14 Final Year 1 constitutional form

The endpoint is:

> **solidaristic legitimacy with extraordinary internal trust and material mutual aid, constrained by weak adversarial architecture and overconcentration of symbolic responsibility in Ichinose.**

Its strengths are:

- low-cost internal cooperation;
- high voluntary legitimacy;
- collective capital;
- membership that survives moral imperfection;
- capacity for universal rescue;
- and an egalitarian resistance to treating ability as entitlement to continued existence.

Its vulnerabilities are:

- exploitation by nonreciprocal opponents;
- resource exhaustion;
- leader self-sacrifice;
- insufficient conversion of social capital into point advantage;
- and underdeveloped internal strategic opposition.

Its final question is:

> **Can Class B build defenses strong enough to preserve trust without teaching its members that trust itself was a mistake?**

# 7. `POLITY-SAKAYANAGI-ORIGIN` - aristocratic optimization and the succession problem

`POLITY-SAKAYANAGI-ORIGIN` finishes Year 1 with the largest point total and the deepest conventional talent pool (`Y1FF-E058`).

Its political strength is not merely that Class A has better students.

The class increasingly becomes a system in which Sakayanagi:

- identifies differentiated ability;
- controls strategic information;
- assigns roles;
- manipulates opponents;
- suppresses rival centers;
- and uses hierarchy to make decisions quickly.

*First File* describes the class as talented and correctly allocated while warning that strategy is monopolized and unity is incomplete (`Y1FF-E038–E040`).

This makes the polity the Year 1 case for **aristocratic optimization**.

The word `aristocratic` is analytical rather than literal. It refers to rule by a leader who believes unequal quality is real, expects superior judgment to govern allocation, and does not regard equal participation in strategic authorship as necessary for legitimate performance.

## 7.1 Katsuragi and Sakayanagi represent competing theories of elite rule

Class A does not begin Year 1 as a unitary Sakayanagi regime.

Katsuragi represents a more defensive, status-preserving model of leadership. His factional position matters because the class initially contains more than one plausible governing center.

Sakayanagi's rise is therefore not simply succession from absence.

It is consolidation.

Her political project reduces the independence of the Katsuragi faction while preserving useful people when their capacity remains worth retaining.

This distinction becomes explicit during the Class Vote.

## 7.2 Sakayanagi's constitutional premise: unequal ability should be governed accurately

Sakayanagi is among the characters most willing to say that people differ substantially in capacity.

She does not solve the moral problem by pretending everyone contributes equally.

The strength of this position is analytical clarity.

A polity that refuses to recognize unequal capability can assign roles badly.

The weakness appears when accurate recognition becomes proprietary allocation:

> I understand your value, therefore I possess the authority to decide what place you should occupy.

That movement from knowledge to jurisdiction is the polity's central ethical risk.

## 7.3 Kamuro: vulnerability converted into service

Sakayanagi's relation with Kamuro offers a small-scale model of the class constitution.

The relation begins through leverage derived from wrongdoing. Sakayanagi converts vulnerability into service. Stable role, usefulness, and even behavioral improvement may follow.

The later stability does not retroactively make the origin consensual.

Politically, the relation demonstrates how Sakayanagi creates governable order:

- identify a hidden fact;
- convert the fact into leverage;
- define a role;
- normalize the hierarchy;
- and extract useful capacity.

The method can produce competence.

It can also normalize domination as pedagogy.

## 7.4 Information asymmetry is not a side effect; it is the governing technology

Sakayanagi's polity functions because the sovereign often knows more than participants about:

- the true target;
- the actual objective;
- the value of a member;
- external bargains;
- and the intended political effect of a procedure.

The Class Vote provides the clearest example.

The class appears to know Katsuragi will be removed. Katsuragi himself accepts the outcome because the factional balance seems decisive (`Y1V10-E076–E077`).

The public certainty is false.

Yahiko is the real target (`Y1V10-E085–E086`).

The sovereign preserves the more useful opponent while removing a loyalist whose loss also disciplines the remaining faction.

The vote becomes ratification of a decision whose true informational basis is monopolized.

## 7.5 The Class Vote: pruning as sovereign government

Sakayanagi's approach is often described as meritocratic.

That is incomplete.

She does not simply remove the objectively weakest member after neutral ranking.

The operation includes:

- strategic usefulness;
- factional deterrence;
- hidden targeting;
- personal grievance;
- and a broader experiment involving Yamauchi and Ayanokoji (`Y1V10-E078–E082`; `Y1V10-E106`).

Yahiko's removal therefore demonstrates that aristocratic optimization is political, not merely technical.

The sovereign decides not only who is capable, but what kind of allegiance the class should contain.

## 7.6 Procedure remains, but substantive uncertainty is removed from below

A-class classmates still cast ballots.

The presence of procedure does not make the decision collectively authored.

When the dominant leader controls the decisive information and the factional majority, the procedure can become a mechanism for legitimating an outcome already selected elsewhere.

This is one of Year 1's clearest distinctions between:

- formal participation;
- and substantive authorship.

The class can appear cohesive because conflict has been resolved vertically before the vote reaches the members.

## 7.7 The polity's strength: role allocation

The strongest defense of Sakayanagi's constitution is that it works.

A high-capacity leader with a strong map of classmates can:

- avoid wasting rare talent;
- select events intelligently;
- protect strategically valuable people;
- and coordinate specialized roles more efficiently than a class that requires broad consensus.

*First File* explicitly recognizes correct role allocation (`Y1FF-E040`).

The point total supplies institutional corroboration without proving moral superiority (`Y1FF-E058`).

## 7.8 The polity's weakness: independent model-building atrophies

Centralization creates an informational paradox.

The better Sakayanagi becomes at knowing everyone, the less necessary it may seem for everyone else to develop strategic knowledge.

That can produce a class filled with strong pieces but weak alternative governors.

Katsuragi's existence partially resists this tendency because he represents an alternative governing center.

But Sakayanagi's consolidation repeatedly reduces his room for independent political action.

The result is short-run clarity and long-run succession risk.

## 7.9 Katsuragi's Volume 11 leak proves opposition survives under repression

Keisei attempts an unauthorized approach to Katsuragi. Katsuragi refuses the proposed bargain but voluntarily leaks predictions of likely A-class events because resentment toward Sakayanagi remains (`Y1V11-E088–E089`).

Sakayanagi's threat against unrelated classmates prevents him from intentionally throwing (`Y1V11-E090`).

This is constitutionally revealing.

The polity has not eliminated internal opposition.

It has made opposition costly.

A regime can therefore look unified while dissent persists privately.

That is different from genuine consensus.

## 7.10 Hashimoto: exit insurance inside elite government

Hashimoto's developing information politics illustrate another vulnerability of centralized elite rule.

When strategic authorship is concentrated and members suspect that the sovereign's private priorities may diverge from theirs, rational subordinates may build personal insurance.

That behavior can look disloyal.

It can also be a predictable response to low participation in collective strategic authorship.

A polity that asks members to trust one ruler's superior map may create strong incentives for ambitious members to develop secret maps of their own.

## 7.11 Sakayanagi and Ayanokoji: private rivalry creates fiduciary risk

Sakayanagi's interest in Ayanokoji is intellectually and personally important.

It is also political because she can use class resources to create conditions for the contest she wants.

Volume 10 shows her preserving Ayanokoji through A-class external praise while simultaneously manipulating Yamauchi as an experimental subject (`Y1V10-E078–E081`; `Y1V10-E107`).

Volume 11 shows the desired A-C pairing partly engineered and the chess contest becoming the symbolic center of the exam (`Y1V11-E042–E045`; `Y1V11-E137–E147`).

The issue is not that leaders may have private motives. Every leader does.

The constitutional issue is **fiduciary externalization**:

> classmates bear the costs of a ruler's private rivalry without possessing equal authority over whether the rivalry should govern collective strategy.

This risk grows as the leader's strategic monopoly grows.

## 7.12 Volume 11: sophisticated performance cannot protect the class from administrative sovereignty

The Sakayanagi polity is powerful enough to create an excellent commander-level chess contest.

It is not powerful enough to control the record layer.

Tsukishiro's substituted command produces the official A-class victory while Sakayanagi herself recognizes that something about the decisive move is wrong (`Y1V11-E139–E143`).

This is politically important because the most centralized and strategically sophisticated student polity is still subordinate to administrative infrastructure.

The sovereign is not sovereign all the way up.

## 7.13 Recognition can become humane without ceasing to be hierarchical

The private replay and post-match walk show that Sakayanagi's relation to Ayanokoji contains more than pure test-case utility. She revises her judgment and communicates through ordinary human warmth (`Y1V11-E143–E147`).

That development matters relationally.

It does not automatically change the class constitution.

A ruler can become more humane in one relationship while retaining highly centralized governance elsewhere.

## 7.14 Final Year 1 constitutional form

The endpoint is:

> **high-capacity aristocratic optimization with excellent role allocation and strategic intelligence, constrained by monopolized authorship, internal opposition, succession weakness, and fiduciary risk from the sovereign's private priorities.**

Its strengths are:

- deep conventional talent;
- strategic clarity;
- fast decision-making;
- role specialization;
- strong threat modeling;
- and a leader willing to recognize unequal ability directly.

Its vulnerabilities are:

- low distributed authorship;
- coercive use of information;
- factional residues;
- exit-insurance incentives;
- succession dependence;
- and the possibility that personal projects become collective obligations.

Its final question is:

> **Can a polity governed by the person with the best map of everyone develop other map-makers before the sovereign's absence, error, or private desire becomes a class-wide crisis?**

# 8. The Class Vote as a constitutional stress test

Volume 10 provides the cleanest comparative experiment in Year 1 because the institution holds the scarcity mechanism relatively constant while the four classes supply different political answers.

The exam is deliberately designed to generate an expulsion after the year has produced none (`Y1V10-E013–E019`).

That fact matters.

The classes are not responding to a naturally occurring shortage. They are responding to an institutionally authored demand that one person cease to belong unless sufficient private capital can buy an exception.

The test therefore reveals two things at once:

1. how each polity governs under scarcity;
2. what theory of membership it is willing to enforce when the school manufactures disposability.

## 8.1 Horikita: accountable triage

Horikita's polity accepts that a choice may have to be made, but she resists anonymous coalition government.

She publicly identifies reasons, names a candidate, and becomes answerable for the decision (`Y1V10-E050–E059`).

The virtue is procedural accountability.

The danger is technocratic confidence under uncertainty. Developmental possibility makes any final ranking of future worth epistemically unstable (`Y1V10-E006–E007`).

## 8.2 Ichinose: universal rescue and equalized involuntary risk

Ichinose's polity first rejects the premise that a member should be abandoned if money can save the person. Members pool resources and buy back Kanzaki's technical expulsion (`Y1V10-E071`; `Y1V10-E087`; `Y1V10-E103`).

The lottery fallback goes further. If rescue becomes impossible, the class does not claim that present school utility creates a moral right to identify the least worthy future (`Y1V10-E105`).

The virtue is egalitarian membership.

The danger is that adversaries can price the class's refusal to lose anyone.

## 8.3 Sakayanagi: sovereign pruning

Sakayanagi centralizes the judgment before the class procedure reaches its apparent conclusion.

Katsuragi is announced as the sacrifice. Yahiko is actually removed. The information asymmetry preserves a more useful rival, destroys a residual factional loyalist, and demonstrates the cost of opposition (`Y1V10-E076–E086`).

The virtue is strategic decisiveness.

The danger is that class procedure becomes a ratification device for a private sovereign calculation.

## 8.4 Ryuuen: voluntary rescue of the former sovereign

Ryuuen is willing to accept expulsion. Followers refuse the outcome and seek a rescue mechanism (`Y1V10-E070`; `Y1V10-E093–E102`).

This is the inverse of ordinary authoritarian protection.

The governed become the protectors of the former ruler.

The virtue is evidence that the class contains attachment and political agency beyond fear.

The danger is that loyalty can simply reinstall personal rule without building a more distributed constitution.

## 8.5 What the comparison establishes

The Class Vote demonstrates that **constitutional form appears most clearly when the institution forces a morally ugly choice**.

No response escapes complicity in the institutional structure.

- Horikita makes sacrifice accountable.
- Ichinose tries to buy out sacrifice.
- Sakayanagi governs sacrifice from above.
- Ryuuen's followers redirect sacrifice to preserve the leader.

The exam therefore does not reveal which class is morally pure.

It reveals which theory of membership each polity reaches for when purity is impossible.

# 9. The Event Selection Exam as a portfolio-government test

If the Class Vote tests membership, the Event Selection Exam tests whether a class can convert a heterogeneous portfolio of abilities into collective performance.

The formal design requires candidate events, participant selection, substitution, information security, and a commander whose intervention is limited but potentially decisive (`Y1V11-E027–E045`).

This is a much richer measure of class government than a single standardized exam.

## 9.1 Possessed ability is insufficient

A class may contain a talented person and still be unable to use the talent.

Koenji is the clearest case. His ability exists. His willingness to deploy it for class purposes is not guaranteed (`Y1V11-E062–E066`).

The exam therefore measures **mobilizable capacity**, not merely latent capacity.

## 9.2 Development can defeat stale intelligence

Sudo's improvement matters because opponents' models are based partly on earlier performance. Ayanokoji explicitly notes that Sakayanagi's information about Sudo has become outdated (`Y1V11-E113–E115`).

This is a political advantage of developmental government.

A class that changes people can make external intelligence obsolete.

## 9.3 Commander quality is knowledge of limits

The best commander is not simply the person who can personally solve the most problems.

The commander must know:

- when a member knows more;
- when intervention will help;
- when intervention will disrupt;
- and what capacity is actually available.

Horikita's increasing reliance on distributed expertise makes her polity better suited to this kind of test than her Volume 1 self would have been.

## 9.4 Ryuuen turns the commander role into psychological warfare

Ryuuen uses command aggressively, treating event selection and psychological destabilization as part of one battlefield (`Y1V11-E123–E130`).

His class demonstrates that portfolio government can be offensive as well as allocative.

The weakness is the same as before: the sophistication resides heavily in one strategic center.

## 9.5 Ichinose demonstrates the limit of internal cohesion

B class remains coordinated, but its planning does not adequately anticipate deniable norm-breaking (`Y1V11-E125–E126`).

The exam therefore distinguishes **cohesion** from **security**.

A group can function very well internally and still be badly matched against an opponent who expands the conflict domain.

## 9.6 Sakayanagi demonstrates elite centralized command

Sakayanagi's candidate portfolio and chess preparation show a polity comfortable with specialization and commander leverage (`Y1V11-E083`; `Y1V11-E137–E147`).

The class performs at a high level.

Yet Tsukishiro's intervention proves that no student constitution controls the whole system.

The strongest portfolio can still be subordinated to the administrator who controls communication and the official record.

# 10. Four information regimes

The most useful comparative lens for the four polities is information governance.

## 10.1 Horikita: increasingly distributed knowledge, incompletely visible meta-knowledge

Horikita's mature leadership depends on learning who knows what.

She increasingly treats:

- Sudo as athletic authority;
- Hirata as social authority;
- classmates as differentiated sources;
- and her own knowledge as incomplete.

This is epistemically plural.

But Ayanokoji occupies a meta-level. He often knows the local knowledge holders, the opponents, the hidden bargains, and the contingencies simultaneously.

The class's problem is therefore not only unequal information.

It is **unknown information inequality**.

Members cannot challenge a power they do not know exists.

## 10.2 Ryuuen: centralized adversarial intelligence

Ryuuen treats information as a weapon whose value increases when opponents lack it.

He is strong at:

- identifying fear;
- collecting vulnerabilities;
- reading incentives;
- and making the cost of concealment high.

The regime's weakness is that coercive information gathering can reduce truthful upward communication. People who fear punishment have reasons to tell the ruler what preserves them rather than what corrects him.

Hiyori and Ibuki become valuable partly because they preserve some capacity for unapproved judgment.

## 10.3 Ichinose: high-trust circulation

Ichinose's polity gains efficiency from relatively open internal relations.

People can pool money, disclose problems, and coordinate without treating every datum as leverage.

The vulnerability is external asymmetry.

A trust-rich network can be exploited when another actor enters the relation only to extract information, manufacture debt, or violate assumed norms.

The solution cannot be total secrecy without destroying the constitution's comparative advantage.

The needed skill is **selective permeability**.

## 10.4 Sakayanagi: sovereign map and controlled disclosure

Sakayanagi's class has the most explicit model of information hierarchy.

The ruler seeks the best map and decides how much of it others need.

This can be strategically efficient because compartmentalization protects operations.

It also makes followers dependent on the sovereign's epistemic accuracy and good faith.

The political risk appears when private motives enter the map. If classmates cannot independently inspect why a strategy exists, they may be unable to distinguish fiduciary strategy from the leader's personal project.

## 10.5 Information quality versus information rights

The four regimes establish a key Year 1 distinction:

> **having accurate information does not settle who has the right to act on it.**

Ayanokoji may correctly understand Kei.

Ryuuen may correctly understand fear.

Sakayanagi may correctly identify talent.

Horikita may correctly see development.

Ichinose may correctly trust long-standing classmates.

The constitutional question begins after accuracy:

- who is affected;
- who can refuse;
- who can correct the model;
- and who bears the consequences if the model is wrong?

# 11. Weakness, development, and disposability

Each polity contains a different implicit theory of what to do with weak members.

## 11.1 Horikita: from defect to trajectory

Horikita begins closest to dispositional ranking.

Her Year 1 development makes trajectory increasingly important. Sudo is not the person his entrance-state performance predicted. Her Kushida project extends the same principle to a much more dangerous case.

The polity's ethical strength is developmental patience.

Its risk is overgeneralization. Not every harmful pattern is equivalent to immaturity, and a leader's desire to rehabilitate someone does not erase the security claims of everyone else.

## 11.2 Ryuuen: usefulness under command

Ryuuen's polity is more openly instrumental.

Weakness is tolerable when the person remains useful to the plan or loyal enough to the structure. The leader can create roles for people who are not individually elite because centralized strategy multiplies their usefulness.

The model can therefore be less academically elitist than it first appears.

Its danger is that a person's claim to protection may remain contingent on usefulness to the ruler.

## 11.3 Ichinose: membership before ranking

Ichinose's class places the strongest presumption against disposability.

The Class Vote demonstrates that differential ability does not automatically create differential entitlement to remain (`Y1V10-E103–E105`).

This protects dignity and permits development.

It can also preserve members whose conduct may impose serious future costs. A non-disposability principle therefore requires mechanisms short of expulsion for correction, security, restitution, and accountability.

## 11.4 Sakayanagi: optimization and pruning

Sakayanagi is most willing to treat continued membership as a strategic allocation problem.

Yet even her pruning is not a neutral ability ranking. Yahiko's expulsion serves factional and symbolic purposes as well as utility (`Y1V10-E085–E086`; `Y1V10-E106`).

The polity demonstrates why the language of pure meritocracy is inadequate.

Whenever a leader decides who is useful, the decision already contains a theory of what the group is for.

## 11.5 Institutional disposability precedes class disposability

The classes do not invent the Class Vote.

The school does.

This matters ethically because student leaders are making judgments inside a structure that has already converted continued membership into a scarce good.

A constitution should therefore be evaluated both for how it responds to scarcity and for whether it begins to naturalize the institution's manufactured scarcity as ordinary moral truth.

---

# 12. Dissent, correction, and loyal opposition

A class's treatment of dissent is one of the best predictors of whether its strengths can survive leader error.

## 12.1 Horikita: corrigibility becomes an explicit norm

Keisei's Year 1 endpoint provides the strongest formulation: support the leader when right, challenge her when wrong (`Y1V11.5-E011–E012`).

Sudo has already demonstrated the practice by opposing Horikita during the Class Vote.

Horikita's development is strongest when followers become harder to govern blindly.

## 12.2 Ryuuen: dissent survives as personality rather than institution

Ibuki and Hiyori can resist or correct Ryuuen.

That is meaningful.

The correction channel remains highly personal. There is no stable constitutional office whose role is to challenge him.

The regime therefore depends on whether sufficiently independent personalities happen to remain nearby.

## 12.3 Ichinose: Kanzaki as necessary friction

Kanzaki's significance is partly structural.

A solidaristic polity risks equating disagreement with harm because members are highly invested in mutual protection.

Loyal opposition prevents cohesion from becoming unanimity theater.

Year 1 does not yet show the mature form of that opposition, but the need is already visible.

## 12.4 Sakayanagi: opposition is tolerated when useful and constrained when threatening

Katsuragi survives because he remains useful. His political independence is narrowed. His Volume 11 leak demonstrates that dissent persists beneath the surface (`Y1V11-E088–E090`).

The polity therefore has opposition without strong protections for opposition.

That is a fragile arrangement. The leader can benefit from rival competence while ensuring the rival cannot become an equal center of authority.

## 12.5 Correction is not the same as veto

No class can function if every dissenter possesses absolute veto.

The Year 1 standard is narrower:

- dissent must be speakable;
- relevant information must be able to reach decision-makers;
- disagreement should not automatically terminate belonging;
- and leader error should be capable of changing future practice.

By that standard, Horikita's polity shows the strongest positive trajectory, Ichinose's contains the strongest relational preconditions, Ryuuen's develops meaningful but personal correction, and Sakayanagi's remains the most structurally resistant to rival authorship.

# 13. Succession - the test that has not yet fully occurred

Year 1 repeatedly asks whether a political order survives the person who currently makes it work.

## 13.1 Ryuuen: succession crisis already experienced

Ryuuen's defeat creates the only direct Year 1 case in which the central ruler loses the basis of office and withdraws.

The polity does not immediately produce a new constitution.

Instead, followers preserve the old ruler (`Y1V07-E101–E105`).

This proves loyalty.

It does not solve succession.

The class still needs an answer to what happens when he is permanently unavailable.

## 13.2 Horikita: formal succession versus hidden succession

The visible question is whether Horikita can lead.

The deeper question is whether the class can survive Ayanokoji's withdrawal.

Because he does not formally rule, his succession problem is easy to miss.

That makes it more dangerous.

A class can prepare to replace the visible leader while remaining dependent on a hidden function nobody knows must be replaced.

The shadow constitution therefore creates **succession without office**.

## 13.3 Ichinose: symbolic-center risk

Class B has more distributed trust than Ryuuen's class, yet it still organizes much emotional meaning around Ichinose.

If she collapses, the class may retain its norms but lose the person who makes those norms feel coherent.

Kanzaki and distributed peer trust are therefore constitutionally important even before succession becomes immediate.

## 13.4 Sakayanagi: strongest present rule, weakest obvious replacement

Sakayanagi's class is perhaps the clearest case of the succession paradox.

The better a sovereign performs, the less urgent alternative leadership appears.

But the lack of alternative authorship means absence becomes more dangerous.

Katsuragi is a partial answer because he has an independent governing philosophy. The process of consolidating against him simultaneously weakens that answer.

## 13.5 Succession as proof of institution

A political order becomes more institutional when:

- roles survive occupants;
- information survives private memory;
- dissent survives personal friendship;
- and collective purpose can be reinterpreted without collapse.

None of the four first-year polities fully passes this test by the Year 1 boundary.

# 14. Trust and coercion as political capital

Year 1 refuses a simple opposition between trust and coercion.

Both can coordinate people.

They differ in cost structure and failure mode.

## 14.1 Trust lowers ordinary coordination costs

Ichinose's polity can move information and money quickly because members assume reciprocity.

Horikita's maturing polity increasingly relies on domain experts who can speak without first proving loyalty through fear.

Trust therefore creates speed of a different kind from authoritarian command.

## 14.2 Coercion can create rapid unity

Ryuuen's fear regime produces decisive action because disagreement becomes expensive.

Sakayanagi's informational sovereignty can similarly compress deliberation.

The immediate advantage is clear.

The hidden cost is that suppressed disagreement may reappear later as poor succession, private resentment, or missing information.

## 14.3 Trust can be exploited

Ichinose's Volume 11 defeat shows that high-trust norms become predictable surfaces for adversarial strategy (`Y1V11-E123–E130`).

Kushida demonstrates a related internal danger: a person trusted by many can convert social access into leverage.

Trust is therefore not automatically safe.

It is a resource that requires governance.

## 14.4 Coercion can generate attachments it cannot explain

Ryuuen's followers remain after coercive supremacy fails.

Sakayanagi's subordinates can develop familiarity inside unequal roles.

Ayanokoji's relationships repeatedly show the same phenomenon at smaller scale.

This does not vindicate coercion.

It demonstrates that human beings can create real attachment inside compromised structures.

Political analysis must therefore judge both origin and later agency.

## 14.5 The strongest Year 1 trajectory is from unanswerable power toward answerable asymmetry

The mature standard is not a fantasy of powerlessness.

Classes need leaders, expertise, information, and sometimes rapid decisions.

The stronger question is whether asymmetry becomes answerable:

- can the subordinate contradict;
- can the leader be corrected;
- can information be audited;
- can protection permit exit;
- and can roles change without relationship annihilation?

This links class politics directly to the relational principle established in `Y1_04`.

# 15. The point economy as constitutional infrastructure

Private points and class points are not merely game mechanics layered on top of politics.

They are part of the political constitution because they determine which values can become enforceable.

## 15.1 Class points convert institutional judgment into collective fate

A student's action can affect the class-wide resource position.

This makes classmates economically interdependent before they have chosen one another.

The class system therefore creates a coercive baseline:

> people must care about one another's behavior because the institution makes their outcomes partially collective.

Every class constitution develops inside that imposed interdependence.

## 15.2 Private points make mercy purchasable

The Mixed Training Camp and Class Vote make rescue contingent on enormous private-point transfers (`Y1V08-E020–E026`; `Y1V10-E013–E019`).

This has a major political implication.

Compassion is not merely a disposition.

It requires fiscal capacity.

A class with stronger collective savings can preserve a member whom another class must lose.

The school therefore transforms economic organization into moral option space.

## 15.3 Ichinose turns private wealth into mutual insurance

Class B's pooling system is the clearest collectivization of the point economy.

Its moral advantage is obvious during the Class Vote.

Its strategic cost is also real: spending twenty million points preserves membership but destroys a major reserve (`Y1V10-E087`; `Y1V10-E103`).

The polity demonstrates that refusing disposability has a price under ANHS rules.

## 15.4 Ryuuen turns accumulated wealth into strategic mobility

Ryuuen's accumulated points and the Katsuragi-linked economic legacy create resources that can be transferred across class boundaries. The rescue transaction with Ichinose converts money into praise votes and political debt (`Y1V10-E098–E101`).

His earlier 800-million-point horizon also reveals that the point system invites thought about purchasing escape from class fate (`Y1V07.5-E039–E041`).

Political imagination therefore includes both climbing the class hierarchy and buying routes around it.

## 15.5 Sakayanagi treats points as one resource among many

Sakayanagi's comparative advantage is not collective savings but control of information, votes, high-capacity students, and class position.

The polity can often solve problems through allocation rather than rescue spending.

This reinforces the aristocratic logic: preserve the strategically valuable pieces and avoid spending resources to pretend all losses are equal.

## 15.6 Horikita's polity has less fiscal cushion and more developmental dependence

Horikita's class cannot rely on the same depth of points or talent as A class, nor the same pooled reserve as B class.

Its path to competitiveness therefore depends more heavily on making existing members better and making heterogeneous abilities work together.

The class's poverty in one kind of capital strengthens the political importance of developmental capital.

## 15.7 Money does not settle value

The point system can price:

- rescue;
- transfer;
- immunity;
- and class position.

The novels repeatedly resist the inference that price equals worth.

A twenty-million-point buyback makes continued enrollment purchasable. It does not mean a human future is worth twenty million points.

That gap between institutional price and human value is one of Year 1's central political tensions.

# 16. Cross-class politics - rivalry, alliance, and exchange

The four polities are not closed systems.

Their constitutions are revealed partly by how they interact with people who do not share their internal norms.

## 16.1 Horikita and Ichinose: alliance without permanent merger

Their cooperation demonstrates that rival polities can create bounded trust.

Volume 11.5 then demonstrates an equally important capacity: the alliance can end because interests diverge without either leader redefining the other as enemy (`Y1V11.5-E059–E063`).

This is mature interstate behavior in miniature.

## 16.2 Ryuuen and Ichinose: exchange between incompatible constitutions

The Class Vote rescue trade is striking because the two polities are normatively different.

Ryuuen's point reserve and Class B's praise votes become mutually useful. Ayanokoji mediates the transaction (`Y1V10-E098–E101`).

The episode shows that political exchange does not require ideological similarity.

It requires a bargain whose terms can be verified.

## 16.3 Sakayanagi and Horikita/Ayanokoji: rivalry as agenda-setting

Sakayanagi's desire for a direct Ayanokoji contest influences opponent selection and event design (`Y1V11-E042–E045`).

Cross-class rivalry therefore becomes a force that can redirect internal class resources.

This is why private rivalry creates fiduciary risk.

## 16.4 Temporary polities reveal transferable capacities

The Mixed Training Camp forces students into cross-class and cross-year groups. Cooking, labor exchange, confession, waiting, and joint responsibility create temporary community (`Y1V08-E088–E105`).

The result matters because class identity is not total identity.

Students can cooperate outside their home constitution and bring learned capacities back into it.

## 16.5 Cross-class trust is politically valuable and politically dangerous

Ichinose's trust in Ayanokoji helps preserve her autonomy during the Class Vote.

Sakayanagi's cross-class understanding of Ayanokoji allows her to protect and test him.

Ryuuen's search for X treats cross-class uncertainty as a threat.

The same porousness that creates rescue and friendship also creates espionage, debt, manipulation, and divided loyalty.

A robust constitution therefore needs boundaries that are permeable without being undefended.

# 17. Informal offices, shadow constitutions, and the politics beneath formal leadership

The official class leader is never the entire constitution.

Year 1 repeatedly creates informal offices whose power can exceed their title.

## 17.1 Hirata as social ombudsman without formal constitutional language

Hirata possesses enormous voluntary legitimacy because classmates experience him as accessible, fair, and protective.

His collapse during the Class Vote reveals how dependent the class has become on that emotional office.

He has no formal veto over expulsion.

Yet when he withdraws social participation, the class loses a major governance function.

## 17.2 Kushida as network infrastructure

Kushida's social access resembles an informal communication ministry.

She can gather and distribute personal information across boundaries Horikita cannot easily enter.

Because the infrastructure is tied to one person's private motives, it is simultaneously an asset and a security vulnerability.

## 17.3 Kei as social authority

Kei's public position among girls makes her politically useful even when conventional academic metrics undervalue her. Her low institutional profile does not measure her ability to shape atmosphere, reputation, and communication.

This is one reason *First File* is so important: a weak formal file can coexist with meaningful political power.

## 17.4 Ayanokoji as shadow executive

Ayanokoji is the strongest Year 1 example of informal power exceeding formal office.

He can influence:

- information flows;
- rescue options;
- opponent selection;
- class reputation;
- private protection;
- and the timing of public intervention.

He often does so while another person bears visible responsibility.

This makes him constitutionally powerful precisely because his office is difficult to name.

## 17.5 Informal power can be legitimate, illegitimate, or mixed

Not every informal actor is a usurper.

Communities need people whose authority emerges from trust and expertise rather than formal title.

The problem begins when:

- power materially affects others;
- affected people cannot identify the power holder;
- and no correction channel exists.

The Year 1 shadow-constitution problem is therefore a transparency problem more than a simple formalism problem.

# 18. Administrative procedural sovereignty above the four classes

Year 1 ultimately reveals that class politics operates under a superior constitutional layer.

The school designs the field in which student constitutions compete.

## 18.1 Examination design is political authorship

A special exam decides:

- which abilities become visible;
- which risks are concentrated;
- whether expulsion exists;
- how rescue is priced;
- whether groups are temporary or class-bound;
- and which kinds of information matter.

The institution therefore does not merely observe merit.

It produces situations in which particular forms of merit become consequential.

## 18.2 The Mixed Training Camp prices leadership

Leaders receive greater reward and greater expulsion risk (`Y1V08-E015–E018`).

Leadership is therefore not just honor.

It is a contract involving concentrated liability.

The design influences who is willing to lead and what kinds of communities can afford rescue.

## 18.3 The Class Vote manufactures disposability

The exam exists because no first-year students had yet been expelled.

That makes expulsion an institutional objective rather than a naturally emerging consequence.

The school then invites the classes to supply political justification for the victim.

This is why class competence cannot settle the ethics of the exam.

A beautifully reasoned execution of a manufactured scarcity can still reproduce the institution's questionable premise.

## 18.4 Student council as near-constitutional power

Manabu indicates that the student council can influence school culture and special-exam concepts (`Y1V07.5-E042–E043`).

Nagumo's politics therefore matter beyond student prestige.

Control of the council can approach control over the rules through which other students become legible and vulnerable.

## 18.5 Tsukishiro and record capture

Volume 11 creates the strongest Year 1 challenge to institutional legitimacy.

The Event Selection Exam can be carefully designed and competently executed. If an administrator can intercept and replace the communication that becomes the official command, the recorded result no longer corresponds to the claimed procedure (`Y1V11-E139–E145`).

This adds the final step to the ability chain:

> possession -> development -> expression -> legibility -> coordination -> **recording**.

Control of recording can override everything below it.

## 18.6 ANHS is not a single mind

Volume 11.5 complicates a totalizing reading of the school.

Ayanokoji seeks oversight through Chairman Sakayanagi, Mashima, and Chabashira rather than treating every adult actor as identical.

Mashima's willingness to intervene against dangerous mismatch and accept punishment for rule-breaking suggests an internal institutional conscience.

The school therefore contains competing authorities and ethical positions.

The political problem is not simply students versus institution.

It is how integrity survives when authority is distributed among actors with conflicting purposes.

## 18.7 The final institutional standard

A legitimate competitive institution needs more than difficult exams.

At minimum it needs:

- an honestly stated measurement object;
- recognition of developmental uncertainty;
- correction and appeal channels;
- rules that do not disguise authored scarcity as natural inferiority;
- and a trustworthy record layer.

Without those conditions, class politics risks becoming sophisticated adaptation to an unaccountable sovereign environment.

# 19. Meritocracy after the four constitutions

The class comparison complicates the title concept of `実力至上主義`.

No polity demonstrates that merit is a single scalar property.

## 19.1 Horikita: merit as developable and relational

Her class shows that present weakness can become future strength and that ability becomes useful through coordination.

The political unit of analysis shifts from:

> who is strongest?

to:

> what arrangement makes different strengths usable without destroying autonomy?

## 19.2 Ryuuen: merit as strategic effect

Ryuuen values what wins.

This expands merit beyond grades but risks collapsing moral constraints into inefficiency. His `勝つための悪逆` language makes the distinction explicit: wrongdoing can remain wrongdoing even when it is instrumentally effective (`Y1V11-E127–E130`).

## 19.3 Ichinose: merit does not determine membership

Class B does not deny ability differences.

It denies that those differences automatically create unequal rights to continue belonging.

This is a crucial distinction between meritocratic allocation of tasks and meritocratic allocation of personhood.

## 19.4 Sakayanagi: merit as hierarchical allocation

Sakayanagi most strongly treats differential capacity as a basis for differentiated roles and survival decisions.

Her class demonstrates the effectiveness of elite allocation and the political danger of allowing the allocator to define the metric, the interpretation, and the consequence simultaneously.

## 19.5 Manabu and Nagumo make meritocracy institutionally contestable

Volume 11.5's discussion of `本当の実力主義` prevents the phrase from functioning as a settled authorial answer.

Nagumo's individual-mobility logic and Manabu's defense of class fate represent different theories of what institutions should do with unequal ability.

The question becomes constitutional:

- when should ability be measured;
- how much time should development receive;
- what support should weaker students receive;
- and how much should an individual's fate depend on the group?

Year 1 does not close the argument.

# 20. Legitimacy matrix - why no polity wins every category

It is useful to distinguish at least four kinds of political legitimacy.

## 20.1 Procedural legitimacy

Were rules and reasons sufficiently visible and answerable?

Horikita's Class Vote leadership improves here.

Sakayanagi's hidden targeting is comparatively weak here despite efficiency.

## 20.2 Sociological legitimacy

Do members actually accept and follow the authority?

Ichinose is extremely strong.

Ryuuen becomes stronger after defeat because followers choose continuation.

Horikita's legitimacy rises over the year.

Sakayanagi possesses strong compliance but incomplete unity.

## 20.3 Epistemic legitimacy

Does the leadership possess enough accurate knowledge to make competent decisions?

Sakayanagi and Ryuuen are strong in adversarial modeling.

Horikita improves by distributing knowledge.

Ichinose's internal social knowledge is strong while adversarial modeling remains weaker.

## 20.4 Moral legitimacy

Does the exercise of power respect members as persons whose futures are not merely resources?

Ichinose's anti-disposability principle is strongest here, though leader self-erasure complicates it.

Horikita improves through development and accountability but remains willing to participate in triage.

Ryuuen and Sakayanagi remain more willing to subordinate autonomy to strategic outcome.

## 20.5 Performance legitimacy

Does the system produce institutional results?

The Year 1 point totals place Sakayanagi's class first, Ichinose's second, Ryuuen's third, and Horikita's fourth (`Y1FF-E058`).

This is real evidence.

It is not a summary of the other four legitimacy categories.

## 20.6 The series refuses legitimacy collapse

The most important conclusion is methodological:

> **no one form of legitimacy should be allowed to swallow all the others.**

Winning does not prove justice.

Voluntary support does not prove moral reform.

Fair procedure does not prove correct judgment.

Kindness does not prove strategic adequacy.

Accurate models do not prove jurisdiction.

# 21. First File - the point table and the limits of institutional summary

*First File* is useful because it turns the year into a filing system.

The four class summaries are remarkably compatible with the second-pass constitutional reading:

| Polity | *First File* emphasis | Constitutional reading |
|---|---|---|
| `POLITY-HORIKITA` | explosive potential, insufficient cohesion | development and aggregation problem |
| `POLITY-RYUEN` | coordinated dictatorship, overdependent on ruler | coercive efficiency and succession bottleneck |
| `POLITY-ICHINOSE` | cohesion, savings, no expulsions, weak result conversion | solidarity as capacity with adversarial weakness |
| `POLITY-SAKAYANAGI-ORIGIN` | deep talent, correct allocation, monopolized strategy | aristocratic optimization and succession risk |

The guidebook therefore strengthens the polity model (`Y1FF-E025`; `Y1FF-E033`; `Y1FF-E037`; `Y1FF-E040`).

It also supplies the strongest warning against turning the model into essence.

The summaries are snapshots of political organization at a boundary.

They do not determine what the classes must become.

This is especially important because Year 1 itself has shown that:

- Sudo can outgrow a low evaluation;
- Horikita can outgrow cooperation E;
- Ryuuen's followers can exceed his fear theory;
- Ichinose's class can survive loss of leader purity;
- and Sakayanagi's internal opposition can persist beneath apparent unity.

A political file can be accurate about present structure and still be incomplete about constitutional development.

# 23. Counterarguments and stress tests

## 23.1 "Horikita's class is secretly an Ayanokoji dictatorship"

Too strong.

Ayanokoji possesses enormous hidden causal power, but Horikita, Hirata, Sudo, Keisei, Kushida, Kei, Koenji, and others retain independent agency. Horikita repeatedly produces choices he did not simply dictate, including student-council refusal, Kushida preservation, public Class Vote leadership, and the demand for auditable testing.

The stronger claim is:

> the polity has a **shadow executive** whose power is insufficiently visible, not a total puppet regime.

## 23.2 "Ryuuen's followers choose him, so the coercive model works"

Incomplete.

Voluntary preservation proves that fear is not the whole relationship. It does not establish that coercion caused the loyalty or that the same loyalty could not have existed under less harmful methods.

The later presence of Hiyori's correction actually weakens the claim that pure domination is the strongest form of authority.

## 23.3 "Ichinose's class loses because kindness is politically naive"

Too crude.

The class's trust produces measurable capacities: pooled capital, coordinated sacrifice, resilience under scandal, and universal rescue. Its vulnerability is more specific: insufficient adversarial security and result conversion (`Y1FF-E037`).

The correct reform target is defense, not kindness itself.

## 23.4 "Sakayanagi proves aristocratic rule is objectively best"

The point total proves strong Year 1 performance, not objective constitutional superiority.

Her polity benefits from deep talent and elite allocation, but the year also records incomplete unity, monopolized strategy, coercive factional management, and private-rivalry risk (`Y1FF-E040`).

A strong first-year result cannot answer succession.

## 23.5 "The Class Vote reveals the true value of students"

No.

The exam is a political mechanism deliberately created to produce an expulsion after none occurred naturally. Its result depends on popularity, coalition-building, external votes, private capital, hidden bargains, and leader strategy as much as on individual capacity (`Y1V10-E013–E019`).

It reveals class constitutions more clearly than it reveals human worth.

## 23.6 "Good leadership means minimizing coercion"

Not by itself.

A leader can avoid direct coercion and still fail through paralysis, information neglect, or inability to protect members.

The stronger Year 1 standard combines:

- effectiveness;
- accountability;
- correction;
- developmental humility;
- and limits on jurisdiction.

# 24. Final Year 1 synthesis

By the end of Year 1, the class competition has become a constitutional laboratory.

The four polities offer different answers to the problem of unequal people under common fate.

`POLITY-HORIKITA` says:

> people contain heterogeneous capacities that can be developed and coordinated, but leadership must learn to rely on knowledge it does not personally possess.

Its future depends on making hidden power answerable.

`POLITY-RYUEN` says:

> people can be coordinated rapidly when a strong ruler understands fear, incentive, and weakness better than they do.

Its future depends on whether voluntary loyalty can become correction and institution rather than merely stronger personal rule.

`POLITY-ICHINOSE` says:

> membership itself has political value, trust is real capital, and unequal ability does not automatically justify unequal entitlement to remain.

Its future depends on building security without destroying reciprocity.

`POLITY-SAKAYANAGI-ORIGIN` says:

> unequal talent is real, strategic judgment should allocate it efficiently, and collective procedure need not imply equal authorship.

Its future depends on whether centralized excellence can create successors and limit the sovereign's private jurisdiction.

None of these answers is sufficient alone.

Horikita needs Ichinose's insight that people cannot be reduced to current usefulness.

Ichinose needs Ryuuen's insight that adversaries may exploit norms they do not share.

Ryuuen needs Horikita's and Hiyori's insight that followers who can correct the leader are more politically valuable than silent instruments.

Sakayanagi needs the constitutional insight that accurate hierarchy is not self-legitimating and that a polity dependent on one superior map remains fragile.

All four need the final institutional lesson of Volume 11:

> even the best internal constitution is vulnerable if the authority above it can alter the record through which performance becomes official.

The strongest final formulation is therefore:

> **Year 1 presents class politics as the problem of converting unequal human capacities into collective action without allowing coordination to become ownership. The most promising political development is not equality of talent or elimination of leadership. It is the emergence of answerable asymmetry: leaders who can be corrected, experts whose knowledge can govern locally without owning others, members whose development changes the political map, and institutions whose records remain trustworthy enough that achievement can belong to the people who produced it.**

This is why the four classes should not be read simply as:

- good class;
- bad class;
- nice class;
- strong class.

They are four incomplete political technologies for making ability social.

Their failures reveal what each technology cannot see.

# 25. Year 1 locked propositions

The following propositions are sufficiently supported at the Year 1 boundary to govern later comparative work unless explicitly revised by later evidence.

1. **Class letters are rank states, not constitutional identities.** Stable polity IDs should remain the longitudinal reference.

7. **Ichinose's solidarity is real political capacity.** It produces resource pooling, survival under scandal, and collective rescue (`Y1V09-E087–E100`; `Y1V10-E071`; `Y1V10-E103–E105`).
8. **Ichinose's main Year 1 weakness is not kindness as such.** It is adversarial security and conversion of social capacity into competitive results (`Y1FF-E037`).
9. **Sakayanagi's polity is not neutral meritocracy.** Allocation is mixed with sovereign discretion, factional governance, hidden targeting, and private motive (`Y1V10-E076–E086`; `Y1V10-E106`).
10. **Sakayanagi's greatest constitutional risk is strategic monopoly.** *First File* explicitly records monopolized strategy and incomplete unity (`Y1FF-E040`).
11. **The Class Vote is a constitutional test more than a transparent merit test.** It exposes each polity's theory of membership under authored scarcity.

17. **Institutional measurement is bounded.** *First File* summaries and ratings can accurately describe present visible states without exhausting future political capacity.
18. **The governing Year 1 political question remains jurisdiction.** Accurate knowledge of another person's ability does not by itself grant the right to decide what that person's future is for.

# 27. Final conclusion

The first year begins with four classes that appear to be ranked containers.

It ends with four constitutions.

The movement is important because it changes what the school competition means.

Class rank is not only a contest among individual students.

It is a contest among ways of organizing people.

One class tries to make difference developable.

One makes difference governable through fear and strategy.

One makes difference survivable through trust and mutual insurance.

One makes difference legible to a sovereign allocator.

Each system sees something true.

Each system is dangerous when its truth becomes total.

Horikita is right that people can develop and that leadership must coordinate unlike capacities. She is wrong whenever superior developmental insight becomes hidden jurisdiction.

Ryuuen is right that conflict contains actors who exploit weakness, norms, and hesitation. He is wrong whenever this becomes a universal theory that reduces every motive to leverage.

Ichinose is right that people are more than their worst act or current utility and that solidarity can itself be a form of power. Her polity remains vulnerable when reciprocity is assumed where it has not been earned.

Sakayanagi is right that unequal ability is real and that bad allocation wastes capacity. Her polity becomes dangerous when the person who sees difference most accurately treats that accuracy as a title to rule without correction.

The political achievement of Year 1 is therefore not the discovery of the ideal constitution.

It is the discovery of a standard by which all four can be criticized:

> **collective power is legitimate only insofar as the people whose capacities constitute it remain capable of becoming more than the roles, rankings, fears, files, and models through which the polity makes them useful.**

That is the class-political form of the Year 1 file/person distinction.

A good constitution needs a model of its members.

A mature constitution must also know that the model is not the person.
