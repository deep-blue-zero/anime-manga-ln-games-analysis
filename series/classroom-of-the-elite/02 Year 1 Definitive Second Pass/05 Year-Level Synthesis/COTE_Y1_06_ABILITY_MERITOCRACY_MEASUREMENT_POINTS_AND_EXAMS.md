# 『ようこそ実力至上主義の教室へ』
## Year 1 — Ability, Meritocracy, Measurement, Points, and Examinations
### What the system can see, what people can become, and the price of turning partial measurements into fate

> **CANONICAL YEAR-1 SPECIALIST SNAPSHOT**
>
> This document is the primary analytical home for `実力 / jitsuryoku`, meritocracy, institutional measurement, class and private points, examination design, developmental ability, and the conversion of bounded performance into consequential judgment across Year 1. It synthesizes the fourteen canonical Japanese novel deep readings, the *First File* paratext audit, the final Year 1 evidence lock, the `THROUGH_Y1` institutional and class-polity ledgers, the Japanese terminology index, and the frozen `Y1_01–Y1_05` specialist layer.
>
> It does not import Year 2, Volume 0, *Second List*, Year 3, adaptation material, wikis, or later-franchise interpretation. `OAA` appears only where *First File* explicitly constructs a retrospective, counterfactual Year 1 model.

# 0. Scope, primary home, and interpretive controls

The title of the series invites a deceptively simple interpretation.

Students differ in ability.

The school identifies those differences.

The most capable rise.

The weak fall.

Year 1 never denies the first proposition. Its characters plainly differ in academic knowledge, physical power, discipline, social access, judgment, strategic imagination, courage, leadership, and willingness to contribute.

The rest of the formula becomes progressively unstable.

Ayanokōji possesses far more ability than he displays.

Kōenji displays enough to prove exceptional capacity while refusing collective use.

Sudō's entrance-state academics conceal an athletic and developmental trajectory that later changes the class.

Ike's ordinary classroom profile says little about his island usefulness.

Hirata's social value is real but difficult to translate into a conventional score.

Kei's influence over female-group atmosphere is politically important while remaining almost invisible to early formal ratings.

Kushida can receive a high cooperation rating while using trust as a weapon.

Sakayanagi's disability lowers physical aggregates without making her politically weak.

Tsukishiro can allow a superior move to occur and then corrupt the channel through which it becomes the official result.

*First File* can accurately report what a student made visible at a particular date and still fail as a total account of the person.

The problem is therefore not whether measurement is possible.

It is:

> **What does a measurement measure, under what conditions, at what time, through which incentives, and with what legitimate consequence?**

This document applies seven interpretive controls.

## 0.1 Year 1 is not anti-ability

The analysis must not respond to metric failure by claiming all students are secretly equal in capacity.

They are not.

The novels repeatedly establish consequential differences. Ayanokōji's exact entrance scores and advanced mathematics are not an egalitarian illusion (`Y1V01-E013`). Sudō's athletic ability is not merely a matter of confidence (`Y1V02-E015`). Kōenji's physical and academic capacity is real even when politically unavailable (`Y1V05-E026`; `Y1FF-E023`). Sakayanagi's strategic cognition is not erased by low physical performance (`Y1FF-E039`, `Y1FF-E048`).

The stronger claim is narrower:

> **real difference does not make every method of ranking, every consequence attached to ranking, or every inference from ranking legitimate.**

## 0.2 `実力` is not a hidden universal stat

The Japanese term `実力` can refer to actual or demonstrated capability—what someone can really do rather than what title, pedigree, reputation, or appearance suggests.

The series repeatedly complicates that intuition.

What a person can “really do” changes with:

- environment;
- preparation;
- health;
- motivation;
- social coordination;
- available information;
- willingness to contribute;
- and whether the institution records the act honestly.

Ayanokōji explicitly identifies inquiry into `実力` as one reason for entering the school (`Y1V01-E018`; terminology anchor `実力`). The title ideology `実力至上主義` appears at the end of Volume 1 as an unsettled problem rather than a settled endorsement (`Y1V01-E031`).

The analytical task is therefore to decompose the concept rather than assume the story hides one final leaderboard beneath all visible metrics.

## 0.3 Every measurement has an object and a date

A score may be accurate about:

- answers given on one exam;
- speed in one race;
- conduct during one observed month;
- willingness to cooperate under one rule structure;
- or publicly demonstrated performance as of one date.

It becomes misleading when the reader silently changes the object from:

> performance under these conditions

into:

> the person's complete and permanent worth.

Horikita's July cooperation E is a credible record of early visible behavior, not a timeless essence (`Y1FF-E012–E014`). Ayanokōji's July C/C-/D/C-/C- profile is credible as the surface he authored, not a discovery of his latent ceiling (`Y1FF-E004–E005`).

## 0.4 Performance, contribution, and personhood must remain distinct

A student may perform poorly in one domain and contribute strongly in another.

A student may possess major ability and contribute little.

A student may contribute greatly and still behave immorally.

A student may be strategically removable without thereby becoming a lesser person.

This distinction is essential for Sudō, Kōenji, Kushida, Kei, Hirata, Yamauchi, and the four class polities.

The school frequently converts performance into consequences affecting belonging. The analysis must not allow that institutional conversion to become a philosophical proof that belonging should be earned only through performance.

## 0.5 Examinations are authored environments, not neutral windows

An examination decides in advance:

- which abilities become relevant;
- what information is disclosed;
- whether cooperation or betrayal pays;
- how risk is distributed;
- whether weakness is buffered or exposed;
- whether the body, reputation, or relationships become test material;
- and what happens to those who fail.

The exam therefore does not merely discover students.

It helps produce the behavior it later calls evidence.

This does not make results unreal. It means results are inseparable from design.

## 0.6 Points price options, not human value

Private points and class points are real institutional resources.

They can purchase:

- goods;
- information;
- a single examination point;
- class transfer;
- administrative reversal;
- rescue from expulsion;
- and strategic cooperation.

The school thereby creates prices for outcomes affecting human futures.

A price is not the same thing as a moral valuation. The fact that twenty million private points can preserve enrollment does not establish that a student's future is “worth” twenty million points.

## 0.7 The Year 1 boundary remains binding

This document ends with Volume 11.5 and *First File*.

The hypothetical OAA material is treated as `FF-RATING`: official retrospective modeling, explicitly framed as what might have appeared **if** OAA had existed. Later OAA practice, later examinations, later class transfers, and Volume 0 explanations are excluded.

Year 1 questions remain open where Year 1 leaves them open.

# 1. Executive thesis

Year 1 is neither a defense of simple meritocracy nor a rejection of merit.

It is a sustained investigation of what happens when **partial observations of real difference are turned into total judgments and attached to unequal futures**.

The school begins with an apparently attractive proposition.

Students receive extraordinary freedom, abundant private points, and access to an elite institution. Their conduct, performance, and adaptation determine class rank and opportunity.

The first reveal is that the institution has withheld the evaluative criteria.

Freedom has already been converted into data.

The next volumes repeatedly complicate the idea that evaluation simply uncovers a preexisting hierarchy.

Volume 2 shows that truth requires evidentiary capacity, proof strength, credibility, and a decision procedure before it becomes institutionally actionable (`Y1V02-E021`).

Volume 3 changes the value of capacities by changing the environment. Camping knowledge becomes important; solitary academic excellence becomes less sufficient; a leader's concealed identity becomes a strategic resource (`Y1V03-E004–E010`).

Volume 4 calls itself a test of `シンキング / thinking`, then measures the management of distrust, class loyalty, behavioral reading, and willingness to betray under anonymous reward (`Y1V04-E010–E015`).

Volume 5 converts bodies into group points, class points, private points, and future academic advantage or penalty. It broadens recognition beyond academics while simultaneously making physical weakness intensify academic risk (`Y1V05-E003–E004`).

Volume 6 makes the pair—not the individual—the operative unit of academic survival. It rewards teaching, question design, self-assessment, secrecy, and the redistribution of exposure to weakness (`Y1V06-E012–E015`, `Y1V06-E029–E031`).

Volume 8 evaluates morality, discipline, order, and initiative while its decisive political operation depends on controlling the institution's causal interpretation. A uniform rescue price has radically unequal effects depending on liquidity and organization (`Y1V08-E121–E142`).

Volume 10 begins from the desired outcome that someone must be expelled, then asks anonymous votes to allocate the victim and grants differential future immunity through the Protection Point (`Y1V10-E013–E019`).

Volume 11 distributes ability across a portfolio of events, formalizes command as intervention into other people's performance, and finally demonstrates that superior performance can be made politically unreal when the record layer is captured (`Y1V11-E028–E036`, `Y1V11-E133–E145`).

*First File* then converts the year into ratings, timelines, point totals, class summaries, and hypothetical OAA numbers. It does not expose those forms as useless. It exposes their limits.

The completed Year 1 ability chain is:

> **possession → development → expression → legibility → coordination → recording → model limitation**

Each step can diverge from the others.

Ayanokōji possesses ability but suppresses expression.

Sudō develops beyond the model built from his initial output.

Kōenji expresses ability without allowing coordination.

Kei and Hirata contribute in ways early metrics only partly recognize.

Horikita increasingly converts distributed capacity into collective action.

Tsukishiro corrupts recording.

*First File* demonstrates that even an honest record remains a bounded model.

The point economy adds a second chain:

> **evaluation → class position → liquidity → available options → survival or exclusion**

A class with pooled capital can purchase mercy that a poorer class cannot. A popular student can receive a future shield against expulsion. Information can become a commodity. Bodily injury can be compensated. A class may imagine buying its way out of class fate.

The locked Year 1 thesis is therefore:

> **Ability is real, plural, developmental, partly voluntary, and socially mediated. Examinations do not merely measure it; they author the environments in which it becomes visible and often transform the people being measured. Points then translate institutional judgments into unequal capacities to buy information, mobility, rescue, and continued enrollment. Meritocratic authority is legitimate only when the object of measurement is bounded and disclosed, developmental uncertainty remains visible, records are trustworthy, consequences are proportionate, and no performance metric is allowed to become the whole person.**

# 2. The conceptual vocabulary of ability

The Year 1 Japanese vocabulary prevents several English-language collapses.

## 2.1 `実力 / jitsuryoku`

`実力` means actual, demonstrated, or effective capability. It carries a contrast with:

- reputation;
- formal rank;
- inherited status;
- empty appearance;
- or untested claim.

But “actual ability” does not necessarily mean a metaphysical essence waiting to be read correctly once.

The series uses changing environments to reveal that actuality is conditional. Ike really can solve practical island problems. That does not make him academically strong. Horikita really is academically capable. That does not make her early leadership socially usable. Kōenji really is exceptional. That does not make his capacity class property.

The important question becomes:

> actual for what purpose, under what conditions, and available to whom?

## 2.2 `能力 / nōryoku`

`能力` is the more general term for ability or capacity. *First File*'s `能力評価` presents standardized axes as an evaluation object.

The distinction is useful. A person may possess `能力` while an institution claims to estimate `実力` through performance. The novels repeatedly show that the route from capacity to demonstrated effectiveness passes through motivation, environment, social relation, and record.

## 2.3 `学力 / gakuryoku`

Academic ability is real and consequential.

It is not synonymous with total ability. Volume 1 makes the distinction explicit (`Y1V01-E012`). Paper Shuffle later demonstrates that academic knowledge itself has multiple social forms:

- knowing;
- teaching;
- designing questions;
- judging a partner;
- and creating a secure class-wide study system.

## 2.4 `評価 / hyōka`

Evaluation is an act performed by an evaluator according to selected criteria.

That grammatical and political fact matters. An evaluation is not identical to the evaluated person. It is a relationship among:

- observer;
- object;
- criteria;
- evidence;
- date;
- and consequence.

## 2.5 `協調性 / kyōchōsei`

Cooperation or cooperativeness appears in *First File* as a standardized category.

Year 1 gives strong evidence that it cannot mean morality, loyalty, or total social contribution.

Kushida's high cooperation can coexist with deliberate sabotage (`Y1FF-E015`). Kōenji's near-bottom cooperation can coexist with extraordinary capacity (`Y1FF-E023`). Kei's low formal cooperation can coexist with practical social authority (`Y1FF-E019`). Ryūen's modeled social contribution of 1 can coexist with the ability to organize collective action through coercion (`Y1FF-E045`).

The category measures something real. It does not answer every social question.

## 2.6 `総合力 / sōgōryoku`

The Event Selection Exam's apparent concern with overall strength is especially important because overall strength is not produced through one average individual.

It emerges from:

- diversity of capacities;
- correct allocation;
- preparation;
- command;
- and the willingness of people to participate.

A class's `総合力` is therefore closer to an institutional property than a sum of private stats.

## 2.7 `実力主義`, `真の実力主義`, and `本当の実力主義`

The year introduces several phrases that sound like definitive ideological answers.

Nagumo promises an `究極の実力主義の学校` and later `真の実力主義`, using ability supremacy as a political program for mobility and removal (`Y1V05-E057`; `Y1V06-E005`).

Volume 11.5 stages another dispute over `本当の実力主義`, contrasting Manabu's class-centered order with Nagumo's more individualized mobility (`Y1V11.5-E013–E019` and terminology anchor `本当の実力主義`).

The repetition does not settle the meaning.

It shows that “true meritocracy” is itself politically contested.

# 3. The Year 1 taxonomy of ability

No single scale can perform the analytical work Year 1 requires. At least ten layers must remain distinct.

| Layer | Governing question | Representative cases |
|---|---|---|
| **Possessed ability** | What can the person actually do under some conditions? | Ayanokōji, Kōenji, Sakayanagi, Sudō |
| **Developmental ability** | What can change through effort, relation, time, or environment? | Sudō, Horikita, Sakura, Ryūen's followers |
| **Expressed ability** | What does the person choose or manage to display now? | Ayanokōji's fifties; Matsushita; Kōenji |
| **Legible ability** | What can another actor or metric infer from expression? | hearings, ratings, reputation, First File |
| **Ecological ability** | What is useful in this environment? | Ike on the island; Sakura as witness; Sakayanagi in politics |
| **Socially usable ability** | Can others coordinate or rely upon the capacity? | Hirata, Kei, Ichinose, Horikita |
| **Distributed ability** | How does a group combine unlike capacities? | Paper Shuffle; Event Selection Exam |
| **Generative ability** | Can the person produce answers not contained in another's model? | Horikita's leadership; developed students exceeding prediction |
| **Political ability** | Can the person shape incentives, rules, interpretation, or collective action? | Ryūen, Nagumo, Sakayanagi, Ayanokōji |
| **Recorded/modelled ability** | What becomes official, retrievable, and actionable? | Tsukishiro's chess interference; First File/OAA |

## 3.1 Possessed ability

Possession is the layer most conventional power-ranking discussion emphasizes.

Year 1 needs it but refuses to stop there.

Ayanokōji possesses advanced academic, physical, strategic, and observational capacities. Kōenji possesses extraordinary physical and academic capacity. Sudō possesses elite athletic potential. Sakayanagi possesses unusual strategic intelligence. Hiyori possesses strong reading and inferential ability.

Without possession, later distinctions become empty.

But possession alone says nothing about:

- willingness;
- timing;
- use;
- coordination;
- morality;
- or recognition.

## 3.2 Developmental ability

Developmental ability is not simply “potential” imagined without evidence. It is the demonstrated capacity to become different through practice, relationship, and changed motivation.

Sudō is the strongest longitudinal example. The student whose aggression and academics made him an obvious early liability becomes more disciplined, more academically engaged, more capable of accepting criticism, and increasingly useful as a domain authority (`Y1V06-E007`, `Y1V06-E031`; `Y1V11-E078`).

His development makes every static early ranking epistemically dangerous.

Horikita develops from solitary academic competence toward the ability to gather, allocate, and revise knowledge about a class. Sakura develops social participation. Ryūen's followers develop forms of agency not reducible to fear. Ike begins turning practical and relational experience into visible contribution.

Development means that time is part of measurement.

## 3.3 Expressed ability

Expression is the interface between possession and observation.

Ayanokōji deliberately records a mediocre swim time and exact entrance-exam fifties (`Y1V01-E008`, `Y1V01-E013`). Matsushita later reveals another deliberate underperformer (`Y1V11.5-E120`). Kōenji displays enough capacity to establish exceptionality but withholds ordinary participation (`Y1V11-E060`).

An institution may honestly record what was expressed and still miss what was withheld.

## 3.4 Legible ability

Legibility is what another actor can make sense of.

Volume 2 provides the clearest early vocabulary. Sakura's testimony may possess `証拠能力` while lacking enough `証明力` to establish the whole causal proposition (`Y1V02-E021`). Truth does not become institutionally effective merely by existing.

The same is true of ability.

A capacity that produces no credible trace may remain politically unreal.

## 3.5 Ecological ability

The value of ability changes when the environment changes.

Ike's camping experience becomes rare and valuable on the island (`Y1V03-E010–E013`). Horikita's illness makes solitary excellence a liability. Sakayanagi's physical limitation matters in athletic scoring while leaving political command intact (`Y1V05-E006`). Sakura's witness role makes a socially anxious student central in an adjudicative environment.

Ecological ability rejects the idea that one rank travels unchanged across every context.

## 3.6 Socially usable ability

Possession becomes socially usable when other people can coordinate, trust, or access it.

Hirata's ability lies not merely in kindness but in making heterogeneous people function together. Kei can read atmosphere and influence the girls' network. Kushida can reach people Horikita cannot. Ichinose can turn trust into organization. Horikita increasingly becomes able to translate specialists into a portfolio.

Kōenji marks the boundary:

> `その才能を君のために使うかどうかを決めるのは私自身だ`
>
> I decide whether to use that talent for you.

Possessed ability is not automatically collective property (`Y1V11-E060`; Japanese anchor `その才能を君のために使うかどうかを決めるのは私自身だ`).

## 3.7 Distributed ability

A class is not simply the average or sum of individual performance.

Paper Shuffle makes pairs the operative unit of survival. The Event Selection Exam requires a class to construct, train, and allocate a portfolio. Distributed ability depends upon network structure:

- who teaches whom;
- who possesses information;
- who can safely bear risk;
- who accepts assignment;
- and whether the leader knows enough to allocate without claiming ownership.

## 3.8 Generative ability

Generative ability is the capacity to produce a response another person's model did not already contain.

It is the deepest challenge to static meritocracy and to developmental authorship.

Ayanokōji may create conditions for development. The developed person can then exceed his script. Horikita increasingly detects and resists hidden influence. Kei refuses betrayal on the rooftop despite the contingency Ayanokōji designed. Sudō's development invalidates stale opponent expectations.

Generative ability is why people cannot be treated as predictable assets without remainder.

## 3.9 Political ability

Political ability changes the environment in which everyone else's capacity becomes valuable.

Ryūen models fear and incentives. Nagumo acquires influence over examination design and year-wide liquidity. Sakayanagi allocates talent and controls information. Ayanokōji authors visibility and contingency. Horikita increasingly builds public systems of coordination and correction.

Political ability can dominate spectacular individual performance because it defines the field on which performance matters.

## 3.10 Recorded and modeled ability

A displayed performance becomes an institutional fact only through recording.

Tsukishiro's interference demonstrates that the record layer can be separated from the act (`Y1V11-E139–E145`).

*First File* adds the final distinction. A record may be honest and still incomplete. Its ratings and hypothetical OAA are models built from selected axes and visible output (`Y1FF-E041–E048`).

The completed formula is therefore:

> **`実力` is what a person can possess, develop, express, make legible, coordinate, and preserve as a truthful result—while every resulting file remains a model rather than the person in full.**

# 4. Ability domains and contested cases

## 4.1. Academic ability — necessary, real, and socially mediated

Year 1 does not minimize academics.

Poor academic performance creates immediate danger. High academic ability produces real advantages. Horikita, Keisei, Hiyori, Ichinose, and Sakayanagi are not merely socially constructed as strong students.

The correction is that academic ability itself contains several distinguishable capacities.

### 4.1.1 Knowing is not teaching

Horikita's first study-group failure comes from assuming foundations her classmates do not possess and treating misunderstanding as evidence of inferiority (`Y1V01-E014`).

Her later improvement depends upon redesigning explanation around the learner, using basketball analogies, and researching Sudō's world (`Y1V01-E019–E020`).

The sequence separates:

- subject mastery;
- diagnosis of another person's misunderstanding;
- explanation;
- motivation;
- and preservation of dignity.

A high-scoring student may possess the first while lacking the others.

### 4.1.2 The midterm measures answers; survival measures institutional intelligence

The Volume 1 midterm officially asks academic questions.

The class's survival depends upon:

- obtaining the correct range;
- finding old examinations;
- identifying a seller through point poverty;
- teaching;
- delaying answer memorization so study still occurs;
- understanding the threshold;
- lowering an average strategically;
- and interpreting the point system literally enough to buy one mark (`Y1V01-E022–E029`).

The formal test remains academically meaningful.

The event's actual `実力` is much larger than the answer sheet.

### 4.1.3 Paper Shuffle formalizes teaching and pair design

Paper Shuffle makes the pair the unit of risk (`Y1V06-E012–E015`).

A class must know:

- who is strong in which subjects;
- which student can teach;
- what weak student can realistically learn;
- how to manipulate preliminary scores without making the manipulation obvious;
- and how to build pair totals that remain safe across every subject.

The strong student is no longer rewarded only for private excellence.

The class asks the strong student to become an institutional buffer.

This is a significant meritocratic choice. It treats superior ability as carrying relational responsibility.

### 4.1.4 Academic hierarchy can distort self-worth

Keisei recognizes after the sports festival that academics had become his sole identity. Sudō's public athletic value forces him to confront the similarity between his contempt for weak students and his own uselessness in another environment (`Y1V06-E031`).

The insight does not make academic excellence less real.

It makes domain exclusivity harder to defend.

### 4.1.5 Question authorship is a higher-order academic capacity

Paper Shuffle requires students to create valid questions and answers for another class. The school reviews them and substitutes easier material if the submission is invalid (`Y1V06-E014`).

This measures more than knowing a curriculum.

It measures:

- understanding what counts as fair difficulty;
- constructing solvable problems;
- anticipating another class's weaknesses;
- and protecting the submission process from sabotage.

A student can therefore know answers without possessing the meta-ability to author a legitimate exam.

### 4.1.6 Scores can become political weapons

Horikita and Kushida use academic wagers to convert an interpersonal conflict into a bounded procedure (`Y1V06-E039–E040`). Horikita later creates a single-subject wager with Ayanokōji so the hidden-ability dispute can be tested through an auditable object (`Y1V11.5-E068`).

The strength of these wagers lies in their narrowness.

They do not claim to measure the whole person. They define one contest, one subject, and one consequence.

### 4.1.7 Academic development destabilizes static models

Sudō's improving attendance, study behavior, and self-control are not spectacular enough to erase his weaknesses. They are enough to invalidate a model that treats his entrance-state academics as destiny (`Y1V06-E007`).

A measurement regime that updates slowly becomes less accurate precisely because the person changes.

## 4.2. Physical ability, embodiment, disability, and aggregate scoring

The sports festival is Year 1's most direct measurement of the body.

It is also one of the clearest demonstrations that bodily measurement can be accurate and politically incomplete.

### 4.2.1 Athletic ability is genuine plural recognition

Sudō's body gives him a route to institutional value that ordinary academics obscure. Individual placements can become private points or future written-test advantage (`Y1V05-E004`).

This is a real broadening of what the school rewards.

A system that recognizes only classroom performance would mismeasure him more severely.

### 4.2.2 Cross-domain conversion is both flexible and punitive

The same mechanism that lets athletic excellence compensate for academic weakness lets athletic weakness worsen future academic risk.

The bottom-ten penalty translates poor athletic aggregate into a written-exam disadvantage.

The institution therefore does not merely recognize plural ability.

It makes domains economically and academically exchangeable.

That can create second chances.

It can also compound vulnerability.

### 4.2.3 Expert bodies do not automatically produce teaching ability

Sudō initially assumes that because his movement is clear to him, weaker classmates should be able to reproduce it. Frustration follows when they cannot (`Y1V05-E017–E019`).

Hirata's adaptive coaching demonstrates a more socially usable athletic intelligence.

A body can know without being able to translate what it knows.

### 4.2.4 Public display changes reputation

Ayanokōji's relay against Manabu makes hidden physical capacity publicly legible (`Y1V05-E056`, `Y1V05-E060–E061`).

The performance does not expose his full scale.

It changes the range of plausible models other people can hold.

Display is therefore irreversible in a way possession is not. Once the class sees enough, later mediocrity requires explanation.

### 4.2.5 Injury turns bodies into political evidence

Ryūen's festival strategy depends on arranging real bodily events whose causal meaning can be disputed. Kinoshita's injury is real. The narrative of deliberate assault is authored from real circumstances (`Y1V05-E031`, `Y1V05-E041–E045`).

The body supplies strong evidence.

It does not supply complete causation.

The later revelation that Kinoshita accepted a 500,000-point share monetizes injury directly (`Y1V05-E066`).

### 4.2.6 Disability exposes the politics of aggregate scores

Sakayanagi's physical disability is a genuine disadvantage in events requiring participant bodies. Her classmates do not treat her as worthless; she remains politically powerful and strategically central (`Y1V05-E006`).

*First File*'s hypothetical OAA gives her elite academics and strong adaptability but a low physical score that depresses the overall aggregate (`Y1FF-E048`).

The aggregate is not mathematically false.

It becomes interpretively false if read as total capability.

### 4.2.7 Kōenji demonstrates the difference between capacity and contribution

Kōenji's physical ability is undeniable. His contribution remains conditional (`Y1V05-E026`; `Y1V08-E104`, `Y1V08-E122`, `Y1V08-E126`).

The school and class face different questions:

- Does he possess ability? Yes.
- Can the class rely upon it? Usually no.
- Does collective need create a right to command it? Not automatically.

This is one of Year 1's most important limits on utilitarian meritocracy.

## 4.3. Social, informational, and political ability

Social ability is frequently misread because it is less spectacular than academic or physical excellence and because morally different actors can possess it.

Year 1 treats sociality as a set of capacities, not a synonym for goodness.

### 4.3.1 Hirata — legitimacy as infrastructure

Hirata can invite, mediate, reassure, teach, and make participation feel voluntary. His role becomes so important that his withdrawal after the Class Vote creates a practical governance crisis.

His ability is socially legible because people willingly respond to him.

It is not unlimited. He struggles when the institution requires discriminating judgment among people he wants to protect equally.

### 4.3.2 Kushida — access, memory, and the danger of metric moralization

Kushida remembers birthdays, maintains broad networks, calibrates social pressure, and makes information travel (`Y1V04.5-E017`). These are genuine abilities.

Her high cooperation rating does not prove loyalty or moral reliability (`Y1FF-E015`).

A metric can correctly record visible coordination and still be dangerously misread if cooperation is equated with benevolence.

### 4.3.3 Kei — atmosphere as political power

Kei's conventional academic and physical file is weak (`Y1FF-E019`, `Y1FF-E044`).

That file does not explain her ability to:

- read social danger;
- influence the girls' network;
- control presentation;
- and execute covert tasks.

Her case shows why formal metrics often undervalue abilities embedded in status systems the institution does not explicitly score.

### 4.3.4 Ichinose — trust as an organizational technology

Ichinose creates offices, circulates information, pools resources, coordinates cross-class aid, and generates voluntary disclosure.

Her high social and adaptive scores in the hypothetical OAA correspond to something real (`Y1FF-E047`).

They do not guarantee victory against actors who exploit reciprocal norms.

The metric identifies capacity without proving strategic sufficiency.

### 4.3.5 Ryūen — coordination through coercion

Ryūen can model fear, desire, incentives, and institutional blind spots. He centralizes devices and information and mobilizes a class rapidly (`Y1V04-E027–E028`).

*First File* gives him social contribution 1 while retaining high adaptability (`Y1FF-E045`).

The number is useful because it distinguishes:

- producing collective action;
- from producing action the institution recognizes as prosocial contribution.

Effectiveness and approved contribution are not the same axis.

### 4.3.6 Hiyori — quiet judgment beyond a weak adaptive file

Hiyori's official and hypothetical files understate the practical subtlety of her observation and class-protective judgment (`Y1FF-E030`, `Y1FF-E032`, `Y1FF-E046`).

Her case is an internal warning against treating low quick-thinking or judgment scores as total inability to read a political environment.

### 4.3.7 Credibility is a form of power

The Sudō hearing demonstrates that who speaks changes what can be believed. Kushida's credibility makes past exam material usable. Ichinose's cross-class legitimacy changes the hearing. Sakura's class affiliation weakens the institutional force of true testimony.

Credibility is not truth.

It is the social capacity to make truth travel.

### 4.3.8 Reputation is an unofficial rating system

Volume 9 shows that repeated allegations can create a distributed moral score without formal procedure. A true shoplifting incident is expanded into a total character identity (`Y1V09-E021–E025`, `Y1V09-E065–E071`).

Reputation functions like an uncontrolled file:

- categories are selected by attackers;
- evidence standards are low;
- corrections may amplify suspicion;
- and the resulting judgment changes political options.

The problem of measurement therefore exists outside official forms as well.

## 4.4. Developmental and generative ability

The strongest critique of static meritocracy is not philosophical assertion.

It is longitudinal evidence that people become different.

### 4.4.1 Sudō as the decisive counterexample

Sudō begins as academically weak, volatile, physically gifted, and politically costly.

Across Year 1 he learns to:

- accept teaching;
- control retaliation;
- recognize how his conduct affects the class;
- apologize publicly;
- listen to Horikita;
- improve attendance and study;
- and exercise domain authority more responsibly.

His development does not make the original weaknesses imaginary.

It demonstrates that the original profile was temporally incomplete.

This is why any Year 1 claim that the Class Vote simply reveals “objective human value” fails. The same institution that once had strong reason to classify Sudō as dangerous later benefits from the person he becomes.

### 4.4.2 Ike — development through recognition

On the island, Ayanokōji withholds his own answer so Ike can teach the class and discover that his experience matters (`Y1V03-E010–E013`).

This is a clean example of developmental recognition.

The capacity existed before the class valued it. Social recognition changes self-understanding, which changes later conduct.

### 4.4.3 Sakura — context-sensitive courage

Sakura's ability to witness, photograph, and speak truth remains constrained by social anxiety. Her development is not well represented by a fixed cooperation score.

The relevant question is whether the environment makes participation survivable.

This is why ordinary friendship can be educationally significant even when no exam rewards it.

### 4.4.4 Horikita — generative leadership

Horikita's strongest Year 1 ability is not academic excellence.

It is the emerging capacity to make other people's abilities usable without needing to possess them herself.

She learns to:

- identify domain experts;
- redesign teaching;
- allocate events;
- gather written information;
- preserve scarce strengths;
- and accept dissent as part of leadership (`Y1V11-E059–E067`; `Y1V11.5-E009–E011`).

This is generative because the class can produce more than her private ability alone contains.

### 4.4.5 Development can invalidate superior prediction

A strategist may correctly model a person at one date and become wrong because the person changes.

Sudō's development, Horikita's increasing inference, Kei's autonomous refusal, and Ryūen's followers' voluntary preservation all demonstrate this.

The error is not always poor initial observation.

Sometimes the world changes because people do.

### 4.4.6 The ethical significance of generative unpredictability

A person who can exceed the model retains something essential that cannot be safely priced or allocated in advance.

This does not eliminate forecasting. Institutions must make decisions under uncertainty.

It limits the authority of those decisions.

The more a system claims the right to terminate a person's future, the more dangerous it becomes to treat current prediction as destiny.

## 4.5. Ayanokōji, Sakayanagi, and the natural/artificial genius problem

Year 1 places Ayanokōji and Sakayanagi at the center of a dispute about whether exceptional ability is born, made, or both.

The local evidence supports the dispute.

It does not permit later sources to settle it here.

### 4.5.1 Ayanokōji as manufactured exception

Sakayanagi identifies him as his father's masterpiece and connects him to the White Room (`Y1V05-E071–E073`).

Within Year 1, the details of that educational system remain incomplete and partly mediated through unreliable adult testimony. What is clear is that Ayanokōji is understood as a product of intensive artificial formation rather than ordinary schooling.

### 4.5.2 Sakayanagi's hereditarian thesis

Child Sakayanagi states that true ability is decided at birth rather than produced by education (`Y1V11-E003`, `Y1V11-E011–E012`).

Her interest in Ayanokōji is therefore philosophical and personal. Defeating him would support a theory of natural superiority against a manufactured masterpiece.

### 4.5.3 The chess contest cannot settle the theory cleanly

The final contest depends upon:

- Horikita's rapid training;
- the board state she creates;
- Ayanokōji's bounded intervention;
- Sakayanagi's own decision-making;
- and Tsukishiro's manipulation of the communication channel.

Even without interference, one chess line would not settle the origin of human ability in general.

With interference, the official result cannot even settle who made the stronger move.

### 4.5.4 Ayanokōji rejects a grand innate-genius explanation

When Manabu describes him as unlike prior opponents, Ayanokōji says only that he worked harder (`Y1V11.5-E086`).

The statement may be modest, defensive, or incomplete. It nonetheless resists the romantic simplification that his capacity requires no labor.

### 4.5.5 Education can produce ability without legitimating the educational method

Even if the White Room successfully produces extraordinary performance, effectiveness would not settle legitimacy.

The same principle applies inside ANHS.

An exam may produce growth. Ayanokōji may produce growth. A leader may produce growth.

Later benefit does not automatically authorize coercion, deprivation, or ownership of the developmental end.

### 4.5.6 The deeper Year 1 question

The natural/artificial-genius rivalry is less important than the institutional question beneath it:

> **Who may claim authorship of another person's excellence?**

A parent may say the child is a product.

A school may say the exam revealed merit.

A strategist may say the environment produced development.

The developed person may answer that the resulting ability is now hers.

Year 1 increasingly sides with that final jurisdiction without denying causal influence.

# 5. A legitimacy test for measurement

Year 1 supports a demanding but not anti-measurement standard.

A measurement is more legitimate when the following questions receive defensible answers.

## 5.1 What is the declared object?

Is the system measuring:

- academic recall;
- physical performance;
- strategic adaptation;
- group coordination;
- popularity;
- moral conduct;
- or some mixture?

A composite score can conceal political choices by presenting unlike categories as one natural total.

## 5.2 Are the criteria knowable?

The Volume 1 class-point reveal demonstrates the danger of hidden criteria. The school can learn from unprompted conduct, but students cannot meaningfully contest a judgment whose calculation remains undisclosed (`Y1V01-E005`, `Y1V01-E011`).

Opacity may produce data.

It weakens procedural legitimacy.

## 5.3 Does the environment distort expression?

Fear, illness, social isolation, threat of expulsion, unequal information, and deliberate underperformance all change what a person displays.

The result may still be real performance under pressure. It should not be silently generalized into performance under every condition.

## 5.4 Can the metric be gamed or authored?

Ayanokōji authors mediocre output. Kushida can optimize visible cooperation while preserving hostile intent. Nagumo can design the examination around the attack. Ryūen can make true recorded facts support a false causal story. Sakayanagi can author praise-vote outcomes. Tsukishiro can alter the official command.

A measurement system changes behavior once people understand what it rewards.

That does not make measurement impossible.

It means the evaluator must distinguish the metric from the underlying purpose.

## 5.5 Is development allowed enough time to appear?

A system that uses present weakness to terminate future participation makes an irreversible decision under uncertainty.

Sudō is the persistent counterexample. Chabashira later admits that student growth can overturn prediction. The Class Vote nevertheless requires a final removal now.

The consequence is more severe than the knowledge justifies.

## 5.6 Can the record be corrected or appealed?

A legitimate system requires more than rules. It needs:

- trustworthy evidence;
- correction channels;
- and actors capable of resisting procedural capture.

Volume 11 demonstrates how record corruption nullifies the merit claim. Volume 11.5 demonstrates that ANHS contains partial internal checks rather than one uniform will.

## 5.7 Is the consequence proportionate to the measurement?

A low test score may justify remedial study.

It does not automatically justify expulsion.

A popularity vote may reveal social position.

It does not automatically justify ending a student's educational future.

A physical aggregate may reveal athletic disadvantage.

It does not define total institutional value.

The more irreversible the consequence, the more demanding the epistemic burden should become.

## 5.8 Does the measured person retain jurisdiction over use?

Kōenji's challenge is analytically necessary. The class may know that he is capable. That knowledge does not itself create authority to requisition his capacity.

Measurement can establish that an ability exists without settling who has the right to direct it.

# 6. The Year 1 progression: from hidden judgment to the limits of the file

The year can be read as a sequence of changing measurement problems.

## 6.1 Volume 1 — hidden behavioral evaluation

The school provides 100,000 private points and says students may live freely. It withholds the fact that ordinary behavior will determine future class income (`Y1V01-E005`, `Y1V01-E011`).

The first problem is therefore not academic ability.

It is whether conduct remains disciplined when visible supervision appears absent.

The school also demonstrates the political danger of hidden criteria. Students receive consequences without access to the full calculation, and class placement quickly becomes social caste.

The midterm then separates learning from institutional survival. The class needs study, teaching, information acquisition, threshold manipulation, and finally the purchase of one point (`Y1V01-E022–E029`).

The formal test measures answers. Survival depends on a much larger system.

## 6.2 Volume 2 — truth, proof, and credibility

The Sudō case moves from score measurement to adjudicative legibility.

True testimony is insufficient unless it qualifies as evidence and possesses enough proof strength (`Y1V02-E021`). Reputation changes plausibility. Collective class incentives make friendly testimony easier to discount. Visible injuries prove violence but not initiation.

Ability now includes the capacity to make truth actionable inside an institution.

## 6.3 Volume 3 — ecological ability under scarcity

The island exam begins with 300 points, converts remaining points into class points, prices bodily welfare, rewards location occupation, and makes leader identity a strategic object (`Y1V03-E004–E007`).

The environment reveals capacities ordinary academics do not:

- camping knowledge;
- practical labor;
- resource management;
- social mediation;
- intelligence operations;
- and leader concealment.

The exam also transforms students by redistributing recognition. Ike becomes useful because the environment makes his experience valuable and Ayanokōji allows him to own the discovery (`Y1V03-E010–E013`).

## 6.4 Volume 4 — thinking as distrust management

The VIP exam claims to test `シンキング`, but the rules turn thought into management of secrecy, class interest, device identity, behavioral strain, network authority, and betrayal (`Y1V04-E010–E016`).

The largest total private reward belongs to cooperation. The fastest class-point gain belongs to successful betrayal. Anonymity lowers the cost of private defection.

The exam measures how people behave inside incentives the school authored.

It does not reveal a context-free quantity called intelligence.

## 6.5 Volume 4.5 — ability outside formal scoring

Summer leisure becomes a counterexample to exam totalization.

Ibuki possesses communication capacity but cannot use it well under social pressure; the text explicitly distinguishes possession from skilled deployment (`Y1V04.5-E006`). Kushida's memory and relationship database remain political abilities. Airi's digital communication becomes context-sensitive capacity. Sudō receives club-based point recognition. Ordinary friendship, gifts, privacy, and refusal generate development without a formal score (`Y1V04.5-E017`, `Y1V04.5-E046`, `Y1V04.5-E058`).

The absence of a major exam does not mean the absence of learning.

## 6.6 Volume 5 — public bodies and cross-domain exchange

The sports festival translates athletic placement into group points, class points, private points, written-test advantage, and future academic penalty (`Y1V05-E003–E004`).

This is plural measurement with a coercive edge.

Sudō's athletic excellence can offset academic weakness. A weak athletic performance can worsen academic vulnerability. Sakayanagi's disability becomes an aggregate competitive disadvantage without erasing her political power (`Y1V05-E006`).

The festival also proves that expert performance is not leadership. Sudō must learn that another body cannot simply reproduce his technique, while Hirata adapts instruction to the learner (`Y1V05-E017–E019`).

## 6.7 Volume 6 — the network becomes the unit of competence

Paper Shuffle pairs students, combines scores, requires minimums, makes classes author questions, and ties attack/defense outcomes to class points (`Y1V06-E012–E015`).

The exam rewards:

- teaching;
- pair optimization;
- self-knowledge;
- adversarial question design;
- secrecy;
- and coordinated trust.

Weakness is not eliminated. Exposure to weakness is redistributed. Strong students become buffers rather than merely top scorers (`Y1V06-E029–E031`).

## 6.8 Volumes 7 and 7.5 — admission, mobility, and competing merit ideologies

Volume 7 reveals that nominal entrance testing follows a prior selection process and that Ayanokōji's admission was exceptional (`Y1V07-E031`). Admission is therefore already curated before students enter the visible competition.

The twenty-million-point transfer rule and Ryūen's 800-million-point horizon make class fate conceptually purchasable (`Y1V07-E046`, `Y1V07-E102`; `Y1V07.5-E039`).

Manabu and Nagumo then articulate competing institutional futures. Manabu wants to preserve graduation where possible. Ryūen treats expulsion as evidence of insufficient value. Nagumo promises cross-class opportunity to selected capable students while also concentrating allocation power in himself (`Y1V07.5-E042–E043`, `Y1V07.5-E083`).

## 6.9 Volume 8 — joint liability and rule authorship

The Mixed Training Camp formally evaluates morality, discipline, order, and initiative. In practice, it measures social adaptability, burden distribution, practical competence, trust creation, and the ability to weaponize trust.

The most important political fact is that Nagumo designs the examination around his strategy rather than merely adapting after rules are announced (`Y1V08-E138`).

This is a scale change.

The powerful actor is no longer only the best examinee.

It is the actor who helps decide what examination everyone else must inhabit.

## 6.10 Volume 9 — reputation as an unofficial measurement regime

The rumor campaign is not a formal exam, but it demonstrates how society measures moral identity.

One true wrongdoing is selected, surrounded by heterogeneous false allegations, and converted into a totalizing claim about Ichinose's character (`Y1V09-E021–E025`, `Y1V09-E065–E071`).

Reputation becomes a distributed file written by repetition rather than official form.

The school intervenes only after rumor volume and liability cross a threshold (`Y1V09-E072`, `Y1V09-E078–E079`).

## 6.11 Volume 10 — compulsory ranking and manufactured disposability

The Class Vote exists because no first-year student has yet been expelled (`Y1V10-E013`).

The institution begins with the desired output—one removal—and asks anonymous praise and criticism votes to allocate it. The Protection Point converts positive social judgment into future immunity, while twenty million private points can purchase an exception (`Y1V10-E014–E019`).

The exam measures social position, usefulness, resentment, coordination, and external reputation.

It cannot establish total human worth.

## 6.12 Volume 11 — portfolio ability and capture of the record

The Event Selection Exam permits academic, physical, game, typing, archery, and other specialized events. Participation limits prevent one elite from carrying every event. Commanders intervene indirectly (`Y1V11-E028–E036`).

The class must therefore know its own portfolio.

Horikita's notebook and assignment work make leadership a problem of distributed knowledge. Kōenji's refusal preserves the autonomy boundary. Sudō's development defeats stale models.

The chess ending then separates performance from official result. Tsukishiro changes the command channel and thereby authors the record (`Y1V11-E133–E145`).

A meritocracy without record integrity is not merely imperfect.

It cannot know who performed better.

## 6.13 Volume 11.5 — auditable testing and “true meritocracy”

Horikita creates a bounded single-subject wager to make the hidden-ability dispute with Ayanokōji measurable (`Y1V11.5-E068`). The wager is narrow and therefore epistemically cleaner than a total-character claim.

At the same time, Manabu and Nagumo remain competing answers to `本当の実力主義`. The year closes without defining one final institutional model.

## 6.14 *First File* — the archive and the person

*First File* supplies calendars, point movements, ratings, class summaries, and hypothetical OAA values (`Y1FF-E001–E003`, `Y1FF-E041–E058`).

Its most important contribution is formal.

The guidebook places Ayanokōji's mediocre file beside acknowledgment that he deliberately authored mediocre performance (`Y1FF-E004–E005`). It preserves Horikita's early cooperation E beside a year that demonstrates substantial development. It gives Ryūen a social-contribution 1 despite obvious political coordination. It gives Hiyori a low adaptability score despite evidence of quiet judgment. It lowers Sakayanagi's aggregate through physical disability while recognizing elite leadership.

The file is useful.

The file is not the person.

# 7. Entrance selection, class placement, and the first institutional hypothesis

ANHS begins measuring before the first visible examination.

Volume 7 reveals that nominal entrance testing follows a prior candidate-selection process and that Ayanokōji entered through an exceptional chairman-authorized route (`Y1V07-E031–E032`).

The school therefore does not receive a random adolescent population and discover merit from scratch.

It curates the population, sorts it, then subjects the selected students to further evaluation.

This matters for every later claim about class outcomes.

## 7.1 Class placement is not a simple descending rank of raw ability

Manabu explicitly rejects the theory that students are placed A through D by a single descending scale of total capacity (`Y1V04.5-E019–E020`).

Horikita's own profile makes the point.

Her academics and physical ability appear compatible with an upper-class student, yet her inability to work with others and her dependence on Manabu's recognition materially limit her institutional usefulness (`Y1FF-E012–E014`).

Class placement therefore behaves more like an institutional hypothesis:

> this student, in this present social and behavioral configuration, belongs in this environment.

A hypothesis can identify something real without defining the whole person.

## 7.2 Placement becomes a social fact that helps produce later performance

Once class letters are assigned, students and teachers attach expectations to them.

Class D is called defective. Class A receives presumptive status. A B-class placement becomes something Nagumo can weaponize as evidence of hidden deficiency when approaching Ichinose (`Y1V09-E005–E007`).

The placement therefore has at least three layers:

1. an institutional judgment;
2. a resource position;
3. a reputation that alters how later evidence is interpreted.

This creates a feedback loop.

Lower placement may identify existing problems. It can also intensify those problems through stigma, reduced confidence, inferior information, or teachers whose own motives affect disclosure.

## 7.3 The class letter is economically consequential

Class points determine position and monthly private-point income. A lower class therefore begins with fewer collective resources and fewer options for buying information, absorbing penalties, or rescuing members.

The ranking is not merely descriptive.

It changes the future probability of success.

This is one reason Year 1 cannot treat final point standings as pure evidence of original merit. Institutional position produces cumulative advantage and disadvantage.

## 7.4 Ayanokōji's entrance scores expose the dependence of sorting on expression

Ayanokōji's exact fifties are not ordinary mediocrity. They are an authored interface (`Y1V01-E013`).

The institutional problem is not simply that the school failed to measure him.

It is that he supplied a performance designed to be measured as average.

A selection system can only infer from the trace available to it. When the subject strategically constructs the trace, the measurement becomes accurate about a persona.

## 7.5 Blood relation is not a metric

Manabu's instruction not to estimate Suzune's ability from blood relation rejects another form of false measurement (`Y1V10` terminology anchor `血縁関係だけで実力を推し量るな`).

Family resemblance, pedigree, and association with an exceptional person can bias expectations in both directions.

The year repeatedly opposes inherited status to demonstrated trajectory without pretending environment and family are irrelevant.

## 7.6 Placement does not settle belonging

The school sorts students into classes.

It does not thereby acquire infallible authority over who deserves to remain.

The Class Vote's forced removal is therefore not simply the logical completion of placement. It is a new and much more severe use of uncertain information.

The epistemic distance between:

> this student currently fits Class D

and

> this student should lose the right to continue developing here

is enormous.

# 8. The midterm: academic measurement inside an information and liquidity market

The first written examination appears to be the simplest meritocratic mechanism in Year 1.

Students answer questions. Scores determine survival. Academic preparation should decide the result.

The actual mechanism is more complicated.

## 8.1 Formal academic ability matters

Students who do not understand the material are at real risk.

Horikita's initial teaching failure demonstrates that advanced knowledge does not automatically produce instructional competence. She assumes foundations the weaker students do not possess and humiliates the learners rather than translating the subject (`Y1V01-E014`). Her later redesign around the actual students, including basketball analogies and research before apology, improves the class's ability to learn (`Y1V01-E019–E020`).

The examination therefore measures academic output, but the output already depends on social conversion.

## 8.2 Information can substitute for some academic preparation

Ayanokōji buys prior examinations from an upperclassman who needs private points (`Y1V01-E022`). He withholds the material until it can function as insurance rather than eliminate motivation (`Y1V01-E023`).

The transaction reveals a secondary market in institutional knowledge.

A class with:

- money;
- cross-year access;
- and a student willing to search

can lower its academic risk independently of raw subject mastery.

This does not make study irrelevant. It means that examination success includes information acquisition and resource allocation.

## 8.3 The threshold itself becomes an object of strategy

Horikita deliberately lowers her English score to influence the passing threshold and accepts personal risk (`Y1V01-E026`).

This is an important early example of ability becoming political.

A student with enough margin can sacrifice part of her visible score to alter the environment for others. The act is not captured by a simple ranking of final marks.

## 8.4 One point can be purchased

Ayanokōji tests the school's claim that private points can buy anything by purchasing the one examination point needed to prevent Sudō's expulsion (`Y1V01-E029`).

The act creates a foundational distinction:

> the academic score and the institutional outcome are connected but not identical.

Sudō did not know one more answer.

He nevertheless remains because liquidity and negotiation alter the consequence.

This can be defended as institutional flexibility. A rigid system would destroy a student over a one-point margin even when classmates are willing to spend scarce resources to preserve him.

It can also be criticized as marketized mercy. Students without money or powerful advocates cannot purchase the same tolerance.

## 8.5 The midterm measures a class's ability to construct survival

The result depends on:

- subject knowledge;
- teaching ability;
- motivation;
- information access;
- public coordination through Kushida;
- threshold strategy;
- private-point liquidity;
- and willingness to intervene.

The formal test is academic.

The actual survival mechanism is institutional and collective.

## 8.6 Examination matrix: the midterm

| Dimension | Finding |
|---|---|
| Official goal | Evaluate academic performance and enforce minimum standards |
| Hidden practical goal | Force Class D to discover collective liability and organize teaching |
| Key asymmetry | Some students possess knowledge, money, or information networks others lack |
| Main exploit | Prior-exam purchase, delayed information release, threshold manipulation, one-point purchase |
| Public winner | The class avoids Sudō's expulsion |
| Hidden author(s) | Ayanokōji, with Horikita and Kushida supplying visible teaching and legitimacy |
| Human cost | Shame, fear of expulsion, and early classification of weak students as burdens |
| Institutional lesson | Academic survival depends on more than the student's own answers |
| Ethical problem | Mercy and continuation become purchasable |
| Longitudinal effect | Sudō remains available for later development; points become political currency |

# 9. Volume 2: credibility as measured social reality

The Sudō case is not a special examination, but it belongs inside the Year 1 measurement architecture.

The school must decide what happened under conditions of conflicting testimony.

## 9.1 Event truth is not self-executing

Sudō's account can be true while remaining institutionally weak.

His reputation affects what administrators and rivals consider plausible. The Class C students possess visible injuries and a coordinated story. Sakura possesses relevant observation but lacks immediate proof and fears public exposure.

Manabu's distinction between `証拠能力 / evidentiary capacity` and `証明力 / probative strength` gives the problem exact vocabulary (`Y1V02-E021`).

A fact becomes institutionally actionable only through a recognized evidentiary form.

## 9.2 Reputation is a shadow score

The school does not assign an explicit credibility number, but prior behavior functions like one.

Sudō's volatility increases the cost of believing him. Sakura's social invisibility makes her testimony harder to mobilize. Kushida's public reputation makes the Class C students expect her as the central negotiator, while Ichinose and Ayanokōji exploit that expectation (`Y1V02-E025`).

Meritocratic systems therefore contain informal metrics even when no chart displays them.

## 9.3 Basketball expands the object of evaluation

Extracurricular results materially affect school evaluation and points (`Y1V02-E011`). Ichinose recognizes that Sudō's athletic ability could become class value (`Y1V02-E015`).

This broadens merit beyond academics.

It also creates a new danger: a student may be tolerated because one domain makes him useful rather than because membership has independent value.

## 9.4 Development becomes evidence against static judgment

Horikita tells Sudō that he progressed and can continue growing (`Y1V02-E041`). Ayanokōji judges Sakura's small act of courage as possibly more important than the formal case outcome (`Y1V02-E038`).

The volume therefore adds a developmental measure the institution does not formally record.

The most consequential outcome may be a change in future agency rather than a point transfer.

## 9.5 The dummy-camera bluff exposes verification limits

The apparent cameras are student-purchased dummies (`Y1V02-E027`, `Y1V02-E030`). The plan works because the opposing students lack time to verify.

The mechanism resembles institutional measurement in miniature:

- an object appears to record;
- people assign authority to it;
- the belief changes behavior;
- and the resulting confession-like retreat becomes real even though the recording capacity was false.

The scene warns that the appearance of measurement can itself become power.

# 10. The island examination: ability becomes ecological

The deserted-island examination is the first large special exam and the first decisive rejection of fixed ability value.

## 10.1 Formal mechanism

Each class receives 300 temporary exam points. Spending and penalties reduce the balance; remaining points convert into class points (`Y1V03-E004–E005`). Occupied spots create deferred rewards under surveillance (`Y1V03-E006`). Leader identity creates a separate high-value inference game (`Y1V03-E007`).

The exam therefore combines:

- survival budgeting;
- territorial competition;
- information security;
- physical endurance;
- practical knowledge;
- leadership concealment;
- and final strategic inference.

## 10.2 The environment revalues people

Ike's camping knowledge becomes immediately useful. He solves fire-starting, identifies edible plants, and contributes to navigation and sanitation (`Y1V03-E010–E013`).

The same student who appears unserious in the classroom becomes important because the institutional ecology changes.

This is the clearest early case of ecological ability.

## 10.3 Recognition can itself be developmental

Ayanokōji deliberately leaves room for Ike to confirm that his own experience helps the class (`Y1V03-E011`).

The intervention matters because public usefulness changes self-conception. The class does not merely consume Ike's knowledge. He learns that the knowledge counts.

An exam can therefore produce development by changing the social meaning of an existing capacity.

## 10.4 Kōenji separates possession from contribution

Kōenji possesses obvious physical capability but retires and imposes a thirty-point loss (`Y1V03-E016`).

The exam records the institutional consequence of nonparticipation, not the absence of capacity.

This distinction becomes increasingly important across Year 1:

> the class's usable power is not the sum of every capacity its members privately possess.

## 10.5 Ryūen treats the point system as an ecology to exploit

Class C spends aggressively and retires, using the zero floor to convert the exam from survival budgeting into an intelligence operation (`Y1V03-E020`).

This is a form of ability the exam may not openly celebrate:

- willingness to abandon the apparent objective;
- understanding of downside limits;
- and conversion of resources into information.

The public score alone does not explain the strategic sophistication.

## 10.6 The leader rule makes identity a resource

# 11. The zodiac examination: trust, betrayal, and information become priced capacities

The zodiac exam is explicitly framed as testing `シンキング / thinking`, one component among social competencies (`Y1V04-E010`).

It does not simply ask who can solve a puzzle.

It asks who can govern distrust.

## 11.1 Mixed-class grouping changes the unit of ability

Students must act inside temporary groups whose members possess incompatible class loyalties.

The relevant capacities include:

- reading motives;
- creating voluntary legitimacy;
- concealing device identity;
- coordinating information;
- resisting betrayal incentives;
- and understanding when another class will defect.

## 11.2 The result table prices moral choices

Cooperation and betrayal receive different private- and class-point transfers (`Y1V04-E013–E014`).

The school does not merely observe trust.

It assigns numerical incentives to destroy it.

This matters because betrayal is no longer an external corruption of the exam. It is one of the institutionally recognized solution paths.

## 11.3 Private reward can separate individual and class interest

A favored person or betrayer can gain privately while imposing class-level consequences.

The examination therefore creates an agency problem:

> What does a student owe the class when the rule system offers a personally rational route to defect?

A simple merit ranking cannot answer that question because the most strategically capable individual may be the person best able to exploit collective vulnerability.

## 11.4 Social ability evades the dossier

The volume includes quantified profiles, yet Kei's caste management, Ichinose's consensual authority, Hirata's moral legitimacy, Ryūen's coercive information sovereignty, and Ayanokōji's layered identity exceed the records (`Y1V04-E066`).

The contrast is formal.

The volume places measurement pages beside a story about capacities the measurement cannot fully contain.

## 11.5 Ayanokōji's official mediocrity becomes strategic protection

Ryūen finds him an implausible mastermind because the official record is mediocre (`Y1V04-E018`). Ayanokōji reinforces the record through performed uselessness (`Y1V04-E029`).

The measured self becomes camouflage.

# 12. Volume 4.5: counter-curriculum and unscored competence

The first summer interlude contains no formal special examination.

That absence is analytically necessary.

The volume reveals capacities whose value is not assigned in advance by the school.

## 12.1 Communication ability has a performance gap

Ayanokōji distinguishes possessing communication capacity from using it under pressure and compares the gap to athletic performance (`Y1V04.5-E006`).

This is a compact theory of expression.

Knowing what to say is not the same as being able to say it in the relevant social environment.

## 12.2 Office does not create power

Manabu states that student-council office has no inherent power; the holder's ability determines what the office becomes (`Y1V04.5-E019`).

The line complicates institutional measurement in both directions.

Formal position is not proof of competence.

Yet competence can become much more consequential when joined to office.

## 12.3 Leisure remains partially outside the score

Water volleyball, lunch wagers, birthdays, shopping, gifts, the elevator incident, and ordinary embarrassment reveal:

- practical problem-solving;
- privacy protection;
- attraction;
- social memory;
- and chosen effort.

Horikita repeatedly translates leisure back into competition and points (`Y1V04.5-E051`). The volume nevertheless refuses to let the wager determine the full meaning of the activity.

## 12.4 Club contribution broadens institutional value

Sudō receives private points for basketball contribution, and other students increasingly recognize his value (`Y1V04.5-E046`).

The institution can reward extracurricular performance.

But the same student remains vulnerable to behavioral and academic evaluation. Multidimensionality does not eliminate conflict among dimensions.

## 12.5 Ordinary goals resist class-rank totalization

The interlude repeatedly allows goals that do not need to justify themselves through Class A:

- seeing a movie;
- giving a gift;
- enjoying a pool;
- helping someone save face;
- protecting privacy;
- or asking another person to spend time together.

These are not anti-ability scenes.

They reveal forms of ability and value whose purpose is not institutional ascent.

# 13. The sports festival: public metricization and cross-domain exchange

The sports festival appears to offer unusually direct measurement.

Bodies run. Placements are visible. Cameras record contact. Event order is locked. Individual performance receives public rank.

The apparent transparency is useful and deceptive.

## 13.1 The festival makes unlike outcomes commensurable

Athletic placement can become:

- private points;
- advantage on a future written test;
- academic risk;
- class points;
- and public reputation (`Y1V05-E003–E004`).

The institution converts physical performance into academic and economic consequences.

This creates flexibility. It also means weakness in one domain can deepen vulnerability in another.

## 13.2 Ability-first allocation distributes burden unevenly

Horikita initially supports ability-optimized allocation and accepts weaker students as necessary sacrifice for class performance (`Y1V05-E008–E010`).

Shinohara's objection is material, not merely emotional. Weaker students lose access to individual rewards and academic protection.

A class strategy that maximizes aggregate outcome can therefore impose concentrated costs on people whose weakness caused the strategy to exclude them.

## 13.3 Visible talent does not prove leadership

Sudō is chosen because his athletic visibility can pull the class (`Y1V05-E017`). He later admits that he assumed superior ability would make leadership easy and resented weaker teammates (`Y1V05-E048`).

His failure distinguishes:

- being best at the task;
- teaching the task;
- adapting to weaker partners;
- and possessing legitimate authority.

## 13.4 Hirata's relational ability is less spectacular and more generative

Hirata adapts coaching to the learner and recognizes that self and other are different (`Y1V05-E019`).

The festival's public scoreboard does not directly record this contribution.

Yet without it, individual athletic capacities cannot become coordinated class performance.

## 13.5 Disability, injury, and equal rules

Sakayanagi cannot participate physically but remains a major political leader (`Y1V05-E006`). Horikita's injury changes her event value and forces withdrawal from the final relay (`Y1V05-E055`).

The festival accurately measures bodies under the chosen conditions.

It does not thereby measure total leadership or strategic worth.

## 13.6 Cameras do not guarantee truth

Ryūen constructs a false causal narrative from real injury and evidence fragments. Horikita's selective audio is defeated by fuller video context; another recording then reveals actual planning (`Y1V05-E066–E069`).

The episode demonstrates:

> more data can correct partial data, but recording remains dependent on selection, context, custody, and interpretation.

## 13.7 The final ranking can make every first-year class lose

Red wins overall while every first-year class loses class points under the year-specific ranking (`Y1V05-E062`).

The same event can therefore produce different winners depending on the level of aggregation.

A public victory is not one thing.

# 14. Paper Shuffle: education as distributed coordination

Paper Shuffle shifts attention from athletic visibility to academic interdependence.

Its most important move is to make the pair, not the isolated student, the operative unit of risk.

## 14.1 Formal mechanism

A preliminary quiz determines pairings. Pair performance in the final examination creates expulsion risk. Students construct questions for an opponent class, while the school reviews and may replace invalid material (`Y1V06-E012–E015`).

The exam measures:

- individual academic knowledge;
- pair construction;
- teaching;
- question design;
- information security;
- prediction of the opponent;
- and class-wide coordination.

## 14.2 Weakness is redistributed rather than eliminated

The pairing rules allow strong and weak students to become combined units (`Y1V06-E029–E030`).

This does not make weakness disappear.

It changes who bears exposure to it.

The exam therefore stages a political question:

> Is the stronger student's obligation to compensate for the weaker student, or is the weaker student an illegitimate burden imposed by institutional design?

## 14.3 Keisei discovers domain dependence

Keisei links his uselessness during the sports festival to his earlier contempt for academically weak classmates (`Y1V06-E031`).

The realization is not that everyone has equal ability.

It is that the moral meaning of weakness changes when the environment changes and the supposedly strong person becomes dependent on another domain.

## 14.4 Question authorship is environmental authorship

Students do not merely take a test.

They help design the opponent's test.

Horikita's early control over question submission and her counter to Kushida demonstrate that merit depends on who controls the environment in which another student's merit is displayed (`Y1V06-E056`, `Y1V06-E059`).

## 14.5 Paper Shuffle rewards institutional creation

The class's developing study infrastructure includes Horikita, Hirata, Keisei, Kei, and others (`Y1V06-E017`, `Y1V06-E029`).

The strongest achievement is not one perfect score.

It is the construction of a system through which unequal students can survive together.

# 15. The Mixed Training Camp: aggregate scoring, leader liability, and the rescue market

The Mixed Training Camp is the most openly institutional examination of social and moral performance before the Class Vote.

The school says the camp is intended to produce psychological growth and cooperation among unfamiliar students (`Y1V08-E006`). It then builds a mechanism in which growth, rank, liability, and rescue are inseparable.

## 15.1 The measured object is a nested group

Students are separated by gender, divided into same-year small groups containing more than one class, and joined with corresponding groups from the other school years (`Y1V08-E007–E012`).

Large-group rank is determined by an average across years (`Y1V08-E013`).

The system therefore makes one student's performance consequential to people:

- from other classes;
- from other years;
- and from a group the student did not freely choose.

The examination measures cooperation partly by compelling interdependence.

## 15.2 The categories are morally ambitious and institutionally vague

The final evaluation includes morality, mental discipline, order, and initiative rather than ordinary academic subjects (`Y1V08-E010`, `Y1V08-E121`).

Those categories may capture meaningful behavior.

They also require interpretation by evaluators. A student can perform initiative in a speech while remaining selfish in private. A quiet student may contribute substantially without creating the expected public form of initiative.

The exam therefore increases the importance of social legibility.

Speech becomes a visible performance of moral and political character (`Y1V08-E066`).

## 15.3 Leadership is priced as concentrated risk

Every small group must name a leader; failure to do so expels everyone (`Y1V08-E015–E016`). Leaders receive doubled rewards for their class, but bear expulsion risk and may name a jointly responsible student subject to school approval (`Y1V08-E017–E018`).

Leadership is therefore not merely status.

It is a contract that concentrates:

- reward;
- liability;
- and the power to redirect part of the liability.

The school teaches leadership by making someone personally disposable.

## 15.4 Rescue is a liquidity test

Expulsion costs class points and can be cancelled only through twenty million private points plus an additional 300 class points (`Y1V08-E020–E021`).

Hirata proposes equal distribution of rewards as collective insurance. Chabashira argues that private points are personal insurance and warns against indiscriminate redistribution. Ayanokōji identifies the counterpoint: pooled reserves can rescue someone and reduce incentives for betrayal or waste (`Y1V08-E023–E025`).

The dispute is not simply generosity versus selfishness.

It is a political-economy question about where insurance should be held:

- by individuals;
- by a class treasury;
- or by trusted leaders.

## 15.5 The exam can create real community

The temporary mixed group develops through cooking, confession, labor, injury, and handshakes (`Y1V08-E096–E103`, `Y1V08-E120`). Hashimoto and Albert display practical competence that academic ranking does not capture (`Y1V08-E064`). Keisei's public vulnerability becomes shared knowledge (`Y1V08-E098–E100`).

The institution creates the container.

It does not fully author the relationship that emerges.

This is a strong defense of high-pressure mixed grouping: forced contact can reveal capacities and common humanity that ordinary class segregation would hide.

## 15.6 Nagumo moves from strategy inside rules to authorship of rules

Ayanokōji concludes that Nagumo conceived the strategy and helped create the examination around it (`Y1V08-E138`). Nagumo's network supplies a sacrificial leader and liquidity, while coordinated testimony manufactures an actionable causal record (`Y1V08-E139–E142`).

This changes the hierarchy of merit.

The strongest actor may not be the student who performs best inside the examination.

It may be the actor who helps decide what the examination will measure.

## 15.7 Official rank and political victory diverge

Manabu wins the official score contest. Nagumo's deeper result is depletion of Class A's insurance and forced political recognition (`Y1V08-E128–E144`).

The exam therefore produces multiple outcomes:

- official score;
- class-point effect;
- expulsion risk;
- future liquidity loss;
- and political succession.

No single ranking contains all of them.

# 16. Volume 9: reputation as an unofficial measurement system

Volume 9 contains no new formal special exam.

Its importance lies in showing that students are continuously measured by the social environment even when no score sheet exists.

## 16.1 Rumor creates a distributed rating system

Anonymous allegations select facts, insinuations, and social vulnerabilities. Repetition produces a public model of the target.

Ichinose's past theft is true. The claim that the act exhausts her moral identity is authored.

The social environment converts:

- one wrongdoing;
- existing admiration;
- uncertainty;
- and anonymous circulation

into a reputational score that affects political capacity.

## 16.2 Trust can be accumulated, spent, and weaponized

Sakayanagi and Nagumo explicitly theorize trust as a weapon. A person with high prior credibility can survive or transmit claims differently from a person with low credibility.

This resembles a market without formal currency.

Reputation becomes stored social capital.

## 16.3 Institutional intervention occurs after private harm becomes public risk

The school initially remains passive while the problem appears local and the target does not report. Broader rumor saturation raises confrontation and institutional costs enough to trigger regulation.

The school therefore appears to govern reputational harm through thresholded institutional risk rather than a comprehensive duty to protect persons.

## 16.4 Ayanokōji buys information through a future-income contract

He offers Kushida half of all future private points for secrets across the school (`Y1V09-E078–E083`).

The bargain prices:

- private confidences;
- information quantity;
- information quality;
- and future neutrality.

It also demonstrates a limit of monetary contracting. Mutual recordings are necessary because payment cannot create trust (`Y1V09-E077`, `Y1V09-E082`).

## 16.5 Reputation is related to merit but not reducible to it

A highly capable person may be politically unusable if nobody believes her. A mediocre performer may acquire influence through trusted status. A true wrongdoing may be amplified until all later competence is reinterpreted as fraud.

Meritocratic institutions that ignore reputation do not thereby escape it.

They merely leave an important measurement system informal and less accountable.

# 17. The Class Vote: popularity, liquidity, and manufactured disposability

The Class Vote is Year 1's most severe conversion of partial social measurement into irreversible consequence.

The school explicitly creates the extra exam because the first-year cohort has produced no expellees (`Y1V10-E013–E015`).

The absence of loss becomes a defect the institution intends to correct.

## 17.1 Formal mechanism

Students cast internal praise and criticism votes. Votes cancel; ballots are anonymous; each student also casts an external praise vote. The unique top student receives a nontransferable Protection Point. The unique bottom student is expelled. Twenty million private points can cancel the expulsion (`Y1V10-E014–E020`).

This mechanism combines:

- popularity;
- friendship blocs;
- public reputation;
- cross-class alliances;
- strategic coalition building;
- and wealth.

It does not measure one stable object called ability.

## 17.2 Anonymous equality produces organizational inequality

Every student receives the same number of votes.

Students do not possess equal capacity to organize those votes.

A large friendship group, class office, external reputation, or sovereign leader changes survival odds. Airi's fear of uselessness reveals how low social visibility becomes institutional vulnerability (`Y1V10-E023–E025`).

The exam therefore measures social embeddedness partly by threatening those least embedded.

## 17.3 Protection Point turns praise into a quantified right to remain

The top scorer receives a person-bound, one-use shield against future expulsion, valued at roughly twenty million private points (`Y1V10-E014`, `Y1FF-E056`).

This is not merely a reward.

It is institutionalized survivability.

Praise becomes future legal protection.

## 17.4 Wealth creates an alternative to judgment

Twenty million private points can cancel the bottom result (`Y1V10-E019`). Ichinose's class can approach that threshold because it has pooled resources; the shortfall forces external bargaining (`Y1V10-E034–E041`).

The right to remain is therefore partly a function of accumulated liquidity and class fiscal culture.

## 17.5 The exam invites pruning under developmental uncertainty

Ayanokōji and Kōenji treat the vote as an opportunity to remove a student judged unnecessary (`Y1V10-E021–E026`). Ayanokōji acknowledges universal developmental possibility but argues that timing and range differ enough to justify selection (`Y1V10-E022`).

The same volume supplies direct counterevidence: Sudō's growth overturns teacher expectations, and Chabashira says future development cannot be fully predicted (`Y1V10-E006–E007`).

The institutional question is therefore not whether some students currently contribute less.

It is whether the available evidence justifies ending their future.

## 17.6 Four classes reveal four measurement philosophies

- Horikita makes criteria public and accepts liability for judgment.
- Ichinose tries to buy universal rescue, then uses a lottery as the most equal fallback rather than rank human worth (`Y1V10-E103–E105`).
- Sakayanagi uses information asymmetry and sovereign allocation to prune.
- Ryūen's class attempts to remove its former leader, then followers purchase his preservation.

The exam measures each class's constitution as much as any individual student.

## 17.7 Hirata's objection remains analytically active

Hirata rejects the legitimacy of classmates ranking another person for sacrifice and offers himself instead (`Y1V10-E038`, `Y1V10-E064`).

His response is politically incapacitating. It is not philosophically empty.

He identifies the institutional authorship the exam tries to hide:

> the class did not independently discover that one person had to become disposable; the school imposed that premise.

# 18. The Event Selection Exam: portfolio merit and capture of the record

The Event Selection Exam is the most sophisticated Year 1 test of collective ability.

Classes propose events, select portfolios, train participants, manage substitutions, protect information, and designate a `司令塔 / commander` with formal intervention powers (`Y1V11-E027–E045`).

## 18.1 The exam measures a portfolio, not a median student

A class needs:

- specialists;
- depth;
- knowledge of member capacities;
- event design;
- training;
- motivation;
- substitutions;
- and command judgment.

This is closer to institutional competence than a single standardized test.

## 18.2 Leadership becomes the ability to make other abilities usable

Horikita cannot personally master basketball, archery, typing, and chess.

Her task is to identify who can, train the portfolio, and allocate roles. Sudō's domain authority and changed opponent profile demonstrate that development can make old data stale.

The exam therefore rewards generative and distributed ability.

## 18.3 Ayanokōji identifies his own missing capacities

He explicitly recognizes deficits in ordinary information gathering and class command (`Y1V11-E053`).

This matters because exceptional strategic intelligence does not automatically produce complete leadership.

Knowing the decisive move is different from maintaining the social and informational infrastructure through which forty students can perform.

## 18.4 Kōenji defines the limit of portfolio accounting

Kōenji insists that he decides whether his talent is used for Horikita's purpose (`Y1V11-E064–E068`). Later analysis concludes that capacity which cannot be controlled should not be counted as available class power (`Y1V11-E087`).

This is not a claim that Kōenji lacks ability.

It is a claim about the accounting unit.

A roster is not a treasury.

## 18.5 Chance can be a legitimate anti-dominance mechanism

Ayanokōji recognizes that chance can prevent the higher-ranked class from converting stable superiority into certainty (`Y1V11-E036`).

A meritocratic exam need not eliminate randomness completely. Some chance can prevent a single dimension of superiority from monopolizing outcome.

The question is whether chance is declared and symmetrically applied, not whether every result is deterministic.

## 18.6 The chess match exposes the record layer

Ayanokōji's stronger intended command is delayed and replaced. Sakayanagi notices the inconsistency. The substituted move becomes the official event history (`Y1V11-E140–E145`).

The exam has now separated:

- possessed strategic ability;
- the command chosen;
- the command transmitted;
- the move played;
- and the result recorded.

This is the completed Year 1 measurement chain.

## 18.7 Administrative capture defeats procedural meritocracy

A fair-looking exam cannot be legitimate if an administrator can alter the communication channel.

The violation is deeper than ordinary cheating because the actor controlling certification can make the false outcome official.

Volume 11.5's teacher coalition recognizes the problem. Mashima agrees that teachers cannot permit authorities to alter tests or results dishonestly (`Y1V11.5-E035–E037`). The school is not one unified corrupt will; it contains internal actors capable of defending record integrity.

# 19. Manabu and Nagumo: two rival meritocracies

Year 1's clearest theoretical debate arrives at the end rather than the beginning.

## 19.1 Nagumo's individualizing meritocracy

Nagumo's `本当の実力主義 / true meritocracy` emphasizes individual mobility and direct results (`Y1V11.5-E042`). His earlier promises to capable students make opportunity itself a source of political loyalty (`Y1V07.5-E083`).

The case for his model is substantial.

- Strong students should not be permanently trapped by weak classmates.
- Fixed class fate can reward free riders.
- Individual effort deserves visible consequence.
- Cross-year competition can reduce complacency.
- Mobility can correct poor initial placement.

## 19.2 Manabu's developmental class order

Manabu values the class because shared fate gives weaker students time to grow and gives stronger students incentives to build collective capacity.

The case for his model is also substantial.

- Current weakness is not a reliable lifetime forecast.
- Distributed groups can make different capacities useful.
- Collective identity can create trust and insurance.
- Direct individual rivalry can destroy cooperation.
- The strongest student's contribution may be developing others rather than escaping them.

## 19.3 The real disagreement concerns the unit and time of merit

Nagumo evaluates the individual more directly and more continuously.

Manabu gives the class a longer developmental horizon.

Neither model eliminates hierarchy.

They disagree about whether merit should be allocated primarily to:

- present individual output;
- or the collective process through which future output becomes possible.

## 19.4 Patronage is Nagumo's unresolved vulnerability

Individual mobility sounds less arbitrary than fixed class assignment.

But if opportunity depends on Nagumo's personal recognition and political network, a formally individualizing meritocracy can become patronage.

The relevant question is not only whether mobility exists.

It is who controls access to it and whether the allocator can be corrected.

## 19.5 Class shelter can become class imprisonment

Manabu's defense also has limits.

The class system forces strong students to bear other people's failures and may preserve students who refuse contribution. It makes class assignment an inherited collective fate even when initial sorting is opaque.

Year 1 therefore leaves the institutional design open.

A mature system may need both:

- collective developmental shelter;
- and legitimate routes for individual mobility.

# 20. The political economy of points

## 20.1. The point economy: from score to political currency

Points are not merely rewards attached to school performance.

They are the medium through which the institution translates behavior, rank, consumption, mobility, rescue, and belonging into exchangeable quantities.

Year 1 contains two principal currencies:

- `class points`, which determine collective rank and monthly private-point income;
- `private points`, which students can spend and transfer.

Special instruments such as Protection Points then create person-bound immunity.

### 20.1.1 Class points create shared externality

A student's conduct affects the income and position of everyone in the class.

The opening reveal makes this brutal: individual lateness, absence, and inattention contribute to a collective zero (`Y1V01-E011–E012`).

This creates a legitimate coordination problem.

It also creates moral pressure to govern peers because another person's behavior becomes one's own economic loss.

Class membership therefore functions partly as compulsory risk pooling.

### 20.1.2 Private points make school rules partially negotiable

The school presents private points as money-like currency, then reveals that they can purchase institutional outcomes.

One written-exam point can cost 100,000 private points (`Y1V01-E029`).

A class transfer is priced at twenty million (`Y1V02-E009`).

Expulsion rescue repeatedly appears at twenty million, sometimes with additional class-point cost (`Y1V08-E021`; `Y1V10-E020`).

The formal rule is therefore not always:

> failure produces consequence.

It is often:

> failure produces consequence unless sufficient capital purchases an exception.

### 20.1.3 A price creates an option, not equality of access

A rule can be formally available to everyone while materially usable only by students or classes with accumulated resources.

This distinction is central.

Twenty million private points is one published price.

It does not follow that every class has an equal rescue opportunity.

Accumulation depends on:

- earlier class rank;
- private transfers;
- savings norms;
- leader control;
- external bargains;
- and willingness to sacrifice consumption.

Formal convertibility can therefore reproduce prior advantage.

### 20.1.4 Points create political capital

Ichinose's class demonstrates the political meaning of pooled savings.

Collective capital becomes a survival mechanism during the Class Vote.

Ryuen uses point incentives and bargains as part of command.

Ayanokōji uses private transfers to influence conduct and acquire options.

Nagumo can make assistance conditional on personal access to Ichinose (`Y1V10-E034–E036`).

Points are therefore not only purchasing power.

They create leverage over relationships and futures.

### 20.1.5 The economy monetizes mercy

When rescue is priced, mercy becomes a capital allocation decision.

A class must ask:

- who is worth saving;
- what future gains justify the cost;
- who contributed to the fund;
- whether one rescue creates risk for everyone else;
- and whether accepting external money creates dependency.

The institution thereby turns moral solidarity into budget politics.

## 20.2. Private points: consumption, freedom, leverage, and priced exceptions

Private points are introduced as consumer abundance.

Their meaning expands steadily.

### 20.2.1 Consumption is the first pedagogical trap

The first monthly grant encourages students to experience points as disposable wealth.

Spending is later reinterpreted as evidence of failure to understand the institution.

The school therefore teaches scarcity by first simulating abundance.

This is not only an economic lesson.

It is a lesson in distrust of appearances.

### 20.2.2 Private points provide real autonomy

Students can choose food, goods, leisure, gifts, and transfers.

Ordinary life depends partly on this economic freedom.

Points allow private preference to acquire material form.

This is why the currency should not be treated only as sinister discipline.

It supports the counter-curriculum as well as the exam system.

### 20.2.3 Private points convert relationships into transactions

Payments can:

- reward cooperation;
- purchase silence;
- compensate risk;
- formalize a bargain;
- or disguise dependency as exchange.

Sudō's modest club contribution and Kinoshita's much larger payment illustrate different scales of monetized participation (`Y1V04.5-E046`; `Y1V05-E066`).

A payment may increase agency by making terms explicit.

It may also exploit unequal bargaining power.

### 20.2.4 Transferability creates shadow governance

Because students can transfer points, they can create unofficial policies beneath formal school rules.

Leaders can subsidize, punish, rescue, and recruit.

Ayanokōji can preserve options without public office.

Ichinose can build a collective fund.

Nagumo can condition access to capital.

The official school remains sovereign, but students acquire a limited capacity to legislate through finance.

### 20.2.5 Class transfer is formally marketized mobility

The twenty-million price of class transfer implies that rank membership is not absolutely fixed (`Y1V02-E009`).

This appears meritocratic because a student may escape a weak class through accumulation.

It is also plutocratic because the option depends on capital.

The rule makes mobility imaginable while preserving extreme scarcity.

Its ideological function may be as important as its actual use: students are told that the structure is open, even when the price makes openness remote.

## 20.3. Class points: collective fate and the politics of shared rank

Class points determine status and monthly income.

They create a constitutional fact: students are evaluated individually but rewarded and punished collectively.

### 20.3.1 Collective scoring can produce cooperation

The shared consequence gives students reason to tutor, coordinate, regulate conduct, and care about capacities outside their own.

A purely individual system might allow strong students to ignore peers.

The class system makes another person's improvement materially relevant.

This supports Manabu's defense that a collective structure can give weaker students time and help (`Y1V11.5-E084–E085`).

### 20.3.2 Collective scoring can produce coercive peer governance

The same externality gives classmates reason to control one another.

A weak or uncooperative student becomes a threat to everyone else's income and mobility.

The class may therefore interpret discipline, surveillance, exclusion, or expulsion as collective self-defense.

The institution does not need to micromanage every student when it can make peers bear the cost.

### 20.3.3 Class points are outcomes, not total constitutional rankings

At the Year 1 boundary the four classes hold 1131, 550, 508, and 347 class points respectively (`Y1FF-E058`).

These numbers matter.

They represent the cumulative institutional results of the year.

They do not prove that the top polity possesses the morally best constitution, the deepest total ability, or the strongest future developmental trajectory.

Points aggregate performance under the examinations the school chose to run.

They are not a neutral referendum on every possible environment.

### 20.3.4 Rank changes the resources available for future rank

Higher class points produce higher monthly private-point income.

Success therefore compounds.

The class with more points receives more capacity to:

- purchase information;
- compensate risk;
- save for rescue;
- and maintain morale through ordinary consumption.

The system is competitive, but not reset between examinations.

Early advantage can become future option value.

### 20.3.5 Collective fate creates the succession problem

If a class depends heavily on one hidden or visible leader, class points can conceal fragility.

A strong result may reflect:

- distributed institutions;
- one exceptional strategist;
- accumulated capital;
- or a temporary coalition.

The relevant constitutional question is not only who produced the points.

It is whether the polity can reproduce performance when the producer is absent.

## 20.4. Protection Points and person-bound immunity

The Class Vote introduces a striking instrument: the Protection Point.

The highest positive vote recipient receives personal protection against one expulsion consequence (`Y1V10-E017–E019`).

### 20.4.1 Protection Points convert popularity into legal insulation

The instrument links peer approval to institutional immunity.

This is not identical to measuring ability.

Popularity may reflect:

- leadership;
- kindness;
- strategic importance;
- fear;
- coalition organization;
- or reputational momentum.

The reward can therefore protect a highly useful student, a beloved student, or a politically organized student.

### 20.4.2 The instrument individualizes security

Class points are collective.

A Protection Point belongs to one person.

It creates a private buffer inside shared risk.

That can encourage exceptional contribution.

It can also create a protected elite whose willingness to impose risk on others is less constrained by equal exposure.

### 20.4.3 Immunity changes future bargaining power

A student holding protection can take risks others cannot.

The point therefore has political effects beyond the single expulsion it can cancel.

It changes negotiation, deterrence, and strategic credibility.

The school has created not merely a reward but a constitutional office without public duties.

### 20.4.4 Protection cannot be inferred as proof of worth

A Protection Point says that the procedure awarded immunity.

It does not establish that the recipient is intrinsically more valuable than the student expelled.

The distinction mirrors the larger document:

> institutional consequence records a rule-governed outcome, not a complete moral comparison of persons.

## 20.5. Marketized mercy and unequal rescue

The repeated twenty-million rescue rule is one of Year 1's strongest political-economic motifs.

### 20.5.1 Mercy becomes conditional on accumulated capital

The school permits exceptions.

This makes it appear less absolute than a system of automatic expulsion.

But the exception is expensive enough to make mercy unequal.

A wealthy class can preserve a member where a poor class cannot.

The rule therefore distinguishes formal mercy from accessible mercy.

### 20.5.2 Rescue prices uncertainty

A class never knows another student's full future value.

Spending twenty million means purchasing the option for that person to continue developing.

The decision resembles investment under uncertainty.

This framing is strategically useful and ethically dangerous.

It can make a human future appear as an asset expected to return class points.

### 20.5.3 Saving one person imposes opportunity cost on everyone

A rescue consumes resources that could fund:

- future transfers;
- exam strategies;
- ordinary life;
- or another rescue.

Solidarity is therefore not costless.

The school designs mercy so that helping one member may increase future risk for the rest.

### 20.5.4 External rescue can become coercive patronage

Nagumo's proposed assistance to Ichinose makes the danger explicit.

Capital offered under personal conditions can turn survival into access, obligation, or intimacy (`Y1V10-E034–E036`).

The transaction is formally voluntary only in a constrained sense because the alternative is a classmate's expulsion.

Resource power can therefore colonize relationships.

### 20.5.5 Ichinose's class demonstrates collective capital as moral infrastructure

The pooled fund is not only money.

It is accumulated evidence that members were willing to defer private consumption for shared security.

Using it to prevent expulsion converts trust into institutional effect (`Y1V10-E103`).

The action is politically impressive.

It does not eliminate the system's critique: only a class that had accumulated enough capital could enact that form of solidarity.

# 21. Record sovereignty: when performance no longer controls outcome

Year 1 ends by separating actual performance from official result.

That distinction is more radical than ordinary unfairness.

A badly designed test may measure the wrong construct.

A noisy test may produce error.

A biased observer may misinterpret evidence.

Record sovereignty concerns the power to decide what action institutionally occurred.

## 21.1 The official record is a stage of ability realization

Ayanokōji's chess instruction must pass through a communication system before it affects the board.

The instruction is part of the performance.

The recorded move is the institutional output.

Tsukishiro controls the channel between them.

This demonstrates that ability becomes consequential only if the record layer preserves it.

## 21.2 Record control can dominate superior performance

The actor who controls the record may defeat the actor with better judgment without outperforming that judgment.

This is a different type of power from exam competence.

It is meta-institutional power.

The school can claim the losing move as Ayanokōji's move because the institution owns the authoritative account.

## 21.3 Appeal depends on evidence outside the captured channel

Sakayanagi recognizes that the move is inconsistent with Ayanokōji's demonstrated line.

Her recognition matters morally and relationally.

It does not automatically reverse the official outcome.

A system capable of correcting record capture would need:

- preserved communications;
- independent review;
- transparent logs;
- and an authority capable of overruling the administrator.

Year 1 does not establish such a safeguard.

## 21.4 Meritocracy without record integrity is ceremonial

A meritocratic institution may publish rules, compare performance, and reward winners.

If an unaccountable official can alter the decisive record, those procedures become conditional performances of fairness.

The ultimate Year 1 measurement question is therefore not only:

> Who performed best?

It is:

> Who possesses authority to certify what performance occurred?

# 22. First File and the hypothetical OAA: the model and the person

*First File* gives Year 1 its most explicit measurement paratext.

It contains dated evaluations, prose summaries, class descriptions, point totals, timelines, and hypothetical OAA values.

These materials are official and analytically useful.

They are not an omniscient character database.

## 22.1 The ratings are dated snapshots

The July profiles preserve early visible states.

Horikita's cooperation E is credible for the student who rejects social dependence and has not yet learned distributed leadership (`Y1FF-E012–E014`).

It would be false as a timeless claim about the person at the end of Volume 11.5.

A rating therefore needs a date as much as a number.

## 22.2 The OAA model is explicitly counterfactual

*First File* asks what ratings might have looked like had the later OAA framework existed during Year 1.

That means the values are retrospective institutional modeling, not in-world Year 1 observations already governing student consequences.

The distinction prevents later system knowledge from being silently projected backward.

## 22.3 Ayanokōji demonstrates the difference between visible output and latent capacity

The hypothetical profile assigns Ayanokōji:

- academics 50;
- physical ability 45;
- adaptability 31;
- social contribution 27;
- overall 40 (`Y1FF-E042`).

The numbers are not random.

They compress what he allowed the school to see.

They are therefore accurate to the authored surface and radically incomplete about capacity.

## 22.4 Horikita demonstrates development beyond the dated file

Horikita's hypothetical OAA values are substantially stronger academically and physically, but cooperation and social contribution remain constrained (`Y1FF-E043`).

The later Year 1 record demonstrates growth in delegation, public responsibility, and generative leadership that a static July-oriented profile cannot contain.

The model is not wrong.

The person moves.

## 22.5 Kei demonstrates undermeasurement of positional social power

Kei receives a low overall profile (`Y1FF-E044`).

The model struggles to represent her access to female-group atmosphere, network influence, and ability to translate otherwise hidden social information.

These capacities are real because strategies depend on them.

They are difficult to score because they are relational and position-specific.

## 22.6 Ryuen demonstrates divergence between contribution score and political authority

Ryuen's social contribution value of 1 captures his incompatibility with cooperative institutional norms (`Y1FF-E045`).

It does not imply political irrelevance.

He possesses major authority, deterrence, and mobilization capacity.

The rating measures contribution according to one institutional conception, not the total capacity to govern.

## 22.7 Hiyori demonstrates quiet judgment that exceeds formal visibility

Hiyori's model gives her strong academics and much lower adaptability/judgment (`Y1FF-E046`).

Year 1 nevertheless shows important inference, independent refusal, and accurate social reading.

Her case warns that quiet, low-frequency judgment can be undercounted when a system rewards visible intervention.

## 22.8 Ichinose demonstrates that strong social metrics do not guarantee result conversion

Ichinose receives high academics, adaptability, and social contribution (`Y1FF-E047`).

Those values accurately register major strengths.

They do not solve adversarial security, coercive opponents, or the conversion of solidarity into class-point success.

A good individual profile cannot substitute for constitutional analysis.

## 22.9 Sakayanagi demonstrates the cost of aggregation

Sakayanagi's physical rating is very low while her academics and strategic ability are high (`Y1FF-E048`).

An overall score necessarily compresses dimensions whose relevance changes by environment.

A physical limitation may dominate one event and be nearly irrelevant in another.

The aggregate is convenient.

The component structure is analytically superior.

## 22.10 The paratext confirms bounded measurement rather than abolishing it

The proper conclusion is not that official ratings are useless.

They provide:

- a common vocabulary;
- dated institutional observations;
- comparable categories;
- and evidence about what the school values.

Their limit is jurisdiction.

A useful model becomes oppressive when it is allowed to define the whole person or authorize irreversible consequence without admitting uncertainty.

# 23. Comparative case studies in measurement error and measurement truth

The strongest Year 1 argument emerges from cases where a metric is partly right and still insufficient.

## 23.1 Ayanokōji: controlled underexpression

Measurement truth:

He often performs at a mediocre visible level and contributes through concealed channels.

Measurement error:

The institution infers ordinary ceiling from deliberately limited expression.

Political consequence:

He gains autonomy from scrutiny while classmates bear the uncertainty created by his concealment.

## 23.2 Sudō: current weakness and future trajectory

Measurement truth:

His early academics, discipline, and classroom reliability are poor.

Measurement error:

Those results are treated as if they exhaust his capacity and forecast.

Political consequence:

Developmental support converts a liability into a major athletic and political asset.

## 23.3 Kōenji: capacity without requisition

Measurement truth:

He possesses exceptional capability.

Measurement error:

A class may count possession as available collective strength.

Political consequence:

His autonomy reveals that ability is not property merely because the institution assigned people to the same class.

## 23.4 Kei: low conventional profile, high network value

Measurement truth:

She is not a top academic or athlete.

Measurement error:

Formal categories underprice atmosphere governance and female-network access.

Political consequence:

Ayanokōji recognizes a capacity the early institutional profile does not make legible and then obtains it through ethically compromised dependency.

## 23.5 Hirata: high social contribution with judgment limits

Measurement truth:

He creates trust, access, participation, and emotional stability.

Measurement error:

A high cooperation profile may hide inability to make discriminating judgments under forced sacrifice.

Political consequence:

The Class Vote breaks the function on which the class depended.

## 23.6 Kushida: cooperation as both capacity and attack surface

Measurement truth:

She is highly socially accessible and can maintain broad relationships.

Measurement error:

Visible cooperation is inferred to be loyal cooperation.

Political consequence:

Trust produces both collective utility and a confidential-information vulnerability.

## 23.7 Ryuen: low norm compliance, high adversarial competence

Measurement truth:

He violates cooperative expectations and imposes serious external costs.

Measurement error:

Low social contribution could be misread as absence of leadership or social intelligence.

Political consequence:

He builds a powerful authoritarian polity whose effectiveness and ethics must be evaluated separately.

## 23.8 Ichinose: high trust capacity, incomplete adversarial conversion

Measurement truth:

Strong social scores can be mistaken for complete strategic adequacy.

Political consequence:

Solidarity survives, but opponents exploit assumptions of reciprocity and the class falls in points.

## 23.9 Sakayanagi: physical limitation and strategic sovereignty

Measurement truth:

Her body constrains participation in physical events.

Measurement error:

An aggregate can make low physical output appear to reduce political power proportionally.

Political consequence:

She remains the sovereign allocator of a high-capacity polity.

## 23.10 Hiyori: low visibility and selective intervention

Measurement truth:

She is not a high-frequency public commander.

Measurement error:

Low visible intervention is treated as low judgment.

Political consequence:

She becomes a valuable independent observer and one of the few people able to refuse Ryuen without abandoning the polity.

# 24. When may measurement legitimately become consequence?

Year 1 does not supply a formal legal test.

Its evidence supports a rigorous evaluative framework.

The harsher and less reversible the consequence, the more conditions should be satisfied.

## 24.1 Construct validity

The measurement should actually test the capacity invoked to justify the consequence.

A popularity vote cannot silently become a total ability ranking.

## 24.2 Notice and transparency

Students should know the relevant rules, scoring object, and stakes before conduct is converted into sanction.

Hidden criteria may test adaptation, but they weaken the fairness of punitive inference.

## 24.3 Agency over the measured conduct

The student should have meaningful control over the performance.

Illness, sabotage, forced pairing, or administrative interference must be separated from voluntary failure.

## 24.4 Comparable opportunity

Participants should have sufficiently comparable access to information, preparation, and resources for comparison to carry the claimed meaning.

Formal equality is inadequate when capital or hidden information dominates access.

## 24.5 Developmental humility

A current score should not be treated as a permanent ceiling without evidence.

Sudō makes this condition unavoidable.

## 24.6 Proportionality

The consequence should match what the measurement establishes.

Poor performance in one exam may justify point loss more readily than permanent expulsion.

## 24.7 Reversibility and correction

Where uncertainty is high, the institution should prefer consequences that allow later evidence to matter.

Expulsion terminates correction.

## 24.8 Record integrity

The official outcome must preserve the act actually performed.

Without this condition, every other safeguard becomes decorative.

## 24.9 Appeal and independent review

A student should possess some route to contest error, sabotage, or captured procedure.

Year 1 repeatedly demonstrates the need while rarely showing a robust mechanism.

## 24.10 Separation of score from personhood

Even a valid negative result should not become a total identity claim.

The institution may judge performance without declaring the person exhausted by it.

## 24.11 Disclosure of collective externalities

When one person's result affects everyone, the design should acknowledge that peer coercion is being produced.

Shared fate is not a neutral background condition.

## 24.12 Resource accessibility

If points can purchase exceptions, analysis must ask who can realistically afford them.

A formally universal price can create unequal justice.

# 25. Ordinary life as counter-measurement

The decimal volumes and *First File* bonus fiction preserve forms of value that the exam system does not fully score.

Students:

- enjoy food;
- exchange gifts;
- become embarrassed;
- read books;
- watch films;
- waste time;
- date;
- build friendships;
- and discover preferences without knowing what class-point return will follow.

These scenes do not prove that ordinary life is outside institutional power.

Private points, surveillance, class rank, and future exams remain present.

They do provide a rival theory of value.

A conversation may matter because it is enjoyable.

A person may matter before the function is known.

Time may be worthwhile without producing measurable advancement.

This is especially important for Ayanokōji.

If every relationship and experience is interpreted as a hidden capacity-building exercise, the analysis reproduces the institution's totalizing metric logic.

The counter-curriculum insists that some goods are ends.

# 26. Counterarguments and adversarial tests

## 26.1 "The school measures broad ability, so its meritocracy is fair"

Strongest version:

ANHS uses diverse environments rather than relying on grades alone. It reveals practical, social, physical, and strategic capacities ordinary schools miss.

Response:

This is a genuine strength. Breadth improves validity.

It does not resolve hidden criteria, resource inequality, manufactured betrayal, irreversible expulsion, or record capture. Broad measurement can still be unfairly designed or illegitimately converted into fate.

## 26.2 "Ayanokōji proves ratings are meaningless"

Strongest version:

If the most capable student can receive mediocre ratings, the system fails.

Response:

The ratings correctly describe much of his visible output. The failure lies in inferring ceiling from strategic underexpression.

Ayanokōji proves the need to define the measurement object, not the impossibility of measurement.

## 26.3 "Sudō proves every weak student should always be retained"

Strongest version:

Because large growth is possible, expulsion is never justified.

Response:

Sudō proves prediction error and the value of developmental infrastructure. He does not prove equal trajectories, unlimited resources, or the impossibility of severe misconduct.

His case raises the burden of justification for irreversible removal.

## 26.4 "The Class Vote identifies the least valuable member through collective knowledge"

Strongest version:

Classmates observe one another over time; aggregate judgment may outperform one leader.

Response:

Collective knowledge is real. The mechanism also contains anonymity, coalition power, reputation effects, compulsory scarcity, and unequal social protection.

It identifies a political loser more reliably than a total-value minimum.

## 26.5 "Points are only genre mechanics"

Strongest version:

The currencies exist to make strategic plots possible.

Response:

Their recurring use to price food, information, mobility, rescue, and expulsion gives them a coherent political-economic function. Genre mechanics can still carry institutional meaning.

## 26.6 "Nagumo's meritocracy is simply more honest"

Strongest version:

Individual performance should determine individual mobility rather than class luck.

Response:

Nagumo correctly identifies the unfairness of collective entrapment. Manabu correctly identifies that individualized sorting can terminate support before development becomes visible.

Year 1 preserves the conflict because each model protects one form of fairness by endangering another.

## 26.7 "OAA solves ambiguity through multidimensional scoring"

Strongest version:

Several dimensions are better than one ranking.

Response:

Yes. Multidimensional profiles are analytically superior to crude aggregates.

They still depend on observed expression, chosen categories, weighting, date, and institutional access to the person. They improve the model without becoming the person.

# 27. Locked Year 1 synthesis

The completed Year 1 evidence supports the following position.

`Jitsuryoku` is real.

Human beings differ in consequential capacities.

Those capacities are not exhausted by grades, athletic results, popularity, class points, or any one institutional profile.

Ability becomes consequential through a sequence:

> possession -> development -> expression -> legibility -> coordination -> recording

At every transition, power intervenes.

A person may lack developmental support.

A person may conceal expression.

An observer may misread the act.

A polity may fail to coordinate the capacity.

An administrator may corrupt the record.

*First File* adds one more limit:

> the resulting model remains smaller than the person.

The school is therefore neither a fraud that measures nothing nor a neutral machine that discovers natural rank.

It is an active author of environments, incentives, currencies, and records.

Its examinations can reveal genuine capacity and produce genuine growth.

They can also:

- reward betrayal;
- socialize individual weakness;
- concentrate risk;
- monetize mercy;
- compound prior advantage;
- manufacture a disposable student;
- and allow administrative power to overwrite performance.

A defensible meritocracy would require more than accurate ranking.

It would require:

- construct clarity;
- developmental humility;
- proportional consequence;
- comparable opportunity;
- accessible correction;
- honest records;
- and continued recognition that performance judgment is not total judgment of personhood.

Year 1 does not deliver such a completed institution.

It gives competing partial models.

Horikita's emerging approach is the most developmental and corrigible.

Ichinose demonstrates that trust and pooled protection are real capacities.

Ryuen demonstrates the power of adversarial intelligence and rapid mobilization.

Sakayanagi demonstrates superior allocation under centralized knowledge.

Nagumo exposes collective entrapment.

Manabu exposes premature individualized sorting.

Ayanokōji exposes institutional illegibility while reproducing its danger through private models of other people.

The unresolved governing question is:

> **How can an institution recognize real difference, develop unequal people, and attach consequences to performance without turning partial knowledge into ownership of another person's future?**

# 28. Canonical propositions carried forward

The following propositions are locked at the Year 1 boundary.

1. `Jitsuryoku` is multidimensional and context-sensitive; no single Year 1 score exhausts it.
2. Possessed ability, demonstrated ability, socially usable ability, and officially recorded ability are distinct.
3. Development changes the object being measured; current performance is not a fixed ceiling.
4. Examinations author environments and incentives rather than merely observing neutral behavior.
5. A valid result may still support an illegitimate consequence if proportionality, transparency, or reversibility is absent.
6. Class points measure cumulative institutional outcomes, not total human or constitutional worth.
7. Private points create real autonomy and real inequality because they can purchase institutional exceptions.
8. Rescue prices continuation without establishing a person's moral value.
9. Protection Points convert social ranking into person-bound immunity and alter future bargaining power.
10. The Class Vote is strongest as evidence of constitutional response to manufactured scarcity, not as a total merit ranking.
11. The Event Selection Exam is Year 1's strongest model of distributed class ability, but its decisive result is invalidated by record capture.
12. *First File* ratings are official, useful, dated, and bounded; hypothetical OAA is retrospective paratext.
13. Ayanokōji's undermeasurement does not make measurement impossible; it demonstrates the politics of expression.
14. Kōenji proves that knowledge of capacity does not create collective ownership of it.
15. Sudō proves that irreversible selection must account for developmental uncertainty.
16. Record integrity is a necessary condition of any meaningful meritocracy.
17. Ordinary life preserves goods whose value cannot be reduced to points or optimization.
18. No model is entitled to become the whole person.

# 31. Final conclusion

Year 1 begins by asking whether students who waste a generous monthly allocation deserve the consequences of failing to understand the school.

It ends by asking whether any institution that controls the conditions, currency, categories, and record of performance may legitimately call its outcome a complete expression of merit.

The answer is neither simple rejection nor endorsement.

The school sees real things.

It discovers capacities ordinary classrooms would miss.

It forces students to coordinate, adapt, teach, plan, endure, and take responsibility.

It also withholds rules, rewards betrayal, compounds capital advantage, prices escape, manufactures sacrifice, and permits administrative interference with the record.

The title's `jitsuryoku` is therefore not one hidden number waiting to be revealed.

It is a contested relation among person, environment, group, and institution.

A person possesses capacities.

Experience changes them.

Choice displays or conceals them.

Observers classify them.

Groups make them usable or waste them.

Institutions preserve or falsify the result.

No stage is politically neutral.

The most defensible Year 1 principle is:

> **Measure performance precisely, treat models humbly, make consequences answerable, preserve the record honestly, and never confuse what an institution can score with everything a person is or may become.**
