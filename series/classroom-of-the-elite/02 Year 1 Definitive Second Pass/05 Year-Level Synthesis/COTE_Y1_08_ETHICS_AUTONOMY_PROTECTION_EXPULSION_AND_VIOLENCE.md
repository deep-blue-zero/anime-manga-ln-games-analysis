# Classroom of the Elite
## Year 1 - Ethics, Autonomy, Protection, Expulsion, and Violence
### Beneficial outcomes do not settle authority: intervention, refusal, dependency, and the right to answer back

> **CANONICAL YEAR-1 SPECIALIST SNAPSHOT**
>
> This document is the primary normative home for the Year 1 ethics of developmental intervention, consent, refusal, protection, dependency, expulsion, privacy, violence, and institutional coercion. It synthesizes the fourteen canonical Japanese novel deep readings, the *First File* paratext audit, the final Year 1 evidence lock, the canonical `THROUGH_Y1` ledgers, and the frozen `Y1_01-Y1_07` specialist layer.
>
> It does not import Year 2, Volume 0, *Second List*, Year 3, adaptation material, wikis, or later-franchise answers. Where Year 1 leaves motive, consequence, or institutional history uncertain, this document preserves that uncertainty.

# 0. Scope, primary home, and interpretive controls

Year 1 repeatedly stages a morally seductive sequence:

1. a person is trapped, weak, afraid, misread, or politically endangered;
2. a more capable actor understands the problem better than the person does;
3. that actor manipulates the environment, withholds information, creates pressure, or uses force;
4. the target survives, grows, becomes more capable, or later expresses gratitude;
5. the reader is invited to admire the effectiveness of the intervention.

The ethical problem begins precisely where the strategic success becomes most impressive.

A beneficial outcome can establish that an intervention worked. It cannot, by itself, establish that the intervener had the right to choose the goal, impose the method, distribute the risks, or conceal the plan from the person whose life was being reorganized.

This document therefore separates five questions that the narrative often compresses into one dramatic climax:

- Was the diagnosis accurate?
- Was the desired outcome good?
- Was intervention necessary?
- Was the method legitimate?
- What obligations remain after the intervention succeeds?

The answers can diverge.

Ayanokoji may understand Kei accurately, want her to survive, and later provide genuine protection while still having acted abusively in manufacturing the dependency through which that protection became indispensable. Horikita may make a publicly accountable judgment during the Class Vote without possessing sufficient knowledge to convert a current evaluation into a final statement about a human future. Hirata may be morally right that the school has no legitimate claim to force a classmate into disposability while becoming unable to perform the discriminating judgment his classmates need. Ichinose may preserve humane solidarity while exposing her class to adversaries who do not reciprocate. Ryuuen may protect his followers and inspire voluntary loyalty while continuing to use intimidation and violence. Sakayanagi may perceive ability accurately while treating superior perception as sovereign jurisdiction.

The analysis applies the following controls.

## 0.1 Explanation is not justification

Trauma, institutional conditioning, strategic necessity, fear, and relational dependency explain conduct.

They do not automatically excuse it.

Ayanokoji's White Room conditioning helps explain why he treats people as developmental systems. Kei's survival structure helps explain why protection becomes central to her. Hirata's childhood trauma explains his refusal to choose a victim. Kushida's recognition economy explains why confidential truth becomes leverage. Ryuuen's political theory explains why fear appears efficient.

None of those explanations settles whether the conduct is permissible.

## 0.2 Effectiveness is not legitimacy

An intervention can be effective and illegitimate.

The Volume 4 operation against Kei produces a useful operative, deters Manabe's group, and eventually contributes to a relationship that offers Kei real safety. Those outcomes do not retroactively authorize blackmail, sexualized coercion, engineered assault, or the discrediting of alternative protectors (`Y1V04-E032-Y1V04-E061`).

The rooftop operation ends Ryuuen's hunt, protects Kei from further retaliation, and breaks fear as the exclusive basis of his authority. It also depends upon Ayanokoji's earlier crisis engineering, his prediction that Kei will be tortured, his withdrawal of contact, and his decision to delay intervention until the ordeal has revealed information he wants (`Y1V07-E052-Y1V07-E096`).

The ethical judgment must therefore preserve both the benefit and the wrong.

## 0.3 Sincere choice is not always fully autonomous choice

A choice may be emotionally sincere even when its feasible alternatives were manipulated.

Kei's refusal to betray Ayanokoji on the rooftop is hers. The scene matters because she does something he did not fully script. Yet the relationship within which that refusal occurs was partly built by him through manufactured danger and monopolized protection.

Her later agreement to continue as his partner is more reciprocal than the original recruitment. Her answer to his confession is a genuine romantic act. Those choices are not fake. They are also made within an information structure he still controls (`Y1V07.5-E099-Y1V07.5-E100`; `Y1V11.5-E127-Y1V11.5-E140`).

The correct formulation is neither "manipulation makes choice unreal" nor "sincerity cleanses the manipulation." It is:

> **A choice can be real while its autonomy remains incomplete.**

## 0.4 Autonomy is not isolation

Year 1 does not define freedom as independence from all other people.

Students depend upon friendship, information, money, credibility, teaching, protection, class membership, and institutional refuge. Manabu's distinction between solitude and isolation, Horikita's development through comrades, Sudou's reliance on academic support, and Ichinose's collective capital all reject an atomistic model of autonomy (`Y1V01-E017-Y1V01-E020`; `Y1V05-E052-Y1V05-E055`; `Y1V10-E044-Y1V10-E052`).

Autonomy is better understood as the capacity to participate in dependence without losing the power to:

- know the relevant terms;
- refuse;
- contradict;
- revise the relationship;
- pursue ends not supplied by the protector or group;
- and remain a person if one's utility changes.

## 0.5 Protection is not ownership

Protection is ethically stronger when it expands the protected person's future options.

It becomes proprietary when the protector:

- creates or knowingly permits the danger;
- makes safety conditional on obedience;
- monopolizes alternatives;
- withholds the real terms;
- or requires continuing dependency as proof of attachment.

Chairman Sakayanagi offers Ayanokoji a form of refuge that respects his expressed wish to remain while preserving ordinary school rules (`Y1V07-E022-Y1V07-E034`). Ayanokoji's protection of Kei is more ethically unstable because he helped author the conditions under which his protection became necessary (`Y1V04-E032-Y1V04-E061`; `Y1V07-E052-Y1V07-E096`).

## 0.6 Developmental improvement is not consent to the method

Horikita can genuinely grow through the island examination even if Ayanokoji's bodily and informational manipulation was not authorized. Hirata can genuinely recover social function after Volume 11 even if the verbal assault used to break his defensive structure was coercive. Ichinose can genuinely experience confession and forgiveness while the rumor-saturation strategy harms uninvolved students and denies her knowledge of the full environment.

Later growth establishes causal consequence.

It does not create earlier consent.

## 0.7 Rule compliance is not justice

The school repeatedly uses formally valid rules to produce ethically troubling outcomes.

The Class Vote is procedurally announced and enforceable. That does not answer whether an institution may manufacture a need for expulsion because no one has yet been expelled (`Y1V10-E013-Y1V10-E019`). A twenty-million-point rescue route may be equally priced while being radically unequal in practical accessibility. A Protection Point may be formally earned while turning popularity into future immunity.

Conversely, breaking or manipulating rules does not automatically prove injustice. The one-point purchase in Volume 1 preserves a student through a transaction that the system itself permits, and Mashima's later willingness to accept punishment rather than expose a student to serious avoidable injury can be ethically stronger than mechanical compliance (`Y1V01-E027-Y1V01-E029`; `Y1V11.5-E034-Y1V11.5-E036`).

## 0.8 Survival value is not human worth

A class may need to decide who contributes, who creates risk, and how scarce resources should be allocated.

Those are real political questions.

They do not authorize the inference:

> low current utility = lesser person = deserved disappearance.

Sudou's Year 1 trajectory is the standing counterexample. Early academic weakness and volatility do not predict his later discipline, athletic authority, loyalty, or ability to dissent from Horikita (`Y1V01-E014-Y1V01-E020`; `Y1V05-E020-Y1V05-E052`; `Y1V11-E071-Y1V11-E088`). Chabashira explicitly acknowledges that development can overturn early expectations (`Y1V10-E006-Y1V10-E007`).

## 0.9 Violence must be disaggregated

Year 1 contains:

- immediate defensive force;
- intimidation;
- torture;
- retaliatory assault;
- disciplinary violence;
- sexualized coercion;
- institutional exposure to foreseeable harm;
- and structural violence through expulsion or deprivation.

These forms cannot be evaluated under one generic category.

Force used to stop an imminent assault differs from violence used to produce obedience. Ayanokoji's physical defeat of Ryuuen's group has a stronger immediate protective justification than Ryuuen's torture of Kei. Yet the broader operation remains ethically compromised because Ayanokoji deliberately allowed the danger to mature and used the confrontation to author Ryuuen's future conduct.

## 0.10 Year 1 remains the evidentiary boundary

Later material may reveal more about the White Room, later expulsions, class transfer, romantic development, institutional politics, or the long-term consequences of these interventions.

Those later answers are not used here.

The judgments below concern what Year 1 itself establishes.

# 1. Executive thesis

The strongest Year 1 ethical thesis is:

> **Beneficial outcomes do not settle moral authority. An intervention becomes more legitimate when the affected person's goal is respected, the danger is not manufactured by the intervener, information and refusal are meaningful, the method is necessary and proportionate, alternatives are considered, risks are not displaced onto uninformed third parties, authority is answerable, and the resulting development leaves the subject more able to contradict the person who helped them.**

Year 1's central ethical conflict is not good intentions versus bad intentions.

Many of its most dangerous actors have goals that are partly intelligible or even good.

Ayanokoji wants classmates to survive and become stronger.

Horikita wants the class to rise without wasting potential.

Hirata wants no one abandoned.

Ichinose wants membership protected.

Ryuuen wants collective victory and sometimes shields followers from consequences.

Sakayanagi wants ability recognized rather than sentimentalized.

Manabu wants institutional order and student development.

Nagumo wants mobility for high performers.

Chabashira wants her class to reach A.

Atsuomi wants to create extraordinary ability.

The moral problem is what each actor treats as permissible once the goal is accepted.

Ayanokoji repeatedly moves from accurate diagnosis to unauthorized jurisdiction. Because he understands the subject's psychology, he assumes authority to decide what pressure will improve her. Because he can protect her, he assumes authority to determine when rescue should occur. Because he can predict future usefulness, he assumes authority to allocate survival. His greatest virtue and greatest ethical danger arise from the same capacity: he sees developmental possibility that static systems miss.

Horikita's Year 1 growth offers a partial alternative. She increasingly states reasons publicly, allows specialists to speak, accepts correction, and treats development as uncertain. Her leadership is not morally pure. The Yamauchi decision during the Class Vote remains an act of triage under institutional coercion, and her Kushida project risks imposing danger on classmates who did not authorize it. Yet public accountability makes her power more answerable than Ayanokoji's hidden sovereignty.

Hirata and Ichinose reveal the opposite danger. Refusing disposability can preserve human dignity while creating paralysis or strategic vulnerability. Their ethics are not disproven by competitive loss. They require institutions capable of protecting humane commitments against bad-faith exploitation.

Ryuuen and Sakayanagi demonstrate that competence and voluntary loyalty do not settle legitimacy. A follower may sincerely choose an authoritarian leader. A sovereign may accurately understand a subordinate. The crucial question remains whether dissent, exit, privacy, and independent purpose survive.

The school makes all of these interpersonal problems structural. It authors scarcity, prices rescue, turns reputation into vulnerability, and sometimes demands that students decide who should disappear. Tsukishiro then reveals that even the official record can be privately captured. The institution is ethically responsible not only for judging behavior but for the environments it creates and the risks it makes rational.

Year 1's final normative principle is therefore the **answer-back standard**:

> **Power is most legitimate when the person affected can understand it, contest it, refuse where possible, demand reasons, survive disagreement, and become someone the powerful actor did not design.**

This standard does not require equality of competence.

It requires asymmetry to remain answerable.

# 2. A Year 1 intervention-audit framework

Every major intervention should be evaluated across separate dimensions. No single favorable result cancels failure on the others.

## 2.1 Goal authorship

Who chose the end?

- the affected person;
- a protector;
- a class leader;
- the institution;
- or an external adult?

An intervention is ethically stronger when it supports an end the subject already owns. Sakura's decision to testify and later confront the stalker has a stronger autonomy basis than Kei's recruitment, where Ayanokoji selects both the problem and the future role (`Y1V02-E019-Y1V02-E038`; `Y1V04-E032-Y1V04-E061`).

## 2.2 Epistemic warrant

How reliable is the intervener's diagnosis?

Superior knowledge matters. It does not create unlimited authority.

Ayanokoji often predicts accurately, but Year 1 also shows model error and surprise: Kei refuses betrayal, Hiyori exceeds his initial classification, Matsushita conceals ability, and ordinary romantic symbols confuse him. A structure that grants jurisdiction merely because someone is usually right magnifies the damage when the model fails.

## 2.3 Imminence and necessity

Is there an immediate danger that cannot wait for ordinary consent?

Emergency intervention has a stronger justification when serious harm is imminent. Pulling someone away from an attacker, using force to end an assault, or preventing catastrophic injury can legitimately override momentary refusal.

Developmental pressure is different. A person being socially immature, strategically weak, or psychologically avoidant does not create the same emergency license.

## 2.4 Proportionality

Does the severity of the method fit the threatened harm and the desired end?

Threatening sexual assault to obtain cooperation is grossly disproportionate. Public humiliation, retraumatization, or broad rumor collateral require far more justification than ordinary teaching, honest confrontation, or temporary withholding of an answer.

## 2.5 Alternatives

Could a less coercive method plausibly have achieved the goal?

The existence of alternatives matters most when the intervener deliberately creates the crisis. Ayanokoji's own Year 1 repertoire proves that he can scaffold, teach, wait, invite, or preserve refusal. The low-coercion cases make the severe cases harder to excuse as mere incapacity.

## 2.6 Information and informed consent

What does the subject know about:

- the goal;
- the risks;
- the intervener's role;
- the alternatives;
- and the future obligations created?

Consent to one surface action does not constitute consent to a hidden developmental curriculum.

## 2.7 Refusal capacity

Can the person say no without losing safety, status, belonging, or protection?

A formal option is not meaningful if refusal predictably produces catastrophic harm. Kei's initial "choice" to become useful occurs after alternatives are intentionally discredited. Satou's romantic approach is ethically cleaner because Ayanokoji can refuse without threatening her safety or social existence (`Y1V07.5-E071-Y1V07.5-E079`).

## 2.8 Legitimate authority

What gives the intervener the right to act?

Relevant sources may include:

- immediate defense of another person;
- fiduciary office;
- explicit delegation;
- class mandate;
- reciprocal relationship;
- or mere superior power.

The last is the weakest.

## 2.9 Risk distribution

Who bears the cost if the plan fails?

Ayanokoji often keeps authorship hidden while classmates, Kei, Ichinose, Hirata, or third-party rumor targets bear the consequences. Ethical accountability is weaker when the decision-maker externalizes risk and remains insulated from judgment.

## 2.10 Dependency created

Does assistance expand the subject's independent capacity or make the intervener indispensable?

Protection that increases future exit is ethically stronger than protection whose success proves the protector must remain.

## 2.11 Transparency and answerability

Can the subject or community reconstruct what happened, challenge the decision, and correct the actor?

Horikita's public leadership becomes ethically important because reasons can be contested. Ayanokoji's hidden sovereignty is difficult to correct precisely because it is hidden.

## 2.12 Reversibility and repair

Can the harm be undone?

A poor grade, a temporary embarrassment, a lost point, an exposed secret, trauma, and expulsion have radically different reversibility. The more irreversible the consequence, the stronger the burden of justification.

## 2.13 Later agency

Did the intervention make the person more capable of self-direction?

This matters, but it is one variable rather than a retrospective permission slip.

## 2.14 Third-party harm

Did the strategy use uninvolved people as instruments?

The rumor-saturation response to Sakayanagi protects Ichinose partly by injuring other students' reputations. The Class Vote bargaining around Ryuuen, Manabe, and Yamauchi redistributes survival among people who do not know the full causal architecture.

## 2.15 Record integrity

Can the result be truthfully attributed?

When Tsukishiro alters the chess transmission, the school cannot legitimately claim that the recorded result reflects the evaluated performance (`Y1V11-E138-Y1V11-E145`). Record corruption is an ethical harm because it deprives actors of recognition, appeal, and control over the institutional consequences of their own conduct.

# 3. A working vocabulary of ethical status

This document does not assign numerical moral scores. It uses five descriptive categories.

## 3.1 Autonomy-supporting assistance

The subject retains substantial knowledge, refusal, and goal ownership.

Examples include:

- allowing Ike to discover the usefulness of his own experience;
- leaving Airi's group-entry decision to her;
- respecting Sakura's signal before disclosing her private identity;
- and rejecting Satou without exploiting her vulnerability.

## 3.2 Bounded paternalism

The intervener controls some information or pressure but does not manufacture severe danger, and the method remains comparatively reversible.

Examples include delaying the past-exam reveal to preserve study effort and demonstrating coordination principles to Horikita.

## 3.3 Coercive developmental authorship

The intervener imposes pressure, conceals the design, or restricts alternatives in order to produce growth.

The gain may be real. The subject's authorship is materially reduced.

Horikita's island ordeal, Ichinose's induced fracture, and Hirata's Volume 11 breakdown belong here, with different severity.

## 3.4 Manufactured dependency or abusive domination

The actor creates or exploits danger, violates bodily or sexual boundaries, discredits alternatives, or makes safety conditional on submission.

Kei's Volume 4 recruitment is the clearest Year 1 case.

Ryuuen's rooftop torture belongs here as direct domination.

## 3.5 Institutionalized disposability or record capture

The institution creates scarcity that demands sacrifice, prices continued belonging, or alters the official record to serve a private objective.

The Class Vote and Tsukishiro's chess intervention are the central cases.

These categories can overlap. An emergency rescue may be justified at the moment of force while remaining embedded in an earlier structure of manufactured dependency.

# 4. The ethical progression of Year 1

## 4.1 Volumes 1-2: minimal procedure, credibility, and protective intervention

The opening volumes establish that fairness cannot mean equal outcome.

Ayanokoji's objection to the initial system is narrower: unequal consequences require minimally credible procedure. The school hides the criteria, lets students spend freely, and only later reveals that freedom has been scored (`Y1V01-E005`; `Y1V01-E011-Y1V01-E012`; `Y1V01-E027`).

The midterm crisis introduces bounded paternalism. Ayanokoji withholds the past exam until students have made a real effort, then purchases one point to preserve Sudou (`Y1V01-E022-Y1V01-E029`). The informational control is ethically mixed, but the method is limited, reversible, and directed toward a goal the class broadly shares.

Volume 2 then distinguishes truth from actionable proof. Sakura is pressured by class need but repeatedly treated as someone whose testimony should be for herself rather than simply extracted as a resource (`Y1V02-E019-Y1V02-E023`). During the stalker incident, Ayanokoji's response is more troubling. He uses location data, allows the scene to mature for stronger evidence, and privately wishes for a still more decisive moment before intervening (`Y1V02-E031-Y1V02-E034`). He then preserves Sakura's authority over disclosure of the Shizuku identity (`Y1V02-E035-Y1V02-E038`).

The scene therefore contains both sides of his Year 1 ethics:

- unusually effective protection;
- and willingness to optimize evidence while another person remains in danger.

## 4.2 Volumes 3-4.5: environmental authorship and dependency

The island exam expands the object of intervention from information to environment.

Ayanokoji can support Ike by stepping back so Ike verifies his own usefulness (`Y1V03-E011`). He can also manipulate Horikita's body, knowledge, status, and keycard access in order to generate the conditions of class victory and personal development (`Y1V03-E050-Y1V03-E052`).

The contrast is crucial. It proves that he possesses lower-coercion methods. His severe methods are choices, not his only available psychological technology.

Volume 4 moves from authored environment to authored dependency. Kei is not merely approached after danger appears. Ayanokoji helps reactivate the conflict, permits assault, controls evidence, invades bodily privacy, uses sexualized coercion, and presents himself as the protector after alternatives have been discredited (`Y1V04-E032-Y1V04-E061`). The operation produces real strategic and later relational gains. It remains the clearest Year 1 case in which accurate understanding becomes abusive jurisdiction.

Volume 4.5 then refuses a simple portrait of incapacity. Ayanokoji can tell Airi she has the right to reject Yamauchi, remain present without deciding for her, and allow her social step to be self-authored (`Y1V04.5-E033-Y1V04.5-E036`). He can also override Kei's explicit refusal during the pool operation, issue commands, use physical force, and later interpret genuine enjoyment as evidence that the method worked (`Y1V04.5-E040-Y1V04.5-E060`).

The lesson is one of the corpus's most important:

> **A later positive emotion does not retroactively validate an earlier overridden refusal.**

## 4.3 Volumes 5-7.5: defeat, torture, rescue, and negotiated partnership

The sports festival places developmental paternalism at class scale. Ayanokoji teaches Horikita through embodied demonstration and leaves later application partly with her (`Y1V05-E020`). He also permits broader defeat because he believes exposure, injury, and failure will produce stronger future actors. The gains are real. The classmates did not authorize being experimental material.

Paper Shuffle provides a better countermodel with Airi: Ayanokoji deliberately does not make the social decision for her (`Y1V06-E034`). The same volume shows Ryuuen's coercive regime escalating through threats of severe violence (`Y1V06-E027`).

Volume 7 then brings the ethical conflict into physical crisis. Ryuuen deliberately recreates Kei's trauma and tortures her to extract a name (`Y1V07-E052-Y1V07-E068`). Ayanokoji has predicted much of this, withdrawn contact, and delayed rescue. Kei's refusal to betray him is therefore a genuine act of agency inside an ordeal he helped make possible (`Y1V07-E062-Y1V07-E069`).

When Ayanokoji enters, force is immediately justified to stop the assault and remove the attackers' ability to continue. Yet the confrontation also serves a second purpose: breaking Ryuuen's theory of fear and controlling the post-conflict future (`Y1V07-E074-Y1V07-E096`). The defensive and developmental purposes must be separated.

Volume 7.5 begins repair without erasure. Kei knows what he did, asks whether an ordinary person could forgive it, and recognizes that she already has (`Y1V07.5-E005-Y1V07.5-E007`). Forgiveness is hers. It does not make him innocent. Their later spoken bargain is substantially more reciprocal because he requests continued help and she conditions it on full protection (`Y1V07.5-E099-Y1V07.5-E100`).

## 4.4 Volumes 8-10: collective coercion and manufactured disposability

The Mixed Training Camp shows that institutions can coerce through ordinary design rather than spectacular abuse. Students are assigned to temporary groups, leaders bear concentrated liability, bodily privacy is reduced, and rescue is priced. Nagumo exploits Tachibana's refusal to burden Manabu and turns trust into a route of attack (`Y1V08-E071`; `Y1V08-E107-Y1V08-E131`).

Volume 9 makes informational harm central. Sakayanagi uses a true wrongdoing to author a total identity for Ichinose. Ayanokoji counters by saturating the rumor environment, injuring third parties so selective inaction becomes institutionally costly, and then intentionally intensifying Ichinose's crisis until confession becomes possible. The intervention offers forgiveness and future orientation. It also treats psychological fracture as a tool.

Volume 10 makes disposability explicit. The school decides that the absence of expulsion requires correction and creates an anonymous process that must produce a victim (`Y1V10-E013-Y1V10-E019`). Every class response reveals a different ethical theory:

- Hirata refuses the legitimacy of choosing and offers himself;
- Horikita makes reasons public and accepts political liability;
- Ichinose seeks collective purchase and then uses a lottery rather than claim knowledge she lacks;
- Sakayanagi treats the exam as sovereign pruning;
- Ryuuen and Ayanokoji bargain through strategic value and future risk.

The volume's normative force lies in the fact that no response is clean. The institution has made inaction itself consequential.

## 4.5 Volumes 11-11.5 and *First File*: repair, record, and reciprocal authorship

The Event Selection Exam contains both a comparatively strong developmental model and a severe institutional violation.

Horikita learns chess through teaching, practice, and genuine competence. She helps create the board state from which Ayanokoji's later move matters. The subject's own ability is real. Tsukishiro then corrupts the transmission channel and breaks the connection between performance and official result (`Y1V11-E133-Y1V11-E145`).

Hirata's recovery is ethically mixed in a different way. Ayanokoji uses blame he does not fully endorse, pushes until Hirata's defensive identity breaks, and justifies the intervention partly through the class's need for the "Hirata part" (`Y1V11-E048-Y1V11-E053`). The later friendship and restored function do not erase the asymmetry.

Volume 11.5 creates stronger answer-back structures. Keisei defines loyalty as support plus dissent. Manabu gives Horikita a question rather than a specification. Ayanokoji yields the Kushida decision to Horikita. Kei explicitly answers his confession and later physical escalation is preceded by clearer requests (`Y1V11.5-E008-Y1V11.5-E011`; `Y1V11.5-E127-Y1V11.5-E140`).

*First File* supplies the epistemic limit. A rating, profile, or retrospective description can be useful without becoming the whole person. The same principle governs ethical intervention:

> **To understand a person well is not to acquire title over her future.**

# 5. Autonomy: self-authorship inside dependence

Autonomy in Year 1 is not a condition of being untouched by other people.

Every important character is shaped by relations and institutions. Horikita's self becomes partly organized around Manabu. Kei's survival structure is built through dependency. Hirata's moral identity responds to a childhood collective trauma. Ichinose's political agency is inseparable from class solidarity. Sudou's development depends on teaching and relational accountability. Ayanokoji's ordinary-life self becomes possible only inside a school chosen partly as refuge.

The ethical question is not whether influence exists.

It is whether influence can be converted into a life the person can own.

## 5.1 Negative autonomy: protection from external control

Ayanokoji's initial understanding of freedom is negative.

He wants distance from Atsuomi, the White Room, and any adult who claims the right to decide his destination. The dorm room, campus enclosure, and separation from family produce an immediate experience of freedom (`Y1V01-E005-Y1V01-E007`). When Chabashira threatens that refuge, he recognizes the contradiction of abandoning freedom in order to preserve freedom (`Y1V03-E030-Y1V03-E031`).

This negative autonomy matters. It protects the minimum right not to be possessed.

It is insufficient.

A person can escape one author's project while reproducing the same grammar over others.

## 5.2 Positive autonomy: choosing what life is for

Year 1 gradually gives Ayanokoji ends not supplied by the White Room:

- friendship;
- peaceful school life;
- ordinary pleasure;
- romance;
- rivalry chosen for interest rather than survival;
- and a desire to leave something behind.

These ends are not strategically irrelevant simply because they lack a class-point yield. They are evidence of positive autonomy: the ability to decide what one's capacities are for.

The same standard must be extended outward. If Ayanokoji is entitled to choose what his ability serves, so are the people he helps develop.

## 5.3 Relational autonomy: the ability to answer back

The strongest Year 1 autonomy is relational rather than solitary.

Horikita becomes more autonomous while becoming more dependent on distributed expertise. Sudou's ability to disagree with her is evidence that their developmental relation has succeeded rather than failed. Keisei's promise to support and correct her makes loyalty compatible with judgment (`Y1V11.5-E008-Y1V11.5-E012`). Hiyori's refusal of Ryuuen's rooftop operation preserves class loyalty without surrendering moral agency. Kanzaki can criticize Ichinose without treating criticism as betrayal.

Relational autonomy therefore requires:

- access to relevant information;
- practical survivability of refusal;
- permission to retain independent ends;
- and a relationship capable of surviving contradiction.

## 5.4 Autonomous refusal can be more important than the choice the intervener wanted

Year 1 repeatedly treats refusal as evidence of personhood.

Sakura rejects the stalker before rescue arrives (`Y1V02-E032-Y1V02-E035`). Horikita refuses the student council even after Ayanokoji and Manabu support it (`Y1V07.5-E045-Y1V07.5-E046`). Kei refuses to betray Ayanokoji under torture (`Y1V07-E062-Y1V07-E069`). Koenji insists that he alone decides whether his talent will be used for the class (`Y1V11-E071-Y1V11-E088`). Kushida refuses Horikita's handshake because continued knowledge and proximity remain threatening rather than reconciled.

These refusals do not always produce the strategically best outcome.

They establish that the person is not only an object of another actor's plan.

## 5.5 Choice under manipulated conditions

The presence of manipulation does not eliminate agency. It changes what kind of agency is being exercised.

Three levels should be separated.

### Surface consent

The person agrees to the immediately stated action.

### Informed consent

The person understands the relevant purpose, risks, alternatives, and obligations.

### Structural autonomy

The person can refuse without losing safety, belonging, or the possibility of a future.

Kei's initial cooperation after Volume 4 contains surface agreement but very little informed or structural autonomy. Her later bargain after the rooftop is stronger because the protection obligation is spoken and she negotiates terms. Her romantic answer in Volume 11.5 is stronger still, but Ayanokoji's private "textbook" curriculum remains undisclosed.

The document therefore avoids the crude conclusion that she never chooses. It also avoids treating each later yes as approval of everything that preceded it.

## 5.6 Koenji and the limit of collective claims

Koenji creates a difficult autonomy problem because he is not merely weak, frightened, or excluded.

He possesses exceptional ability and refuses to make it class property.

A class can reasonably impose shared rules and expect members to bear some costs of collective life. Yet the claim that a person's talent exists does not establish that the group owns its exercise. Horikita can bargain, persuade, and design around him. She cannot convert epistemic recognition into ownership (`Y1V05-E026`; `Y1V11-E071-Y1V11-E088`; `Y1FF-E023`).

The strongest Year 1 answer is contractual rather than proprietary:

- specify shared obligations;
- make incentives visible;
- preserve defined zones of refusal;
- and do not pretend involuntary extraction is cooperation.

# 6. Ayanokoji's developmental ethics

Ayanokoji is the central normative problem because he combines three traits that usually appear separately:

- extraordinary capacity to diagnose people;
- genuine interest in preserving or developing them;
- and willingness to control conditions they cannot inspect.

His ethics cannot be summarized as benevolent or amoral.

He is most humane and most dangerous through the same faculty.

## 6.1 The strongest case for his methods

The consequential defense is substantial.

Across Year 1, his interventions:

- prevent Sudou's expulsion;
- keep the class alive through examinations;
- help Sakura testify and confront a stalker;
- allow Ike's practical knowledge to become visible;
- protect Horikita's class from sabotage;
- preserve Kei;
- stop Ryuuen;
- help Ichinose confess;
- create an alternative that protects her autonomy during the Class Vote;
- restore Hirata's social function;
- and help Horikita become a more capable leader.

A critic who ignores these outcomes misreads the text.

## 6.2 The strongest case against his methods

The autonomy objection is equally substantial.

He often:

- selects the goal;
- withholds his role;
- controls the information environment;
- restricts alternatives;
- permits suffering to mature;
- distributes costs to people who cannot judge him;
- and interprets later growth as confirmation of his right to have imposed the process.

This is not incidental harshness. It is a developmental philosophy.

## 6.3 Why his superior knowledge does not create jurisdiction

There are three reasons.

First, knowledge can be accurate and bounded. The file is not the person.

Second, prediction can fail. Kei's rooftop refusal, Hiyori's independent agency, Matsushita's concealment, and his ordinary romantic miscalibration demonstrate this.

Third, even perfect prediction would not by itself create ownership. Knowing that a person would later thank you for an imposed ordeal does not authorize the ordeal in advance.

## 6.4 The relevance of available alternatives

Ayanokoji's low-coercion interventions are morally important because they establish an internal comparison set.

He can:

- wait;
- teach;
- demonstrate;
- invite;
- leave a decision to the subject;
- respect privacy;
- or decline to exploit information.

When he instead manufactures dependency or intensifies crisis, the method cannot be defended merely by saying that he knows no other way.

## 6.5 The accountability deficit

Ayanokoji's power is difficult to regulate because the people affected often do not know he acted.

Hidden authorship blocks:

- informed criticism;
- reciprocal explanation;
- institutional review;
- and restitution.

Horikita's increasing ability to detect him is therefore ethically significant. She does not eliminate the shadow constitution, but she begins making it contestable.

## 6.6 The possibility of reciprocal authorship

Year 1 ends without completing Ayanokoji's moral development.

It does establish a criterion.

He becomes less proprietary when he:

- accepts that another person may choose an end he dislikes;
- asks rather than commands;
- makes terms visible;
- allows dissent;
- and values the person after her original function changes.

The decisive future test is not whether his interventions continue to work.

It is whether he can tolerate development he did not author.

# 7. The Kei case: coercive origin, autonomous excess, and genuine later relation

Kei is the hardest Year 1 ethical case because every simple verdict destroys evidence.

The following propositions are all true within the Year 1 boundary:

- Ayanokoji's original recruitment is abusive.
- He later protects her in concrete and costly ways.
- Kei's rooftop refusal is autonomous and morally meaningful.
- Their partnership becomes increasingly negotiated.
- She forgives him.
- Their romance is explicit and reciprocal by the end of Year 1.
- The relationship still contains concealed developmental purpose and unequal information.

## 7.1 Dependency before Ayanokoji

Kei's dependency is not created from nothing.

Her public status, false relationship with Hirata, social authority, and fear of becoming an unprotected target are adaptations to earlier trauma. The need for a host is a survival structure rather than a moral defect.

This matters because Ayanokoji recognizes a real vulnerability.

It does not excuse how he uses it.

## 7.2 Volume 4: manufacture rather than discovery

The operation becomes ethically severe because he does more than identify dependency.

He contributes to a situation in which:

- old hostility is reactivated;
- assault occurs;
- evidence is controlled;
- bodily privacy is invaded;
- sexualized threats are used;
- alternative protectors are discredited;
- and he becomes the only actor who appears both capable and informed (`Y1V04-E032-Y1V04-E061`).

This is manufactured dependency.

The desired outcome - a reliable social operative protected from Manabe's group - does not justify the means.

## 7.3 The pool incident and the fallacy of retrospective consent

Volume 4.5 provides a smaller but clarifying case.

Kei refuses the operational assignment and later refuses exposure and swimming. Ayanokoji says she lacks a refusal right, issues a command, and physically pushes her into the pool. She later retaliates, laughs, and appears to enjoy part of the outcome (`Y1V04.5-E040-Y1V04.5-E060`).

The later enjoyment is relevant to her experience.

It is not evidence that the earlier refusal was meaningless.

Retrospective consent is not consent.

## 7.4 The rooftop ordeal

Ryuuen's conduct is direct torture and coercion. He recreates trauma to obtain information and treats breaking Kei as a demonstration of his theory of fear (`Y1V07-E052-Y1V07-E068`).

Ayanokoji's responsibility is different but serious.

He does not pour the water or strike her. He predicts the danger, withdraws contact, permits the operation to continue, and arrives only after the ordeal has yielded the test and confrontation he wants. His eventual rescue cannot be evaluated apart from that prior decision architecture.

## 7.5 Kei exceeds the script

Kei's refusal to name him matters because it cannot be reduced to successful conditioning.

Ryuuen gives her substantially accurate reasons to believe Ayanokoji manipulated and abandoned her. Betrayal would provide immediate relief. She still refuses (`Y1V07-E062-Y1V07-E069`).

The choice may remain entangled with dependency, pride, identification, and fear. It is nonetheless hers.

The event is one of Year 1's strongest demonstrations that the developed person can exceed the developer's model.

## 7.6 Rescue without innocence

Ayanokoji's entry ends the immediate violence. He absorbs the physical confrontation, defeats the attackers, secures evidence and witnesses, and closes routes of retaliation (`Y1V07-E074-Y1V07-E096`).

Those acts are protective.

Protection does not make him innocent of having allowed the danger to mature.

## 7.7 Forgiveness belongs to Kei

By Volume 7.5, Kei knows enough to name both Ryuuen's abuse and Ayanokoji's orchestration. She asks whether an ordinary person could forgive him and recognizes that she already has (`Y1V07.5-E005-Y1V07.5-E007`).

Forgiveness is an exercise of her agency.

It does not function as a moral pardon granted to the reader. A victim's later attachment cannot be used to declare that the violation was secretly justified.

## 7.8 Partnership and spoken terms

The relation improves when the terms become more explicit.

Ayanokoji requests her continued help. Kei agrees on the condition of complete protection. The bargain remains unequal, but it is more answerable than the original recruitment (`Y1V07.5-E099-Y1V07.5-E100`).

Her social intelligence also becomes a source of influence rather than merely extracted utility.

## 7.9 Romance and hidden curriculum

The Volume 11.5 confession is reciprocal. Kei answers him. The relationship contains ordinary desire, anticipation, embarrassment, and chosen contact. Later physical escalation includes clearer requests and refusal paths than the Volume 4 coercion (`Y1V11.5-E127-Y1V11.5-E140`).

The ethical incompletion remains Ayanokoji's concealed curriculum. He privately frames romance partly as education and imagines dependency correction. Kei consents to the relationship she knows, not to every developmental objective he privately assigns it.

The Year 1 formulation is therefore:

> **genuine reciprocal romance inside an asymmetrical hidden curriculum, preceded by manufactured dependency and transformed - but not purified - by Kei's autonomous choices.**

## 7.10 The future ethical test

The relationship becomes more reciprocal if it can survive Kei becoming:

- less dependent;
- less strategically useful;
- more informed;
- more capable of refusal;
- and interested in ends Ayanokoji did not select.

Year 1 does not answer that test.

# 8. Protection models across Year 1

Protection is not one moral practice.

## 8.1 Chairman Sakayanagi: bounded refuge

Chairman Sakayanagi's protection is comparatively autonomy-respecting.

He helps create the institutional space in which Ayanokoji can remain, but he does not promise exemption from every rule or require personal obedience. His protection responds to Ayanokoji's stated goal rather than replacing it (`Y1V07-E022-Y1V07-E034`).

Its strength is boundedness.

## 8.2 Hirata: universal and exit-oriented protection

Hirata wants to protect everyone, avoid social collapse, and prevent exclusion.

His model preserves membership but becomes unable to discriminate under forced conflict. During the Class Vote, his refusal expresses a genuine moral indictment of the institution while leaving classmates without an operational answer. Offering himself is courageous and self-destructive.

Hirata's protection is ethically generous and politically fragile.

## 8.3 Ichinose: solidaristic protection

Ichinose converts trust and pooled resources into collective insurance.

Her class's no-expulsion commitment is not sentimental theater. It creates real political capacity. Its vulnerability is that rescue depends on enough collective capital and norms opponents may exploit.

Her lottery fallback during the Class Vote is ethically important because it refuses to disguise ignorance as meritocratic certainty.

## 8.4 Manabu: fiduciary protection

Manabu's protection is tied to office and obligation.

He attempts to preserve students while imposing standards and accepts that authority should be restrained by public role. His relation to Tachibana exposes a blind spot: exemplary distance can cause loyal subordinates to conceal danger in order not to burden the leader (`Y1V08-E107-Y1V08-E131`).

## 8.5 Ayanokoji: particular and sovereignty-oriented protection

Ayanokoji protects selected people intensely.

The strength is effectiveness. The danger is private hierarchy. He decides whose future receives exceptional insurance, often without public criteria or reciprocal oversight.

Protection becomes sovereignty when only the protector knows:

- who is guaranteed;
- what the guarantee requires;
- and which other people may be sacrificed to preserve it.

## 8.6 Ryuuen: authoritarian protection

Ryuuen's regime is coercive, but it is not devoid of protection.

He absorbs risks, coordinates aggressively, and later acts in ways followers interpret as valuable enough to preserve him. Voluntary loyalty after defeat proves that fear did not explain the whole relationship.

It does not erase the violence through which the hierarchy was built.

## 8.7 Sakayanagi: protective possession

Sakayanagi can recognize talent and protect selected subordinates within her strategic order.

Kamuro shows the cost. Being given a role may interrupt self-destructive behavior and provide purpose. The role originates in coercion and retained leverage. Protection and use are difficult to separate when the sovereign controls the secret that makes exit dangerous.

## 8.8 White Room protection: development as ownership

The White Room's implied defense is that deprivation produces extraordinary capability and prepares the child to survive a harsh world.

Atsuomi's ownership claim reveals the fraud at the center. If protection culminates in "you are my property," the protected person's future was never the governing end (`Y1V07-E023-Y1V07-E027`).

# 9. Violence: defense, domination, discipline, and authorship

## 9.1 Defensive force

Force can be justified when necessary to stop imminent serious harm.

Ayanokoji's physical intervention on the rooftop meets much of that immediate standard:

- Kei is under active assault;
- the attackers remain capable of continuing;
- verbal resolution is implausible;
- he uses overwhelming but nonlethal force;
- and he secures the aftermath.

The immediate fight is more defensible than the broader plan that allowed the assault to mature.

## 9.2 Coercive violence

Ryuuen's political violence is designed to produce obedience and information.

Phone seizures, threats, targeted injuries, and rooftop torture make pain part of governance (`Y1V04-E027-Y1V04-E028`; `Y1V05-E033-Y1V05-E045`; `Y1V06-E027`; `Y1V07-E052-Y1V07-E068`).

The fact that followers later choose him does not make earlier submission voluntary.

## 9.3 Disciplinary violence

Manabu's early physical treatment of Suzune is ethically serious.

His diagnosis of her imitation and stagnation may later prove partly correct. His later recognition may release her from the demand to copy him. Neither fact retroactively authorizes physical intimidation against a younger sibling.

Correct diagnosis does not convert violence into pedagogy.

## 9.4 Sexualized violence

Ayanokoji's Volume 4 handling of Kei crosses a distinct boundary.

The method uses body contact, restraint, commands, exposure, and a credible rape threat to obtain compliance (`Y1V04-E049-Y1V04-E055`). It cannot be assimilated into ordinary adolescent attraction or defended as merely severe negotiation.

The later romance does not revise the classification of the earlier act.

## 9.5 Institutional violence

The school inflicts harm through rules rather than fists.

Examples include:

- hidden criteria that convert ordinary conduct into collective punishment;
- tests that expose students to injury or bodily humiliation;
- leader penalties that make other people's failures personally catastrophic;
- expulsion mechanisms;
- and record manipulation.

Structural harm is not less real because no individual administers a blow.

## 9.6 Violence as narrative spectacle

Year 1 often makes strategic domination aesthetically satisfying.

The rooftop confrontation is exciting because Ayanokoji's hidden capacity becomes visible and Ryuuen's reign collapses. That narrative pleasure can obscure Kei's ordeal or transform violence into proof of superior coolness.

The ethical reading must preserve the formal seduction without adopting it as moral argument.

## 9.7 Proportionality after victory

Once a threat is neutralized, continued force requires new justification.

Ayanokoji's rooftop violence is also meant to destroy Ryuuen's confidence and establish a future deterrent. The operation remains nonlethal and controlled, but it extends beyond immediate rescue into psychological authorship.

The scene is therefore both justified defense and ethically mixed domination.

# 10. Bodily autonomy, privacy, and sexual ethics

## 10.1 The body as evidence surface

Bodies in Year 1 become sources of information:

- injuries support accusations;
- athletic performance becomes points;
- scars reveal trauma;
- illness changes leadership capacity;
- bath exposure creates hierarchy;
- and physical contact tests concealed ability.

The body is therefore never merely private or merely decorative.

## 10.2 Sakura: protection with preserved disclosure authority

During the stalker incident, Ayanokoji uses surveillance infrastructure and delays for evidence. Yet he also waits for Sakura's signal before disclosing the Shizuku identity (`Y1V02-E031-Y1V02-E038`).

The mixed judgment is important:

- his evidentiary optimization exposes her to risk;
- his privacy restraint recognizes her jurisdiction over a different form of exposure.

## 10.3 Kei: bodily privacy as leverage

Kei's scar and bodily vulnerability become information Ayanokoji controls.

His lack of visible disgust later matters to her. Recognition is real. The route by which he acquired the knowledge remains coercive.

A person can feel seen and violated in the same encounter.

## 10.4 The pool and ordinary coercion

The pool episode shows why ethical scrutiny cannot be reserved for extreme torture.

A command, physical push, or forced public role can violate refusal even when the tone is comic and the result becomes enjoyable. Comedy changes reader affect; it does not erase the in-world boundary.

## 10.5 Communal bathing and institutionalized exposure

The Mixed Training Camp's communal bath converts bodies into visible hierarchy under the language of moral education. Students cannot simply opt out of the social meaning produced by the setting (`Y1V08-E071`; `Y1V08-E148`).

The scene demonstrates that institutional design can coerce visibility without an individual predator.

## 10.6 Later romance and changed protocol

By Volume 11.5, Ayanokoji's approach to physical intimacy includes clearer requests and acknowledgment of refusal. This is genuine procedural development.

It should not be exaggerated into completed ethical equality. Kei still lacks knowledge of the private curriculum through which he interprets the relationship.

## 10.7 Satou as a cleaner romantic countermodel

Satou's approach gives Ayanokoji a comparatively ordinary choice.

She expresses interest. He can accept or refuse. Rejection causes pain but does not threaten her safety, social status, or institutional future. He does not exploit her vulnerability after refusing (`Y1V07.5-E071-Y1V07.5-E079`).

The contrast shows what consent looks like when protection and survival are not hidden stakes.

# 11. Expulsion and the ethics of disposability

Expulsion is the Year 1 mechanism through which institutional evaluation becomes existential.

A poor score does not merely communicate weakness. It can terminate membership, education, relationships, and the future through which a student might have changed.

This creates two distinct questions:

1. When may an institution impose exclusion as a consequence of conduct or failure?
2. Who may claim that a particular person is the correct object of that exclusion?

Year 1 does not answer either question with simple abolition or simple meritocracy.

## 11.1 The legitimate case for consequences

A class cannot function if deliberate sabotage, violence, chronic refusal, or serious misconduct has no consequence.

Kushida poses a real security threat. Yamauchi knowingly cooperates in an attack on his own class. Ryuuen uses violence. Koenji's radical autonomy externalizes costs to others. Weakness and wrongdoing are not identical, but a humane polity still needs discipline.

The strongest defense of expulsion is therefore not that low-value people deserve removal. It is that an institution may sometimes need a final sanction when conduct makes shared membership impossible and lesser interventions have failed.

## 11.2 The epistemic problem

Expulsion is irreversible enough that evaluators need unusually strong knowledge.

Year 1 repeatedly demonstrates that they do not possess it.

- Sudou changes.
- Horikita changes.
- Kei exceeds her survival model.
- Ryuuen's followers reveal loyalty after defeat.
- Hirata recovers after collapse.
- Koenji's full contribution remains uncertain.
- Ayanokoji's file is radically incomplete.

Current performance is weak evidence of permanent developmental ceiling.

## 11.3 The institutional problem: the school manufactures the victim

The Class Vote is not a response to one independently established offense.

Tsukishiro explains that no first-year student has yet been expelled and the examination is designed to correct that condition (`Y1V10-E013-Y1V10-E019`; `Y1V10-E109-Y1V10-E111`).

The institution first decides that someone must disappear.

It then asks students to generate the justification.

This reverses legitimate adjudication.

## 11.4 Anonymous judgment: value and danger

Anonymity can protect honest speech from retaliation. Students may possess real concerns they cannot safely express under an authoritarian leader, dominant clique, or emotional norm.

But anonymity also allows:

- coalition discipline;
- rumor-driven judgment;
- blame without reasons;
- and concealment of strategic deals.

The Class Vote becomes more ethically troubling because anonymity is paired with forced scarcity and expulsion.

## 11.5 The Protection Point

The positive vote reward converts popularity into future immunity.

This may recognize social contribution. It may also reward:

- image management;
- coalition size;
- low-conflict behavior;
- or strategic vote concentration.

The Protection Point is not a pure merit certificate. It is a political asset produced by a particular electorate under a particular rule (`Y1V10-E013-Y1V10-E019`).

## 11.6 Horikita: public reasons and accountable triage

Horikita's decision to identify Yamauchi is ethically stronger than hidden manipulation in one important respect: she states reasons and accepts political liability.

Public accountability permits challenge.

It does not prove substantive justice.

Her argument depends on present contribution, sabotage, and future risk. Yamauchi's conduct gives the judgment a stronger basis than mere low scores. Yet the examination still forces a final outcome inside a compressed timeframe and provides no meaningful rehabilitation process.

## 11.7 Hirata: moral refusal and political paralysis

Hirata sees the institution's demand as illegitimate.

His refusal protects the proposition that classmates are not inventory. Offering himself rejects the authority to rank another person's right to continue.

The weakness is operational. His universal care cannot tell the class what to do when nondecision also produces harm.

Hirata's failure does not refute his moral claim. It demonstrates that humane principles need procedures capable of acting without reproducing disposability.

## 11.8 Ichinose: purchased rescue and lottery

Ichinose first attempts collective rescue through pooled points.

This is a powerful expression of solidarity and an exposure of point inequality. A class with sufficient capital may preserve everyone; a poorer class confronting the same moral commitment may be unable to do so.

When full rescue is impossible, a lottery refuses to claim knowledge the class does not possess. It distributes the imposed risk equally rather than pretending one member has been proven least human or least redeemable.

The lottery can look evasive because it does not optimize competitive value. Ethically, it is a form of epistemic humility.

## 11.9 Sakayanagi: sovereign pruning

Sakayanagi accepts the premise that the exam may be used to remove a strategically undesirable member.

Her strength is decisiveness and accurate local knowledge. Her danger is proprietary sovereignty. The class's human portfolio becomes material for one leader's judgment, and the leader's private rivalry can distort fiduciary responsibility.

## 11.10 Ryuuen and Ayanokoji: covert survival allocation

The Ryuuen outcome is produced through bargaining, follower loyalty, strategic utility, and alternative sacrifice.

Ayanokoji helps create conditions in which Ryuuen can remain and other risks are redistributed. This may preserve a valuable rival and a class leader whose followers sincerely want him back. It also illustrates private sovereignty: people affected by the trade do not know the whole architecture through which survival was allocated.

## 11.11 Yamauchi as person and political threat

The ethical critique of expulsion must not erase Yamauchi's agency.

He is not merely an innocent victim of a cruel system. He participates in sabotage and acts against classmates. Treating him as a person means holding conduct accountable as well as refusing to reduce him to conduct.

The missing institutional middle is important:

- transparent charge;
- evidence;
- opportunity to respond;
- proportionate sanction;
- and a path short of irreversible removal.

## 11.12 Kushida and the limit of anti-disposability

Horikita's refusal to treat Kushida as immediately disposable expresses a serious developmental principle.

It also imposes risk on classmates whose secrets and futures are exposed to Kushida's deliberate hostility. Horikita may not claim unilateral jurisdiction to gamble with other people's privacy merely because she sees recoverable value.

Anti-disposability requires accountability to those bearing the danger.

## 11.13 A Year 1 expulsion standard

Expulsion becomes more defensible only when:

- the conduct is serious and specifically established;
- relevant rules were knowable;
- lesser and more reversible sanctions have been attempted or are plainly inadequate;
- the process allows evidence, response, correction, and appeal;
- developmental uncertainty is acknowledged;
- the institution has not manufactured the need for a victim;
- and the consequence is not a disguised instrument of another actor's private strategy.

The Class Vote fails several of these conditions.

# 12. Truth, privacy, reputation, and informational violence

Information can protect autonomy by exposing lies and making power answerable.

It can also become a weapon more invasive than physical force.

## 12.1 Event truth versus total identity

Ichinose stole.

That event is true.

Sakayanagi's attack becomes ethically abusive when the event is used to author a total identity: kindness becomes fraud, later conduct becomes evidence of permanent corruption, and the target loses the right to a future beyond the event.

The Year 1 moral distinction is:

> **accountability for an act does not authorize total possession of the person's meaning.**

## 12.2 Kushida: confidential truth as coercive capital

Kushida's danger is not fabricated information.

She possesses genuine secrets acquired through trust. That makes her threat more serious, not less.

Confidential truth can function as hostage material. The people who disclosed did not consent to future political use. Her high social accessibility and cooperation can therefore coexist with severe moral unreliability.

## 12.3 The rumor saturation response

Ayanokoji's response to Sakayanagi attacks the information environment rather than only the original claim.

By multiplying rumors, he makes selective institutional inaction harder and helps prevent Ichinose from being defined by one controlled disclosure. The strategy is effective.

It also harms third parties whose reputations become instruments. Their lack of involvement in Ichinose's crisis does not make their injury morally negligible.

## 12.4 Confession under authored pressure

Ayanokoji offers Ichinose a nonjudgmental route to confession and a credible proposition that wrongdoing need not become permanent identity.

He also privately frames the remaining work as breaking her heart and deliberately sustains pressure around a wound he did not create.

The ethical status is mixed:

- the end restores agency;
- the method partly treats psychological fracture as a means;
- and the subject does not know the full architecture.

## 12.5 Sakura and plural privacy

Sakura's Shizuku identity demonstrates that privacy is not simply concealment of a false self.

Different contexts allow different real aspects of her to exist. Exposing the private persona in the name of truth can therefore violate rather than reveal the person.

Ayanokoji's restraint before disclosure is one of his more autonomy-respecting acts (`Y1V02-E036-Y1V02-E038`).

## 12.6 Surveillance as protection and violation

The same phone location function helps find Sakura and illustrates how easily care can become tracking (`Y1V02-E031`). Dummy cameras deter misconduct without actually recording. Real recordings can correct accusations. Partial recordings can mislead. Cameras can also normalize exposure.

The ethics depend upon:

- purpose;
- notice;
- scope;
- retention;
- access;
- and whether the observed person can contest interpretation.

## 12.7 The right to be forgotten versus the duty to remember

Year 1 does not formulate a complete right to be forgotten.

It does establish that records should not become immutable moral identity. Sudou's early conduct, Horikita's July cooperation rating, Ichinose's theft, and Ayanokoji's authored mediocrity all show why dated information must remain dated.

Memory is necessary for responsibility.

Permanent totalization is a different practice.

# 13. Institutional coercion and legitimate authority

## 13.1 Enrollment does not equal consent to unknown rules

Students voluntarily attend ANHS only under severe informational asymmetry.

They do not know the first evaluative criteria, the full expulsion architecture, the future examinations, or the extent to which ordinary behavior will become class liability. Enrollment cannot plausibly function as blanket consent to every later risk.

## 13.2 Hidden criteria and manufactured choice

The opening month gives students abundant points and apparent freedom, then retrospectively reveals that conduct was judged (`Y1V01-E005`; `Y1V01-E011-Y1V01-E012`).

The school may argue that adaptability is part of the test.

Ethically, hidden criteria weaken accountability because students cannot distinguish education from entrapment.

## 13.3 Chabashira: authentic knowledge, fabricated threat

Chabashira's coercion is especially revealing because it uses true background knowledge to make a false threat credible.

She converts Ayanokoji's refuge into leverage: assist her Class A ambition or risk expulsion and return to his father. He later exposes that she received no such instruction (`Y1V03-E029-Y1V03-E031`; `Y1V07-E033-Y1V07-E034`).

The class may benefit from his activation.

The method is an abuse of adult authority.

## 13.4 Nagumo and trust as institutional leverage

Nagumo's power extends beyond winning within rules.

He can influence exam design, mobilize point networks, and use interpersonal trust as a one-use political asset. The Tachibana trap is ethically troubling because it reaches Manabu through a person who withholds her vulnerability out of loyalty (`Y1V08-E043-Y1V08-E046`; `Y1V08-E074-Y1V08-E075`; `Y1V08-E107-Y1V08-E131`).

Keeping an explicit promise does not make the surrounding operation noncoercive.

## 13.5 Manabu and fiduciary restraint

Manabu's model is not noncoercive, but it is more office-bound.

He treats authority as responsibility, values procedure, and attempts to separate personal rivalry from harm to uninvolved students. His failure to cultivate a successor and his emotional distance show that restraint alone does not create a healthy institution.

## 13.6 Mashima and conscientious implementation

Mashima provides the strongest Year 1 adult example of legitimate resistance inside office.

He distinguishes ordinary exam risk from preventable serious injury and accepts personal sanction rather than allow formal procedure to become an excuse for avoidable harm (`Y1V11.5-E034-Y1V11.5-E036`).

His action demonstrates that professional duty includes judgment about the purpose and limits of rules.

## 13.7 Chairman Sakayanagi and bounded protection

Chairman Sakayanagi uses office to protect a student's voluntary choice while preserving the ordinary institutional framework. This is not complete neutrality; it is a form of fiduciary judgment.

The ethical strength is that the protected student's purpose remains primary.

## 13.8 Tsukishiro: procedural capture

Tsukishiro represents the collapse of legitimate authority.

He holds office, controls infrastructure, and can produce an official result. The private mandate to remove Ayanokoji corrupts the public educational function. Surveillance, communication, and record become instruments of a concealed external purpose (`Y1V10-E109-Y1V10-E111`; `Y1V11-E138-Y1V11-E145`).

The wrong is not only unfairness to Ayanokoji.

It injures Sakayanagi, Horikita, the classes, and the meaning of the examination itself by falsifying what their performance established.

## 13.9 Atsuomi and the ownership fallacy

Atsuomi's claim is the most explicit ethical error in the Year 1 corpus:

> I created the conditions that made you; therefore you belong to me.

Causal contribution does not create title over a person.

The White Room's effectiveness at producing capacity cannot authorize the deprivation of ordinary life, unchosen purpose, or lifelong ownership (`Y1V07-E022-Y1V07-E027`; `Y1V07-E040-Y1V07-E041`).

## 13.10 Authority requires division and appeal

Year 1 suggests that developmental institutions become safer when no actor simultaneously controls:

- the goal;
- the test;
- the observation;
- the punishment;
- and the official record.

Division of authority is not bureaucratic inefficiency. It is protection against proprietary development.

# 14. Duties created by intervention

A person who authors another's crisis acquires obligations that do not disappear when the target improves.

## 14.1 Duty of rescue

If an actor knowingly permits danger in order to obtain evidence or development, that actor assumes a heightened duty to intervene before avoidable harm becomes excessive.

This applies to the stalker scene and far more strongly to Kei.

## 14.2 Duty of disclosure

Where possible, the intervener owes the subject enough information to understand what was done and why.

Hidden authorship may sometimes protect strategy or third parties. Permanent concealment also denies the subject the ability to judge the relationship.

## 14.3 Duty of restitution

A successful outcome does not cancel costs.

Restitution may require:

- safety;
- privacy;
- repair of reputation;
- material compensation;
- opportunity to exit;
- acknowledgment of wrong;
- or surrender of future leverage.

Ayanokoji's later protection of Kei partially responds to danger. Year 1 does not show an explicit acknowledgment of the Volume 4 wrong.

## 14.4 Duty not to monopolize the benefit

A developer should not make herself the sole route through which the subject can exercise new capacity.

Kei becomes more autonomous when her social influence, friendships, and identity are not reducible to service as Ayanokoji's operative.

## 14.5 Duty to tolerate changed utility

If the person later becomes less useful, the relation should not collapse into disposal.

This is one of the strongest tests of whether care was for the person or for the function.

## 14.6 Institutional duty for authored scarcity

When the school creates a forced-choice examination, it bears responsibility for the harms the rule makes rational.

It cannot treat student cruelty as pure revelation if the design rewards betrayal, concentrates liability, or requires a victim.

# 16. Ethical profiles of major actors

## 16.1 Ayanokoji: developmental insight without stable self-limitation

**Principal virtue:** he sees possibility where institutions and peers see fixed defect.

**Principal failure:** he treats superior understanding as jurisdiction.

**Best Year 1 mode:** scaffolding that lets another person discover and choose.

**Worst Year 1 mode:** manufacturing dependency and withholding rescue.

**Decisive need:** accept correction from people whose lives he can influence.

## 16.2 Horikita: accountable judgment under incomplete knowledge

**Principal virtue:** increasing willingness to state reasons, use distributed expertise, and revise.

**Principal failure:** risk of treating development as a project she may impose upon unwilling subjects and classmates.

**Best Year 1 mode:** teaching adapted to the learner and public leadership answerable to dissent.

**Worst Year 1 risk:** the Kushida project as unilateral gamble with collective privacy.

## 16.3 Hirata: dignity without a workable theory of tragic choice

**Principal virtue:** refusal to reduce belonging to utility.

**Principal failure:** paralysis and self-erasure when conflict requires discrimination.

**Best Year 1 mode:** presence, mediation, and exit-oriented protection.

**Worst Year 1 risk:** universal care becoming a demand that no one state necessary but painful judgments.

## 16.4 Ichinose: solidarity with insufficient adversarial defense

**Principal virtue:** membership protection, shared capital, and forgiveness without purity.

**Principal failure:** norms of reciprocity can become exploitable assumptions.

**Best Year 1 mode:** collective rescue and trust that preserves rival agency.

**Worst Year 1 risk:** centrality of one trusted leader making private collapse a polity-wide vulnerability.

## 16.5 Ryuuen: protection and voluntary loyalty inside coercive politics

**Principal virtue:** accepts danger, acts decisively, and understands incentives.

**Principal failure:** makes fear, pain, and information monopoly legitimate tools of rule.

**Year 1 development:** followers' voluntary preservation proves authority can exceed fear.

**Unresolved question:** whether that sociological legitimacy can limit violence rather than merely strengthen command.

## 16.6 Sakayanagi: epistemic accuracy without sufficient fiduciary restraint

**Principal virtue:** recognizes real difference and allocates ability effectively.

**Principal failure:** treats superior perception as a title to use people.

**Year 1 danger:** private rivalry can become a cost imposed on classmates who did not choose it.

## 16.7 Manabu: office-bound responsibility with relational blind spots

**Principal virtue:** restraint, procedural seriousness, and concern for institutional continuity.

**Principal failure:** emotional distance causes others to hide burden and danger.

**Year 1 growth:** recognition of Suzune becomes ethical when he releases her from specification.

## 16.8 Nagumo: mobility through patronage

**Principal virtue:** challenges rigid class shelter and recognizes initiative.

**Principal failure:** concentrates evaluation, resources, and access in a personal network, making merit depend on recognition by the sovereign evaluator.

## 16.9 Chabashira: teacher authority captured by personal regret

**Principal virtue:** real knowledge of class potential and later capacity for professional correction.

**Principal failure:** converts a student's refuge into leverage for her own unresolved Class A project.

## 16.10 Chairman Sakayanagi and Mashima: bounded institutional protection

Both provide Year 1 evidence that adult authority can protect without claiming ownership.

Their ethical strength lies in:

- responding to the student's stated end;
- limiting private interference;
- accepting role-bound responsibility;
- and preserving review rather than personal dependence.

## 16.11 Atsuomi and Tsukishiro: ownership and capture

Atsuomi represents developmental ownership.

Tsukishiro represents procedural capture.

One claims the person because he authored the environment.

The other claims the result because he controls the record.

Together they define the outer ethical danger of Year 1: power over both who a person becomes and what the institution says that person did.

# 17. Strong counterarguments and limits

The ethical reading should confront the strongest defenses of coercive strategy rather than treating them as obviously unserious.

## 17.1 Counterargument: outcomes justify the methods

Ayanokoji's harshest interventions frequently succeed.

Kei survives and becomes more capable. Horikita develops. Ichinose returns to political life. Hirata recovers. Ryuuen's reign of fear is broken. The class avoids repeated disaster.

### Response

Outcome matters. It is not the only moral fact.

If success were sufficient, then any actor who predicts correctly would acquire retrospective permission to impose suffering. That rule would reward unaccountable risk-taking because the failed cases disappear from the victory narrative.

The better standard asks whether comparable benefit could have been achieved with less coercion, whether the subject owned the goal, and whether the actor accepted liability for possible failure.

## 17.2 Counterargument: superior knowledge creates paternalistic authority

Ayanokoji often sees what the subject cannot. Horikita is socially self-defeating. Kei is trapped in dependency. Ichinose cannot confess. Hirata cannot act. A less informed person may reject the very intervention needed to become freer.

### Response

Superior knowledge can strengthen the reason to advise, warn, teach, or intervene in emergency.

It does not create general title over another person's developmental process.

Three limits remain:

- the model may be wrong;
- the person may reasonably value a different end;
- and a valuable future is still the person's rather than the diagnostician's property.

## 17.3 Counterargument: teenagers cannot be expected to give fully informed consent

Schools, parents, and teachers routinely impose developmental structures because adolescents lack complete foresight.

### Response

Year 1 does not require an impossible standard of adult independence.

It requires proportionality and increasing participation. The younger or less informed person should receive as much explanation, choice, and contestability as circumstances permit. Coercion becomes less legitimate when the authority conceals a private objective, manufactures danger, or uses the student's dependence for unrelated gain.

## 17.4 Counterargument: ANHS students accepted harsh competition by enrolling

The school is elite, voluntary, and explicitly oriented toward results. Students should expect demanding rules.

### Response

Consent to a demanding school is not blanket consent to undisclosed criteria, manufactured expulsion, preventable serious injury, or falsified records. The legitimacy of a rule still depends on notice, relation to educational purpose, proportionality, and honest administration.

## 17.5 Counterargument: Kei's forgiveness settles the relationship

The person most affected chooses to remain, forgive, help, and enter romance. External critics should not replace her judgment with their own.

### Response

Her judgment must be respected.

Respecting it means recognizing the reality of her forgiveness and romance. It does not require converting forgiveness into proof that no wrong occurred. A victim may forgive an actual wrong. That is what gives forgiveness meaning.

## 17.6 Counterargument: refusal under torture proves the plan succeeded

Kei's rooftop choice demonstrates a stronger self. If the ordeal produces autonomy, perhaps the design is vindicated.

### Response

The choice is admirable because it exceeds the coercive environment, not because torture is a legitimate educational instrument. A person can create meaning from abuse without making abuse permissible.

## 17.7 Counterargument: expulsion is necessary for institutional seriousness

Without a final consequence, sabotage and nonperformance may become rational. A class cannot protect everyone indefinitely.

### Response

A final sanction may sometimes be defensible.

The Class Vote remains troubling because the institution first demands a victim and then asks students to produce a rationale. Legitimate expulsion should respond to specific established conduct through proportionate, reviewable process rather than manufacture disposability as educational spectacle.

## 17.8 Counterargument: Ryuuen's followers choose him, so his rule is legitimate

Voluntary return after defeat shows that he offers value beyond fear.

### Response

It strengthens sociological legitimacy.

It does not erase the conditions under which preferences formed, the history of coercion, or the continuing availability of violence. A former authoritarian may acquire genuine loyalty without becoming ethically democratic.

## 17.9 Counterargument: the genre aesthetic makes ordinary moral standards misplaced

The story is a strategic thriller. Manipulation and high-stakes exams are the dramatic grammar.

### Response

Genre convention explains why the acts are narratively pleasurable. The novels themselves repeatedly raise questions of ownership, freedom, protection, proof, and personhood. Ethical evaluation is not imported against the text; it follows the conflicts the text creates.

## 17.10 Counterargument: Ayanokoji is himself a victim and should be judged less harshly

His developmental grammar was imposed on him before he could choose it.

### Response

This substantially mitigates blame and deepens tragedy.

It does not eliminate responsibility once Year 1 demonstrates that he can recognize alternatives, experience doubt, and sometimes choose less coercive modes. Victimhood explains inherited methods; it does not grant permanent immunity from responsibility for reproducing them.

# 18. Core distinctions locked for Year 1

## 18.1 Explanation is not justification

A complete account includes causal history without treating cause as permission.

## 18.2 Effectiveness is not legitimacy

A strategy may work and remain wrongful.

## 18.3 Sincere choice is not full autonomy

A person may choose genuinely inside conditions another actor manipulated.

## 18.4 Protection is not ownership

Safety should increase future exit and agency rather than make the protector indispensable.

## 18.5 Dependency is not love

Dependency can coexist with love, enable love, or distort love. It is not proof of love by itself.

## 18.6 Forgiveness is not innocence

Kei's forgiveness belongs to her and presupposes something to forgive.

## 18.7 Development is not retroactive consent

Growth after pressure does not authorize the pressure before it occurred.

## 18.8 Rule compliance is not justice

A formally valid exam can be ethically illegitimate; a conscientious breach can serve the institution's proper purpose.

## 18.9 Competitive value is not human worth

Current contribution may inform strategy without exhausting personhood or developmental possibility.

## 18.10 Recognition is not jurisdiction

Seeing a person accurately does not confer ownership of her future.

## 18.11 Refusal is not necessarily betrayal

Hiyori, Keisei, Sudou, Horikita, Kei, Kushida, and Koenji demonstrate different ways refusal can preserve selfhood or improve collective judgment.

## 18.12 Violence is not one moral category

Defense, torture, intimidation, discipline, retaliation, and structural exclusion require separate judgments.

## 18.13 A beneficial relationship can have an illegitimate origin

Later reciprocity transforms the relation without rewriting its history.

## 18.14 Public accountability is not substantive correctness

Horikita's visible reasons make power contestable; they do not guarantee the judgment is just.

## 18.15 Institutional price is not moral price

Twenty million points can buy an administrative option. It cannot measure what a person's future is worth.

# 20. The answer-back standard

The ethical center of Year 1 can be operationalized through ten questions.

Before endorsing a developmental intervention, ask:

1. **Whose goal is being pursued?**
2. **Did the intervener create or amplify the danger?**
3. **What does the subject know?**
4. **Can the subject refuse without catastrophic loss?**
5. **Is the method necessary and proportionate?**
6. **Were less coercive alternatives seriously considered?**
7. **Who bears the risk and collateral harm?**
8. **Can the intervener be challenged and corrected?**
9. **Does the result expand independent capability or deepen monopoly dependence?**
10. **Can the person become someone the intervention did not predict?**

A relationship, leader, or institution need not answer all ten perfectly to possess legitimacy.

The more severe and irreversible the intervention, the less failure it can tolerate.

The standard is especially demanding when the actor controls several layers at once:

- diagnosis;
- environment;
- protection;
- evaluation;
- and official record.

That concentration is what links Ayanokoji's hidden sovereignty, the White Room, and Tsukishiro despite their different scales and motives.

# 21. A positive ethics of development

The critique of coercion should not end in nonintervention.

Year 1 repeatedly shows that refusing to help can also abandon people to violence, false judgment, isolation, or institutional machinery.

A positive developmental ethics contains the following commitments.

## 21.1 See capacity beyond current metrics

Static weakness should not become destiny.

## 21.2 Translate rather than humiliate

Horikita's improved teaching works when she changes the explanation rather than merely condemning the learner.

## 21.3 Scaffold without stealing discovery

Ike's island recognition and Airi's group entry are strong examples.

## 21.4 State reasons

Public reasons make leadership contestable and permit learning from error.

## 21.5 Preserve meaningful refusal

The ability to say no is not inefficiency. It is evidence that the person remains a participant rather than material.

## 21.6 Use the least coercive sufficient method

Emergency force may be necessary. Developmental pain should not be manufactured merely because it is narratively efficient.

## 21.7 Repair the harms intervention creates

Protection, explanation, restitution, and surrendered leverage may be required after success.

## 21.8 Build plural support

A person is safer when no single protector controls all routes to security, status, and belonging.

## 21.9 Accept unplanned development

The subject's divergence from the model is not necessarily failure. It may be the strongest evidence of self-authorship.

## 21.10 Keep records bounded

Evaluations should remain dated, domain-specific, correctable, and smaller than the person.

# 25. Final synthesis

Year 1 is full of people who can correctly identify what another person needs.

That is not enough.

Ayanokoji sees that Horikita requires interdependence, that Kei requires protection and a route beyond parasitic survival, that Ichinose requires a future beyond one wrongdoing, that Hirata cannot continue through universal appeasement, and that Ryuuen's fear regime must be broken.

Horikita sees that Sudou can become more than his early record, that Kushida possesses abilities the class may need, and that leadership cannot remain solitary excellence.

Hirata sees that an institution demanding a victim is morally wrong.

Ichinose sees that membership can survive wrongdoing.

Ryuuen sees that people respond to fear, desire, and credible protection.

Sakayanagi sees ability beneath public appearances.

Manabu sees that a school order needs standards, restraint, and continuity.

The ethical failures begin when accurate perception is translated into title.

> I understand you, therefore I may decide what you should become.
>
> I can protect you, therefore you should depend on me.
>
> I can rank you, therefore I may decide whether you continue.
>
> I created your ability, therefore you belong to me.
>
> I control the record, therefore my version is what institutionally happened.

Year 1 rejects each inference without retreating into passivity.

People do need intervention.

Sakura needs help against the stalker. Kei needs the rooftop assault stopped. Sudou needs teaching and procedural rescue. Ichinose needs a route through shame. Hirata needs confrontation. Classes need judgment. Schools need discipline. Violence can require force. Institutions cannot preserve every option forever.

The mature question is not whether to act.

It is how to act without treating the person as material.

The strongest positive cases share a pattern:

- the goal is substantially the subject's;
- information is sufficient for meaningful participation;
- refusal survives;
- the method does not manufacture severe danger;
- power states reasons and accepts correction;
- the benefit expands rather than monopolizes future agency;
- and the person can become someone the helper did not design.

The strongest negative cases reverse that pattern.

The White Room defines the purpose, environment, curriculum, and future, then converts development into ownership. Ayanokoji's Volume 4 operation concentrates diagnosis, danger, evidence, protection, and role assignment in one hidden actor. The Class Vote manufactures a victim and invites students to mistake forced scarcity for proof of deserved disappearance. Tsukishiro controls the communication channel and makes the official record answer a private mandate.

The final Year 1 ethical proposition is therefore:

> **The right to help does not include the right to own the helped person's future. Protection is most legitimate when it makes itself less necessary; development is most legitimate when the developed person can reject the developer's purpose; leadership is most legitimate when judgment is public and correctable; and institutional power is most legitimate when no actor can privately control the test, the danger, the rescue, and the record at once.**

Ayanokoji's Year 1 development is morally significant because ordinary life, friendship, rivalry, and romance begin producing values his strategic grammar cannot fully contain.

It is morally incomplete because he continues to translate care into curriculum and insight into jurisdiction.

His decisive task is not to stop understanding people.

It is to let the people he understands answer back.
