## Plain narrative reconstruction

In 1777, the mermaid king Rowan celebrates the birth of Lara, his sixth daughter, and declares that every daughter is beautiful regardless of color. Several years later Lara is already unusually exploratory. A disguised caretaker tells Rowan that Lara may be special and recalls the dead queen. Rowan rejects both the implied hierarchy among his daughters and prophecy itself, which royal law forbids. The disguised figure reveals herself as Grace, Rowan's exiled sister and the girls' aunt. Grace warns that Lara's light could become a blade that destroys the castle; Rowan invokes the death penalty for violation of the ban and orders an attack, but Grace escapes.

As a child, Lara explores beyond the palace with her sister Luna. They find human objects, including a doll. Luna repeats the received description of humans as ugly and cursed and warns that merely touching their possessions is a grave sin. Lara secretly keeps the doll. At ten, Lara receives the royal ring that permits her to swim farther while formally barring her from the human world. Rowan says that mermaids live by light and that human ugliness must not contaminate it. Lara tells Luna she wants to see humans at least once; Luna calls the idea foolish.

At fifteen, Lara reaches the surface during a ship fire. She rescues a human prince from the sea and leaves him on shore. Back home, Rowan confronts her with the human relics she has repeatedly collected and the land sand found in her room. When Lara asks whether humans truly are ugly, Rowan declares the question more sinful than the prohibited act and confines her for one hundred mornings.

Grace visits the imprisoned Lara, identifies herself as her aunt, and offers a potion that can turn a mermaid into a human. A magic mirror displays Lara's unstated wish to escape the constriction of father, castle, and law and meet the prince again. Grace says the mirror tests the viewer's heart; its breaking marks how strongly Lara's heart moved. She initially withdraws the offer as a provocation, calling Lara a coward. Lara answers that she is not a coward but a great fool trained to obey her father. Grace counters that people are born twice—once to exist and once to live—and that neither Rowan nor law can make Lara's choice.

Grace then specifies the bargain. The transformation is temporary; Lara must find `本当の愛` (true love) before she vanishes into foam. Grace explicitly distinguishes this from `恋心` (romantic infatuation), defining the required love as a light born only in the deepest heart. She takes Lara's voice, explaining that one word in the surface world's harsh, sensitive environment can become grounds for exclusion, and claims that true love is a wordless light. At the same time, she openly treats Lara's undertaking as a story she wants to watch and a test of love between mermaid and human.

Lara becomes human, meets the prince, and spends an apparently happy interval at his estate. On a later voyage her mermaid body becomes visible. The prince calls that form ugly and repulsive, draws a sword, and rejects her. Lara says she loved him, then adopts the evaluative structure she learned at home: Rowan had said mermaids were beautiful and humans ugly, but the prince's response leads her to conclude that she herself was the ugly one. She dies and dissolves.

Lara awakens again in her mermaid body inside a shell in the ruined underwater palace. Grace's mirror shows the intervening crisis. Rowan accuses Grace of harming Lara, while Grace insists Lara chose. The palace's light disappears; members of the court interpret the broken law as a curse upon the royal line. Rowan says he cannot forgive Grace but nevertheless requires her power to move the kingdom. At the end of an unspecified, enormous passage of time, Rowan believes the mermaids can only fade. He gives Lara his remaining light because she is a princess who must receive it. He still calls her sinful, but tells her he loves her.

Confronted with the result, Lara blames her wish to become human and offers body and life to recover her father and everyone else. Grace says Rowan and the others are asleep rather than dead: she gathered their faint light into shells, and only Lara can awaken them. Grace demands that Lara prove her resolve by piercing Grace with her own hand. Lara acts; Grace reveals another potion and blesses the brave princess. A wave carries Lara into a coastal Japanese city in 2026. Mari, hurrying with her dog Gon, sees Lara arrive across the sky and intercepts her fall. The episode closes their startled face-to-face contact with the captioned statement, `これが……出会い`—“this was the meeting.”

## Governing argument I: curiosity precedes the prince

The episode makes romance an accelerant, not the origin, of Lara's boundary crossing. Before she knows a prince exists, child Lara is bored by the palace, explores ruins, keeps a human doll despite being told even touch is sinful, and wants to see the people above the water. The tenth-birthday ring gives this desire a political form: it is both a grant of movement and a portable sign of the one frontier she may not cross. Rowan's formula moves from ontology to hygiene—humans are ugly and cursed; mermaids live by light; therefore contact risks defilement (cues 78-86). Yet Lara's question to Luna is practical rather than romantic: what would happen if she simply met them (cue 96)?

The rescue in `SYL-E01-S05` matters because it reverses the kingdom's moral vocabulary before Lara can theorize that reversal. The supposedly cursed being is helpless; the supposedly vulnerable mermaid acts. Static images across sheets 011-014 move from burning human vessel to Lara carrying the unconscious prince through light and onto shore. The episode does not require the prince to be worthy in order for Lara's act to be ethical. Her later attraction can therefore fail without retroactively reducing her surfaceward desire to “wanting a man.”

This distinction revises the easiest version of legacy hypothesis `SYL-H0005`. Episode 01 establishes genuine male-directed romantic investment, but it also establishes a wider appetite for unknown people and worlds that is older than that investment. Any later Lara monograph that treats curiosity as merely courtship-driven will have to ignore the episode's own chronology.

## Governing argument II: Rowan makes care and law speak through the same mouth

Rowan is already a major ideological axis because Episode 01 gives him neither a simple tyrant's consistency nor a loving father's innocence. At Lara's birth he refuses color hierarchy and calls any daughter beautiful. When a disguised Grace calls Lara special, Rowan answers that all his daughters are special and dear (cues 35-42). His skepticism toward prophecy can be read as an attempt to protect the child from being reduced to a foretold role. But the same answer immediately invokes a law that forbids prophecy, and within a minute Rowan invokes death for violation. Equal personhood is defended through sovereign violence.

The contradiction sharpens when Lara returns from the surface. Rowan knows of her repeated collection of land objects but draws the line at evidence of bodily crossing. Lara does not deny the act; she asks whether his account of humans is true. Rowan says that this utterance is “far more sinful” than what she did (`今の言葉は……お前のしでかしたことよりも……はるかに罪深い`, cues 130-133). Law is not merely a safety code here. It polices which propositions can be entertained. His punishment confines her until one hundred mornings have passed, making paternal protection and political detention materially indistinguishable.

The mirror history does not erase that coercion, but it prevents a flat verdict. In catastrophe Rowan first accuses Grace, then accepts that the survival of his people requires the power of the witch he exiled. He complains of her but uses her assistance to reach a new dwelling. At the end he gives Lara his last light. Even this tenderness remains institutionally phrased: Lara “as princess” must receive it, and he loves her however sinful she is (cues 273-279). He does not retract the judgment that helped teach her to interpret rejection as proof of personal ugliness. The gift is real, costly love; it is not an apology.

Rowan's E01 problem is therefore not hypocrisy in the thin sense that he secretly lacks principles. His actions are ideologically continuous around the preservation of royal order, but his concept of preservation repeatedly subordinates the person's interpretive freedom. Crisis forces him to cross his own boundary—depending on Grace—and private love finally exceeds institutional survival through sacrifice. What remains open is whether later material lets that private act revise the polity, or whether Lara is simply made responsible for restoring an order that never recognized her question as legitimate.

## Governing argument III: Grace offers authorship by controlling the page

Grace correctly identifies something Rowan refuses to permit: Lara's inner life can authorize a decision. Her maxim about being born once to exist and again to live places mere biological or dynastic membership below self-directed existence (cues 181-185). The mirror also refuses Lara's protective self-description. It shows the wish she hesitates to speak and physically breaks when her heart moves. In this sense Grace creates a counter-institution where desire can count as knowledge.

But Grace does not simply return agency. She stages it. She knows the wish before Lara volunteers it, taunts her as a coward, withholds and restores the potion, defines the success condition, takes her voice, and repeatedly calls the undertaking a story she wants to watch. The rationale for voicelessness is especially unstable. Grace says a single word can cause exclusion, citing Rowan as precedent (cues 199-203). That diagnosis is insightful: Lara has just been punished more harshly for questioning doctrine than for crossing a boundary. Yet Grace's remedy removes Lara's capacity to speak in the very world whose recognition will determine her survival. Protection and experimental control are inseparable.

After Lara's death, Grace again frames coercion as proof of authorship. She tells Lara that the family can be saved only by her, then demands that Lara pierce her. The static progression shows Lara forcing the weapon through the mirror/Grace image and then receiving the second potion. Grace's blessing acknowledges courage, but the choice was produced inside a threat to everyone Lara loves. Episode 01 therefore strengthens the two-sided form of `SYL-H0008`: Grace enables self-authorship and is also an in-universe author who controls the available genre, stakes, information, and means of expression. The alternatives are not freedom versus control; they are two rival regimes of controlled choice.

## Governing argument IV: “true love” is defined against infatuation before romance fails

The Japanese wording prevents the potion plot from being reduced to “win the prince's romantic love.” Grace says Lara must obtain `本当の愛`, then immediately says it differs from `恋心など`—romantic feeling or infatuation—and calls it a radiant thing born only in the heart's deepest part (cues 191-195). She later describes it as `声ならぬ光そのもの`, a light without voice (cues 207-208). At E01, Grace nevertheless assumes the test will reveal the value of love growing between a mermaid and a human, and she first sends Lara to the prince. The episode distinguishes the categories without yet identifying the successful referent.

The prince's rejection is both personal and ideological. When Lara's body becomes legible as mermaid, he calls it ugly and repulsive and interprets the prior relationship as deception. Lara's answer is not retaliation. She affirms that she loved him, then turns two inherited binaries against herself. Rowan had said the mermaid was beautiful and the human ugly; the human says the mermaid is ugly; Lara resolves the contradiction by making herself the uniquely ugly term (cues 229-234). This is more than heartbreak. It is the internalization of a legal-moral vocabulary in which difference must identify a contaminant.

The episode consequently establishes Lara's attraction to and love for a male target without establishing that romance is the ultimate account of love in the series. It also denies the prince interpretive authority over Lara's rescue, courage, or personhood. His nonreciprocity is not itself a moral fault; his disgust and threat are. Later relationship analysis must keep those propositions separate.

## Form: shells, mirrors, rings, light, and the long route to a “meeting”

The episode's objects do causal and argumentative work.

- **Shells** hold Lara at birth, receive her after death, and preserve the family's remaining light. A shell is therefore womb, coffin, archive, and promise rather than a single symbol. Grace's account in cues 297-303 makes the restoration quest a problem of stored personhood.
- **Mirrors** expose desire and reconstruct inaccessible history, but the images are mediated by Grace's device. Their breaking marks emotional force and transition; it does not guarantee neutral truth about causality. The mirror is simultaneously evidence surface and instrument of pressure.
- **The royal ring** is granted as recognition and restriction. In the static progression it remains visible during transformation, rejection, and the mirror history, tying Lara's body to an institution even outside its territory.
- **Light** names royal life, ethical value, magical resource, inheritance, and love. Episode 01 deliberately overloads the term. Rowan's claim that mermaids live through light is partly supported by the palace's extinguishing and the family's fading, but the moral equation between light and purity remains his ideology, not a demonstrated law.
- **Foam, star, and wave** repeatedly abstract Lara's body at death and passage. The later wave is visually exuberant rather than terminal: the same medium that carried erasure becomes transport into a new story layer.

The episode spends more than twenty-one minutes reaching its nominal starting relation. Three historical dates—1777, 1787, and 2026—compress Lara's biological childhood, first death, and long suspension before the story can say “meeting.” The final Mari sequence releases the tragic register through scale and collision: Lara arrives like a projectile; Mari and Gon initially read the event from an ordinary street; the two end inverted and physically close in the moonlit static compositions. This is a clear tonal reversal, but without audio or continuous motion it would be false precision to claim a particular comic beat or vocal rhythm.

The end-credit tribute to Hans Christian Andersen makes the adaptation lineage explicit as paratext within the episode. Episode 01 is not merely reproducing *The Little Mermaid*: it relocates the fatal romance inside a political prehistory and treats the famous ending as the condition for a second beginning.

## Character and relationship deltas

**Lara.** Her source-verified baseline combines curiosity, covert rule-breaking, rescue competence, romantic idealization, courage under coercion, and a severe tendency toward totalizing self-blame. The first three precede the failed courtship. Her offer of body and life for the family is an action, but it is made after she accepts causal responsibility more completely than the evidence warrants.

**Rowan.** He is verified as father, king, Grace's brother, guardian of royal law, source of the human-contamination doctrine, and the person who transfers the last royal light to Lara. E01 is sufficient to confirm the owner-requested dedicated monograph as analytically warranted, though not ready for full-series synthesis.

**Grace.** She is verified as Lara's aunt and an exiled witch. She has exceptional knowledge and power but is not established as omniscient. Her explanations repeatedly mix accurate diagnosis, theatrical provocation, withheld information, and self-interested spectatorship.

**Luna.** She is verified as Lara's sister and childhood companion. Her teasing gives Lara a nearby relationship inside the palace, while her disgust toward human artifacts shows inherited law reproduced laterally rather than only imposed from above.

**The original prince.** He is first a recipient of Lara's unilateral rescue, later an object of her declared love, and finally an agent of bodily disgust and threatened violence. No mutual “true love” is established.

**Mari.** Her name and modern identity are textually introduced at the episode's end. The static progression supports alertness, rapid bodily intervention, and surprise. No affection, attraction, friendship, or durable obligation can yet be inferred from an impact-and-rescue meeting.

## Counterreadings and unresolved questions

The strongest charitable reading of Rowan is that the ban protects a vulnerable magical people from a human society the prince quickly proves capable of violent disgust. Episode 01 gives this account real evidence. It does not, however, explain why asking whether humans are ugly is more culpable than crossing the boundary, or why death is the proper response to prophecy. Safety may motivate the law without exhausting its ideological function.

The strongest charitable reading of Grace is that voicelessness shields Lara from a world where speech can trigger exclusion and that the trial gives Lara the only available means to rescue her family. The problem is that Grace creates or controls both tests, enjoys Lara's story as spectacle, and delays crucial information about the family's survival until Lara offers her own life. Later episodes must determine whether this is strategic pedagogy, cruelty, limited means, or some combination.

The palace's loss of light follows Lara's transgression, and the court calls the royal line cursed. Temporal sequence alone does not establish whether Lara's act, the broken law, Grace's magic, an external cosmology, or institutional panic causes the catastrophe. Rowan's willingness to blame Grace and Lara's willingness to blame herself are character responses, not causal proof.

The mirror is visually treated as a source of remote and historical images, but its truth conditions remain unclear. Grace controls it, it responds to desire, and it can break. The account of Rowan's final sacrifice is strongly presented but remains mediated. Later corroboration can strengthen or revise the mechanism without retroactively changing what Lara knows at E01.
