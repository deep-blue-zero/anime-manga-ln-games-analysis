## Finding and being found: Lisa and Kōta's reciprocal light

E06 gives Kōta a history that revises his apparent E04-E05 role as an obedient masked messenger. He begins as Lisa's round, anglerfish-like companion: a being whose literal light lets him locate life, minerals, places, and worlds inside darkness. Young Lisa values the ability without demanding another shape. Lara's quick movement from fear to apology confirms that the creature is not presented as a disposable monster waiting for human improvement.

The later rescue makes finding reciprocal. Kōta locates Lisa in the darkness of human captivity, but his reason for doing so is that Lisa had already found him. Her earlier praise turned an odd capacity into a recognized gift, and his memory gives recognition causal force: being found enables him to become the finder. The episode therefore refuses a simple rescue hierarchy. Kōta can release Lisa because Lisa first made his existence meaningful to him.

Lisa's request that he remain `私を照らす光` changes the literal lure into a relational office. She does not merely command a retainer to continue serving. When Kōta says his role can end and she should return to the others, Lisa rejects his self-removal. Their continued bond is chosen at the point where instrumental usefulness could have expired. Kōta then accepts a human name from Lisa and vows to follow her. The naming could support a possession reading—Lisa authors the identity of a devoted subordinate—but the rescue dialogue prevents that from being sufficient. Kōta's loyalty arises from remembered recognition, and Lisa explicitly refuses a future that discards him after use.

The episode does not name this relation romantic. Nor is Kōta originally human, so their bond cannot straightforwardly refute Lisa's narrower verbal claim that mermaids and humans cannot love. It does, however, expose the instability of fixed-body essentialism. Both cross forms through potion; each continues to recognize the other through transformation; and their attachment rests on chosen persistence rather than species sameness. Lisa lives a relation whose ethical logic is more open than the doctrine she later imposes on Lara.

## Captivity, spectacle, and the limits of trauma as law

Lisa's human hatred has a concrete history. Humans do not merely misunderstand her. They restrain her, strip her of control over her belongings, construct an eroticized monster narrative around her eyes and lower body, expose her for profit, and confine her in darkness. The showman converts a vulnerable body into evidence for a species myth: the mermaid is cursed, seductive, and dangerous by nature. This is public violence, not an abstract prejudice Lisa invents.

That history explains the emotional force of Lisa's E05 absolutism without proving its universal conclusion. A human boy rescues her from the same regime. Decades later, a human scientist spends his finite lifetime trying to preserve her, and Lisa lives under a human name inside a human scientific and educational institution. E06 therefore distinguishes warranted memory from valid general law. Lisa has excellent reasons to fear human institutions and still lacks grounds to make every mermaid-human future impossible.

Her 2026 role as a museum guide reverses the exhibition structure without erasing it. In captivity, humans explain the mermaid's body to other humans while she remains caged and voiceless. At the museum, Lisa becomes the authorized explainer of aquatic life to children. She controls the instructional frame, presents migration and return as knowledge, and keeps her own nonhuman body secret. The reversal grants public competence but depends on concealment. She can teach humans how fish return home while living indefinitely away from her own underwater home and waiting for Lara to restore it.

The biwa trout's return to its native river gives that contradiction a quiet formal shape. Lisa's public lesson naturalizes return as a life pattern. Her own project also seeks return, but unlike the fish's migration it requires another person's light and a political restoration whose desirability is not questioned. Homecoming is simultaneously biological explanation, private longing, and a demand directed at Lara.

## Rowan: sacrifice, dynastic restoration, and delegated waiting

E06 decisively revises the most charitable available reading of Rowan's E01 final act. He did give Lara his remaining light. He did save a daughter he continued to love. In 1931, however, he states why he wants her revived: Lara possesses the light needed to restore the castle, and he desires the restoration of the castle and the family's glory. Rescue is therefore both parental and dynastic. The two motives cannot be cleanly separated, and neither cancels the other.

Rowan treats princess light as political infrastructure. When the original castle fails, he explains the disaster through Lara's failed human relation and the absence of her productive light. Later he assigns Lisa to wait for Lara in his place. The chosen daughter's future labor will rebuild the state; the older daughter receives the centuries of custodial labor needed to secure that future. Rowan himself chooses sleep. Delegation preserves the king's project across his inactivity by distributing obligation through his daughters.

His response to Lisa's legs repeats the kingdom's beauty discipline at the moment she returns after capture and difficult travel. He calls the human body ugly before asking how she survived or what it cost to reach the family. Lisa's E05 shame about her legs is thus not only parallel to Lara's self-condemnation after the prince's rejection. It is directly ratified by the father whose project she serves.

The scene also makes Rowan's dependence on Grace undeniable. He despises the witch and her mansion, yet Grace built the refuge at his request, guides the displaced family there, preserves the site, supplies the transformations that let Lisa and Kōta survive on land, and determines when the humans must leave. The royal order cannot reproduce itself without the agent it declares vile. Grace mocks this dependence, but indispensability does not make her benevolent: she retains information, medicine, spatial authority, and possible observational access. Rowan's sovereignty and Grace's infrastructure constrain one another.

This evidence confirms the need for the owner-requested Rowan monograph. Rowan is not merely a backstory father or source of a single taboo. He organizes the series' central ideological conversion: love becomes light, light becomes dynastic resource, daughters become its producers and custodians, and protection becomes the language through which imposed futures are naturalized.

## Human knowledge without ethical openness

Lisa's collaboration with Horikoshi Seizō is the episode's most important complication of the E05 human-knowing thesis. Lara comes to know humans through ordinary participation, error, repair, conversation, and desire for further encounter. Lisa also enters human institutions and explicitly values a human epistemology of `仮定と検証`—hypothesis and verification. Yet she does so while continuing to call humans hateful and reliance on them horrifying.

Knowledge therefore does not automatically produce ethical openness. Lisa can recognize that a different knowledge system may do what mermaid taboo forbids, recruit Seizō's expertise, preserve his research, and inhabit its institutional descendants without revising her species judgment. Contact can remain instrumental. Evidence can be methodologically respected while persons remain morally generalized.

The episode does not make that collaboration worthless. Seizō cannot reproduce Grace's medicine exactly, but the unlike imitation delays dissolution. A human lifetime yields incomplete yet real survival. Lisa's dismissive account at the grave—humans decorate their short lives with exaggerated words, and he spent his life producing a fake—is undercut by the vial Kōta is drinking. Lisa and Kōta live long enough to continue searching because the “fake” works partially. Human limitation is neither omnipotent progress nor meaningless failure.

Seizō's language also places science inside the light motif without equating it with magic. He is captivated by the medicine's brilliant light, speaks of a star of life, and seeks an invisible source and essence. The research cannot see or reproduce the light fully. Its merit lies in continuing to ask, test, and preserve partial effects under uncertainty. This is almost the opposite of Lisa's species doctrine, which converts one historical catastrophe into a finished universal answer.

The century montage makes survival administrative as well as biochemical. Lisa becomes Shiomi Lisa, Kōta becomes Minase Kōta, research passes through institutions and public presentation, and by 2026 Lisa works at a museum. Their human lives are not disguises worn for a brief visit. They depend on names, records, occupations, laboratories, and medicines accumulated across generations. Lisa's commitment to a pure mermaid future is sustained by dense participation in the human world she rejects.

## Four uses of light

E06 pluralizes “light” so strongly that no single moral or magical definition can remain adequate.

1. **Relational recognition.** Kōta's lure finds worlds in darkness, but Lisa calls him her light because finding becomes reciprocal acknowledgment and continued presence.
2. **Dynastic resource.** Rowan wants the special princess's light to restore castle and family glory. Light is something another body must produce for the polity.
3. **Object of inquiry.** Seizō sees light in the medicine as an unknown property of life. It calls for hypothesis, verification, and questions whose answer may exceed one lifetime.
4. **Claim on another person.** Lisa asks why Lara will not illuminate “us.” Family desperation converts Lara's capacity into a debt whose delayed payment feels like abandonment.

These uses overlap without becoming identical. Kōta's light protects a relation by attending to a person in darkness. Rowan's light protects a polity by extracting capacity from a chosen princess. Scientific light sustains inquiry precisely because its nature is unknown. Lisa moves between the first and fourth: she knows what it means to be saved by someone's freely continued presence, yet speaks of Lara's light as something the family is owed.

That contrast sharpens rather than simplifies Lisa. She is not merely a hypocrite. A centuries-long survival crisis, a delegated duty, repeated bodily symptoms, and the sleeping family make her demand intelligible. The ethical problem is that intelligibility becomes entitlement. Lara's independent life, work, friendship, and uncertain process of knowing humans register to Lisa primarily as delayed illumination.

## Grace's medicine and the politics of dependency

Grace's potion has become an infrastructure no one else can fully reproduce. It permits cross-form embodiment, prolonged land survival, and perhaps access to otherwise impossible relations. Its scarcity also gives Grace power over time. She can tell Lisa and Kōta to return to the surface, dismiss questions because they will disappear when the medicine runs out, and rely on the fact that every alternative remains a degraded copy.

Lisa's response is to steal, recruit human science, and stockpile imitation. These acts show substantial agency and persistence. They do not free her from Grace's system. By 2026 the imitation is failing, bodily symptoms recur, and the search for the witch becomes urgent. The green-lit refrigerator filled with vials initially appears to represent a reserve; Lisa and Kōta's bodies reveal that quantity cannot replace lost efficacy.

The broken mirror turns dependency into discovery. A failed medicine container strikes the glass, and the fragments disclose Grace and the Otsu house. This formally links the exhausted copy to the original maker: the sign of pharmaceutical failure opens a visual route back to the witch. Kōta's question about continuous surveillance remains unanswered. What is established is that Grace's image/location can appear through the broken reflective surface and that Lisa recognizes enough to act on it.

## Lara, Mari, and involuntary disclosure

Lara is largely absent from the episode's central history, but Lisa interprets her absence as withheld rescue. When Lara's earlier light reaches the dormant site, Lisa sees her human legs and recalls their argument. `どうして私たちを照らしてくれない` transforms uncertainty about Lara's process into a moral question: why is the special princess not already serving the family? The sentence is the sibling-level version of Rowan's dynastic logic.

The post-credit sequence returns from Lisa's concealed century to Lara's present friendship. Mari offers an ordinary sibling analogy: she once stopped speaking to her brother for a month after a fight. Lara begins to tell her about Lisa. The comparison neither trivializes two centuries of royal crisis nor resolves it; it gives Lara a peer context in which sister conflict can be narrated rather than obeyed as destiny.

The body then speaks before the explanation can proceed. Lara's red mermaid tail becomes visible on a public bridge, and Mari physically steadies her. This is the strongest involuntary disclosure yet: the secret body is no longer confined to Grace, the household's private rescue, or Lara's own controlled account. An unidentified blond passerby appears to observe the event. Static evidence does not establish the person's identity, the ownership or function of the fish-emblem phone, or whether the phone, drifting foam-like material, failing potion, stress, or another mechanism triggers the change.

What is clear is Mari's immediate position. She is beside Lara when the concealed body appears and supports rather than abandons her. No romantic classification follows. The relation gains another kind of evidence: friendship is present at the instant Lara's socially dangerous form can no longer be kept private.

## Episode-level synthesis

E06 makes Lisa's doctrine legible by reconstructing the life that produced it. She endured human capture and spectacle, crossed forms, found her family only to receive delegated custodial duty, relied on Grace's finite medicine, recruited a human scientist, and maintained survival across nearly a century while waiting for Lara. Her control is not groundless malice. It is a survival project built from trauma, duty, love, technical adaptation, and time.

The episode simultaneously shows why that project cannot be treated as sufficient authority over Lara. Lisa's own life contradicts closed species categories: a transformed nonhuman companion becomes her chosen light; a human rescues her; another human devotes a lifetime to medicine that materially prolongs hers; human institutions become her operating environment. None of those facts requires Lisa to love humanity, but together they prevent her hatred from functioning as neutral knowledge.

Rowan supplies the political center. He saved Lara and wants the productive light only she can provide; he loves his daughters and assigns their lives to restoration; he condemns Grace while depending on her refuge and medicine; he sleeps while Lisa waits. His project converts familial care into dynastic labor. Lisa inherits that logic and gives it the urgency of someone whose body is now failing.

Against that inheritance, the episode offers two alternative epistemologies. Kōta and Lisa's best relation begins when each finds the other and refuses disposability. Seizō's best work begins from an unknown light and accepts incomplete verification across finite time. Both are practices of remaining answerable to what is not already mastered. Lara's E05 desire to know humans belongs nearer to these open practices than to the royal demand for a predetermined result.
