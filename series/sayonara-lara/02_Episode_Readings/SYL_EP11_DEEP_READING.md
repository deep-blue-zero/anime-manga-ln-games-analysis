## The dream before the ban

The episode relocates the landward impulse from Lara's exceptional love to an older royal family quarrel. Grace and Rigmor first share a dream of seeing human freedom; Grace is curious about legs, experiments and knowledge. A royal elder demands princess-light for an already dimming castle and treats her exploratory practice as transgression. Young Rowan initially defends her thoughtfulness, then offers strategic praise because her search might serve the castle. Grace rejects this capture: she feels light in the land dream *for herself*. This matters for Rowan's monograph. The E10 father who calls human legs ugly once helped defend their imagined possibility; the later ban is historical reorientation after grief, not a timeless species axiom.

Grace obtains a dangerous transformative medicine from a forbidden area and asks Rigmor to come. Rigmor has fallen in love with Rowan, decides to remain, and insists Grace's dream remains beautiful. Grace hears abandonment and makes it a solitary quest. The sisterly split does not make either woman's love false: Rigmor's romantic choice and Grace's exploratory desire are different goods under a dynastic system demanding a productive light. Rigmor/Rowan produce a brilliant castle-filling light and daughters, which gives the court its favored example of “true love.” It does not establish that every princess must repeat that pair, that all land loves fail, or that blood sacrifice is a cure.

Rowan's later account says humans killed Rigmor and that the mermaids remember what short-lived humans forget. The mirror narrative shows a human beloved rejecting Grace's mermaid form and ordering violence; a blade erupting in Grace's catastrophe; Rigmor reaching her through ring/light; and Grace's own later declaration that she killed the beloved with tears. The episode thus supplies strong evidence of species violence and Grace's involvement, while complicating Rowan's single-cause indictment. Without continuous AV, the exact physical wound, order of blow, and causal separation among blade, soldiers, and Rigmor's intervention remain bounded. Rowan's decision to ban humans and exile Grace is explicitly his political response, not the sea world's independent moral truth.

## Two coercive interpretations of Lara's light

Rowan uses a mirror that he says tests its viewer's heart to force Lara through this history. A magical projection is powerful evidence that the story stages a past, but its selection and his accompanying speech are not neutral. He wants Lara to see Grace as corruptor and to accept his E10 demand to cleanse the blade with a beloved's blood. Yet the history gives Lara a rival: Rigmor says light can exist anywhere if one can live for love. Grace sees in Lara's earlier foaming not a failed princess, but a person who rejected the blade's demand for recompense at the cost of her own life. This revises Rowan's assertion that the blade's hunger is Lara's fixed true desire or that injury is inevitable.

Grace's own reading is not pure emancipation. She has her attendants gather all Lara's foam, uses Rowan's entire light to revive her, and spends immense magic to suspend the family. Rowan's willingness to give the light remains a real paternal sacrifice, but he also imagines Lara as anti-human weapon. Grace sleeps the court partly to shield a future Lara from that plan, while taking away others' present agency and putting herself in a depleted fish vessel. Her second-birth language in E09 now has literal and existential force: she preserves Lara's existence so Lara might live otherwise. But Grace's hope that Lara realize *her* unfinished dream burdens the child with another inherited purpose. Her human-romance route and silence about mortality are therefore ethically examinable, even where her opposition to Rowan is justified.

The mirror's self-implication is especially important. Grace's longing for the land was affirmed by Rigmor; she herself was rejected as monstrous by a human and has lost light after the death. Rowan and Grace each want Lara to resolve a crisis their generation made, though they propose opposite destinations and injury. Lisa's sisterly protection in E10 repeats Rowan's language of a “soiled” heart, despite surviving through human help. E12 must ask what Lara actually chooses after seeing rather than simply let either elder's theory win by inheritance.

## Mari's ordinary refusal and Grace's renewed sight

The shore encounter is a flashback to Mari's childhood, not the next step of the E10 boat mission. Young Mari finds the weakened fish, hears its wish for a quiet end, and refuses to leave it. Grace subsequently says she sees beautiful light. The precise supernatural source is not proved, but Mari's visible care gives an ethical counterexample to Rowan's universal human condemnation and to Grace's earlier despair that no light could exist on land. It also explains why the otherwise imposing witch became Gon in this particular household.

The closing instruction `掟ではなく 愛のために生きろ` (“live for love, not law”) rebukes Rowan's prohibition, but does not define love as a single romance or absolve Grace's coercion. Its speaker and precise address are not confidently recoverable from the aligned text alone. The historical speech and closing larger-form image cannot establish survival after E10. The episode ends at an unresolved present conflict, not a completed cure.
