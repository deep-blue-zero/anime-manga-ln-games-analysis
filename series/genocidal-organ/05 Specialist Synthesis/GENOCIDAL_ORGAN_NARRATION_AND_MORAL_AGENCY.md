# Narration and the audience of moral agency

Clavis Shepherd repeatedly seeks someone who can tell him what his acts mean. His mother supplies a reassuring frame in the dreams; the doctor can explain injury but cannot decide what remains of her experience; Lucia offers an account of chosen responsibility; the counselor assures him that his murderous will is his. The epilogue makes Clavis himself an author addressing a public whose response he intends to alter. This sequence supports a formal argument beyond a general description of unreliable narration: **the novel distinguishes having another person recognize one's agency from acquiring authority over that person's judgment.** Clavis's need for an answering voice can sustain an ethical relation, but it can also recruit other people into a story whose terms he controls. His final claim to own the burden coincides with a violently unequal distribution of the power to answer. This is the bounded G3 argument registered as GO-CL089; its inherited distinctions remain in [the current claims owner](../03%20Longitudinal%20Ledgers/GENOCIDAL_ORGAN_CLAIMS_PREDICTIONS_AND_REVISIONS_LEDGER.md#go-g2-current-arguments).

## Recognition precedes an explanatory position

The opening makes readers recognize damaged bodies before it gives them a responsible agent or a secure frame of experience. Apparent movement gives way to the dead girl; possible speech is assigned to the boy whose words cannot be recovered. The mother then directs Clavis's looking and corrects his classification of the gathering. He recognizes his own decay through her instruction. This is not simply withheld information awaiting a thriller's solution. The reader has already entered an affective relation to people whose histories and last words remain missing. The later dream/waking frame explains how these images belong together without retrospectively making their different deaths equivalent. [GO-U001-E003–011; GO-U002-E006–009; CL001–006.]

The mother's authority is particularly strong because the philosophical claim arrives in an intimate familiar manner. In the later dream, being flesh is offered as release rather than imprisonment, and Clavis accepts the reassurance because this is how his mother used to correct him. The narration subsequently explains that domestic form. A reader can understand why the proposition reassures him without treating a dream-mother as an independently speaking philosopher or the novel's unqualified metaphysical authority. The remembered living mother also permits his enlistment: her authority cannot be reduced to a continuous coercive prohibition. [GO-U007-E001–007; CL028/040.]

This distinction between an utterance's truth and its relational force becomes indispensable later. A sentence can change Clavis's feeling of responsibility without proving its speaker's access to his motives. The dream's reassurance, Lucia's comfort and clinical reassurance differ in speaker, evidence and purpose. Their common efficacy does not collapse them into one form of manipulation. What recurs is Clavis's susceptibility to a recognized voice that can give his experience a workable shape.

## The narrator's corrections invite trust without giving omniscience

Clavis often corrects himself in front of the reader. He calls the easy account of his father's death a lie, admits that Williams and Leland knew an Alex he did not, and revises his reading of the doctor's hesitation. These corrections are actual evidence of reflective work. An interpretation that treats every assertion as a conscious deception cannot explain why the narrative preserves these costly admissions. Conversely, the act of correcting an interpretation does not guarantee that its replacement has become transparent truth. His later certainty about Alex's suicide still lacks Alex's unavailable answer. [GO-U005-E003–006/E012–013; GO-U014-E004; GO-U015-E005; CL020–021/049.]

The distinction is temporal as well as epistemic. The maternal episode disclosed after Clavis's intention to confess gives readers the content of a difficult past; its immediate representation does not itself establish a spoken telling to Lucia. Later evidence can clarify a conversation, but the frozen immediate forecast remains underdetermined at its original horizon. Similarly, the later account of hearings establishes public testimony without making every earlier chapter a verbatim congressional transcript. Retrospective narration has an occasion and an intention, but neither identity of addressee nor identity of transcript follows automatically. [GO-U013-E004; GO-U014 opening; GO-U028-E002/E004; PR004/CL044/087.]

This disciplined uncertainty matters to the literary argument. If every eloquent passage were accepted as a direct record of an actual conversation, Clavis would acquire recipients he may only be imagining. If the epilogue were allowed to turn every earlier omission into a proved calculated lie, it would erase the scene-bound anxiety and revision the text actually represents. The account's performance context reweights trust; it does not supply a universal decoding key.

## A voice can be effective without being sovereign

The early maternal assent, **はい**, has lethal efficacy through authorization, thumb authentication and withdrawal of support. It is not an autonomous verbal spell. Clavis's synthetic military voice is likewise effective although software reconstructs an audible utterance that did not physically exist in that fluent form. Agency is exercised through a channel and an institution. Neither mediation makes the act unreal. [GO-U002-E001–002/E008/E013; CL005/008.]

His professional self-account makes this more disturbing by preserving the target's individuality. **想像してから、殺す** compresses a trained sequence: vivid person-imagination is useful to successful killing. A narrator who knows how to build a plausible person-image cannot be assumed to regard that image's subject as someone whose judgment can interrupt the operation. This is the sharp limit of equating narrative empathy with ethical restraint. The target's humanity can be recognized and operationally used. [GO-U002-E004–005; GO-U005-E010; CL009/022.]

The stronger countercase to a wholly instrumental Clavis is not absent. He changes his plan when Lucia demands arrest and public judgment. Another person's expressed requirement actually interrupts his declared intention to kill John. The reader need not infer the whole of Lucia's mind to see that her words make a practical difference. This is a relation in which an answer changes what he will do, rather than merely confirms what he wishes to feel. [GO-U025-E004; GO-U027-E004–007; CL010/084.]

## Wanting guilt creates a dependence on witnesses

The counseling scene makes the ownership problem precise. **罪から逃れたいのではない** rejects the claim that Clavis simply wants an excuse. He fears that the self has been diluted too far to own its guilt, and therefore that the felt intensity of survival is unreal. His isolated wish, **お前が殺したのだ、と誰かに言ってほしい**, seeks an answer from someone other than the counselor. The Japanese locates the desire in an addressed attribution: someone must say that *he* killed. [GO-U019-E011–012; CL028/059/068.]

His demand is serious, but it is not sufficient evidence of an ethical improvement. A witness may become valuable principally as a guarantor of the speaker's own reality. Clavis wants Lucia's punishment or forgiveness; Lucia eventually demands a public process in which the protected population must know and choose. The difference is not between false feeling and true politics. It is between a desired verdict on his self and a proposed redistribution of responsibility beyond their intimate relation. Her request exceeds the role he had reserved for her. [GO-U023-E005; GO-U024-E008; GO-U027-E004/E009–010; CL054/059/084.]

Nor does bodily intervention settle this dispute by removing every chooser. The needle scene verifies a narrower separation between registering injury and feeling painfulness. Fear, grief and tactical discretion remain elsewhere. The narration makes an altered body a condition of Clavis's choices while refusing an externally inspectable point from which he could certify all their origins. The literal distinction between **痛いことがわかった** and **痛さは、感じなかった** is stronger evidence than a general assertion that he has no feelings. [GO-U019-E013; GO-U021-E006–008; GO-U022-E014–017; CL009/061/070.]

## The final audience receives a story and loses an ordinary reply

The epilogue changes the narrative stakes through a conjunction, not a wholesale reversal. Clavis tells classified truths, exposes a scandal and risks prosecution. He also deliberately composes with John's editor, performs his manuscript as a score, and wishes Americans to kill one another. Public disclosure is consequently both an actual breach of secrecy and the means of intended coercion. The story's truth-bearing capacity does not make its circulation innocent. [GO-U028-E004/E007–009; CL050/084/087–088.]

Sound and script keep this from becoming a simplistic theory of the spoken word. Clavis emphasizes rhythm and singing, but his language is also transcribed and circulated through the information fabric. The **まぶたのない耳** image reverses the repeated possibility of closing one's eyes: the telling seeks an audience it can reach across channels, rather than a conversation whose recipients knowingly accept its terms. The text favors an operative fictional instrument while withholding the algorithm and exact medium-specific parameters. It does not establish that the Japanese novel in the reader's hands literally contains the weapon, or that every earlier paragraph is the encoded English manuscript. Those attractive metaleptic extensions lack the required identity proof. [GO-U028-E007–008; CL051–052/064/087.]

This final condition gives the opening's missing voices a new significance without solving them. The dead boy's unavailable words, the mother's unknown wish and Alex's unrecovered motive had limited what Clavis could claim on others' behalf. The final telling gives him unprecedented power to act on living listeners while preserving a similar asymmetry of access: his own burden is narrated at length, their independent responses are largely replaced by a national causal account. The argument is not that all narration silences people. The novel has already shown Lucia answering and changing action. It is that first-person ownership becomes morally dangerous when it treats a narrated intention as authority to decide other people's exposure to harm.

## Counterreading and remaining limits

The strongest alternative emphasizes the testimony's public value and Clavis's acquired willingness to bear guilt. Both are positively supported. The chosen death-world for America may be read as a destructive repudiation of the privilege that had allowed external war to remain background. Yet that reading cannot absorb the personal stockpile, the rifle killing and the final comfort into an uncomplicated sacrifice. He distributes physical costs to compatriots while claiming the decision's moral burden for himself. [GO-U028-E008–009.]

The opposite alternative makes his remorse a pure fraud and every self-correction a device for flattering the audience. That is less well supported. The maternal breakdown, the anxiety about genuine guilt and the selective grief for Lucia remain represented experiences. Their reality is compatible with coercion and self-serving judgment. A stronger account therefore retains his anguish while withholding its claim to authorize the destruction.
