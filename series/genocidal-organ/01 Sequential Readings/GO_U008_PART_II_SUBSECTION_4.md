## Many names, one building, a particular teacher

The Kafka reference moves from the preceding friends’ argument into the opening sentence about Gregor Samsa, immediately followed by the reminder that Kafka wrote in German. The narrator’s account of Czech political and linguistic history then arrives at small practical problems: several names for one building, an older person speaking through several languages, a visitor trying to hear the difference. The separate short paragraphs about building and speaker give those examples emphasis before the narrative settles into a room and conversation (E001).

This history belongs to the narrator’s exposition. Its literary function does not depend on treating the account as an independently verified survey. It establishes a distinction that the later discussion needs: languages demonstrably organize labels, social access and practical difficulty, while the stronger proposition that each language encloses an entire reality will be contested. One object with several names provides a concrete starting point rather than a proof of that later theory.

ルツィア offers tea and explains Czech’s difficult inflections, then qualifies the striking example of more than two hundred forms: word order and pronunciation are the more general difficulties for her students. Her authority is specific and conversational, capable of correcting the narrator’s amused extrapolation. She stresses face-to-face instruction; the narrator adds that even his technologically simulated military training retains actual language classes. Teaching is also her livelihood, conducted in her home. Communication has material conditions: a room, an interlocutor and bodily practice, alongside the words being explained (E002).

## Cover, curiosity and the wish to keep her smiling

The narrator compliments her English. Her answer names its hegemony, but his usual irritation at criticism of American power does not arrive. He counters with a claim about Japanese web diaries. The source explicitly identifies his occupation as a cover, and his subsequent question about her diary habit as an attempt to reach her past. His description of advertising turns the cover into an image of insertion: commercial material occupies the gaps of otherwise harmonious sites and films. Her answer instead imagines the web filled with words about human lives. The same network is available as advertising space, traffic statistic and narrated experience (E003).

The source does not independently certify his traffic claim; a cover conversation can use true information as well as deception. The deliberate falsehood we can identify is his assumed professional identity, followed by his staged surprise about her education. He admits he already knew about Massachusetts and cannot confidently judge whether he concealed that knowledge. That self-doubt is more diagnostic than a general claim of perfect operational skill (E004–05).

Her explanation of studying language’s structural “bones,” rather than its attractive “flesh,” is carried in the Japanese through the grammar/glamour play and ruby. His response joins the joke, and she claims an unusual pleasure in Chomsky’s work. The narrator interprets her recurring smile as sincere enjoyment of communication. Enjoyment is his reading of her expression, supported by the exchange and her later less formal voice; it is not independent access to her mind. Her expertise can be a genuine source of pleasure without establishing trust in the stranger (E004).

The conversation pauses when he asks about her research. He distinguishes the observable pause from a possible suspicion he assigns to her: she may wonder why he wants to know. She answers cautiously that she studied how language affects human behavior. When her smile returns, he says his relief comes from not wanting to see her frown, rather than fearing exposure. Intelligence elicitation and attraction are thus simultaneously present. His avowed personal response need not erase operational concern elsewhere; it identifies what he recognizes as this moment’s reason (E005; GO-CL031).

The strongest competing reading is ordinary professional hospitality meeting an interested customer. The passage gives that reading substantial support: she teaches for a living, answers linguistic questions and has no disclosed knowledge of his assignment. What exceeds an exclusively commercial scene is clearest on his side—his explicit self-correction about relief, his admiration and his later discovery that their speech has become collegial. Her corresponding private intention remains less accessible.

## A language account that makes distinctions

His first interpretation of her research is a familiar claim about language forming reality, illustrated by the expanding number of Inuit snow terms. She explicitly rejects that identification. Her story traces inflation through repeated transmission; the narrator responds by imagining fashionable conversationalists acting as carriers of the anecdote. In the same scene, information becomes an object whose circulation can alter it. Her historical and numerical account is represented expertise, not an external bibliography supplied by this reading (E006).

Her explicit formulation is `思考は言語に先行する`—thought precedes language, in my gloss (N53/L50). Her central distinction is between thinking and one of its objects: language is something thought handles, rather than a larger frame enclosing thought. The narrator’s appeal to thinking in English does not settle the question, and she answers it directly. Her beaver/teeth analogy challenges the inference that possessing an evolved capacity means thinking through that organ as an exclusive medium. The scientist-image example then supports her claim that thought can precede its symbolic output. When he cannot imagine visualizing infinity, she allows differences between people’s realities while attributing them to thought rather than language (E006–07; GO-CL032).

The narrator is receptive for a reason already available in his history: he experiences words as definite things outside himself, making the idea of a linguistic filter attractive yet alien to his own felt experience. This extends GO-CL015’s earlier language-object account. The conversation does not turn that phenomenology into a scientific demonstration. Rather, a specialist’s explanation gives him a framework that seems to fit it. Her broader research question about language influencing behavior and her rejection of language determining reality are also distinct propositions; rejecting the latter need not reject every possible effect of speech (E005–07).

## An organ that speaks, and a listener who remembers

Asked what language is, she begins with a communication tool and corrects herself to `器官`—organ, in my gloss. His question explicitly tests the bodily comparison against kidneys, intestines, arms and eyes; her assent makes the category materially consequential. Meanwhile he notices her familiar, colleague-like register. The theory is offered through a conversation that is itself changing their relationship (E008).

Her question about abstraction arising from material existence leads to a remark about soul and brain, then a late inquiry about his religion. His mind returns to Alex pointing toward his temple and locating hell there. The memory supplies a meaningful complication: Alex’s belief in God coexisted with that bodily location of hell. The narrator answers for himself that he is not religious. His recollection does not supply a new confession from Alex or explain Alex’s later death (E009).

Their joke about her asking too late repairs the possible offense. The exchange preserves ease through a topic that could divide them, while her acknowledgment of often being too late adds self-deprecation rather than an exposed biography. Her earlier flirtatious joke and current warmth support an engaging voice; they do not establish a particular romantic intention toward him.

She next explains language as an adaptation enabling individuals to exchange predicted information, alongside a self/other distinction needed for comparison. The narrator takes up the account: the brain function allowing this very conversation joins the nose of an elephant and neck of a giraffe. His reflection on the still-unmatched filtering of internal organs counters the isolation of language as a uniquely divine mystery. The passage asserts these ideas through speaker and listener; no experiment or independent scientific authority is added by this analysis (E010–11).

This is also a precise extension of the preceding subsection’s flesh conversation. Matter had been distinguished from imprisonment; now language and selfhood are considered parts of the narrator’s own flesh. His earlier sense of words as external objects and this account of an internal capacity need not cancel one another: object of thought and bodily capacity to communicate are different levels. GO-CL028v2 keeps their connection while retaining that distinction.

## Adaptation leaves a question open

The narrator tests the account’s human exclusivity: would assuming every evolved being must have language be human arrogance? Her hypothetical crow civilization answers by making a sharp beak its imagined universal. The analogy dislodges the human norm without denying the particular usefulness of human language (E012).

His final thought then changes the direction of the bodily analogy. Even an adapted organ can be imagined as contributing to its bearer’s extinction. The saber-toothed tiger example belongs to his private reasoning; its scientific validity is not established here. The formal turn is nevertheless clear: **being an organ does not, for him, settle whether a capacity is safe**. He understands the rejection of linguistic determinism yet keeps a question about dangerous consequences. The contrast is with uncomplicated confidence in adaptation, not a demonstrated present mechanism of destruction. This objection is internal, after her last spoken reply, and she has not heard or answered it before the boundary (E012; GO-CL032).
