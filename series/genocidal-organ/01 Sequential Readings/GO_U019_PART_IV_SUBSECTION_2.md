# GO-U019 — wanting the guilt to be one's own

クラヴィス does not ask to be relieved of responsibility. He fears that the military's adjustment of his feelings might make him insufficiently present to own it. The counseling room develops this fear through the same distinction that troubled his decision about his mother: registering something and feeling it need not coincide. Before departure for India he tests that distinction on his finger. The subsection ends with pain known and pain unfelt, providing a concrete sensory result while leaving the ownership of murderous intention unresolved.

## The question interrupts the aim

The isolated **この殺意は、自分自身の殺意だろうか。** precedes an optical-sight sequence in which one figure falls and another takes its place (E001). Fragmented anatomy and small, cheap lives turn repeated shots into a perception of expendability. Yet killing is said to matter as a means to a mission, rather than the mission's end in itself. Tactical instrumentality and the narrator's question about his own agency occupy the same opening.

That opening does not explicitly locate or date a new India firefight. The account moves into the final day of counseling during preparations; its ending is the day before departure. Recalled, imagined or generalized combat remains a material range of readings for the initial vignette. Its second occurrence of the question about **自分自身** is an inquiry into who owns the represented readiness to kill, not evidence that the current mission has already occurred.

The counselor answers with assurance that the will is his (E002). The contrast is immediately unequal: categorical professional reassurance meets a question whose conditions the patient cannot independently inspect. The practiced smile and the narrator's expectations about psychology do not by themselves establish fraud. They do establish his sense that an existential question is receiving a familiar clinical answer.

## A prior choice becomes the justification for adjustment

The final counseling day gives the procedure its operational name, **戦闘適応感情調整** (E002). The account combines localized frontal masking, drug delivery and dialogue. It is routine before missions, and the narrator describes cumulative unease after repeated treatment. The current young Jewish clinical psychologist is unnamed; shared profession and religious identification do not establish that he is Williams's previously reported counselor.

The counselor argues that hesitation belongs to an adolescent style of self-examination while the actual world proceeds (E003). His cold-treatment analogy makes the client a prior chooser: one chooses a hospital and tools, while the body does the healing. Likewise, coming to treatment follows the choice to fight. クラヴィス accepts that he chose war. His objection remains whether choosing combat also settles the authenticity of wanting its associated trauma removed.

The phrase **倫理的ノイズ** recasts an impediment as interference with appropriate performance. The text explicitly identifies the “filter” as a metaphor, then describes an interaction between masking selected functions and military psychological dialogue. Reading a literal installed ethical filter would erase that qualification. Equally, treating the dialogue as merely ornamental would ignore its stated role in the procedure.

## A distributed brain does not settle a responsible person

The narrator's exposition presents emotion as a fast route through value judgments that reason cannot calculate in time (E004). The counselor offers modules and judgment libraries rather than a single internal commander. Conscience is among the capacities explained through distributed neural sites; the witness's literal **扁頭体** is retained rather than silently corrected to a different Japanese spelling.

These are confidently stated explanatory accounts within the fiction. The text is not an independent validation of real neuroscience or a demonstration that every claimed module can be precisely controlled. The counselor expressly limits the resolution of present mapping. That limit matters next to the narrator's knowledge: he pretends unfamiliarity with an account of the brain he has already heard. His compliance in conversation therefore does not prove intellectual trust or that the counselor knows everything he is thinking.

Pain masking gives their discussion a familiar military example. Keeping the **知覚** or recognition of pain while suppressing its felt **感覚** separates two functions that ordinary speech often joins. DARPA's development and the larger possibility of changing conduct or personality are reported in that exposition. The current lack of finely adjustable mapping prevents possibility from becoming demonstrated unrestricted control.

## The wrong binary returns from the hospital

The explanation recalls the summer when he chose his mother's death (E005). Brain slices appear as marble-like patterns on a wall. He repeats the question whether she has consciousness, later recognizing that he was asking a question whose terms did not adequately fit what the doctor was trying to explain. Even his present understanding remains qualified.

The physician asks about religion and proceeds with the explanation despite the reply that he has none. The old sleep-based division between consciousness present and absent is rejected. クラヴィス supplies fainting and sleep as examples, but leaves “and death” unspoken. That omission is a formal sign of the pressure on the conversation; it is not a spoken admission to the physician.

The doctor's account describes **五七二** modules and experiments in which a person denies visual experience while responding to a thrown ball (E006). These are experiments recounted in the medical explanation, not a newly witnessed trial in this room. The distinction between having information and possessing its felt quality gives the battlefield treatment a disquieting relation to bedside judgment.

Roughly twenty stages between sleep and wakefulness, variable module activity, drugs and memory complicate any uniformly present “I” (E007). The crowd comparison develops the physician's account through the narrator's language: ten thousand, a thousand or a smaller group can be called a crowd until the threshold becomes disputable. It should not be converted into an invented direct quotation by the physician.

The medical scene reaches a social definition that has not been settled: how much active organization counts as consciousness or as the person. The mother's partial loss does not yield a stated threshold at which she stops being sufficiently his mother. Faced with a decision, he cannot produce that threshold himself. This extends the earlier withdrawal narrative's uncertainty; it neither supplies her wishes nor proves that her remaining experience was absent. The physician explains a difficulty without being shown to order withdrawal.

## The ethical distinction rests on an intention he cannot certify

Back in the present, emotional masking becomes an intentional dilution of the “I” (E008). The conscience is treated as an emotional component rather than reason's sovereign opposition to feeling. The counselor's disaster and immediate-injury examples explain stronger responses to proximity; the narrator tests the implication with a child whose skull is broken by emotion's hammer. The clinician answers with an ordinary nod. That undramatic reaction gives his explanation professional continuity precisely where the narrator makes it sound grotesque.

The account does not simply oppose bad emotion to good reason. Conscience itself is included among the responses to be temporarily reduced for survival. The counselor's cultural account of American soldiers remains his stated rationale, rather than an analyst's universal psychological law.

Asked whether the procedure is brainwashing, he denies it and turns to the drug–poison analogy (E009). Intention distinguishes permissible use from abuse. クラヴィス internally questions whether an intention to make killing easier and reduce ethical hesitation is itself ethical. He cannot give a decisive answer. Neither the clinician's analogy nor his patient's unease establishes that every manipulation is identical, or that this one is unquestionably justified. The ethical criterion is especially unstable because the procedure is partly about the conditions under which a person forms judgments.

## Expensive lives are prepared to destroy cheap ones

Some comrades call counseling unnecessary, but the source does not identify these anonymous speakers as Williams or Leland (E010). The narrator concedes that care can show the army's concern for its people. He also explains it economically: salaries, training and technology make the human soldier a costly component. Artificial alternatives have disappointed; the living brain's useful redundancy resists economical reproduction.

That account includes claims about public tolerance for casualties, military AI, private military recruitment and law. They belong to his institutional exposition, without independent documents or external verification here. Its literary relation to the opening is clear: children are cheaply expended at one end of the sight, while the protected professional is maintained at substantial cost at the other.

The contrast with earlier trauma care is explicit. The present procedure is anticipatory, preparing future killing, rather than simply repairing damage after it. The counselor's vaccination image names war as a hazardous country. The projected objection about not needing an injection remains inside that analogy; it does not establish that a vaccine was administered.

## He wants to retain the qualification for guilt

The narrator strongly identifies a misunderstanding: the counselor has placed him with tougher colleagues who believe themselves already immune (E011). He says he is more fragile. His anxiety reverses a familiar desire for exoneration: **罪から逃れたいのではない**. He fears lacking the qualification to bear the guilt because the self that incurred it has been thinned. Calling this only an attempt to escape blame would contradict the passage's grammar and subsequent wish.

He also gives a forceful account of why he clings to war. Near death, he feels his own continued life with exceptional intensity. He accepts the possible labels of thrill or adrenaline dependence and states that he prioritizes his survival over others' lives. This is a substantial first-person motive avowal, not a tentative inference that should be dissolved into endless caveats. It remains his explanation of himself rather than an independent exhaustive clinical diagnosis.

His conditional reasoning is more uncertain. If his murderous will belongs to the counselor and chemicals, then his intense experience of living may also be false. The existence of counseling threatens him even before any particular method or content is shown responsible. He can hide his motive from himself under military patriotism and comradeship, but losing his own culpability would take away the reality he wants to experience.

This deepens the earlier relief through acknowledged responsibility and the later desire for punishment without retroactively rewriting those states. The apparent paradox is coherent: guilt is painful, yet owning it can make an act and its actor feel real.

## A wish for testimony meets a limit on self-verification

**お前が殺したのだ、と誰かに言ってほしい。** stands alone (E012). He wants someone other than this counselor to confirm that the guilt and will are his. The request seeks an external witness to authenticity, even though outside language is also one route by which his conduct may be affected.

The sheet on his head is said to monitor aspects of brain activity. The narrator expressly denies that exact thoughts can yet be read, despite the module count. The following account of software constructing a psychological model and supplying advice carries **ことだろう**: it is an inference, accompanied by actually noticed adjustments of an ear cuff. The source does not disclose the software's real model, the clinician's precise access or a successful reading of the patient's secret question.

Repeated **ぼくは知らない** marks specific missing knowledge: how the questions function, how answers are judged and what past words have done to emotion or reason. Language can work outside what he consciously registers, but the impossibility of certifying his own will does not prove that all of it is externally manufactured.

The counselor asks whether he now seems able to kill children. The narrator honestly replies **殺せると思う**. Both the disturbing readiness and **と思う** matter. It is an answer given during preparation, not a completed new killing or a claim to certainty about every future action.

## The finger supplies a narrower, firmer result

The ending explicitly moves to the day before leaving for India, after adjustments, drugs, coordination, training, simulations and planning are finished (E013). The procedure is now narrated as performed. At a mirror he sticks a needle into his finger:

> 痛かった。痛いことがわかった。
>
> なのに、痛さは、感じなかった。

The transition from the event and its recognition to **なのに** and the comma-separated denial makes the conceptual distinction a bodily test. He does not report that nothing registered. He knows that it hurt, yet lacks the felt painfulness.

This is strong local evidence for the depicted sensory separation. It is not an independent assay of every emotional module, a demonstration that his murderous will belongs to someone else, or a resolution of responsibility. Its precision keeps the final uncertainty consequential: a concrete manipulation can be experienced without the affected person thereby gaining an external standpoint on the whole self.
