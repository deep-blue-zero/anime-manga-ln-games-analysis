## Deep reading

### Meaning, design and the reality attached to words

The commandeered truck joins two forms of consumption. Its Japanese commercial writing has survived transfer into a mobile gun platform; the Americans have meanwhile discarded fashionable high technology in order to look plausible in stolen, dirtied clothes. The narrator includes himself in the childish pleasure of military equipment. His irony therefore has an implicated speaker, rather than an untouched observer looking down on local improvisation (E001–02).

Alex initially admires the unread writing as design. The narrator’s correction is precise: the meaning has not vanished; these observers cannot retrieve it. The distinction makes opacity relational. He then recalls games, his mother’s description of his linguistic attachment and a felt world in which words constrain people as concrete entities. This gives his sensitivity to language a personal history and an asserted phenomenology. His analogies to mathematicians and Einstein explain how he wants that sensation understood; they do not independently establish a cognitive theory (E002–03; GO-CL015).

The later mosque patterns and the beautiful piano music return this aestheticizing attention inside the assassination. Narrator and reader move through an intelligible operational plan while cultural forms remain attractive partly through not being understood. A plausible alternative is simply atmospheric characterization: the details individualize an educated, self-conscious soldier. The recurrence of his earlier explanation of cultural coolness during the actual approach makes a further connection persuasive. His way of seeing can turn an opaque world into design even while bodies and orders give that world severe material consequences (E012).

### Epistemic limitation becomes a chosen division of labour

The narrator says he can feel words as real while nations and peoples fail to become vivid lived realities for him. He imagines other men making those abstractions real and issuing orders from American institutions. This is his account of his limitation and of the division of labour that lets him kill for a nation he cannot vividly imagine (E004).

His excursus on history is consequently active argument, not neutral background. He labels the denial stories he lists nonsense, yet uses their circulation to argue for history’s essential nonexistence, before revising the familiar victor’s-history formula into competition among subjective accounts. The distinction matters: he is describing a struggle over historical narration, while his stronger ontological conclusion exceeds what the existence of competing stories alone establishes. His self-positioning as a CNN-and-pizza consumer gives that doubt a specific protected vantage. The repeated constancy of pizza against changing wars makes his distance comic and incriminating at once (E005).

The apparently grateful line about others issuing orders is punctured by his next admission. He too hands over **判断する自由** (analyst’s gloss: “the freedom to judge”) to others. This directly strengthens GO-CL010: his earlier criticism of Williams does not make him exempt from the same practical delegation. The strongest material counterreading is that this is candid recognition of unavoidable institutional specialization. The text supports that constraint; no individual can reconstruct the entire conflict before acting. But specialization does not exhaust this speaker’s formulation. He names judgment an inconvenient burden, recognizes surrendering it, and continues toward a chosen killing. That affirmative self-implication is stronger than either a portrait of pure obedience or of critical thought as resistance (GO-RV007).

### Recognition through a stolen body; coercion through registration

GO-PR002 receives an actual outcome. At the checkpoint, swallowed tags return their previous holders’ data and the guard admits the team. The preferred identity-for-passage result is instantiated, rather than merely left possible. Gel wrapping and swallowing were already observed in GO-U003. This subsection explains the readout, stomach retention and string retrieval; the forecast claims only identity-for-passage, not these newly disclosed readout/retrieval specifics (E007,E011).

The narrator mocks a system that treats received data as sufficient identity. His comparison with American biometric pizza delivery also shows how deeply authentication inhabits ordinary consumption in his own society. The following account of boys being tagged develops a harsher inversion: registration makes them administratively visible precisely by incorporating them into a killing force. The commercial goods comparison calls that movement **昇格** (analyst’s gloss: “promotion”). Its bitter force depends on visibility as an actual need where civilian records have disappeared, alongside the terrible bargain through which that need is met (E008; GO-CL016).

Some boys’ purported desire for tags is the narrator’s generalized explanation, not testimony collected from them. The visible coercion is nevertheless forceful: boys are lined up and implanted; the narrator describes enlistment against death and incorporation into the group responsible for familial violence. Calling some of it volunteering does not remove those conditions. His next comparison includes American operators as more expensive monitored bodies. The local and American arrangements are materially unequal, yet the parallel commodity vocabulary resists a simple opposition between dehumanized others and wholly autonomous observers. The strongest rival emphasis is that tags supply needed practical recognition rather than only domination. That is part of the supported reading, and is exactly what makes incorporation attractive and coercively effective.

The team’s later withdrawal and burial of the tags further limits technological inevitability. A credential that opens one gate may expose them at the next. They change tactics, abandon borrowed identity and restore adaptive concealment. Information’s usefulness depends on the particular authority reading it (E011).

### Feeling, counterfactual rescue and deliberate nonintervention

The narrator directly sees executions. His denial of a total lack of sadness is explicit, and his practical choice is equally explicit: he continues the mission. He invokes three subordinates, a rare chance to reach a moving commander and a counterfactual in which rescuing present victims lets that commander kill more people later. That is the reasoning he offers for nonintervention, rather than an observed comparison of two possible futures (E009; GO-CL017).

This consequential reasoning has real tactical support in the narration. An intervention could expose a four-person team; the target is mobile; the narrator bears responsibility for his men. The reading should therefore confront a serious triage argument, not dismiss it as a transparent excuse. Its costs remain present in the text: people die while the operators move on, and the narrator cannot confidently place himself outside killing by another’s will. His compressed self-commands to discard questions make the management of attention part of that choice. The reported rapid switch under emotional adjustment adds a present operational case to GO-CL009, while his later small scream makes a total-invulnerability portrait untenable. The unit does not directly retest every earlier claim about profiled targets; it demonstrates different kinds of affect in one mission (GO-RV008).

Alex’s answer about hell supplies a distinct voice. Williams treats the visible place as hell; Alex points instead to an inescapable interior that survives closed eyes and an ordinary return to America. His response holds that psychological/religious burden together with continued professional action. Leland’s question about heaven elicits admitted uncertainty, not the same certainty Alex assigns his experienced hell. The narrator’s guess that Alex may confess after returning is still his guess. The conversation gives Alex an articulated account of experience without authorizing the reader to invent its specific past cause (E010).

### A planned death becomes an intolerable question

The narrator finds target A alone; target B is absent from this inspected room and expected meeting. He changes the operation into an interrogation. His assurance that A is not his target is identified as a lie, and he already expects to kill this man. The final killing therefore cannot be explained solely as a new intention produced by panic. What changes is the encounter’s emotional and epistemic course (E013).

A’s account makes B a reported former culture/information deputy who has left a message through a messenger. The quoted message is nested inside A’s speech and spans two source paragraphs. Neither the narrator nor reader sees the paper, and A initially says he did not know B was American. Absence from the inspected room, A’s report and the narrator’s conclusion that B will not arrive are separate kinds of access. Alex’s final suggestion of intelligence error remains a suggestion (E014,E020).

A initially speaks in familiar legitimating categories: government, people, self-determination and antiterrorism. His own speech then breaks that frame as he recalls coexistence and rejects the adequacy of his first causal explanation. The narrator first hears opportunistic confession. A explicitly says **告解ではない** (analyst’s gloss: “this is not confession”), and the narrator then becomes convinced of his genuine bewilderment. That correction deserves affirmative weight: the local narration presents recognition of an unexpectedly real problem, rather than leaving sincerity evenly balanced by default. The strongest counterreading remains material, however. A is a threatened captive and an experienced security actor; self-exculpatory performance is possible, while the narrator supplies the psychological interpretation. Genuine loss of a reason is the preferred reading within this focal account, not independently observed access to A’s mind (E015–16; GO-CL018).

The narrator demands why A has killed. A answers with **わたしはなぜ殺してきた** (analyst’s gloss: “why have I been killing?”). Blank paragraphs isolate that self-question. Its explicit first person is important: the reply is not simply a grammatical accusation of the narrator. Yet its placement makes a powerful parallel with the narrator’s just-admitted delegation of judgment. He has relinquished the burden of deciding why to kill; now he needs another killer to possess a reason. The later unexpressed subject in the shortened why-questions lets the pressure reverberate without changing the established speaker sequence into invented dialogue (E017).

The adaptive camouflage makes that likeness visible. Prolonged physical contact causes the narrator’s coating to copy A’s uniform and decorations. The literal mechanism is his own equipment; the imagined spreading madness is expressed through comparisons. A’s ceremonial dignity also collapses into pleading, including a shift from formal first person to **俺**. The narrator’s commands shrink into a voice he admits is a small scream. This is a local breakdown of composure in the face of unexplained agency, strongly supported without diagnosing either man or turning the similes into a demonstrated external infection (E018).

### The sound that outlasts the target

The narrator kills A through a precise throat-cutting and heart-stabbing sequence. The repeated declarations that the man and commander are dead give the intended operational result firm grammatical closure. The music then proves to have ended without his noticing. Ordinary perceptual time seems to return, and the gunshots persist (E019).

The earlier cultural/aesthetic attention remains in the blood’s painterly comparison, but the scene has made the narrator bodily vulnerable to what he hears. Alex’s ordinary professional question and systematic images of the corpse contrast with that disturbance. The images document the target-death result; they offer no resolution of A’s missing motive or B’s announced departure. Most sharply, the final **だろうな** marks the narrator’s expectation that local massacre will continue. The continuing shots are heard; the duration is inferred. This ending does not disprove every possible longer-term benefit of assassination. It does prevent the present target-death closure from standing in for the immediate end of collective killing (E020; GO-CL019).
