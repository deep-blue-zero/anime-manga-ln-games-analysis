## A conversation heard from across the street

The first reply comes from ウィリアムズ, watching from their base opposite ルツィア・シュクロウプ's home/classroom. His teasing shows that the ostensibly private visit was available to a third listener. クラヴィス denies steering it toward cultured conversation; his partner does not accept the denial. Their familiar contest over attractiveness and literary competence continues the earlier Kafka joke. ウィリアムズ now makes his ignorance an alleged advantage: a deliberate opening for a woman to correct him. クラヴィス calls that opening a crater, then imagines the unwelcome spectacle of CIA personnel lecturing experts in special operations. Expertise is relational: a conversational tactic can become an insult when it misjudges its recipient (E001).

The joke later returns as an action. クラヴィス knowingly says he wants to read Kafka in Czech, offering a more accomplished version of the very tactic he mocked. The difference between the partners is refinement of the device rather than one man's complete sincerity (E010).

## What the nose collects without smelling

Asked about the target, クラヴィス recalls examining the room for rings, photographs, disorder and other signs of a man. He found none. A nasal patch nevertheless collected airborne molecules; a separate device recorded and analyzed them through a body-salt transmission connection. The distinction is exact: his conscious nose did not perceive the scent although the attached sensor picked it up. Instrumentation enlarges access beyond his felt experience (E002).

The identified men's fragrance is not an independently unique identification of ジョン・ポール. The attribution combines the readout with the CIA's categorical assertion that no male visitor entered after his disappearance. ウィリアムズ then reports no semen scent and turns it into a sexual joke. Neither the negative result nor his comment proves that no sexual contact occurred. The passage shows an invasive inference-making practice, with its technical and institutional dependencies still visible.

クラヴィス recognizes his own part in that invasion. Reading ルツィア's profile feels like peeping into another life; telling himself it is work does not, by his account, make him less vulgar than ウィリアムズ. He closes the file in disgust, then enters an intelligence network he describes as a fellowship of peepers. His recoil changes the interface rather than ending the activity. The self-criticism is substantial, but it does not become refusal of the mission (E003; GO-CL033).

## A shared network and a rumor with its seams showing

## The safety felt in passing through a gate

The first large spacing break moves from the base and network to actual street surveillance. Repeated `認証し` makes authentication a bodily rhythm before the narrator explains it. Following ルツィア through transit and shopping means repeatedly proving and notifying his identity. He includes himself among ordinary people who feel safer with each checkpoint, then explicitly calls the imagined progression toward limitless safety a delusion. Passing a checkpoint is still movement between places (E006).

That criticism coexists with a positive argument about security. The narrator recalls executions and tagged child soldiers in places without effective legal constraint. In his formulation, abstract permission to do anything can leave boys with only death or conscription: a freedom that denies freedom to live. His proposition that freedom exists through relations and tradeoffs has serious counterexamples behind it, rather than being merely an empty official slogan. Yet the earlier account of felt safety supplies a limit to his defense: habitual reassurance is not evidence that each additional intrusion secures its promised benefit (E007; GO-CL034).

The source does not settle this tension by assigning a simple political position to ルツィア. She briefly glances at a demonstration and continues walking; クラヴィス explicitly says he cannot infer her attitude toward freedom from it. His analysis of her shopping as a privacy-for-freedom exchange is his interpretation. The tail itself adds an undisclosed intrusion to her ordinary authentication, conducted in shifts with ウィリアムズ and CIA support personnel. Her shopping does not establish consent to their surveillance (E006–08).

The freedom/love analogy then leads into a highly particular gaze. クラヴィス dwells on her eyes and reads their appearance through European/American and cinematic categories. The French expressions in ruby layer attraction with the same cultural repertoire used in conversation. Those categories characterize his desire and cultural self-positioning; they do not establish women's national psychology or ルツィア's private melancholy. A statement that freedom and love reside in relationships is followed by a relationship deeply unequal in information (E007–08).

## A recognized identity that conceals the person

After the second spacing break, the room conversation establishes a month of lessons. Disclosure order is clear; no explicit date fixes the contract scene's exact position relative to the street episode. クラヴィス explains the arrangement as a simple way to enter regularly without arousing suspicion. Authentication now serves intimacy and concealment together: the device recognizes his advertising-worker identity and records a contract with the language school. The later address **ビショップ** is explicitly his false-ID name (E009, E011).

The transaction demonstrates successful use of this identity, not a failed scanner or a disclosed universal method for defeating biometrics. Identity recognized by infrastructure and personal truth supplied to an interlocutor are different things. Nor does subsequent talk about a wife and children establish クラヴィス's actual household: it occurs within the business persona and its assurances (GO-CL023v3/031v2).

ルツィア takes up the staged Kafka error and discusses borrowed language and uncertain belonging. クラヴィス offers Kafka and Nabokov as possible support for thought not being framed by language. That is his literary analogy, not an experiment proving a cognitive theorem. More immediately, the speakers are making a shared intellectual language while one uses a borrowed personal identity. The source makes that formal correspondence available without requiring an assertion that either speaker consciously intends it (E010).

ルツィア says he truly loves literature. He deflects into an account of professional dilettantism and turns the compliment into flirtation. His narration then refuses a clean true/false split: at least a third is sincere, he says, while his expression performs a breezy persona. The fractional formulation is his own comic self-measurement, not a basis for calculating his emotions. Intellectual pleasure and attraction remain real within a deliberately managed performance (E011).

ルツィア also participates in setting boundaries. When he asks about a former partner, she calls the question intrusive and invokes his supposed family. He uses that family position to claim that intimate talk is safe; she counters that married people can still pursue others. His claim to impose old-fashioned ethics by free will belongs to this persuasive exchange, not an independently tested rule of conduct. Her eventual account follows hesitation and counterquestioning. She is neither a transparently trusting subject nor an inaccessible blank (E012).

## Newly attended information, an imagined destination

The former partner studied language, met her at MIT and, according to what he told her, received DARPA funding for a Defense Department language project. She explicitly does not know its contents. クラヴィス recognizes the funding detail as new while explaining that the dossier already mentioned a state-supported language project. He and ウィリアムズ had not connected linguistics with the subsequent activities and had treated the detail as unimportant. The shift is therefore partly one of attention to already available information, not discovery of an entirely absent professional past (E013; GO-CL036).

The chain remains ルツィア reporting a former partner's account, joined by クラヴィス to his dossier knowledge. It establishes neither the project's actual technique nor military responsibility for later massacres. Her further recollection gives their relationship a history: dating, an abrupt disappearance, her return to the Czech homeland and work outside a university. Her inability to feel like remaining in academia is her self-description; the text supplies no independent diagnosis or complete private motive (E014).

Her account of John's Ballard reading gives the inquiry another vocabulary. She differentiates dry, cruel, fantastic prose from a film's presentation while acknowledging substantial story fidelity. This is an embedded account of other works, not admission of those novels or films as new primary witnesses. クラヴィス hears attraction to the end of things and tries to imagine the landscapes the target liked (E015).

The final move is explicitly his. Literary taste becomes a question about an emptied Earth, then an elaborate imagined scene of abandoned civilization. No new target confession turns that picture into ジョン・ポール's demonstrated aim. The strongest discovery is instead クラヴィス's recognition of his own strange peace: the imagined landscape resembles his familiar dream-country of the dead. The subsection began with another man claiming to read his pleasure from across the street; it ends with a pleasure he discovers in himself (E016; GO-CL035).

That likeness is ethically unsettling without proving that クラヴィス wants a massacre or shares the target's intentions. His peaceful dead-country experience was already differentiated from Alex's inner hell. The new ending extends the problem of differentiated response: a frightening image can fit an intimate place of relief. Comfort does not make the imagined catastrophe an accomplished event, a justified policy or a reliable forecast.
