# GO-U006 — the language that makes war workable

The promised prevention depends on changing how the operators know their target.

## Clothes, bodies and admission

The opening order to avoid identification works through clothes, civilian transport and discretion. Williams dislikes meeting superiors without a uniform because ordinary clothes expose individual taste to strangers. The uniform offers both a professional identity and relief from making a personal choice. At the Pentagon, however, the narrator describes biometric identity as stored elsewhere: official and visitor dress have become difficult to distinguish, and bureaucratic style has become civilian fashion. Appearance now circulates as a parody while secure identity sits in a paid infrastructure (E001–02; GO-CL023v2).

This is a precise limit on the apparent anonymity. The pair conceal their summons from observers, yet pass repeated bodily checks and authenticate the meeting-room door. Exterior discretion and institutional legibility coexist. The progression from clothes to veins, retina and facial geometry makes that division physical. The narrator’s distaste for artificial-muscle porters also keeps an embodied reaction inside a space that treats extraordinary technical arrangements as routine.

Door signs turn other countries into committee agendas. The narrator calls their easy intervention an absence of diplomatic ethics, then joins Williams in a joke that treats the restricted room itself as another world problem to be administered. Williams’s Kafka reference ends with his admission that he has not read Kafka. The name is useful social shorthand here, rather than evidence of a demonstrated literary education (E003). Their joking fluency establishes participation in the institution before the narrator begins diagnosing its language.

## A service that contains bodies

The room first looks to the narrator like people watching pornography. He immediately specifies the actual image: an immobilized captive, watched by senior intelligence and legislative figures. The first impression makes elite spectatorship uncomfortable; it does not establish a pornographic clip or the viewers’ sexual motives. Rockwell identifies the two operators as Unit G, and the briefing identifies the captive as a first-layer suspect arrested in the latest Somalia operation (E004–05).

Erika Sales represents a private military contractor. Her language of proposals, staffing, implementation and customer approval gives the work a familiar commercial grammar. The account includes logistics and reconstruction but explicitly extends outsourcing to combat itself. This is not an absence of every approval structure: the briefing describes resident federal approvers, a budget route and SOCOM’s favorable assessment of a plan. Outsourcing changes who executes violence and how it is described; it does not simply eliminate the state (E006–08).

The narrator’s response makes the argument more demanding than a denunciation of euphemism. He admits excitement at an imagined future in which war can be budgeted, planned and ordered. He also feels mocked by the description of his own work as controllable procedure. His account calls the verbal management of mass death a kind of literature and connects it to strategic abstraction. The historical and philosophical references remain his attributed account, not independently verified Kahn or Wittgenstein scholarship.

Then three short paragraphs recall blood on a wall, bodies shot and burned, and the dead girl. Their placement interrupts the smooth pitch with particulars already admitted in Part I. Sales’s words form another layer over these images, seeming to describe a battlefield on which nobody kills or dies. Yet the narrator ends this recognition with surprise, feeling and freshness rather than a clean withdrawal of his attraction. He sees the concealment and experiences the novelty. Critical perception therefore coexists with susceptibility to the administrative form (E008; GO-CL024). The words can enable practical coordination as well as moral insulation; the source makes their relation uncomfortable without proving that every planner forgets every body.

## Knowing a reason and not knowing a conversion

The captive’s visible fear surprises Williams, whose initial model expects a blustering militant chief. The timing code matters because restraint makes a stopped and moving image difficult to distinguish. When the clip resumes, the format changes into two visibly indented, explicitly labeled interviewer/Ahmed exchanges, separated by the observers’ commentary. DIA dates the clip to a week earlier. We receive a presented recording and its selected dialogue, not direct access to Ahmed’s mind or an independently inspected complete interrogation archive (E005,E009–11).

The interrogator invokes a contract, a claimed UN authorization and a promise of protection against unjust violence under stated conditions. Ahmed asks whether the detention already is unjust violence. When the questioner grounds legitimacy in wider approval, Ahmed answers that people in his own country also approved their acts. These replies expose a weakness in the offered majority distinction; they do not by themselves establish that all forms of violence have equal legitimacy. The law, resolution and historical example remain statements inside the recorded encounter.

Williams initially relaxes into the familiar category of a fanatic committed to being right. DIA then calls the continuation literary. The continuation shifts the question from whether Ahmed can give a reason to how so many people acquired the reason so quickly. Ahmed says he understands why the victims had to be killed, while tentatively accepting that the idea was absent a year earlier. He can acknowledge the result without explaining the rapid transformation. This difference between a rationale and the formation of a rationale is the passage’s substantive causal puzzle (E010–11; GO-CL025). Captive self-exculpation and leading questions remain material alternatives to transparent testimony. Fear and coherent replies do not certify innocence, a clinical condition or an involuntary mechanism.

The later account of Somalia complicates the narrator’s easy classification of reformers becoming dictators by supplying the constructive history and the speed of its reversal. Sales and Rockwell describe people collecting weapons, rebuilding schools and administration, and achieving peace themselves. Rockwell’s disclosed service in the earlier failed intervention gives him a personal connection; his distinction between political and military failure is his own assessment. He says his continued attention included donations. These are situated reports, not proof that foreign intervention created the peace. The scene gives the country an achieved constructive history before its reported collapse (E012–14).

## Attention, secrecy and a causal account

The economic problem is then visibility: a peaceful place still cannot attract investment if its earlier image persists. Sales’s report leaves open undiscovered resources and identifies labor and tourism as possibilities; Williams identifies the obstacle of investors’ and visitors’ reluctance. DIA takes over only after Sales has been dismissed. Her absence from the subsequent account is observable; her private knowledge of it is not (E014–15).

The narrator recognizes the gap between a fact and getting powerful audiences to know it. His own admission that work, pizza and the war-film preview displaced attention to Somalia makes the earlier domestic episode a concrete example of that scarcity. The film title, already named in GO-U005, is repeated here; no separate film witness has been admitted. PR is introduced as work that can move politicians, journalists and possible resources toward a neglected country. This supplies an affirmative need for mediated publicity before the target is identified as the former PR worker hired as cultural/publicity adviser. Communication is not introduced solely as deception (E012,E015–16; GO-CL026).

The narrator anticipates the adviser’s identity and reads the national reversal through an imagined neighbor’s familiar account of a respectable person who unexpectedly becomes a murderer. That personification gives the country a narrative character, while the preceding account of its population’s work resists treating it as a single mind. DIA, CIA and the narrator now present the target as a cause of recurring massacres. This is the working operational account the scene strongly advances. Its supporting information is summarized rather than shown, and the repeated unexplained-how phrasing preserves the absence of an actual mechanism (E016–19; GO-CL022v2).

The timing of official knowledge also remains differentiated. The narrator says planners had known what operators were not told, and challenges the room about knowledge before his first order. CIA answers the challenge affirmatively and admits attempts to capture the target before requesting the assassination operation. Her later explanation describes an initial suspicion of involvement followed by causal conviction. Pre-assassination knowledge and attempts are admitted; the chronology should preserve that affirmative force while distinguishing early suspicion from later certainty, whose exact transition date is not supplied. The silent glances are observed, while the narrator’s account of careers and a search for a scapegoat interprets them. Security compartmentalization remains a material explanation alongside institutional defensiveness; no private withholding motive is established (E017–18).

Williams’s cold objection to CIA calling overseas its garden makes professional resentment audible. His actual words reject the possessive metaphor and claim that it causes mistakes. The narrator supplies the more specific account of contempt for amateur quasiwar. Rockwell’s reprimand and Williams’s qualified apology preserve the hierarchy even during the breach. This is broader evidence of Williams’s professional self-positioning, with his precise private motive still attributed to his friend’s reading.

The narrator also revisits A’s genuine-seeming question. He now recognizes its likeness in the video: a perpetrator can have motives and purposes yet still ask why the country became this way. The recurrence strengthens the earlier causal puzzle without removing either man’s reported participation in killing (E019).

## A task that requires waiting

The apparent repetition of an assassination order conceals a change in method. The narrator understands tracking before Williams does. Previously the unit entered conflict zones as an equipped team following intelligence; now it must find and follow a target believed to be in Europe. That location remains an official belief, not an observed present location (E020).

More consequentially, a discovery and a killing opportunity no longer authorize immediate killing. Rockwell requires them to find out whether the target is cultivating another massacre somewhere. The assassination objective remains, but the operation’s advertised preventive purpose requires knowledge before execution. The authority that praises their killing record is asking them to suspend its usual use. Their J2 appointment and temporary headquarters attachment give the task a changed institutional route, with command described as joint between the Information Army and DIA (E021; GO-CL027).

Rockwell’s hand on the narrator’s shoulder and insistence that only they can prevent the next atrocity make an order personal. His last statement remains a possibility about what the target may be doing now. The subsection closes with a burden and an uncertain future, not discovery, successful prevention or a completed killing.
