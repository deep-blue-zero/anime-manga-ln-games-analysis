This reading ends with an unnamed first-person narrator walking beside his mother and the victims he first saw as wounded bodies. The unit makes that movement emotionally intelligible while leaving the experience’s ontological status unresolved. Its strongest early proposition is a continuity between intimate bereavement and anonymous collective death. It does not yet identify a perpetrator, a military organization, the narrator’s occupation or a linguistic killing mechanism. The mother’s assertions and the narrator’s changing perceptions remain attributable utterances and experiences, rather than verified rules of the fictional world.

## Deep reading

### Attention first promises a story, then withdraws it

The girl initially appears to be entering a space. The Alice comparison briefly permits an imagined adventure and purposive movement; the immediately disclosed head injury destroys that possibility (E003). The prose makes the reader experience a mistaken animation before recognizing a corpse. The boy receives a related, subtler animation: the bullet seems to make a decision about its exit, while his teeth and parted lips suggest a message that he cannot actually deliver (E004). The narrator does not recover his words. Personification grants agency to an instrument and hypothetical expression to a dead child while withholding any human killer. That distribution is a formal fact worth retaining; it is not proof that the narrator is evading his own responsibility.

The physical account mixes minute spatial/temporal estimates with smell, color and damaged anatomy (E004–E005). Its apparent evidentiary precision makes the later expansion into impossible perception more disruptive. The burning bodies’ fetal posture and interlocked limbs connect destruction with images of infancy and collective form. The narrator’s attention includes vulnerability and tenderness, not only an impersonal catalogue. Yet the unit gives no attack chronology, national identity, command chain or demonstrated cause beyond reported bodily injuries. Naming an atrocity’s responsible organization here would import knowledge the passage has withheld.

### The scene changes at the point where everyone is dead

A repeated death statement changes tense before a door opens onto the narrator’s mother (E006). The transition supplies no ordinary journey or temporal bridge. The mother is first presented as a body whose expression has been deliberately arranged; then she speaks and directs the narrator’s attention (E006–E007). This sequence makes resemblance to inner peace something manufactured and assessed, rather than transparent access to her mind. The phrase 偽りの安らかさ belongs to the narrator’s judgment of the embalmed face. It does not establish what his mother felt when alive, or a general indictment of every funeral practice.

The narrator attributes embalming to a Washington-state legal requirement. That report introduces jurisdiction, a specialist and practical intervention into the handling of death (E006); its actual legal accuracy has not been independently established and is not required as a premise of the literary reading. The narrow institutional inference is that, in this account, death is socially processed as well as physically suffered. An alternative is that the mortuary detail primarily marks the narrator’s dissatisfaction with a familiar death, with little institutional argument intended. Both fit the unit. Further textual recurrence would distinguish an institutional pattern from this local source of estrangement (GO-CL004).

### Recognition depends on the mother’s instructions

The mother tells the narrator where to look; he turns, accepts the sight of historical multitudes and then notices his own decay only after she points it out (E007–E008). Recognition is staged as an interaction, not an omniscient inventory. His explicit inability to explain knowing that a headless person smiles is especially diagnostic: the narration retains certainty of perceived meaning while confessing the absence of the perceptual means that would normally justify it (E007). This makes the experience epistemically unstable without making every emotional statement false.

The mother’s next correction draws authority from a remembered childhood gesture (E009). She rejects his proposed category of an afterlife and names the scene as いつもの世界, continuous with their lives. She supplies a conceptual classification as well as comfort. The narrator’s tears become tears of relief, and familiar faces become legible within the multitude (E010). Her assertion is therefore consequential in the scene even though neither her independent knowledge nor its world-level truth is established. The narrator’s acceptance gives the statement emotional force; it does not turn it automatically into the novel’s final doctrine (GO-CL001–GO-CL003).

### Joining is an action whose conditions matter

The mother takes his hand, invites him and leads; the narrator nods and walks (E011). The school-entry recollection makes this movement feel like a renewed dependent initiation into a social world. Directional influence is clear. Coercion, a threat, an order backed by force, or a tested capacity to refuse is not depicted. It would therefore be excessive to describe the scene either as fully informed autonomous consent or as demonstrated compulsory obedience. He acts within a frame of belonging and reassurance that she has helped supply (GO-CL003).

The final company includes the same girl, boy and burned villagers who first appeared as objects of horrified inspection. Their return changes their relation to the narrator: observer and observed now move together. Benjamin’s reported childhood-cancer death and the father’s head-destruction description place heterogeneous deaths beside the victims’ injuries (E010–E011). This does not erase differences in culpability or make every death morally equivalent. A stronger supported claim is that anonymous collective death is brought into proximity with the narrator’s own attachments and vulnerability. The cause and agency of the father’s death remain unresolved; the active wording alone is not a sufficient suicide determination.

### Language is a frame, not an established weapon

The epigraph precedes the contents and Part I. Its attributed calculation limits what human words express within a larger field of language (E001). Beside the boy’s hypothetical last words and the narrator’s inexplicable recognition of a smile, it can frame an opening concerned with expression beyond or short of available speech. That is a supported interpretive juxtaposition, not an empirical Vedic-language claim and not an announced causal mechanism. The mother’s intelligible corrective speech also provides a counterweight: language here does transmit a classification and change the narrator’s response. The epigraph may be a wider unresolved horizon rather than an explanatory key for every scene detail (GO-CL005).

### What kind of experience is this?

Speaking dead people, discontinuous space and the narrator’s discovery of his own decay support an oneiric or visionary reading (E006–E011). **The subsection never explicitly labels the experience a dream.** A nightmare of grief, a literal supernatural encounter, an allegorical opening and an unclassified narrated vision remain distinguishable possibilities. The mother’s denial of an afterlife is character speech within the experience; it cannot independently settle that experience’s ontology (GO-CL006).

The political reading also has a limit. The passage’s insistence on continuity can challenge a safe separation between a private life and distant mass death. But universal mortality and mourning provide a strong alternative explanation for the same gathering. With no identified perpetrator or narrator role, an assertion that the narrator confesses a particular crime or that a named state has been exposed would be unsupported. The opening’s force comes partly from requiring participation before supplying that explanatory apparatus. Explanation, responsibility and justification remain separate unanswered questions.
