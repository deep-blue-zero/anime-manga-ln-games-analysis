# 09 — Failure, Care, Autonomy, and Developmental Risk
## Who may fail, who carries the consequences, and when challenge becomes abandonment or control

## Scope and responsibility

This document owns the ethical problem defined by the synthesis architecture:

> **Who is permitted to fail, who carries the consequences, and what distinguishes ethical developmental challenge from abandonment or control?**

Its primary concern is not whether a character succeeds, nor whether a producer is personally kind, nor whether an institution is efficient. The object is the relationship among:

- incomplete readiness;
- professional challenge;
- vulnerability;
- fear;
- failure;
- injury and illness;
- performer choice;
- adult authority;
- peer care;
- institutional cost;
- retries;
- recovery;
- and accountability.

The analysis therefore preserves several ownership boundaries.

Document 03 remains the canonical home for Producer's overall developmental philosophy and decision grammar. Document 05 retains collective belonging and recoverability as properties of Cinderella Project. Document 06 retains institutional power, resource allocation, and the Producer/Mishiro production-philosophy dispute. Document 07 will own performance, song, audience, success, and recognition. Document 08 owns persona and mediated self-authorship. Document 11 will own Japanese voice and register. Those domains appear here only when they answer the ethical question of developmental risk.

The strongest series-level conclusion is:

> **THE IDOLM@STER CINDERELLA GIRLS does not oppose risk. It opposes risk that is opaque, unbounded, non-revisable, or detached from responsibility. Its preferred developmental challenge is bounded, interpretable, supported, revisable, and meaningfully owned by the performer, while the adults and institutions that create the conditions remain accountable for the consequences.**

That conclusion requires two symmetrical refusals.

The series does not endorse paternalistic safety as the highest good. The girls are repeatedly allowed to enter situations for which they are not fully ready, to make choices whose outcomes cannot be guaranteed, to try identities that may not fit, to accept opportunities that strain existing relationships, and to return to public work before every internal uncertainty has disappeared.

But it also does not endorse a libertarian fantasy in which autonomy transfers all responsibility onto the young performer. Adults still have obligations to frame expectations, protect bodies, monitor when coping strategies stop working, absorb operational costs, explain decisions, revise prior judgments, and intervene when a developmental experiment becomes avoidance or harm.

The ethical center lies between those failures.

# I. Working distinctions — the series' ethics cannot be reduced to “supportive” versus “unsupportive”

## 1. Developmental challenge

A developmental challenge places a performer in a situation whose demands exceed her present comfort or complete mastery, but where the gap is bounded enough that experience can become usable evidence.

Episode 3 is the earliest clean example. Producer and the trainer explicitly agree that New Generations is not fully ready. The evaluation is `正直 まだ早いように思えて` and `ギリギリ及第点`, yet the trio is still permitted to appear as Mika's backing performers (`CG-PERF-0001`). The ethical justification is not that unreadiness is irrelevant. It is that the risk is scaffolded: Mika, staff, trainers, existing choreography, rehearsal, a limited backing role, and an established live structure bound the possible failure (`CG-PROD-0005`).

A challenge becomes developmental because there is enough structure for failure to produce learning rather than mere exposure.

## 2. Abandonment

Abandonment occurs when an authority invokes freedom, trust, or experience in order to withdraw from responsibilities the performer cannot reasonably carry alone.

Episode 7 retroactively identifies this danger in Producer's `無口な車輪` ideal. His silence had been a defensive overcorrection from prior over-direction, not a mature form of respect. `私は逃げていたのかもしれません` makes the diagnosis explicit (`CG-PROD-0013`). Non-domination cannot mean refusing to explain what one knows while retaining structural authority.

Likewise, E10's performer freedom is explicitly paired with `責任取るのはプロデューサーの仕事だし` (`CG-PROD-0019`). The adult may allow the girls room to act; he does not thereby stop being responsible for the conditions under which they act.

## 3. Protection

Protection is legitimate intervention against a risk whose immediate cost the performer is poorly positioned to assess or absorb, especially bodily danger or acute impairment.

The clearest case is E13. Minami wants to perform despite fever, but Producer refuses. The canonical claim is deliberately strong: respect for agency does not abolish adult safeguarding authority over immediate bodily safety (`CG-ETHICS-0001`).

The series therefore rejects the proposition that every performer preference is dispositive merely because it is sincerely expressed.

## 4. Control

Control appears when an authority takes over a choice that should remain meaningfully attributable to the performer, or when one person's care becomes a claim to define another person's desire.

Miku's E19 over-responsibility is the peer-scale version. She tries to make Asterisk `最高` so Riina will never regret the unit, but Riina rejects the premise that Miku must decide or guarantee her feelings. Joining Miku was Riina's own choice (`CG-REL-0042`). Care becomes controlling when it tries to eliminate the other's right to uncertainty, dissatisfaction, or later revision.

At institutional scale, the same problem appears whenever centrally authored opportunity architecture is mistaken for performer consent. E20 is careful: Mishiro's offers are not simple coercion, but consent occurs under real asymmetry (`CG-INST-0026`). Agency exists, yet it is power-bounded.

## 5. Support

Support changes conditions around a choice without replacing the choice.

The mature formulation arrives in E24:

> `選んだその先で あなたは一人ではありません`

Producer places relational support *after* Uzuki's choice rather than in place of it (`CG-REL-0057`). Support does not mean choosing for her; autonomy does not mean leaving her alone.

## 6. Failure

The anime repeatedly refuses to treat failure as one undifferentiated category.

A performance can be technically successful, publicly recognized, and personally experienced as failure at the same time. New Generations' E06 debut demonstrates exactly that (`CG-PERF-0004`). A television segment can be audience-effective while developmentally costly to the performer (`CG-PERF-0008`, `CG-PROD-0017`). A rehearsal strategy can involve sincere effort while being methodologically counterproductive (`CG-CHAR-0028`, `CG-CHAR-0047`). A leader can be temporarily absent without becoming illegitimate (`CG-CHAR-0031`).

The ethical question is not simply “did failure occur?” It is:

> **What failed, for whom, under what conditions, who interprets it, and what becomes possible afterward?**

## 7. Disposability

Disposability is the conversion of current failure or nonperformance into a conclusion that the person may therefore be removed from the relevant field of value.

E23 makes this explicit. Mishiro's `雲に隠れた星に価値など無い` treats invisible light as functionally equivalent to darkness; Producer answers `星は今もそこにあります` and insists Uzuki remains a necessary member (`CG-ETHICS-0003`).

The episode does not prove an unlimited duty to retain every performer under every imaginable condition. Its narrower ethical claim is more defensible:

> **temporary failure of visibility does not by itself establish absence of value.**

## 8. Recoverability

Recoverability is the possibility that failure need not become final identity.

Document 05 owns recoverability as a property of the Project. Here its ethical significance is that a system capable of retries can expose people to meaningful challenge without making every mistake existential.

A recoverable environment can say:

- this result was bad;
- this method stopped working;
- you hurt someone;
- you were not ready;
- you need to stop;
- your current explanation is incomplete;

without immediately concluding:

- therefore you are disposable;
- therefore you no longer belong;
- therefore you have no future here.

That distinction structures almost the entire series.

# II. E01–E03 — aspiration enters professional life through bounded risk, not guaranteed readiness

Uzuki's early history already places failure before formal series success: as Document 02 established, her idol desire survives a prior failed audition and continued training. The important ethical premise is not that failure makes her more deserving. It is that prior nonselection is compatible with continued possibility. Her professional life begins in a field where present judgment is consequential without being metaphysically final.

Episode 1 then establishes a small but foundational ethical trait in Producer. When Rin challenges his interpretation of her, he can say `それは分かりません` rather than fabricate certainty (`CG-PROD-0002`). That matters for later risk because developmental authority becomes dangerous when it treats prediction as knowledge.

The first live in E03 is the series' earliest explicit challenge case.

The trio is not fully ready (`CG-PERF-0001`). Their placement is nevertheless ethically different from negligence because the challenge is bounded (`CG-PROD-0005`):

- they are not asked to headline;
- they enter existing choreography;
- Mika provides a competent senior center;
- trainers and staff remain present;
- rehearsal exposes deficits before public performance;
- the live itself has professional infrastructure;
- the girls have a limited function inside a larger event.

The series thereby defines one of its most important developmental propositions very early:

> **readiness need not be complete for experience to be justified, but incomplete readiness increases the obligation to scaffold the experience.**

The fried-chicken cheer adds peer regulation to the scaffold. `フライドチキーン！` is comic, but its function is concrete: a shared bodily ritual reduces self-consciousness immediately before entry (`CG-REL-0004`). The girls do not become fearless. They become able to act while fear remains.

Producer's post-live line is equally important:

> `この体験をしっかり 自分のものにしてください`

The experience is not self-interpreting (`CG-PROD-0006`). Merely surviving challenge does not guarantee development. Someone must later metabolize what happened.

This is the seed of Document 09's full ethical model:

1. exposure;
2. bounded conditions;
3. real agency;
4. interpretable consequences;
5. subsequent integration.

Remove the boundaries and risk becomes negligence. Remove agency and it becomes paternalistic simulation. Remove interpretation and experience can become misleading. Remove the possibility of retry and one error becomes too expensive to teach.

# III. E05 — fairness is not equal timing; it is intelligible relation among effort, evaluation, opportunity, and future

Episode 5 shifts the ethical problem from readiness to allocation.

Miku's protest is sometimes easy to dismiss because the series later proves that she has more development ahead of her. The evidence does not permit that dismissal. Her grievance is specific: she has worked, expected debut to follow, sees others selected, and cannot discover what `もっと頑張ればいいの？ / もっとってどれくらい？` is supposed to mean (`CG-CHAR-0010`).

The debut allocation itself is not arbitrary. It depends on timing, prior visibility, creator availability, company expectations, and a song opportunity (`CG-INST-0004`). Minami's own response proves that selection does not feel like simple reward; she converts it into obligation because she lacks stage experience and fears she may not be ready (`CG-CHAR-0011`).

The ethical problem is therefore not unequal opportunity as such.

The stronger E05 principle is:

> **When effort is being asked of developing performers, they need some intelligible relationship among effort, evaluation, opportunity, and future even when the organization cannot promise equal timing.** (`CG-PROD-0010`)

This is an information ethic.

A performer can tolerate waiting more fairly when waiting remains legible as part of a developmental process. Opaque delay produces a different kind of risk: effort becomes impossible to calibrate, and the person may respond by escalating effort without knowing whether effort is relevant to the decision at all.

That pattern will recur later in Uzuki's crisis. When the problem is not adequately named, `頑張ります` can become an all-purpose response to a question effort alone cannot answer.

Miku's protest is therefore not merely emotional immaturity. It identifies a structural danger in developmental systems:

> **If the path is opaque, conscientious effort can become self-punishing because the performer cannot tell whether more effort is useful, sufficient, or even addressed to the real problem.**

The ethical remedy is not guaranteed promotion. It is legibility.

# IV. E06–E07 — failure can be misrecognized because success itself requires framing

New Generations' debut is the series' first major case in which the event and the participant's interpretation diverge.

The audience calls Mio by name and responds positively (`CG-PERF-0020`). The performance is technically completed and socially recognized, yet Mio experiences it as personal failure because the crowd is far smaller than the reference event she had internalized (`CG-PERF-0004`).

Her conclusion is not simply irrational. Producer had allowed Mika's concert scale to become the operative comparison without sufficiently framing the difference between senior spectacle and rookie event (`CG-PROD-0011`).

This produces an ethical lesson that goes beyond “manage expectations.”

> **Adults who place developing performers into evaluative situations share responsibility for the comparison frameworks through which those performers are likely to interpret the result.**

The problem is not that Mio was permitted to fail. In one sense she did not fail at all. The problem is that the experience was insufficiently framed before she was asked to make meaning from it.

Episode 7 then refuses both punitive and exculpatory responses.

Producer corrects his earlier use of `当然` and explains that a normal rookie-scale result is not the same as inevitable failure. Photographs and testimony supply counterevidence. Mio accepts `そっか 失敗じゃなかったんだ`, but does not conclude that nothing went wrong. She relocates responsibility to her own flight and asks to continue (`CG-CHAR-0013`).

That is a mature failure model:

- the event is reclassified more accurately;
- unjustified self-condemnation is corrected;
- genuine harmful conduct is still acknowledged;
- return is requested rather than presumed;
- continued membership is possible.

Producer's own correction is equally important. His `無口な車輪` stance had turned respect into avoidance (`CG-PROD-0013`). The ethical alternative is neither command nor withdrawal but accompaniment: explain, listen, contest false conclusions, and remain present while the performer makes the next choice.

E06–E07 therefore establish:

> **Failure is ethically usable only when interpretation itself is revisable.**

A developmental system must be able to say that the performer's first story about what happened is not necessarily the final truth—without thereby denying her subjective experience.

# V. E09 — audience usefulness does not settle performer welfare

Candy Island's variety work gives the series one of its cleanest distinctions between professional utility and performer experience.

Chieri panics. Her apology becomes funny television because an experienced MC turns the moment into usable content (`CG-PERF-0008`). From the audience or program perspective, the segment works.

But the series refuses the inference:

> it worked on television, therefore nothing ethically problematic happened.

Producer asks `大丈夫ですか？` and whether she can continue (`CG-PROD-0017`, `CG-PROD-0018`). The line `仕事ですから` matters because care is occurring inside a professional demand, not outside it. The answer is not that fear cancels work automatically.

Chieri's response is central:

> `怖いけど 笑顔も自信無いけど`
>
> `だけど皆に勇気貰えたから`
>
> `一緒にやりたい`

Her development is not a confidence cure. She is still afraid (`CG-CHAR-0018`). Her agency consists in choosing participation with fear still present.

E09 thus differentiates at least four states:

1. **audience success** — the content lands;
2. **performer distress** — the performer is genuinely overwhelmed;
3. **professional feasibility** — she may still be able to continue;
4. **performer ratification** — she herself wants to continue.

No one axis resolves the others.

This is essential to later developmental ethics. A producer cannot use audience response as a substitute for checking performer state. But performer discomfort is also not automatically proof that the challenge was unethical. The question is whether discomfort remains inside a field of meaningful choice, support, and revisability.

# VI. E10–E13 — autonomy becomes credible only when dependence, bodily limits, and substitution are permitted

## 1. E10 — freedom without adult disappearance

Dekoration is intentionally given room to act because Producer judges that `自由に行動させたら面白いユニット` may suit them (`CG-PROD-0019`). The line is immediately bounded by responsibility.

This is the series' clearest rejection of a false autonomy ideal:

> **A performer can be trusted with local initiative while the adult retains responsibility for the environment.**

The girls become able to prioritize the audience while separated from him (`CG-PROD-0020`). Their agency is real because it has been internalized, not because supervision has become metaphysically unnecessary.

Rika's foot pain adds an equally important bodily dimension. Her agency is not demonstrated by hiding pain or “pushing through.” She discloses the problem, asks Kirari to carry her, and helps redesign movement around the constraint (`CG-REL-0011`, `CG-CHAR-0022`).

This revises a common heroic model of professional resilience.

> **Mature agency includes the ability to disclose limitation, accept dependence, and redesign the task.**

A culture in which asking for help counts as failure would have made Rika less autonomous, not more.

## 2. E11 — consent can be real under opportunity pressure without becoming unconstrained

Asterisk's formation complicates the autonomy model. Miku and Riina can refuse the unit, but refusal delays one member's CD debut (`CG-INST-0007`).

The choice is therefore neither:

- coerced in the sense that refusal is impossible;
- nor free in the sense that alternatives carry no institutional cost.

The series later validates Asterisk through performer ratification, conflict, co-authorship, and chosen continuation. That later legitimacy does not erase the original pressure.

Document 09 therefore adopts a power-sensitive consent model:

> **A choice may be meaningfully authored even when made under asymmetric opportunity pressure; ethical analysis must preserve both the authorship and the pressure.**

This becomes crucial in the second cour.

## 3. E12 — collective care regulates failure instead of locating a defective person

The rope sequence is one of the series' best small-scale representations of a recoverable learning environment.

Chieri fails and apologizes. The group does not turn the mistake into evidence that she is the weak member who blocks everyone. They reset rhythm and try again (`CG-REL-0014`).

The acoustic structure strengthens the point: the episode moves from a song about differentiated dreams joining together into bare count, bodies, failed timing, repair, and renewed onset (`CG-PERF-0027`). Togetherness has to be manufactured through retries.

Mio separately learns that her harder effort had become `空回り` (`CG-CHAR-0028`). The ethical message is not anti-effort. It is anti-equation:

> **more effort is not always more care, more leadership, or more development.**

LOVE LAIKA's `不安は半分こ出来た` makes the same point relationally (`CG-REL-0015`). Care does not eliminate anxiety. It changes who has to carry it alone.

Anya's nocturnal care for Minami similarly refuses the idea that leaders must first become invulnerable before they may care for others (`CG-REL-0013`).

## 4. E13 — safeguarding draws a non-negotiable boundary

Minami's fever crystallizes the distinction between support and protective authority.

She wants to perform. Producer refuses (`CG-ETHICS-0001`).

The series does not stage this as a betrayal of her agency. Her immediate physiological condition changes what responsible authority may permissibly allow.

This is the strongest evidence against interpreting later performer autonomy as absolute sovereignty.

The same crisis also demonstrates why substitution matters ethically. Ranko can cover Minami's performance function without becoming Minami; the audience notices Minami's absence (`CG-REL-0017`). The Project absorbs the operational problem without requiring the ill person to prove her worth by overriding bodily limits.

Chieri's self-blame is answered through distributed competence: each member does the part she presently can (`CG-REL-0018`). Minami later tries to translate illness into leadership invalidation, but peers reject `リーダー失格` (`CG-CHAR-0031`).

The ethical architecture is therefore:

- bodies are not infinitely available to work;
- functions can sometimes be covered;
- coverage does not erase the absent person;
- illness does not prove moral or leadership failure;
- the collective has an obligation to absorb some contingency.

This is recoverability made material.

# VII. E14–E16 — developmental care becomes a principle, but principles require tests

By E14 Producer's habit of asking Rin whether idol work is enjoyable has become routine rather than crisis-only care (`CG-PROD-0026`). That is a quiet but significant development. Care is more credible when it occurs before collapse.

E15 then forces Producer to make the ethical stakes explicit. He says that people walk differently and rejects a production method that ignores those differences enough to risk making performers lose their smiles (`CG-PROD-0029`).

This is not a claim that all negative emotion is unethical. The series has already shown fear, embarrassment, frustration, pain, illness, and conflict inside legitimate development. `笑顔` here functions as a boundary on prolonged alienation from the work, not as a command that performers remain cheerful.

Mishiro's counterposition is not ethically empty. E15 explicitly distinguishes developmental ethics from efficiency without declaring efficiency intrinsically immoral (`CG-ETHICS-0002`). An institution must allocate finite resources; delayed results affect others; carrying developmental uncertainty has costs.

The ethical disagreement is about acceptable evidence and acceptable cost.

E16 turns Producer's principle into a trial. `Power of Smile` is authorized only conditionally; Mishiro provides neither support nor interference, sets a deadline, and attaches consequences to failure. This is itself a form of developmental risk at departmental scale.

Miku's dilemma supplies the performer-level test. Producer does not tell her simply to obey or rebel. He explains consequences for Asterisk, notes Riina appears to want Miku to decide, and asks for the method by which Miku herself can genuinely smile (`CG-PROD-0035`).

This is care through context rather than substituted choice.

But the series is careful not to romanticize choice under institutional pressure. The Miku/Nana episode works because peers, history, audience response, and professional consequences all become evidence. A choice is stronger when the performer knows what she is choosing *among*.

The ethical lesson is:

> **Autonomy depends partly on informational conditions. A nominal option is not enough if the person cannot understand the stakes, alternatives, or evidence relevant to the choice.**

# VIII. E17–E18 — challenge may be imposed, but authorship can be recovered; coping may help, but care must notice when it becomes avoidance

## 1. E17 — appropriation does not erase the ethics of constraint

Rika and Mika both receive professional forms they did not choose.

Rika is assigned a kindergarten-like presentation that conflicts with her desire to seem older. Mika faces an adult-luxury campaign under departmental pressure. Both later appropriate the assignment in ways that re-establish authored continuity (`CG-MEDIA-0011`).

This gives Document 09 a necessary caution:

> **A good developmental outcome does not retroactively prove that every coercive or poorly negotiated condition that produced it was justified.**

Rika's `だって私は私だしー` shows that a constrained form can become usable through appropriation (`CG-CHAR-0043`). Mika's eventual adjustment likewise shows professional growth. But Mika also reproduces pressure downward and later realizes that she had spoken too harshly partly because she was taking her own frustration out on Rika (`CG-CHAR-0044`).

Care can fail through displaced burden.

Miria and Mika's conversation supplies the repair. “Older sister” stops meaning invulnerability when Miria recognizes that older sisters also sometimes need to cry (`CG-REL-0037`). The ethical gain is not just emotional catharsis. It is the rejection of care roles that deny the caregiver's own needs.

## 2. E18 — not every coping strategy deserves to be removed, and not every effort deserves to be intensified

E18 is perhaps the series' most precise pedagogy episode.

Producer deliberately gives Chieri and Kanako a stretch assignment because he believes it is `今のお二人に必要な仕事` (`CG-PROD-0037`). This is not neutral scheduling. He is intentionally exposing them to difficulty for developmental reasons.

That creates an obligation to monitor the result.

Chieri initially uses `カエルさん` as an avoidance ritual. Kanako responds to perceived failure by escalating diet and training. Both strategies are sincere. Both eventually interfere with the work.

Producer does not ban reassurance in principle. He first helps Chieri search for a four-leaf clover, later interrupts coping when it blocks outward attention, and finally permits/reframes the clover as something that helps her face forward (`CG-PROD-0038`, `CG-MOTIF-0023`).

This yields a sophisticated care principle:

> **The ethical value of a scaffold depends on what it enables, not on whether it looks dependent.**

A charm that enables Chieri to look outward can be useful. The same symbolic apparatus used to avoid contact can be maladaptive. Maturation is not the ritual's disappearance; it is changed function.

Kanako's case makes the corresponding effort principle explicit. She believes more discipline will repair professional inadequacy, but the strategy impairs her work (`CG-CHAR-0047`). Producer's responsibility is therefore not to praise effort indiscriminately.

He tells them that they were already trying hard; what was absent was usable enjoyment and smile (`CG-PROD-0039`).

Again, the series refuses a simplistic comfort ethic. Chieri still feels nervous. Courage is enough capacity to face outward while fear remains (`CG-CHAR-0046`, `CG-MOTIF-0024`).

Ethical development may preserve discomfort while changing its relation to action.

## 3. Peer care is plural rather than expert-monopolized

Sachiko, Sae, and Yuki help through different competencies (`CG-REL-0040`). One supplies a blunt standard, another translates the care under the bluntness, and the group normalizes difficulty through shared memory.

No single mentor voice is sufficient.

This matters because care itself can become authoritarian when one helper's model of what another person needs becomes unquestionable. Cinderella Project's strongest peer-care scenes are distributed enough to permit correction among caregivers.

# IX. E19 — care becomes unethical when it claims ownership over another person's future feeling

Asterisk's crisis provides the series' sharpest peer-level analysis of over-care.

Miku wants to make Asterisk `最高` so that Riina will never regret joining her. The intention is loving. The structure is controlling.

Miku has converted Riina's future feeling into her own responsibility.

Riina repairs this by asserting that choosing Miku was her own decision (`CG-REL-0042`). Miku cannot guarantee the absence of future regret because doing so would require ownership of Riina's evaluation.

The distinction is crucial:

> **Care may take responsibility for one's conduct toward another person. It may not assume responsibility for ensuring that the other person will never change, regret, desire something else, or interpret the relationship differently.**

Producer's behavior in the same episode supplies the adult analogue. He offers to facilitate a three-person conversation, but when Miku asks him to trust Asterisk's relationship, he does. When Riina approaches him and stops herself, he does not force disclosure (`CG-PROD-0040`).

This is calibrated non-intervention.

Not intervening is ethical here because:

- the performers possess the relevant relational standing;
- there is no immediate bodily danger;
- communication remains possible;
- the conflict is theirs to author;
- Producer remains available if requested.

Contrast this with E07. Silence there was withdrawal because Producer possessed necessary interpretive information and was avoiding dialogue. E19 non-intervention is mature precisely because it occurs after he has learned when his presence would displace rather than enable performer agency.

Natsuki's autonomy claim adds professional cost. She rejects Mishiro's band opportunity even after being warned there may be no next chance, because the conditions do not fit her conception of rock (`CG-INST-0024`).

The series does not guarantee that self-authorship is rewarded.

> **A meaningful right to refuse must include the possibility that refusal has real opportunity cost.**

Otherwise autonomy would be tested only when choice is cheap.

# X. E20–E21 — autonomy becomes ethically serious when one person's legitimate growth hurts people who did nothing wrong

The second cour's most difficult autonomy problem is not coercion. It is **externality**.

Mishiro offers Rin Triad Primus and Anastasia solo work without formally dissolving their existing units (`CG-INST-0026`). Producer's own principle is turned back on him: intent belongs to Rin and Anastasia, not to him (`CG-PROD-0041`).

If he protects New Generations or LOVE LAIKA by vetoing the opportunities, he risks treating existing relationships as ownership claims over member development.

If he permits the opportunities, other people may be hurt.

The anime refuses to pretend that autonomy is painless.

## 1. Anastasia — choosing alone can itself be developmental content

Anastasia says she wanted to consult Minami but intentionally did not because she had never decided something alone and believed change had to begin there (`CG-CHAR-0050`).

This is ethically uncomfortable precisely because Minami is entitled to feelings about the consequence. But Minami refuses to transform care into a requirement of prior permission. She supports the choice (`CG-REL-0044`).

The distinction is:

> **Relational consideration matters, but a close relationship does not automatically acquire veto rights over a person's developmental decisions.**

Producer accordingly moves from predicting happiness to asking whether Anastasia wants to move forward despite uncertainty (`CG-PROD-0042`).

## 2. Rin — uncertainty becomes a reason to test, not a reason to wait forever

Rin initially plans to refuse Triad Primus. One rehearsal produces `新しい何か`, and she says:

> `分からないから それを確かめたい`

Lack of certainty becomes a reason for exploratory action (`CG-CHAR-0051`).

This is a mature risk posture. She does not know whether the new path will be better. The point of the experiment is to generate knowledge unavailable in advance.

Karen and Nao make the trial more ethical by proposing a bounded rehearsal and accepting that Rin may not come (`CG-REL-0045`). The possibility becomes performer-owned through limited experimentation rather than immediate irreversible commitment.

## 3. Mio — harm does not prove illegitimacy

Mio experiences Rin's new desire as `ニュージェネじゃ出来ない？ 私達とじゃダメなの？` (`CG-NG-0006`). Her hurt is real.

The series does not resolve this by proving she was wrong to be hurt. Nor does it prove Rin wrong because the choice caused hurt.

That is a major ethical distinction:

> **A choice can be legitimate and still impose relational cost. Harm and wrongdoing are not identical categories.**

Mio's later solo decision begins partly under that relational pressure (`CG-NG-0008`). Producer explicitly rejects her inference that Rin's new desire means Mio's prior leadership failed (`CG-PROD-0043`). A leader is not ethically responsible for preventing every independent desire in the people she leads.

## 4. E21 — collective care becomes interpretive work rather than forced synchrony

The Project initially fears that people are no longer moving `足並みそろわなくて`. Ranko, Anzu, Kirari, and others shift the conversation toward shared interpretation of asymmetric paths (`CG-REL-0047`). Minami calls her own solo preparation an `冒険` while still caring about Anastasia (`CG-CHAR-0052`).

The mature care model is therefore not:

> everyone should remain synchronized so no one feels abandoned.

It is:

> **the collective should become capable of understanding and supporting non-synchronous development without pretending that asymmetry has no emotional cost.**

---

# XI. E22 — professional responsibility includes emergency command and the ability to stop maladaptive effort

The Autumn Live brings several forms of risk together.

First, the event is both public entertainment and a bureaucratic survival test (`CG-INST-0028`). Producer deliberately reduces the performers' immediate attention to the audience and stage rather than loading the managerial stakes onto them. This becomes even clearer in E25, but the ethical principle is already visible: not every burden known to the adult should be transferred to the performer.

Second, Fumika becomes unable to continue. Producer reorganizes the running order under incomplete information, then accepts accountability upward (`CG-PROD-0045`).

This is not the domain for participatory deliberation. Live operational risk can require fast command.

The series' autonomy ethic is therefore domain-sensitive:

> **The more immediate and safety/operations-bound the problem, the stronger the justification for decisive adult authority. The more identity/desire-bound the problem, the stronger the requirement for performer authorship.**

Third, Uzuki's effort language begins to malfunction.

Her historically positive `頑張ります` narrows into recursive self-pressure (`CG-VOICE-0032`). She keeps promising to work harder without identifying what the work is for. Producer responds by telling her to leave for the day because she appears unwell (`CG-PROD-0046`).

This is a small but decisive ethical move:

> **Care may require interrupting effort rather than rewarding it.**

A developmental culture that praises persistence indiscriminately can become dangerous precisely for conscientious people. Uzuki's reliability makes it easier for others—and for herself—to treat more effort as the default cure.

Miho supplies the countermodel. She still becomes very nervous but has a stable positive orientation toward the work (`CG-CHAR-0054`). Development does not require elimination of vulnerability; it requires a workable relation to vulnerability.

---

# XII. E23 — respecting autonomy includes the duty to revise one's own earlier respect

Episode 23 is the ethical center of the entire specialist document.

Uzuki asks to suspend work and return to basic training. Producer initially agrees because the request appears plausibly necessary for moving forward. He later says:

> `ですがこのままでは良くないと 今は思っています`

Respect for autonomy does not make the original judgment irreversible (`CG-PROD-0047`).

This is perhaps the series' most important distinction between autonomy and passivity.

> **To respect a person's choice is not to declare the first explanation she gives for that choice permanently beyond interpretation.**

Uzuki's training is sincere. She works on stamina, fundamentals, and steps and finds the school calming (`CG-CHAR-0055`). But the strategy translates an identity-level fear into technical deficits where effort always supplies an answer.

Producer therefore creates a mini-live as a reality test. If her stated problem is unreadiness to rejoin New Generations, a bounded New Generations opportunity should reveal whether technical preparation is actually the barrier (`CG-PROD-0048`). Her continued reluctance supplies new evidence.

The ethical structure is careful:

- he does not immediately override her original request;
- he observes outcomes;
- he revises his own judgment;
- he creates a bounded test rather than issuing a total command;
- he keeps dialogue open.

## 1. Individual care has real externalities

Uzuki's withdrawal creates substitution and scheduling labor (`CG-INST-0031`). Rin's concern affects Triad Primus rehearsal. Mishiro is therefore not inventing the cost when she says the crisis is affecting the department.

This matters because individualized care becomes ethically unserious if it treats others' burdens as invisible.

Mishiro's remedy is exclusion: `切り捨てろ` (`CG-INST-0032`). Her reasoning is system-preservational. If one nonperforming member continually consumes resources and creates spillover costs, removing the source may protect the rest.

Producer's model accepts the cost but disputes the inference.

The series does not prove that carrying every developmental crisis indefinitely is sustainable. Instead, it makes E23 a test case in which the accumulated evidence about one particular performer justifies continued investment.

## 2. Peer care must update its model of the cared-for person

Mio and Rin realize that Uzuki may `抱え込んじゃう`. This is an in-character inference, not omniscient diagnosis, but it revises their assumption that her reliability means she requires less care.

Mio's later apology is ethically precise:

> `何かね安心してた`
>
> `どんな時も笑って 頑張りますって言ってくれるって`
>
> `そんな訳ないよね`
>
> `ごめんね 気づけなくて`
>
> `もっかい友達になろうよ 今から`

She recognizes a care cost hidden by Uzuki's stability and proposes relational re-contracting rather than restoration of the old assumption (`CG-REL-0054`).

The broader principle is:

> **Care can fail not only through neglect, but through overconfidence in a person's reliability.**

A person who habitually supports others may become less visible as someone who also needs support.

## 3. Adult care must know when peer standing is superior

Rin and Mio want to speak to Uzuki themselves. Producer facilitates immediately. Mio notices that he is not saying `こちらに任せて下さい`; he answers `あの時とは違います` (`CG-PROD-0049`).

The line makes the ethical development explicit.

Producer is not the universal caregiver. His authority does not grant the best relational access in every situation.

Care becomes more ethical when the adult can identify **who has standing to do what**.

## 4. Temporary nonperformance does not equal disposability

The confrontation between Mishiro and Producer gives the document its strongest value claim.

Mishiro:

> `雲に隠れた星に価値など無い`

Producer:

> `星は今もそこにあります`
>
> `シンデレラプロジェクトに 必要なメンバーです`
>
> `私は彼女を待ちます`

The canonical claim is deliberately bounded (`CG-ETHICS-0003`). The series does not prove that all institutions must retain all nonperformers indefinitely. It does reject the inference that present invisibility itself proves absence of value.

This is where failure and personhood most clearly separate.

> **A performer can presently fail to produce visible value without the series treating that state as exhaustive evidence about her future or her worth to the relationships already built around her.**

## 5. Waiting becomes ethical only after intervention

`待ってる` would be weak if it meant passive delay. By the time Producer and Rin say they will wait, they have already:

- challenged Uzuki's explanation;
- created a mini-live test;
- visited her;
- confronted the defensive use of effort language;
- elicited her actual fear;
- defended her institutional place.

Waiting becomes a promise not to force the timetable **after** active care has clarified what is being waited for.

That distinction prevents patience from becoming another name for abandonment.

---

# XIII. E24 — mature autonomy is self-authored risk under relational support, not confidence before action

Episode 24 resolves the ethical problem without giving Uzuki certainty.

The Project conversation offers her plural precedents rather than one standardized answer (`CG-REL-0056`). Members describe fear, failure, adventure, different pacing, persistent traits, and continuing uncertainty. Care becomes a field of examples, not a prescription.

Producer then says:

> `光はそこにあります`
>
> `今のあなたには見えていないだけで`

His claim is epistemic before it is consoling: inability to perceive value is not proof that value is absent (`CG-PROD-0050`).

Uzuki finally names the fear:

> `自分の事 / 怖いんです`

She fears undertaking the search for differentiated value and finding nothing (`CG-CHAR-0058`). Technical repetition was safer because effort could always generate a measurable next task.

Producer does not answer with certainty that he can prove her hidden essence. He says `選んで下さい` (`CG-PROD-0052`).

This is the mature risk formulation:

> **The decisive step is not action after confidence; it is choosing whether to move toward a possibility that cannot be guaranteed.**

Uzuki's answer remains uncertain:

> `あるか分からないけど…信じたい`
>
> `確かめたい`

Uncertainty changes function. It no longer justifies retreat; it motivates inquiry (`CG-CHAR-0059`).

Producer then supplies the complementary care promise:

> `選んだその先で あなたは一人ではありません`

The order matters (`CG-REL-0057`).

1. Uzuki chooses.
2. Support accompanies the chosen path.

Not:

1. the support network decides;
2. Uzuki complies.

Nor:

1. Uzuki chooses;
2. everyone withdraws in the name of independence.

E24 thus arrives at the series' mature autonomy model:

> **authorship without isolation; support without substitution; uncertainty without paralysis; risk without guarantee.**

Even her return to public performance occurs before complete self-definition. The blank star remains blank; she may act before she can write a final answer about herself. That formal point belongs to Document 10, but ethically it reinforces the same proposition: requiring completed certainty before re-entry would simply turn self-knowledge into another qualification test.

---

# XIV. E25 — mature developmental systems protect attention, preserve disagreement, and make recovery reusable

The finale does not remove risk from the system. It shows what the earlier ethical development has made possible.

Producer's pre-Ball instruction reduces enormous institutional stakes to:

> `今日は笑顔で楽しんでください`

He protects performer attention from burdens that properly belong to management (`CG-PROD-0053`). This is the mature opposite of E06, where insufficient expectation framing allowed the performer to internalize the wrong comparison scale.

Adults still disagree about how much developmental uncertainty an institution should carry. Mishiro's castle argument warns that protecting currently unshining performers without limit may erode aspirational legitimacy (`CG-INST-0036`). Producer admits he cannot see every star (`CG-PROD-0054`). The finale therefore refuses to convert the ethical model into omniscience.

This matters for Document 09 because a humane developmental ethic can become self-flattering if it assumes every person should always be retained, every risk will eventually pay off, or every act of support is vindicated by success.

The series offers a more disciplined conclusion:

- developmental systems need room for retries;
- present failure is incomplete evidence;
- support has costs;
- institutions have legitimate constraints;
- authority is sometimes necessary;
- choices can hurt others without becoming wrongful;
- people can remain uncertain while moving;
- and no producer can know every future in advance.

The Ball and the post-Ball plurality make differentiated continuation sustainable, but they do not prove that developmental risk has been abolished. They prove that the institution and collective have become better at **carrying** it.

# XV. A taxonomy of failure — what exactly is permitted to fail?

The series' ethics becomes clearer if its recurring failures are classified by object.

## 1. Technical failure

Examples include missed timing, stage mistakes, insufficient stamina, forgotten lyrics, or incomplete skill.

Technical failure is usually treated as trainable. It becomes dangerous when performers convert it into global personal worth or when adults fail to distinguish local deficit from person-level judgment.

## 2. Interpretive failure

Mio's E06 collapse is primarily interpretive: the audience event is misclassified through an inappropriate comparison frame.

Interpretive failure can require counterevidence and conversation rather than more training.

## 3. Relational failure

Mio's flight, Miku's over-responsibility, or the New Generations E20 rupture involve harm in how people understand or respond to one another.

Relational failure cannot be repaired solely through better professional technique.

## 4. Method failure

Mio's over-rehearsal, Kanako's escalating self-discipline, Chieri's avoidance ritual, and Uzuki's recursive basic training show methods that are sincere but no longer functional.

Method failure is especially important because praising effort can prolong it.

## 5. Leadership failure

The series frequently resists globalizing leadership mistakes. Minami's illness does not make her `リーダー失格`; Mio's inability to anticipate Rin's desire does not invalidate prior leadership.

Leadership is evaluated through responsiveness and correction, not invulnerability.

## 6. Institutional failure

Path opacity, poor expectation calibration, overly narrow evaluation, or inability to absorb individual recovery costs are not performer failures at all.

The series repeatedly relocates responsibility upward when the surrounding system helped produce the problem.

## 7. Identity-level failure

Uzuki fears the most radical possibility: that inquiry into herself may reveal no differentiated value.

The series does not solve that fear by proving she possesses one exclusive scarce trait. It changes the ethical standard from “prove unique essence before acting” to “choose whether to test possibility while supported.”

## 8. Failure of visibility

A performer may be temporarily absent, unable to perform, or unable to produce the expected public sign of value.

E23 rejects the equation of invisibility with nonexistence (`CG-ETHICS-0003`).

# XVI. A taxonomy of care — care is not one behavior

## 1. Preparatory care

Training, rehearsal, expectation framing, assignment design, and bounded exposure.

E03 demonstrates that preparation makes incomplete readiness ethically usable.

## 2. Informational care

Explaining why decisions occur, what the stakes are, what outcomes mean, and what options remain.

E05 and E06 show the costs when this is insufficient.

## 3. Protective care

Stopping performance because of fever; interrupting maladaptive effort; acting rapidly during live contingency.

Protective care can legitimately override immediate preference in domains where harm is acute and the adult carries professional responsibility.

## 4. Relational care

Listening, confronting, apologizing, visiting, waiting, providing reassurance, or sharing anxiety.

Relational care is not monopolized by adults and often belongs more properly to peers.

## 5. Scaffolding care

Allowing charms, rituals, partners, rehearsals, or limited experiments that make a difficult act possible.

Scaffolds are evaluated functionally: do they enable engagement, or have they become avoidance?

## 6. Interpretive care

Helping another person produce a more accurate account of what happened without invalidating her feelings.

E07 is the central example.

## 7. Boundary-respecting care

Knowing when not to force disclosure or seize control.

E19 shows that non-intervention can be mature when the relevant domain belongs to the performers.

## 8. Longitudinal care

Holding evidence about a person's value across a temporary period in which she cannot currently access that evidence herself.

Producer's E24 belief in `あなただけの“笑顔”` belongs here, though Document 03 owns the full Producer analysis.

## 9. Accountability care

Care that still names failure and consequence.

Miku's E24 reincorporation scene is important because she can condemn abandoning work while still caring about Uzuki. Belonging is not made conditional on perfection, but neither is belonging used to erase accountability.

---

# XVII. Autonomy — five conditions for meaningful authorship

Across the series, autonomy is strongest when five conditions are present.

## 1. The person can understand the decision object

A choice made with no intelligible account of stakes is formally voluntary but developmentally weak.

This is why Producer increasingly supplies context rather than only permission.

## 2. Refusal is possible, even if costly

Asterisk and Natsuki show that refusal may carry professional cost. Cost does not automatically negate autonomy, but impossible refusal would.

## 3. The decision can be tested at bounded scale when uncertainty is genuine

Triad Primus begins through rehearsal rather than immediate total reorganization. Uzuki's mini-live functions as a diagnostic test. Bounded experiments reduce the need for false certainty.

## 4. Others may respond to the consequences

Autonomy is not immunity from other people's feelings, professional evaluation, or relational renegotiation.

Rin's legitimate choice still hurts Mio. Miku's legitimate desire to protect Asterisk still gets challenged by Riina.

## 5. Support remains available after the choice

`選んだその先で あなたは一人ではありません` gives the mature formulation.

Autonomy is not ethically complete if an institution says “you chose it, therefore all consequences are yours.”

# XVIII. Protection versus control — a domain-sensitive test

The series does not offer a mechanical rule, but the evidence supports a practical matrix.

| Domain | Stronger default | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Acute bodily safety | Protective adult authority | Performer may not be well positioned to absorb immediate harm; adult retains safeguarding duty |
| Live operational emergency | Decisive professional command | Delay can magnify harm across many people |
| Technical training method | Collaborative revision | Expertise matters, but method must respond to performer state |
| Professional assignment | Informed negotiation | Institutions control scarce opportunity but performer uptake remains morally relevant |
| Persona / identity | Performer authorship | External construction without ratification risks alienation |
| Unit relationship | Peer-level authorship | Producer lacks privileged access to every interpersonal meaning |
| Career exploration | Bounded experimentation | Future value cannot be fully known in advance |
| Identity-level uncertainty | Self-authored risk with support | No adult can legitimately manufacture the performer's final answer |

The table explains why the same series can defend Producer's refusal to let Minami perform, his decision to let Dekoration roam, his refusal to veto Rin's rehearsal, and his intervention against Uzuki's isolated training without contradiction.

The domains differ.

# XIX. Challenge versus negligence — six criteria

A developmental challenge is most ethically defensible when the following are true.

## Criterion 1 — the challenge has a plausible developmental purpose

E03 and E18 are intentionally selected because experience may build capacity.

## Criterion 2 — the scope is bounded

Backing performance, one interview/VTR assignment, one rehearsal, one mini-live: bounded experiments limit catastrophic consequence.

## Criterion 3 — there is competent infrastructure around the performer

Trainers, staff, seniors, peers, technical systems, and substitution capacity matter.

## Criterion 4 — failure remains interpretable

The person can learn what happened rather than merely absorb shame.

## Criterion 5 — the authority monitors whether the method is still working

E18 and E23 are especially strong because Producer revises his view when coping or training stops functioning developmentally.

## Criterion 6 — the performer remains a participant in the next decision

The end point is not “the adult predicted correctly.” It is greater capacity for the performer to choose and act.

Negligence becomes more likely as these conditions disappear.

# XX. Support versus authorship — the series' recurring ethical temptation

Caregivers in Cinderella Girls repeatedly face the temptation to convert concern into authorship.

Producer initially assumes too much interpretive control, then overcorrects into silence. Miku tries to guarantee Riina's future feelings. Mio initially treats leadership as responsibility to pull New Generations toward a shared future. Mishiro treats organizational survival as justification for centrally pruning unstable possibility. Uzuki herself converts others' perceived brilliance into a private qualification test she has never actually been asked to pass.

The mature correction is not the disappearance of influence.

People continue to:

- advise;
- criticize;
- assign work;
- create opportunities;
- protect;
- confront;
- evaluate;
- wait;
- and sometimes command.

What changes is the claimed object of authority.

> **Support may shape conditions, provide evidence, protect bodies, and challenge explanations. It should not claim ownership of another person's desire merely because the supporter cares deeply or holds professional power.**

---

# XXI. Failure versus disposability — why retries matter ethically

A developmental institution cannot promise that everyone will succeed.

What it can determine is whether failure automatically becomes exclusion.

Cinderella Girls repeatedly constructs environments where failure can be followed by:

- explanation;
- a second rehearsal;
- a redesigned movement;
- substitution;
- a changed assignment;
- peer repair;
- re-entry;
- or another professional path.

This does not remove consequence. Miku's protest creates tension. Mio's flight hurts the unit. Natsuki's refusal may cost future opportunity. Uzuki's withdrawal creates real scheduling labor. Fumika's inability to continue forces live restructuring.

But failure is not automatically identity.

The ethical significance of recoverability is therefore profound:

> **Retries redistribute the cost of being unfinished.**

Without retries, every developmental experiment asks the performer to wager her entire belonging on one result. With retries, institutions and relationships share some of the cost of learning.

That cost-sharing is not infinite. Document 06 rightly preserves Mishiro's challenge: institutional slack is finite. But the series clearly treats some capacity to absorb unfinishedness as part of what makes development possible at all.

---

# XXII. Care and externalities — who pays for another person's development?

One of the easiest ways to sentimentalize the series is to discuss care without discussing cost.

The anime does not allow that.

Uzuki's withdrawal requires Kanako to substitute and staff to reorganize work (`CG-INST-0031`). Rin's concern disrupts Triad Primus rehearsal. Miku/Riina's unit refusal would delay debut. Natsuki's refusal means resource planning may not produce its intended return. Minami's illness requires substitution. Fumika's live incapacity requires running-order changes.

Developmental care therefore generates externalities.

Three ethical positions are possible:

1. **individual absolutism** — the performer's need overrides all external cost;
2. **system absolutism** — external cost justifies removing or overriding the unstable individual;
3. **bounded absorption** — the system carries some cost where evidence justifies continued developmental investment, while acknowledging that the cost is real.

The series consistently favors the third without proving a universal numerical threshold.

That is why Producer's E23 defense of Uzuki is case-specific rather than a general welfare entitlement. His argument relies on accumulated evidence about her value, relation, and developmental history. Mishiro's warning remains live because a system that absorbs all costs indefinitely would cease functioning.

The mature ethical problem is not whether cost exists. It is **how much uncertainty an institution should carry before concluding that continued carrying is no longer justified**.

The anime intentionally leaves that threshold open.

---

# XXIII. Peer care — why the girls increasingly become each other's developmental infrastructure

Adult support is necessary but insufficient.

The series gradually makes peers responsible for forms of knowledge adults cannot fully possess.

Examples include:

- Uzuki mediating Producer's recruitment invitation for Rin;
- Anya sharing Minami's anxiety;
- Dekoration redesigning movement around Rika's pain;
- Sachiko/Sae/Yuki providing differentiated mentoring;
- Miku and Riina challenging each other's care assumptions;
- Ranko turning past substitution into reusable knowledge;
- Karen and Nao protecting Rin's New Generations relationship;
- Mio and Rin confronting Uzuki with relational evidence Producer cannot supply alone;
- the Project sharing plural developmental histories in E24.

Peer care is ethically powerful because peers often know what an authority cannot know.

But peer care also has limits. The girls misread one another, carry too much, and sometimes assume stability where vulnerability exists. E26's comic Rin-chocolate misunderstanding later reinforces the boundedness of relational knowledge, but the mainline already establishes it.

Thus:

> **Closeness creates standing and evidence, not omniscience.**

The mature care system is plural partly because no one person—producer, executive, teammate, or friend—has exhaustive access to another person's needs.

---

# XXIV. Accountability — care does not erase standards

The series' developmental ethic would be weak if every harmful act were dissolved into compassion.

Instead, accountability repeatedly survives care.

Mio apologizes for fleeing. Miku can condemn professional abandonment while welcoming Uzuki back. Producer accepts managerial accountability for live decisions. Mika recognizes displaced harshness. Miku is challenged when care becomes controlling. Uzuki eventually has to confront the fact that training had become avoidance.

The important distinction is:

> **accountability names what must be answered for; disposability decides whether answering for it is even allowed.**

A recoverable system keeps accountability possible because the person remains in relation long enough to repair.

Punitive exclusion can sometimes look stricter while actually avoiding the harder work of development.

Conversely, unconditional reassurance can look caring while preventing the person from seeing what she needs to change.

The series' best care scenes therefore combine:

- accurate naming of the problem;
- refusal of global condemnation;
- preserved belonging or support;
- and a next action that remains meaningfully authored.

# XXV. Longitudinal ethical progression — what changes from E01 to E25?

## E01 — uncertainty may be admitted

Authority does not need to pretend complete knowledge.

## E03 — under-readiness can justify bounded exposure

Development requires experiences slightly beyond present mastery.

## E05 — fairness requires legibility, not identical timing

Opaque progress makes effort impossible to calibrate.

## E06–E07 — experience requires interpretive repair

A performer's first failure story can be wrong without her feelings being unreal.

## E09 — audience success does not settle performer welfare

Professional utility and personal cost must be evaluated separately.

## E10 — freedom and adult responsibility coexist

Autonomy is not adult disappearance.

## E11 — meaningful choice can exist under real opportunity pressure

Agency and constraint can be simultaneously true.

## E12 — failure can be collectively regulated

The group learns to retry without locating a defective person.

## E13 — bodily safeguarding can override immediate preference

Autonomy is not absolute in acute safety domains.

## E15–E16 — development becomes an explicit ethical philosophy

Different pacing is treated as relevant evidence, but the philosophy itself must survive institutional testing.

## E17 — successful appropriation does not retroactively justify coercion

Good outcomes and good process are separate questions.

## E18 — challenge requires monitoring; coping must be judged by function

Care can interrupt both maladaptive reassurance and maladaptive effort.

## E19 — care must not own another person's future feeling

Non-intervention can become mature when peers own the relevant relational work.

## E20–E21 — legitimate autonomy can hurt others

Relational cost does not automatically prove wrongdoing or justify veto.

## E22 — command remains necessary in operational emergencies; effort can require interruption

Domain matters.

## E23 — autonomy is revisable; support has externalities; temporary invisibility is not disposability

This is the mature crisis of the framework.

## E24 — self-authored risk replaces certainty as the criterion for movement

Support follows rather than substitutes for choice.

## E25 — the system becomes better at carrying uncertainty without claiming omniscience

Developmental ethics becomes institutional capacity rather than one person's benevolence.

# XXVII. What the series does **not** argue

## 1. It does not argue that suffering is inherently developmental

Pain, fear, illness, humiliation, and conflict produce no automatic moral benefit.

Development depends on what the person and support system can do with the experience.

## 2. It does not argue that more freedom is always better

E13 bodily safeguarding and E22 operational command disprove that.

## 3. It does not argue that more protection is always better

Overprotection would prevent E03 challenge, E20 exploration, and E24 self-authored risk.

## 4. It does not argue that performer choice is perfectly unconstrained

Asterisk, Krone, and Natsuki show opportunity pressure and unequal bargaining position.

## 5. It does not argue that discomfort means exploitation

Chieri can choose participation while afraid; Miho can remain nervous while enjoying the work.

## 6. It does not argue that professional usefulness means performer wellbeing

E09 and E18 explicitly separate those outcomes.

## 7. It does not argue that care entitles the caregiver to determine desire

Miku/Riina, New Generations, and Producer's later corrections all reject that.

## 8. It does not argue that every stated preference must be obeyed indefinitely

Uzuki's E23 case shows why autonomy must remain revisable as outcomes generate new evidence.

## 9. It does not argue that failure has no consequences

Lost opportunities, damaged relationships, substitutions, schedule changes, and institutional costs remain real.

## 10. It does not argue that every temporarily failing performer must always be retained

`CG-ETHICS-0003` is case-specific. The anime rejects premature inference from invisibility to worthlessness; it does not provide a universal retention rule.

## 11. It does not argue that a good outcome proves a good process

E17's appropriation cases are the clearest safeguard.

## 12. It does not argue that adults or peers can know the performer completely

The mature ethic depends on epistemic humility.

# XXVIII. Prospective versus retrospective corrections

## Correction 1 — E03 is not “they were ready after all”

The episode explicitly says they were not fully ready. The meaningful point is scaffolded risk.

## Correction 2 — Miku's E05 protest is not merely impatience

Her demand identifies a real path-legibility problem.

## Correction 3 — Mio's E06 debut did not simply fail

Its multiple success axes must remain separate.

## Correction 4 — Producer's E07 silence was not respectful autonomy

It was avoidance produced by overcorrection.

## Correction 5 — Chieri's E09 fear does not disappear when she chooses to continue

Agency under fear is the supported formulation.

## Correction 6 — Dekoration freedom is not laissez-faire supervision

Responsibility remains with adults.

## Correction 7 — Minami's E13 desire to perform is not dispositive

Immediate bodily safety justifies protective override.

## Correction 8 — performer-owned persona does not make every professional constraint unethical

E17 shows appropriation can create real authorship, while process ethics remains independently evaluable.

## Correction 9 — coping aids are not inherently immature

Their function determines whether they support or obstruct action.

## Correction 10 — autonomy does not mean relationships are painless

Rin and Anastasia can make legitimate choices that still hurt others.

## Correction 11 — respecting Uzuki's withdrawal did not obligate Producer to preserve it unchanged

Outcomes supplied new evidence; revising support was part of respecting development rather than betraying autonomy.

## Correction 12 — waiting is not passivity in E23–E24

It becomes meaningful only after confrontation, testing, evidence gathering, and defense of membership.

## Correction 13 — Uzuki's return is not based on certainty

E24 explicitly preserves unresolved self-definition.

## Correction 14 — the finale does not prove Producer's developmental ethic universally correct

# XXX. Final specialist synthesis — development requires permission to remain unfinished without making unfinishedness consequence-free

The ethical achievement of THE IDOLM@STER CINDERELLA GIRLS is not that it creates a world where young performers are protected from failure.

They are not.

They enter stages before complete readiness. They are publicly evaluated. They receive unequal opportunities. They panic on camera. They hurt one another. They get sick. They overtrain. They misread audiences. They accept work they did not design. They reject opportunities and risk losing future ones. They discover new desires that destabilize old relationships. They withdraw. They create scheduling burdens. They fail to know what they want. They sometimes cannot even produce the public traits by which they had previously been valued.

The work's ethical question is therefore harder than whether failure should be allowed.

It asks:

> **What kind of environment lets failure remain information rather than verdict?**

The answer emerges gradually.

Failure must be specific enough to interpret. Challenge must be bounded enough to survive. Expectations must be legible enough that people can understand what a result means. Bodies must be protected even when ambition argues otherwise. Fear need not disappear before action becomes meaningful. Dependence need not count as weakness. Coping strategies may remain if they enable rather than prevent engagement. Caregivers must not claim ownership of another person's future feeling. Adults must know when to act and when to yield. Performer choice must be meaningful, but stated choice cannot become a shield against every later piece of evidence. Institutions must absorb some cost of unfinished development, while acknowledging that the cost falls on real peers, staff, schedules, and opportunities.

Most importantly, failure must not immediately become disposability.

That principle reaches its most explicit form when Uzuki becomes temporarily unable to produce the visibility through which the institution normally recognizes value. Mishiro asks the hardest institutional question: if a star cannot shine, why should the system continue to carry it? Producer answers not by denying the cost, but by denying the epistemic leap from present invisibility to nonexistence.

Then E24 prevents his defense from becoming paternalism. He cannot make Uzuki's choice for her. He can hold evidence, create conditions, defend time, and refuse premature disposal. But at the threshold itself he says:

> `選んで下さい`

And after the choice:

> `選んだその先で あなたは一人ではありません`

Those two sentences together express the mature developmental ethic more precisely than either could alone.

The first prevents care from becoming control.

The second prevents autonomy from becoming abandonment.

The series' most mature model is therefore neither perfect protection nor sovereign individualism. It is **supported self-authorship under uncertainty**.

A person may be asked to take risks without being guaranteed success. She may fail without being told the failure is unreal. She may be held accountable without being treated as disposable. She may need others without surrendering authorship. She may choose differently without proving prior relationships false. She may remain afraid without being professionally invalid. She may return before she has a final answer about herself.

The system, in turn, earns the right to challenge only by retaining responsibility for what challenge can cost.

> **Development requires permission to remain unfinished without making unfinishedness consequence-free.**

That is the ethical equilibrium the anime spends twenty-five episodes learning how to hold.
