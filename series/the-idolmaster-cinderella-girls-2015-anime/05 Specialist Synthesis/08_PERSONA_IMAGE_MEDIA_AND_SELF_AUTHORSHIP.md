# 08 — Persona, Image, Media, and Self-Authorship
## Constructed selves without false selves

## Scope and responsibility

This document answers the architecture-defined question:

> **What is the relation between person, public image, aspiration, camera, costume, and authored identity?**

Its primary object is the anime's repeated refusal of a simple opposition between a supposedly artificial public persona and a supposedly authentic private self. It examines how professional images are made, who participates in making them, how performers inhabit or resist them, how media changes the form in which identity circulates, and what it means for an identity to be constructed without therefore being false.

This document does **not** absorb the specialist responsibilities assigned elsewhere.

- Document 02 remains the canonical home for New Generations as an internal relational system.
- Document 03 owns Producer's developmental responsibility and mature decision grammar.
- Document 04 owns the individual character and unit-ecology syntheses.
- Document 05 owns Cinderella Project as collective, network, community of practice, leadership system and structure of belonging.
- Document 06 owns 346's institutional power, branding sovereignty, resource allocation and competing production philosophies.
- Document 07 will own performance, songs, audience, success and recognition as such.
- Document 09 will own the primary ethics of care, autonomy, failure and developmental risk.
- Document 10 will own the visual-metaphor system and recurring object/space motifs.
- Document 11 will own the Japanese-language and performed-vocal-state analysis.

Those categories necessarily touch this document, because persona exists through institutions, performances, images, voices, costumes and audiences. They are used here only where they directly explain **mediated identity and authorship**.

The governing claim is:

> **THE IDOLM@STER CINDERELLA GIRLS does not define authenticity as the absence of construction. It defines the most credible professional identities by the performer's capacity to recognize herself in a constructed form, to exercise meaningful authorship within it, to revise it as she develops, and to use it as a relational interface without being reduced to it.**

A public image can therefore be highly artificial in technique while remaining personally sincere. A private contradiction does not automatically expose a public fraud. A costume can constrain, enable, or be appropriated. A camera can flatten, preserve, translate, circulate or furnish counterevidence. An institution can build a form the performer comes to inhabit, or impose one that creates alienation. A familiar style can be freely re-chosen after alternatives are tested. The series' mature model is not “be spontaneous.” It is **remain an author inside mediation**.

# I. A working vocabulary — person, persona, image, role, brand, and authorship are not synonyms

The series becomes easier to read once several categories are kept distinct.

## 1. Person

The **person** is not an invisible essence accessible only in private. The anime never gives us a pure, unmediated Shimamura Uzuki, Maekawa Miku, Tada Riina, Kanzaki Ranko, Abe Nana or Moroboshi Kirari standing outside every social context. They are always speaking to someone, doing something, remembering something, aspiring toward something, being filmed, preparing for work, resting after work, or imagining a future.

Private space therefore supplies additional evidence about a person, but it does not automatically outrank public space as the location of truth.

## 2. Persona

A **persona** is a relatively stable, communicable organization of signs and behaviors through which a performer becomes legible to others. It may involve:

- lexical habits;
- props;
- costume;
- gestures;
- recurring jokes;
- aesthetic vocabulary;
- media conventions;
- audience calls;
- stylized self-description;
- or a deliberately emphasized trait.

Miku's cat identity, Nana's Usamin identity and Ranko's gothic-theatrical language are unusually explicit examples. But the concept also applies more softly to Kaede's elegant senior-idol image, Anzu's anti-work comedy, Kirari's `ハピハピ` mode, Riina's rock aspiration and the early public formulations of New Generations.

The crucial point is that **persona describes organization, not fraud**.

## 3. Public image

A **public image** is the version of the performer made legible through a particular professional channel: photograph, PR video, television segment, live, CD promotion, magazine shoot, stage costume, commercial tie-in, ambient store BGM, acting role or other mediated form.

Because the channel selects and compresses, public image is always partial. Partiality is not yet deception.

## 4. Role

A **role** is a bounded professional or fictional position the performer occupies for a task. Roles can align closely with an existing persona, conflict with it, extend it, or deliberately ask the performer to inhabit something else.

Rika's kindergarten-school television assignment, Mika's adult cosmetics brief and Mio's acting work are role problems before they are essence problems.

## 5. Brand

A **brand** is a more externally organized promise of recognizability and repeatability. It can belong to a performer, unit, department or corporation. Branding matters because professional identity must circulate across contexts, but brand can become dangerous when repeatability is mistaken for exhaustive personhood.

Document 06 owns the institutional politics of that danger. Here the concern is what happens to the performer when public form becomes too rigid or insufficiently authored.

## 6. Aspiration

An **aspiration** is an identity claim whose desired future exceeds present competence or present institutional reality.

Riina is the clearest case. Her rock identity is sincere before it is technically mature. The anime refuses to make expertise a prerequisite for sincere aspiration while also refusing to pretend aspiration and expertise are identical (`CG-CHAR-0008`, `CG-CHAR-0025`, `CG-CHAR-0048`).

## 7. Self-authorship

**Self-authorship** does not mean solitary creation without producers, cameras, stylists, directors, partners or audiences. Idol identity is structurally co-produced.

The useful question is instead:

> **Does the performer possess meaningful standing in determining what the form means, whether she recognizes herself in it, how she inhabits it, and how it may change?**

Authorship can therefore occur through:

- invention;
- selection;
- refusal;
- negotiation;
- modification;
- appropriation;
- reinterpretation;
- practice;
- or re-choosing a familiar form after alternatives have become available.

## 8. Alienation and non-alienation

The most useful mature distinction is not:

> natural / artificial.

It is:

> **alienated / non-alienated relation to constructed form.**

This is an analytical vocabulary rather than a phrase spoken by the characters (`CG-MEDIA-0010`). A form is comparatively **non-alienated** when the performer can inhabit it as something she recognizes as hers, can act through it rather than merely being displayed by it, and retains some capacity to revise or reject its meaning.

A form becomes comparatively **alienating** when an externally authored image overrides the performer's relation to herself, demands a version she cannot meaningfully inhabit, or treats her as only the carrier of someone else's preferred representation.

This distinction is deliberately scalar. The series repeatedly shows mixed cases rather than a clean moral binary.

# II. Episode 2 — professional identity begins as a representation problem, not a revelation problem

The first substantial professional task given to the emerging New Generations trio is not a live performance. It is `宣材写真`: promotional photography (`CG-MEDIA-0001`).

That ordering is architecturally important.

The girls must become **representable** before the series asks them to become fully autonomous performers. The blank studio turns self-presentation into a visible problem. The requests are deceptively simple:

- smile;
- relax;
- act normally;
- give the camera something usable.

Yet `普通` immediately proves difficult. `もっと普通に` produces the answer `普通って難しい`.

The scene rejects the fantasy that professional photography merely records a stable self already available for extraction.

Direct self-monitoring creates stiffness or overperformance. Relational play, movement and interaction precede the more successful photographs (`CG-PROD-0003`). Professional construction has **not** disappeared. Lighting, framing, photographer, studio, selection and pose remain. What changes is the condition under which expression becomes usable.

This gives the series an early principle that will remain intact through E22:

> **A good professional image is produced, but production works best when it creates conditions in which the performer can become expressive rather than demanding that she manufacture the correct expression by force of self-surveillance.**

The difference is not construction versus spontaneity. Both outcomes occur inside a designed photographic situation.

The scene also establishes a representational boundary around Producer. When invited into the group image, he declines: `皆さんでどうぞ` (`CG-PROD-0004`). He helps create the image but does not become one of the represented idols.

Later episodes complicate this separation—his own clothing and grooming become organizational representation in E14—but E02 already distinguishes **the labor of producing an image from the people whose identities the image publicly carries**.

# III. Episodes 3–4 — the camera becomes visible as a mediating technology

Episode 3 supplies an early contrast between **performing inside an inherited structure** and authoring public speech.

New Generations succeeds as backing dancers because the choreography, musical time and stage roles already provide a professional grammar. At the later open microphone, that scaffold thins and spontaneous public speech becomes difficult (`CG-PERF-0002`). The episode does not imply that structured performance is fake. It shows that **different media/performance forms demand different degrees and kinds of authorship**.

Episode 4 then makes the camera itself a central object.

Its opening re-presents E03's `TOKIMEKIエスカレート` material in a quieter, camera-bounded form immediately before the PR-video assignment (`CG-MEDIA-0008`). Lived stage event has already become reproducible media. The same performance can now circulate in a different sensory and institutional form.

The PR-video sequence expands this into an ensemble experiment.

The original temptation was to say that the camera “reveals the real person.” The source audit explicitly revised that claim (`CG-MEDIA-0002`). The camera records **bounded public versions whose relation to the person differs by performer**.

That revision is foundational for the entire series.

A camera can capture something useful or unexpected. It can also create pressure, invite self-consciousness, reward a selective trait, or allow a performer to stage herself deliberately. It is neither neutral truth machine nor automatic falsifier.

The Cinderella Project's responses reveal several different modes of mediated identity.

## 1. Miku — persona as prepared professional craft

Miku consciously activates the cat-idol register and carries cat ears so she is ready for work (`CG-CHAR-0005`). Her complaint—`これじゃ全然みくの魅力が伝わらないよ`—is not embarrassment that the camera failed to expose a hidden ordinary self. It is professional dissatisfaction that the recording has not communicated the form she wants to present.

Her persona is therefore already:

- deliberate;
- materially supported by props;
- linguistically marked;
- audience-oriented;
- and evaluated by Miku herself.

## 2. Chieri — preparation can be sincere without being sufficient

Chieri arrives with a prepared self-introduction, but direct camera attention destabilizes her (`CG-CHAR-0006`). A clover draws her attention outward; the camera then obtains a usable smile.

The lesson is not “preparation is fake.” Chieri's preparation is real work. The problem is that the act of monitoring herself as an object for the camera overwhelms the performance.

Her case therefore parallels E02: mediation becomes more successful when attention can attach to something meaningful rather than remain trapped in self-inspection.

## 3. Ranko — persona as self-authored world

Ranko does not need the camera to invent her aesthetic. She arrives with a developed symbolic vocabulary and dictates important conditions of her representation (`CG-CHAR-0007`).

She also performs one of the series' most elegant demonstrations of authored mediation when the ordinary 5 p.m. civic announcement becomes `天使の声` (`CG-MOTIF-0017`).

This is not delusion replacing shared reality. Other characters can decode the referent. Ranko **aesthetically re-describes a common soundscape**.

Persona here is a way of authoring relation to the world.

## 4. Riina — persona as sincere future claim

Riina's `クールでロックなアイドル` identity is not yet supported by robust technical knowledge (`CG-CHAR-0008`). Yet the anime does not treat the discrepancy as a lie.

Her public identity is partly **proleptic**: she inhabits the language of a person she is still trying to become.

That will matter enormously later. Self-authorship can be temporally ahead of expertise.

## 5. Anzu — refusal can itself become professional content

Anzu's anti-work language is both sincere preference and recognizable public material. Yet she also says `杏もアイドルの端くれ` and understands that filming creates some obligation to present something (`CG-CHAR-0009`).

Her refusal is therefore neither pure private truth nor pure manufactured gimmick. It is a selectively intensified part of the person that can become content.

Taken together, E04 establishes the series' central anti-essentialism:

> **Different performers can relate to mediated identity through craft, anxiety, symbolic authorship, aspiration, refusal, play or selective exaggeration. No one mechanism has privileged access to the “real self.”**

# IV. Media does not merely display identity — it transforms, preserves, circulates, and sometimes corrects it

The series gradually expands the meaning of media beyond “camera pointed at idol.”

## 1. Media transforms event into reproducible object

E04's opening transformation of E03's live material already demonstrates that a performance changes status when it becomes recorded and replayable (`CG-MEDIA-0008`).

The event had been local, embodied and temporally bounded. Recording turns it into something that can be revisited, circulated and positioned next to later professional work.

## 2. Media can preserve counterevidence without becoming neutral truth

In E07, photographs of New Generations' debut contradict Mio's shame-dominated recollection (`CG-MEDIA-0003`). Producer does not say that the photograph exhausts what happened. He says he thinks it shows a good smile.

Mio realizes that she had been looking almost exclusively at audience quantity.

The photograph therefore functions as **counterevidence to a totalizing internal narrative**. It does not become infallible objective truth.

This distinction matters throughout the series. Mediated images can preserve evidence that memory or self-judgment suppresses, but every image remains framed and partial.

## 3. Media lets identity circulate beyond bodily presence

Candy Island's `Happy×2Days` moves among fan encounter, released single and television self-promotion (`CG-PERF-0022`). Dekoration's recorded music becomes shopping-environment BGM while the performers are physically absent (`CG-MEDIA-0009`). E14 shows growing circulation of solo songs and media work without enough evidence to equate visibility with quantified commercial success (`CG-MEDIA-0006`).

Professional identity becomes **portable**.

The person is not only what an audience encounters face-to-face. Songs, images, interviews, programs and branded environments carry selected forms into spaces where the performer is absent.

That portability is professionally necessary and ethically dangerous in the same way all representation is: the form can travel farther than the person's immediate capacity to explain or revise it.

## 4. Media can become developmental practice

By E17–E18, media work is no longer just publicity. Producer explicitly argues that variety television can draw out idol individuality (`CG-PROD-0036`). `とときら学園` then becomes an operating production ecology in which performers can try formats, receive feedback and propose new work (`CG-INST-0022`).

The medium can therefore participate in development.

But the series refuses to let “usable media” equal “good development,” a distinction that becomes decisive in E18 and will be addressed below.

# V. Ranko — the clearest case of persona as translation rather than disguise

Ranko's arc gives the series its most concentrated answer to the question:

> **What does it mean to professionally mediate a person whose self-presentation is already highly stylized?**

The wrong answer would be to search for the ordinary girl underneath and discard the theatrical layer.

The anime instead asks production to achieve higher resolution.

## 1. Her aesthetic distinctions are real distinctions

Producer initially collapses Ranko's dark/gothic imagery toward generic horror. Ranko explicitly dislikes horror (`CG-CHAR-0016`).

The conflict matters because low-resolution categorization can misrepresent a person even when it uses superficially adjacent imagery.

Producer's early question—whether the distinction is really important—later becomes the recognition that it is `大事` (`CG-PROD-0015`).

Production succeeds when it learns why the distinction matters.

## 2. The drawing is not an obstacle to communication; it is communication

Ranko's drawing becomes an intermediate authored representation (`CG-MEDIA-0004`). Producer asks:

> `ここからこの絵のイメージに至るには どのような経緯があるのですか？`

The question is about **path**, not replacement.

He does not demand that the image be translated into plain speech before it can count as evidence. The drawing itself becomes part of a shared interpretive process.

This gives the series a sophisticated model of mediated communication:

> **When direct explanation is difficult, another authored medium can become a bridge without being treated as less truthful merely because it is symbolic.**

## 3. Props can be commitment rather than decoration

Ranko's parasol, books and drawings are explicitly read as `拘り` (`CG-MOTIF-0005`).

The objects are not neutral accessories pasted onto a generic idol. They are evidence of sustained aesthetic commitment.

Document 10 will own their motif life. Here their importance is that **material styling can testify to authorship**.

## 4. Successful production translates rather than normalizes

The final Rosenburg Engel form preserves Ranko's symbolic vocabulary (`CG-PERF-0007`). The retrospective sound audit strengthens this: the song places collaboration—`貴方と手を携え`, `新たな旋律`—inside the gothic field rather than requiring collaboration to appear in ordinary language (`CG-PERF-0021`).

Her later `私の声を聴き取ってくれて` therefore follows a professional artifact that has already demonstrated what “hearing” produced.

The deepest result is:

> **Another person can enter Ranko's authored imaginative world without replacing it.**

The relation develops inside persona.

This is why theatrical speech and direct gratitude cannot be ranked as false versus real (`CG-VOICE-0005`). Both belong to Ranko's repertoire.

Ranko's case becomes the series' strongest argument that **mediation can increase fidelity rather than reduce it**, provided the production process learns the performer's distinctions instead of flattening them.

# VI. Miku and Riina — construction, aspiration, contradiction, and the right to remain unfinished

Asterisk is especially useful because Miku and Riina represent two different ways a public identity can be constructed without being fraudulent.

Miku's cat identity is **highly developed craft**. Riina's rock identity is **developmentally incomplete aspiration**.

The series validates both, but for different reasons.

## 1. Miku — professional construction can be personally owned

By E11, Miku's relation to cat-idol identity is impossible to reduce to an involuntary quirk. She calls the cat ears `商売道具` and later, even more explicitly, `お仕事に大事なアイテム` (`CG-CHAR-0024`, `CG-CHAR-0042`).

In E16 she says:

> `ネコはみくにとって アイデンティティーなの`

and explains that putting on the ears changes the atmosphere.

This is a crucial formulation because it joins three things often treated as opposites:

1. **identity**;
2. **professional technique**;
3. **deliberate construction**.

The ears are not sacred because they are natural. They matter because Miku has made them part of how she works.

Her persona is therefore better understood as a **practiced interface**.

It communicates a recognizable form, helps her enter a performance state, organizes audience expectation, and gives her a professional vocabulary she knows how to use.

None of that means the cat persona exhausts Miku.

## 2. Private contradiction does not expose public fraud

E11 deliberately gives Miku a comic contradiction: she promotes cat imagery while disliking fish (`CG-MEDIA-0005`).

Her answer—`それとこれとは別ニャ`—is almost a thesis statement for the series.

A public image does not need to include every private preference to remain sincere.

If Miku were required to like every culturally associated “cat” trait before her persona counted as authentic, professional self-presentation would become an impossible demand for total biographical consistency.

The anime instead permits **selective authorship**.

Miku chooses the cat field as a professional identity without surrendering the rest of herself to the field's stereotypes.

The same applies to her serious work discipline. `アイドルは体が資本だから` coexists with comic cat styling. Seriousness does not require the cat language to disappear (`CG-CHAR-0024`).

Even her vulnerable E05 confession only modulates the density of cat-speech markers; it does not produce a clean “persona off” state (`CG-VOICE-0002`). Her more serious E11 register similarly reduces ornamentation while remaining recognizably Miku (`CG-VOICE-0008`).

The result is not two selves. It is **one person with multiple context-sensitive realizations of an authored persona**.

## 3. Riina — aspiration can be sincere before competence catches up

Riina presents the opposite risk of misreading.

Her rock identity can look fraudulent because her concrete knowledge is initially thin. She sometimes relies on vague vocabulary, cool-sounding references or attitude while her guitar skill remains basic (`CG-CHAR-0008`, `CG-CHAR-0025`).

But the anime repeatedly refuses the conclusion:

> expertise gap = false identity.

Instead, Riina's “rock” functions as a **future-oriented self-description**.

She is not simply reporting what she already is. She is naming the direction in which she wants to become more capable.

Natsuki's introduction in E14 and E19 is therefore productive precisely because it gives aspiration an external standard. Natsuki can actually play; she asks concrete questions; Riina becomes more willing to admit difficulty and report practice (`CG-PERF-0029`, `CG-CHAR-0048`).

Maturation does not require Riina to confess that she was never rock.

It requires her to make the identity more epistemically responsible.

She moves from:

> “rock is whatever I say feels rock”

through confrontation with expertise toward:

> “this is difficult, I am practicing, I still intend to improve, and Asterisk is the place in which this identity has become mine.”

That is the force of `アスタリスクが私にとってのロックなの` (`CG-CHAR-0049`).

The line should not be read as contempt for technique. It comes after technique has become more visible.

It is a claim about **authorship of developmental location**.

## 4. Asterisk — recognition without assimilation

E16 makes this explicit at the relational level. Riina does not become a cat idol. She still refuses the ears. What changes is that she recognizes Miku's refusal to abandon her own form as `ロック` (`CG-REL-0035`).

That is not aesthetic convergence.

It is a form of mutual authorship recognition:

> **I do not need your identity for myself in order to recognize your right to author it.**

Asterisk therefore becomes one of the series' clearest demonstrations that compatibility can be built from **respected difference**, not hidden sameness.

# VII. Anzu, Kirari, and Kaede — public identity can be selective without being exhaustive

Several supporting cases reinforce the same principle from different angles.

## 1. Anzu — self-caricature as authored compression

Anzu's anti-work identity is comic, repeatable and professionally useful. It is also not simply false.

She really does prefer rest and avoidance. But she also understands professional obligation, performs when needed, can act strategically, and demonstrates competence that the anti-work image does not foreground (`CG-CHAR-0009`).

Her persona is therefore a form of **self-caricature**: a real trait is amplified into a recognizable public grammar.

Caricature becomes false only if the audience or institution mistakes the emphasized trait for the whole person.

The series does not.

## 2. Kirari — stylization is not emotional shallowness

Kirari's `ハピハピ` mode can easily be misread as a surface mask because it is so consistent and so highly stylized.

E10 complicates that inference. When she feels responsible for disruption, characteristic verbal markers diminish and guilt becomes visible (`CG-CHAR-0021`).

Yet this does not retroactively expose cheerfulness as fake.

E18 makes the opposite point even more clearly. Kirari discusses her height, clothing, work with Anzu, audience enjoyment and why she can smile **inside the established stylized register** (`CG-VOICE-0026`). Serious reflection does not require a plain voice.

The stylization is flexible enough to carry serious content.

Thus:

> **A persona can be non-exhaustive without being false.**

Kirari has emotional states the `ハピハピ` register does not always foreground. That only proves she is more than the public grammar, not that the public grammar is fraudulent.

## 3. Kaede — mature professionalism contains many registers without ranking them by truth

E15 extends the argument to an established senior idol. Kaede can possess:

- elegant public image;
- pun-loving ordinary humor;
- practical backstage labor;
- intimate relation to a small venue;
- and commanding stage presence.

The episode does not designate one as the uniquely authentic Kaede (`CG-MEDIA-0007`).

This matters because persona theory is often easiest to misread in eccentric characters. Kaede shows that the same principle applies to polished senior professionalism.

A mature professional identity may contain **multiple socially legible modes** without requiring an “off-stage truth” to debunk the stage image.

# VIII. Episode 16 — the decisive authorship crisis

Episode 16 turns the series' earlier persona observations into an explicit conflict over who is entitled to define a professional identity.

Mishiro orders departments to reconsider performer `キャラクター`, reduce variety-oriented work, strengthen artist branding and align the resulting forms with corporate prestige (`CG-INST-0018`).

The crucial correction is that Mishiro is **not anti-construction**.

She wants `スター性` and `別世界のような物語性`.

Her project is itself highly aestheticized.

The conflict is therefore not fantasy versus reality.

It is **centralized authorship versus performer-recognized authorship**.

## 1. Nana — the identity that looks most artificial becomes the strongest test

Abe Nana is the ideal case because Usamin is visibly elaborate:

- fictionalized origin;
- stylized language;
- props and decoration;
- recurring calls;
- choreographed audience participation;
- sustained comic mythology.

If construction automatically implied falsity, Usamin would be the easiest identity to dismiss.

Instead, the episode makes the opposite case (`CG-CHAR-0041`).

Usamin material permeates Nana's private environment. She continues its grammar away from visible audience contexts. She explicitly says:

> `ウサミンは キャラとかじゃないんですよ`

and connects the identity to a long vocational dream:

> `もうちょっとで 夢叶いそうだったのになぁ`

> `やっぱりまだ諦めたくなーい`

Her private fatigue remains real. The work has bodily cost.

But fatigue does not reveal Usamin as counterfeit. It reveals that a sincere professional identity can be **demanding to sustain**.

That is a much richer account of persona than “mask.”

## 2. Plainness can be more alienating than theatricality

Under institutional pressure, Nana attempts a more ordinary `安部菜々` presentation. The result is less fluent and less legible to the audience. The crowd asks `誰？`; the familiar interaction grammar is missing (`CG-VOICE-0023`, `CG-PERF-0031`).

Miku intentionally restores the Usamin call, the audience joins, and Nana's professional fluency returns.

This gives the series a decisive demonstration:

> **Removing visible artifice can produce a less authentic professional state when the artifice was performer-owned.**

The “plain” form is not automatically true merely because it is less stylized.

## 3. Persona can be audience technology without becoming audience property

The Usamin call changes the room. It creates an addressable relation between performer and audience (`CG-PERF-0031`).

Miku recognizes this before she turns persona defense into an argument (`CG-REL-0034`). She is not merely defending eccentric self-expression. She has observed that Usamin **does professional work**:

- forms an audience;
- changes atmosphere;
- generates participation;
- encourages other performers;
- creates recognizable affective continuity.

Persona is therefore a relational interface.

But the interface does not become audience property. Audience recognition does not authorize others to decide that Nana must remain Usamin forever.

The performer remains the relevant author.

## 4. Miku's formulation joins identity and tool

Miku's own E16 language supplies the theoretical bridge:

> `ネコはみくにとって アイデンティティーなの`

> `ネコミミはお仕事に 大事なアイテムなの`

Identity and tool coexist.

This makes the series' position unusually clear:

> **A professional device can become part of identity when the performer owns the relation to it.**

## 5. Authorship/non-alienation is a better model than artificial/natural

`CG-MEDIA-0010` formalizes the mature conclusion.

Both Miku and Nana visibly construct their personas. Yet the forms are performer-owned and professionally effective. The externally mandated “plain Nana” state is less authored and less fluent.

The relevant distinction is therefore not:

> mediated / unmediated.

It is:

> **owned mediation / alienated mediation.**

This does not mean self-authorship guarantees comfort or success. It means the performer is not merely being represented by a form she cannot recognize as hers.

## 6. `作られた笑顔` does not mean “anything constructed is false”

Producer's `Power of Smile` presentation warns against `作られた笑顔` and values `本物の笑顔` (`CG-PROD-0034`).

Taken superficially, this could appear to contradict E16's defense of constructed persona.

It does not.

The episode itself supplies the correction. Highly constructed Usamin and cat-idol forms can mediate genuine enjoyment and audience relation. What Producer rejects is not **all production**, but the reduction of performer affect to an externally demanded surface response.

The better distinction is:

> **a smile can be produced by a mediated, highly constructed situation without being an alienated smile manufactured solely to satisfy an external image command.**

That nuance becomes indispensable in E17 and E22.

# IX. Episode 17 — authenticity can survive constraint through appropriation

E16 might tempt a simpler theory:

> authenticity means refusing externally imposed forms.

Episode 17 immediately complicates that.

Rika and Mika are both given professional forms they did not originate. Neither resolution consists solely of escape.

Instead, they **appropriate** the assigned form.

## 1. Rika — self-continuity does not require surface continuity

Rika initially equates maturity with externally visible adultness. She wants to resemble Mika, wants adult-coded clothes, and resists the child-coded kindergarten presentation (`CG-CHAR-0043`).

Two peers offer apparently contradictory advice.

Anzu:

> `何を着たって自分は自分`

Kirari:

> `自分らしく工夫して オシャレすると`

> `自分がテンションアップアップに なれるかどうかが重要`

The episode refuses to choose one proposition over the other (`CG-REL-0036`).

Anzu preserves **continuity of person** across costume.

Kirari preserves the **real effect of mediation** on affect and performance.

Rika needs both.

She does not have to become a different person because of the costume. But the costume still matters because she has to find a way to inhabit it.

The resolution is `だって私は私だしー`, immediately followed by self-directed styling and the `セクシーカリスマギャル` introduction.

This is not essence triumphing over clothes.

It is **authorship returning inside clothes**.

## 2. A form can stay fixed while the relation to it changes

Rika's rehearsal is repeatedly stopped for low energy and insufficient smile. The final broadcast uses the same general television format, but her performance becomes more fluent after she personalizes it (`CG-VOICE-0024`).

The professional machinery did not disappear.

The causal change is her relation to it.

This gives the series a powerful anti-romantic principle:

> **Self-authorship does not require escaping professional form; it can consist of finding enough room inside a form to make it usable as one's own.**

## 3. Mika — appropriation can occur in a prestige-oriented brief as well

Mika's adult cosmetics assignment pushes her away from the familiar gal-oriented image. She experiences that pressure before reproducing some of it downward toward Rika.

Yet her final solution is not simple refusal.

She inserts a characteristic pose into the adult campaign. The creative side initially questions it, then recognizes `芯` and `力` (`CG-MEDIA-0011`).

Again, externally initiated form and performer authorship coexist.

## 4. Appropriation does not retroactively justify coercion

This is one of Document 08's most important guardrails.

Because Rika and Mika successfully appropriate their assignments, it would be easy to infer:

> the initial pressure was therefore good.

The evidence does not support that.

`CG-MEDIA-0011` explicitly retains the distinction. A performer can create something meaningful inside a constrained condition without the constraint itself becoming ethically legitimate.

Self-authorship is demonstrated by the performer's capacity to **transform the relation to the form**, not by a retroactive absolution of whoever imposed it.

## 5. Identity is iterative rather than essential

E17's title and musical framing describe roads made by walking, detours, stumbling and repeated refinement of `私らしさ` (`CG-MOTIF-0022`).

This gives self-authorship a temporal dimension.

The self is neither:

- a fixed inner essence waiting to be expressed;
- nor an infinitely arbitrary surface that can be rewritten from outside without loss.

It is **path-dependent**.

What counts as “me” is partly accumulated through prior commitments, experiments, failures, relationships and re-appropriations.

---

# X. Media work is not synonymous with self-expression — E18 separates utility, fluency, grounding, and ratification

E18 is important because it prevents the series' authorship model from becoming sentimental.

A media artifact can be **professionally usable** even when the performer does not experience the work as a developmental success.

## 1. Chieri and Kanako — usable awkwardness is still awkwardness

Their first location segment is judged usable because the footage has `初々しい` value (`CG-PERF-0033`).

But the performers correctly recognize that they did not perform the interview task well.

This creates a necessary distinction:

> **media utility ≠ performer fluency ≠ performer comfort ≠ developmental success.**

A production can benefit from nervousness without nervousness therefore becoming the ideal state for the person.

This guards against a common interpretive error in idol media: assuming that because vulnerability is charming on screen, the process producing it is automatically good for the performer.

Document 09 will own the ethical dimension. Here the media-theory point is enough:

> **The camera can convert an imperfect state into usable content without resolving what that state means for the person.**

## 2. Grounded representation is still performance

The Edo-kiriko segment gives a complementary lesson (`CG-PERF-0034`).

Generic praise fails when the craftsman asks:

> `切子のどこが好きなんだい？`

The later attempt improves because the girls observe details, admit what they do not know, and ask concrete questions.

The result is more credible representation.

But it remains professional media performance.

The series does not equate groundedness with lack of mediation. Instead:

> **Credible representation requires a sufficient relation to the represented object, not the abolition of professional form.**

## 3. Anzu and Kirari — marketability does not settle ownership

Their body/persona contrast becomes professionally valuable. Ratings and stakeholder praise support the format (`CG-REL-0039`).

Yet Producer checks Kirari separately, and Anzu later asks what she actually thinks.

Kirari ratifies the pairing and connects it to her own enjoyment and audience `ハピハピ` response.

The series therefore refuses two simplifications:

- marketability proves exploitation;
- marketability proves consent.

Neither follows.

The relevant question is whether the performer **recognizes and ratifies the professional form**.

That makes ratification part of authorship.

---

# XI. Riina and Natsuki — self-authorship does not abolish standards, technique, or consequence

Riina's later arc matters because a shallow theory of authored identity could become empty relativism:

> If I say it is rock, it is rock; therefore no external standard matters.

E14 and E19 reject that conclusion.

Natsuki's presence creates an **audible and embodied standard** (`CG-PERF-0029`). She has practiced skill, genre knowledge and a relation to rock that Riina can observe rather than merely name.

Riina's response is development, not identity collapse.

By E19, she speaks more concretely about practice and difficulty:

> `CDの真似してみたりするんだけど 難しくて`

> `でも絶対上達する`

(`CG-CHAR-0048`)

The anime therefore distinguishes:

- **authority over whether an aspiration matters to you**;
- from **authority over whether you have already achieved its competencies**.

Self-authorship governs the first more strongly than the second.

Riina can sincerely choose rock while still being wrong, inexperienced or incomplete about rock practice.

This is one of the series' most important checks against treating authenticity as epistemic immunity.

## 1. `アスタリスクが私にとってのロック` is a commitment, not an anti-skill slogan

When Riina says Asterisk is her rock (`CG-CHAR-0049`), the statement follows:

- exposure of her knowledge gaps;
- increased practice;
- Natsuki's more developed performance;
- and explicit acknowledgment that improvement takes time.

The phrase therefore means something closer to:

> **This is the relationship and professional form in which I recognize the rock identity I am trying to become capable of inhabiting.**

It does not mean that technique is irrelevant.

## 2. Mishiro's proposed band clarifies the authorship issue

The proposed idol-rock band would be professionally resourced. Songs, costumes, concept and live plan would be prepared by first-rate staff, and Mishiro guarantees success if the performers execute the plan (`CG-INST-0023`).

Nothing in the episode proves that the resulting art would be bad.

The tension is **distribution of authorship**.

A professionally excellent form can still be alienating if the performer is asked primarily to execute a concept she does not recognize as the place where her identity should develop.

## 3. Natsuki makes the cost of refusal explicit

Natsuki refuses Mishiro's plan because singing as someone else's ordered execution does not fit her conception of rock. Mishiro warns that another opportunity may not come; Natsuki accepts the risk (`CG-INST-0024`).

This matters because self-authorship is not presented as costless sovereignty.

A performer can choose authorship over an opportunity and lose real resources.

The series therefore avoids two fantasies at once:

- institutions have no legitimate role in shaping professional form;
- self-authorship means every personally preferred path can be pursued without tradeoff.

The actual model is negotiated, path-dependent and materially constrained.

---

# XII. Acting and role distance — a professional role can create knowledge rather than conceal the self

Mio's E21 acting work adds another useful complication.

Persona and self-authorship are often discussed as though professional form must express something already possessed by the performer. Acting demonstrates a different possibility.

Mio initially struggles: she stumbles, apologizes, relies heavily on the script and needs redirection. Later she sustains the dramatic role well enough that Rin describes the result as `ドキドキ` and Uzuki calls it `素敵` (`CG-PERF-0041`).

Most importantly, Mio says the work helped her begin to understand Rin's feelings.

The role is therefore not merely:

> a false identity worn over Mio.

It becomes **epistemic practice**.

By inhabiting a fictional other, Mio gains language for a real relational problem.

This establishes a further principle:

> **Not every authored professional form needs to be autobiographical in order to be personally meaningful.**

A role can be deliberately non-self while still changing the performer.

This is one reason the series' persona theory cannot be reduced to “accurate self-expression.” Professional art also includes disciplined movement away from immediate self-description.

E26's mock historical production later confirms this in paratextual form: recognizable member-specific idioms become portable material inside fictional roles (`CG-PERF-0051`). Mature identity can be reused, redirected and played with rather than either frozen or discarded.

---

# XIII. Uzuki — when a historically successful public image stops being automatically available

Uzuki is the series' most important late test because her public professional image is comparatively **low-concept**.

She is not organized around a flamboyant persona like Miku or Nana, nor an aesthetic world like Ranko, nor a strong aspirational label like Riina's rock.

Her most repeatable public asset is `笑顔`.

That apparent simplicity creates its own danger.

## 1. E22 — direct image instruction cannot recover the old strength

At the magazine shoot, Uzuki is told:

> `ポーズ固いなぁ`

> `はい二人とも笑って`

Miho produces the requested expression. Uzuki is told:

> `卯月ちゃん表情固いよ`

(`CG-MEDIA-0012`)

This is a direct echo of E02's image-production problem, but now the stakes are different.

Early Uzuki could be described by others as someone whose smile emerged readily inside relational activity. By E22, the professional instruction to reproduce that image cannot recover it.

The episode therefore shows that **a public image can be historically true and presently unavailable**.

That does not make the earlier image fake.

Nor does the present failure prove that Uzuki has “lost her real self.”

It means her relation to the form has changed.

The image has become something she is trying to reproduce under comparative pressure rather than something emerging from the conditions that once sustained it.

## 2. A successful brand can become alienating when the person can no longer inhabit it

Uzuki's smile is dangerous precisely because other people have good evidence for it.

Producer selected her through smile language. Peers value it. Audiences recognize it. The series repeatedly associates it with relational effect.

But once Uzuki's own self-conception becomes unstable, the instruction “smile” risks turning a once-living professional strength into a demanded sign.

This is the same authorship problem E16 dramatized with Nana from the opposite direction.

- Nana suffers when a performer-owned theatrical form is removed.
- Uzuki suffers when a historically successful familiar form is demanded after her relation to it has destabilized.

The series' mature persona theory therefore cannot be:

> preserve the old persona at all costs.

It must be:

> **preserve the performer's capacity to author her relation to public form, including the possibility that a formerly successful form may need to be re-entered, revised, or temporarily withheld.**

## 3. E24 — public re-entry precedes restored polish

Uzuki's Christmas re-entry is staged in ordinary school uniform between Rin and Mio's idol costumes (`CG-PERF-0046`).

Document 10 owns the Cinderella-costume implications, and Document 07 owns the performance argument. For persona theory, the key point is simpler:

> **the anime lets Uzuki test public presence before restoring external polish.**

Her return is not validated by a newly manufactured perfect image first.

The person re-enters action, and polished idol representation can follow.

This reverses the alienating order exposed in E22.

## 4. E25 — public identity becomes inhabited plural speech

The finale's `こんにちはシンデレラガールズです` follows `夢をみてる / 夢を生きてる / 夢を信じている` (`CG-PERF-0050`).

The plural self-introduction does not erase differentiated individual identities.

It presents “Cinderella Girls” as an **inhabited professional category** rather than an image externally applied to people who have not yet entered it.

Public identity is credible because the series has shown the labor, disagreement, divergence and authorship required to inhabit it.

## 5. E26 — familiar continuity can itself be self-authored

The supplementary fashion vignette supplies a useful low-stakes endpoint (`CG-CHAR-0062`).

Uzuki tries multiple alternative styles, including member-coded possibilities. Producer approves nearly everything, which proves insufficient to determine her preference (`CG-PROD-0056`). The question eventually returns to Uzuki.

She chooses her familiar presentation.

This is important because development is often misread as visible transformation.

The series instead allows:

> **trying alternatives and freely choosing continuity.**

The familiar self is not automatically evidence of stagnation. Once alternatives are genuinely available, remaining similar can be an authored choice.

# XIV. The camera and media system — nine recurrent operations

Across the series, media performs at least nine analytically distinct operations. Treating all mediated representation as one thing obscures the actual structure.

## 1. Selection

A camera or edit selects some traits and excludes others.

E04's PR videos make this visible. Anzu's refusal, Miku's cat presentation, Chieri's clover smile and Ranko's symbolic staging are all bounded selections.

## 2. Compression

A professional image condenses a complex person into a repeatable public grammar.

Miku's cat identity and Anzu's anti-work identity are both compressions. The danger begins when compression is mistaken for exhaustive description.

## 3. Transformation

Recording changes the status of an event. E04's re-presentation of E03's live material (`CG-MEDIA-0008`) is the clearest early example.

## 4. Circulation

Songs, photographs, BGM, television and magazines allow professional identity to travel beyond bodily presence (`CG-PERF-0022`, `CG-MEDIA-0009`, `CG-MEDIA-0006`).

## 5. Evidence preservation

Images can preserve aspects of an event against distorted memory. Mio's E07 photographs demonstrate this (`CG-MEDIA-0003`).

But preserved evidence remains framed evidence.

## 6. Translation

Media can make a difficult-to-explain identity shareable. Ranko's drawing and final professional concept are the strongest examples (`CG-MEDIA-0004`, `CG-PERF-0007`).

## 7. Amplification

A trait that exists in the person can become more visible through professional construction. Anzu's reluctance, Miku's catness and Riina's rock aspiration are all amplified in different ways.

## 8. Constraint

A medium can demand specific poses, costumes, rhythms or affective outputs. Rika's E17 assignment and Uzuki's E22 shoot make constraint visible (`CG-MEDIA-0011`, `CG-MEDIA-0012`).

Constraint does not determine alienation by itself; the performer's ability to appropriate the form matters.

## 9. Developmental affordance

Media can create a task in which the performer learns something she did not already possess. `とときら学園`, the Edo-kiriko interview and Mio's acting are examples (`CG-PROD-0036`, `CG-PERF-0034`, `CG-PERF-0041`).

This taxonomy explains why “media” is neither simply mask nor mirror.

It is a **production environment that changes what can become visible, repeatable, knowable and learnable**.

What matters is not conformity to one authenticity script, but the ongoing relationship between person and professional mediation.

# XVI. Self-authorship is co-authorship, not sovereign isolation

The term **self-authorship** can mislead if it suggests that the idol alone creates everything associated with her public identity.

The anime consistently shows the opposite.

Professional identities are co-produced by:

- performers;
- producers;
- directors;
- photographers;
- costume and styling staff;
- songwriters and composers;
- other idols;
- media formats;
- institutional opportunities;
- and audiences.

Ranko's successful professional form is collaborative. Rika's solution emerges from Anzu and Kirari's different advice. Nana's Usamin identity becomes publicly effective through audience call-and-response and Miku's intervention. Mio's acting requires direction. Uzuki's image is relationally recognized by peers and Producer.

The relevant question is therefore not:

> Did the performer make this alone?

It is:

> **Does co-production preserve her standing as a subject of the form, or reduce her to an object through which someone else's concept is executed?**

That distinction explains why externally supplied material can be legitimate.

A producer can propose a unit. A director can assign a role. A stylist can select clothes. A company can create an opportunity. An audience can participate in a call. None of these automatically destroys authorship.

The danger appears when the performer has insufficient room to:

- understand the form;
- recognize herself in it;
- contest it;
- adapt it;
- or decide what relationship she wants to maintain with it.

Self-authorship in this series is therefore **relational agency inside professional co-production**.

# XVII. Construction, truth, and the limits of “authenticity” language

“Authenticity” is useful only if carefully defined.

The series repeatedly defeats several common binaries.

## 1. Persona is not fake; private behavior is not automatically true

Miku's fish dislike, Riina's mint dislike, Kirari's guilt, Nana's fatigue and Kaede's ordinary humor all expand the person without invalidating the public form (`CG-MEDIA-0005`, `CG-CHAR-0021`, `CG-MEDIA-0007`).

## 2. Spontaneity is not automatically more authentic than preparation

Chieri's prepared introduction fails under camera pressure, but the lesson is not that preparation is false. New Generations succeeds inside structured choreography before it can author open-mic speech (`CG-PERF-0002`). Professional competence often depends on structure.

## 3. Polish is not automatically alienating

Ranko's highly produced final presentation is more faithful to her than the earlier low-resolution horror proposal. Usamin's elaborate performance is more performer-owned than plain Nana under external command.

## 4. Refusal is not the only form of agency

Rika and Mika appropriate imposed forms (`CG-MEDIA-0011`). Self-authorship can occur through modification rather than exit.

## 5. Appropriation does not justify coercion

A good outcome inside pressure does not retroactively make the pressure ethically good.

## 6. Audience recognition does not own the performer

Usamin works as an audience grammar, but audience familiarity does not eliminate Nana's authority over whether to continue it.

## 7. Expertise does not create identity, but identity does not create expertise

Riina's arc requires both propositions to remain true (`CG-CHAR-0048`, `CG-CHAR-0049`).

## 8. Media utility does not equal performer success

Chieri and Kanako's usable awkwardness in E18 is the cleanest case (`CG-PERF-0033`).

## 9. A photograph can be evidence without being complete truth

Mio's E07 photos challenge her memory without becoming neutral omniscience (`CG-MEDIA-0003`).

## 10. A familiar self can be newly chosen

Uzuki's E26 styling experiment makes continuity compatible with authorship (`CG-CHAR-0062`).

Because of these distinctions, the best series-level use of “authenticity” is not:

> showing the unfiltered real person.

It is:

> **a sufficiently non-alienated relation between person and mediated professional form, sustained by meaningful authorship, revisability and recognition.**

# XVIII. Longitudinal progression — from being photographed to choosing how the image remains yours

The persona/media architecture develops in a clear sequence.

## E02 — representation

The girls discover that “be normal and smile” is not a transparent instruction. A professional image must be made (`CG-MEDIA-0001`, `CG-PROD-0003`).

## E03 — inherited form versus authored speech

Structured performance can succeed before spontaneous public self-presentation becomes fluent (`CG-PERF-0002`).

## E04 — mediation

The camera becomes visibly selective. Different performers bring radically different relations to public form (`CG-MEDIA-0002`).

## E07 — evidence

A photograph can preserve counterevidence to destructive self-interpretation without becoming complete truth (`CG-MEDIA-0003`).

## E08 — translation

Professional production becomes legitimate when it can translate an authored symbolic world without normalizing it (`CG-MEDIA-0004`, `CG-PERF-0007`).

## E09–E10 — circulation

Unit identity becomes portable through released music, television self-promotion and environmental BGM (`CG-PERF-0022`, `CG-MEDIA-0009`).

## E11 — selective public truth

Private contradictions do not debunk professional identity. Miku and Riina can be publicly stylized and privately more complicated at the same time (`CG-MEDIA-0005`).

## E14–E15 — professional plurality

Visibility expands; Riina's aspiration encounters a stronger external standard; Producer's own appearance becomes organizational representation; Kaede demonstrates mature coexistence of elegant image, ordinary humor, labor and stage authority (`CG-PROD-0027`, `CG-MEDIA-0006`, `CG-MEDIA-0007`).

## E16 — ownership

Persona becomes an explicit authorship conflict. Nana and Miku prove that highly constructed identities can be performer-owned; plainness can be more alienating than theatricality (`CG-MEDIA-0010`).

## E17 — appropriation

Authenticity survives professional constraint when the performer can alter her relation to the assigned form (`CG-MEDIA-0011`).

## E18 — differentiation of outcomes

Media usefulness, developmental fluency, comfort and ratification are separated (`CG-PERF-0033`, `CG-REL-0039`).

## E19 — accountable aspiration

Riina's identity becomes more technically grounded without being surrendered, while Natsuki demonstrates that authorship can carry opportunity cost (`CG-CHAR-0048`, `CG-INST-0024`).

## E21 — role as learning

Mio's acting shows that mediated professional identity need not always be direct self-expression; inhabiting a fictional role can create knowledge (`CG-PERF-0041`).

## E22 — image failure

Uzuki's historically strongest public register stops being directly reproducible under instruction (`CG-MEDIA-0012`).

## E24–E25 — re-entry and inhabited identity

Uzuki returns to public action before restored polish, and “Cinderella Girls” becomes a lived plural professional self-description rather than merely an external image (`CG-PERF-0046`, `CG-PERF-0050`).

## E26 — range after resolution

Established identities become portable role material, and Uzuki can test alternatives before re-choosing the familiar (`CG-PERF-0051`, `CG-CHAR-0062`).

The complete progression can therefore be summarized as:

> **image is first something the performer must produce for the camera; then something the camera selectively mediates; then something that can circulate, translate, constrain and preserve evidence; finally something whose legitimacy depends on whether the developing performer can continue to author her relation to it.**

# XIX. What public image communicates, and what it necessarily hides

The protocol asks not only whether a form is authored, but what it communicates and what it hides.

Every successful public image in the series performs both operations.

Miku's cat identity communicates:

- readiness;
- comic energy;
- recognizable audience grammar;
- professional commitment;
- self-selected aesthetic continuity.

It hides or leaves backgrounded:

- fish aversion;
- body-management discipline;
- vulnerability;
- moments when serious speech becomes less ornamented.

Riina's rock image communicates:

- desired direction;
- taste aspiration;
- commitment to a field;
- relationship to coolness and independence.

It can hide:

- uncertainty;
- incomplete knowledge;
- how much practice remains;
- domestic ordinariness.

Ranko's theatrical image communicates:

- symbolic imagination;
- aesthetic specificity;
- intensity;
- chosen distance from ordinary phrasing.

It can hide:

- difficulty translating directly;
- fear of horror despite dark aesthetics;
- ordinary preferences that help Producer understand the image's causal history.

Nana's Usamin image communicates:

- vocational fantasy;
- continuity;
- audience participation;
- comic mythology;
- professional energy.

It can hide:

- fatigue;
- physical cost;
- uncertainty about how long the identity can be sustained.

Uzuki's smile communicates:

- approachability;
- joy;
- relational warmth;
- a capacity other characters genuinely experience as meaningful.

It can hide:

- comparative insecurity;
- lack of differentiated self-description;
- the amount of effort required to preserve the image once confidence collapses.

The point is not that the hidden material is “truer.”

It is that **representation is necessarily selective**.

A healthy professional system must therefore resist turning a successful image into a total theory of the person.

That is the persona-level counterpart to Document 06's institutional warning against premature closure.

---

# XX. The institution must not reduce the person to the image — but it cannot operate without images

Document 06 established that 346 cannot avoid branding, visibility, evaluation and public representation. An idol institution that refused all image construction would cease to function professionally.

Document 08 adds the corresponding person-level constraint:

> **The institution needs stable public forms, but those forms remain legitimate only if they retain enough permeability for the person to continue developing.**

This produces a durable tension.

Institutions need:

- recognizable names;
- repeatable concepts;
- marketable formats;
- consistent promotional material;
- audience legibility;
- staff coordination;
- and a usable story about who the performer is.

Performers need:

- room to exceed that story;
- protection from being mistaken for only the story;
- opportunities to revise or complicate it;
- and standing to say when the form no longer feels inhabitable.

Mishiro's E16–E17 language makes the danger of corporate image sovereignty explicit (`CG-INST-0018`, `CG-INST-0020`). But the same episodes also show that performer authorship does not mean refusing professional format altogether.

Rika succeeds on television. Mika succeeds in the cosmetics brief. Nana succeeds through an elaborate character. Ranko succeeds through highly designed production.

The question is not whether an institution may construct images.

It is:

> **Does the institution treat image as a negotiable professional form around a developing subject, or treat the subject as raw material for an image whose meaning is owned elsewhere?**

That distinction is the persona/media equivalent of the Producer–Mishiro authorship dispute without duplicating Document 06's institutional analysis.

# XXI. Guardrails for later specialist and final synthesis

The mature corpus should preserve the following constraints.

1. **Do not write “persona = fake.”** The series repeatedly disproves that equation.
2. **Do not write “private = real.”** Private contexts expand evidence but do not possess automatic ontological priority.
3. **Do not write “spontaneous = authentic.”** Professional structures can enable genuine expression; unstructured exposure can also produce paralysis.
4. **Do not treat professional construction as inherently alienating.** Ranko, Miku, Nana, Rika and Mika all demonstrate forms of credible construction.
5. **Do not treat performer ownership as solitary authorship.** These identities are co-produced through staff, peers, media and audiences.
6. **Do not use self-authorship to excuse lack of competence.** Riina's arc explicitly keeps aspiration and expertise distinct.
7. **Do not use successful appropriation to legitimate the original coercion.** A performer can salvage or transform a constrained form.
8. **Do not treat marketability as proof of comfort or consent.** E18 separates ratings from performer ratification.
9. **Do not treat media utility as equivalent to developmental success.** `初々しい` footage can be usable while the performers correctly judge their skill insufficient.
10. **Do not treat audience recognition as ownership.** Persona can organize audience relation without surrendering performer authority.
11. **Do not treat an established public strength as permanently available.** Uzuki's E22 magazine shoot is the clearest counterexample.
12. **Do not treat continuity as failure to develop.** E26 shows familiar presentation can be re-chosen after alternatives are tested.
13. **Do not make every professional role autobiographical.** Mio's acting demonstrates the developmental value of role distance.
14. **Do not treat one successful image as an exhaustive account of the person.** Representation is necessarily selective.
15. **Do not let E26 overwrite E25.** Its fashion and acting material are supplementary confirmation of post-resolution range, not the mainline finale's governing proof.

---

# XXII. Final specialist synthesis

The 2015 anime begins its persona problem before most of its idols possess strongly articulated personas at all.

Episode 2 asks newcomers to stand before a camera and somehow become professionally legible. `もっと普通に` fails because “normal” is already a performance demand once a camera is present. The series never returns to a fantasy of unmediated representation after that point.

Episode 4 broadens the field. Some performers prepare a persona. Some freeze. Some arrive with a fully authored aesthetic world. Some aspire toward a category they do not yet understand. Some turn refusal itself into content.

Episode 8 shows that professional production can become **more** faithful through mediation when it learns the performer's distinctions. Episode 11 shows that private contradiction does not cancel public sincerity. Episode 16 turns the matter into an explicit authorship conflict: Nana and Miku prove that visible artificiality can be deeply owned, while enforced plainness can be alienating. Episode 17 then prevents refusal from becoming the only model of agency by showing Rika and Mika making constrained forms their own. Episode 18 separates marketability from comfort and media utility from development. Episode 19 makes aspiration accountable to practice without surrendering the right to choose what the aspiration means. Episode 22 shows that even a historically successful public image can stop being inhabitable when the performer loses her relation to it. Episodes 24–26 finally show re-entry, plural public identity, portable role play and freely chosen continuity.

The resulting model is neither romantic individualism nor institutional constructivism.

The idol is not simply:

> a hidden true self waiting to be revealed.

Nor is she simply:

> whatever image the institution successfully markets.

She is a **developing person whose professional identity is made through mediated forms that she does not create alone but must retain meaningful authorship within**.

That is why costume can matter without determining essence.

That is why a fictional planet can be vocationally sincere.

That is why cat ears can be both identity and equipment.

That is why “rock” can be real before it is expert but must become more accountable as experience grows.

That is why a camera can preserve a smile without owning its meaning.

That is why an imposed kindergarten costume can become usable only after Rika re-authors how she inhabits it.

That is why an ordinary school uniform can be enough for Uzuki's public return.

And that is why choosing the familiar self after trying alternatives can count as development.

The specialist conclusion is therefore:

> **Cinderella Girls treats persona as an authored relation between person and public form, not as a layer hiding the person. Media makes identity selective, repeatable, portable and governable; self-authorship keeps those forms from becoming totalizing definitions. The mature idol is not the person who escapes construction. She is the person who can inhabit, revise, contest, extend and sometimes re-choose constructed forms without being reduced to them.**

That conclusion should feed Documents 07, 09, 10 and 11 without replacing their narrower questions, and it should later feed the final full-series synthesis only after those specialist documents have stabilized.
