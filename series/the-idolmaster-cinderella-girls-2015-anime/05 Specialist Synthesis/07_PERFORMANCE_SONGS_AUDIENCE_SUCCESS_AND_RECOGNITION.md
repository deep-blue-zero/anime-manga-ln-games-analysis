# 07 — Performance, Songs, Audience, Success, and Recognition
## What public performance can reveal, create, test, and make mutually legible

## Scope and responsibility

This document owns the performance question prescribed by the synthesis architecture:

> **What does public performance do that ordinary interaction cannot?**

The answer cannot be reduced to “the girls sing and prove they have improved.” Throughout the 2015 anime, performance functions as several different kinds of event at once:

- a professional task with technical demands;
- a media object that can be recorded, replayed, promoted, and evaluated;
- an encounter between performers and audiences;
- an arena in which private expectations collide with public evidence;
- a test of whether a persona, unit, or professional form is usable;
- a way of discovering what a performer wants before she can fully say it;
- a way of creating social relations that did not exist before the act;
- a source of memory that later performances reinterpret;
- an institutional evaluation instrument;
- and, at the series' most ambitious moments, a form of knowledge production.

This document therefore integrates the completed E01-E26 music/sound audit with the existing 410-claim evidence ledger. It does **not** take over neighboring analytical domains. Document 02 remains the canonical home for New Generations' internal relational system. Document 03 owns Producer's full developmental philosophy. Document 05 owns collective belonging and recoverability. Document 06 owns institutional power and the Producer/Mishiro philosophical dispute. Document 08 owns persona, image, media, and self-authorship. Document 09 owns the ethics of developmental risk. Document 10 will own fairy-tale and spatial motif architecture. Document 11 will own Japanese voice and performed vocal states.

Performance intersects all of those domains, but the present question is narrower:

> **What becomes knowable, actionable, memorable, or socially real only because a performance occurs?**

The strongest series-level conclusion is:

> **THE IDOLM@STER CINDERELLA GIRLS treats performance not as a final display of already-completed identity, but as a public experiment in which technical competence, audience relation, self-understanding, professional form, and collective coordination can become evidence for one another without collapsing into a single measure of success.**

This is why the anime's stages matter even when a performance is not triumphant, even when the audience is small, even when the performance is commercially ambiguous, and even when the performer herself cannot immediately interpret what happened.

The performance arc is not simply:

> practice -> success -> larger success.

It is closer to:

> **borrowed structure -> exposed limits -> owned public form -> interpretable recognition -> reusable performance knowledge -> differentiated common time -> plural professional authorship.**

# I. The first methodological requirement — “success” is plural

The most important analytical rule in the performance corpus is negative:

> **Never call a performance simply successful or unsuccessful until the relevant axis is specified.**

The governing method requires four distinct success axes:

1. **commercial / market success**;
2. **technical / professional success**;
3. **social / recognitional success**;
4. **personal / experiential success**.

Artistic/expressive and institutional success may be added when the evidence supports them, but they are not substitutes for the four core categories.

This distinction is not abstract methodology imposed from outside the anime. Episode 6 makes it textually necessary.

## 1. Commercial / market success

Commercial success concerns externally measurable market uptake or circulation: sales, ratings, paid attendance, chart or media performance, monetizable reach, or other evidence that a professional object succeeds in the market.

The anime often shows **circulation** but does not always provide enough data to support strong commercial claims. Candy Island explicitly promotes `Happy×2Days` as a released debut single (`CG-PERF-0022`); solo songs and media appearances visibly circulate after Summer Fest (`CG-MEDIA-0006`); television variety work generates points and broadcast utility; 346 continuously allocates opportunities according to assumptions about visibility and market value.

But applause, a busy venue, a released CD, or a television appearance should not automatically be converted into quantified commercial success. The evidence is frequently insufficient.

A mature synthesis therefore uses commercial language conservatively.

## 2. Technical / professional success

Technical success concerns whether the performer completes the professional task with sufficient functional competence:

- song and choreography;
- stage timing;
- MC;
- acting;
- interview technique;
- variety response;
- emergency audience holding;
- recording;
- or other task-specific demands.

Technical success can coexist with low confidence, personal dissatisfaction, or even visible error.

New Generations completes its E06 debut. Asterisk's E19 live remains musically and publicly viable even when Miku believes her stage mistake ruined it (`CG-PERF-0036`). Mio's acting in E21 becomes a sustained professional performance after earlier stumbling and script dependence (`CG-PERF-0041`).

The technical axis asks:

> **Did the work function as professional work?**

It does not answer whether the performer valued the experience.

## 3. Social / recognitional success

Social success concerns whether the performer becomes legible to others as a performer in a meaningful relation:

- audience members recognize or name her;
- a call receives an answer;
- fans send letters;
- another performer treats her performance as a reference point;
- a professional observer recognizes unit-specific strengths;
- a crowd follows an invitation;
- a public identity becomes addressable.

The crucial E06 correction is that social recognition can exist even when the performer experiences personal failure. The audience repeatedly calls Mio by name and responds positively (`CG-PERF-0020`), yet Mio interprets the event through an inappropriate comparison with Mika's established audience and declares `私アイドルやめる` (`CG-PERF-0004`, `CG-PERF-0006`).

Recognition is therefore not identical with recognition **being interpretable**.

## 4. Personal / experiential success

Personal success concerns the performer's own relationship to the work:

- did the experience become meaningful to her?
- did she want it?
- did she recognize herself in it?
- did it supply usable knowledge?
- did she experience fulfillment, pride, or chosen forward movement?

LOVE LAIKA's E06 mini-live is technically successful and explicitly personally positive for both Minami and Anastasia: `私嬉しいです / 私も` (`CG-PERF-0005`). New Generations' immediately adjacent debut is technically complete and socially recognized but personally disastrous for Mio (`CG-PERF-0004`).

The contrast is deliberate. A similar distinction appears later when Mio's E21 acting is strongest as **personal/relational success** because the work helps her understand Rin's feelings; the episode does not provide evidence of major commercial acclaim (`CG-PERF-0041`).

## 5. Artistic / expressive success — when warranted

A fifth axis becomes useful when a work's expressive integrity is itself the issue.

Ranko's E08 performance succeeds artistically because the finished object preserves her symbolic distinctions rather than flattening them into generic horror or generic idol language (`CG-PERF-0007`, `CG-PERF-0021`). Asterisk's `ΦωΦver!!` succeeds as a unit form because it contains cat/cute and rock vocabularies without making either disappear (`CG-PERF-0024`).

This is not reducible to audience size.

## 6. Institutional success — when warranted

An event can also succeed as evidence about an organization.

The E22 Autumn Live makes Cinderella Project's differentiated unit strengths legible to an executive observer (`CG-PERF-0042`). E25 makes the coexistence of multiple professional forms materially public. E26's second-generation Project proposal later suggests that the first cohort's developmental model has become institutionally repeatable, but that conclusion belongs primarily to Document 06.

The performance document uses institutional success only where the live itself becomes the evidence field.

## 7. The axis model prevents moral and interpretive mistakes

Several recurrent errors disappear once the axes are kept separate:

- a technically completed performance need not feel personally successful;
- audience laughter does not prove performer comfort;
- a small audience can provide strong recognitional success;
- large exposure can be artistically or personally inadequate;
- a performance can be institutionally useful while remaining ethically costly;
- a performance can be personally transformative without evidence of commercial success;
- an audience can recognize a performer whom the performer does not yet know how to recognize herself.

The E06 debut is the foundational case because it establishes the anime's refusal to let one metric colonize all the others.

# I-A. Performance is not one medium — live, recording, variety, acting, MC, and mediated replay test different competencies

The series' use of the word “performance” becomes analytically weak if every public task is treated as a lesser or greater version of the concert stage. Different media expose different relationships among preparation, spontaneity, audience, persona, technical skill, and self-interpretation.

## 1. Live song performance

The conventional idol live is the most obvious public form, but even within this category the dramatic function changes.

E03 is apprenticeship inside Mika's established grammar. E06 is a first owned unit debut and an audience-expectation crisis. E13 is a deliberate test of whether recognition can be interpreted under reduced scale. E15 is historical relation made publicly present. E19 uses live music to test the permeability of artistic identity. E22 turns live performance into both institutional evaluation and emergency system. E24 makes a stage the site of uncertain re-entry. E25 makes many differentiated histories co-present.

A “live” therefore cannot be assigned one fixed narrative meaning.

## 2. Recording

Recording changes the temporal relation between performer and public.

LOVE LAIKA's E05 recording is heard while other idols are still imagining their own futures (`CG-INST-0015`). By E06, `Memories` has become a stable public unit object (`CG-PERF-0018`). The professional form can therefore be made in one time and encountered in another.

Recording also converts possibility into path dependency. Once a song exists, it can circulate, be rehearsed around, promoted, and attached to a unit identity. But the series does not infer that recording fixes the performers' entire future.

## 3. Mediated replay and promotional performance

E04's camera-bounded replay of E03 material demonstrates that a live can become **media evidence** (`CG-MEDIA-0008`). The event can be reframed after the performers leave the stage.

E09 extends this principle: Candy Island's song exists inside fan encounter and television self-promotion (`CG-PERF-0022`). The unit must not only perform a song; it must learn to speak about the song as a professional object.

Promotional performance therefore requires a different competence from singing. The performer represents a representation.

## 4. Variety television

E09, E16, E17, and E18 make variety work a distinct performance domain.

Variety performance rewards:

- timing;
- recovery;
- willingness to become material;
- audience reading;
- interaction with hosts or craftspeople;
- comic flexibility;
- and sometimes the capacity to redirect an imposed format.

This domain is especially useful because it separates **broadcast utility** from **performer experience**. Chieri can produce usable content through visible nervousness (`CG-PERF-0008`, `CG-PERF-0033`). Rika can initially fail to inhabit an assigned role and later make it professionally fluent through self-authored appropriation (`CG-PERF-0032`). Chieri and Kanako can discover that credible location work depends on attention to the object and person in front of them rather than generic praise (`CG-PERF-0034`).

Variety therefore tests relational responsiveness more aggressively than a fixed song often does.

## 5. Acting

Mio's E21 stage work demonstrates that acting is not merely idol performance with dialogue instead of singing.

A fictional role creates **distance** between performer and self. That distance can become a tool. Mio's performance gives her a practical way to inhabit concerns about enclosure, outside possibility, and stepping into another world (`CG-PERF-0040`). Her later claim that acting helped her understand Rin more accurately (`CG-PERF-0041`) makes the medium's distinct epistemic function explicit.

Acting can teach by requiring the performer to organize a perspective that is not reducible to autobiographical sincerity.

## 6. MC and audience holding

MC belongs neither completely to song nor ordinary conversation.

It is public speech under performance conditions. E03 shows how difficult that threshold is when a prepared song stops. E09 shows an expert host converting vulnerability into program rhythm. E11 and E16 show performer-specific calls establishing interactional grammar. E22 makes MC an operational resource under crisis.

MC therefore becomes one of the clearest measures of movement from **being carried by structure** toward **being able to carry public structure for others**.

## 7. Performance medium determines what counts as evidence

A technically successful song proves little about interview skill. A good promotional photograph proves little about spontaneous MC. Strong acting does not automatically imply strong singing. Variety competence may depend on vulnerability management in ways a choreographed live does not.

The anime's professional world becomes richer as it stops treating “idol ability” as one scalar trait.

That is why later institutional observers can see differentiated unit strengths (`CG-PERF-0042`) and why the finale can contain multiple professional routes without making some of them degraded forms of the “real” stage.

The table makes several patterns visible.

First, **commercial evidence is frequently the least complete axis**. The anime is much more interested in what work means to performers, audiences, peers, and institutions than in giving a detailed sales ledger. Later reception research could supplement that question, but it should not be smuggled into the anime-only analysis.

Second, technical success is often necessary but rarely sufficient. New Generations' E06 debut and Asterisk's E19 live are the clearest examples.

Third, social recognition is often present earlier than the performer can use it. Mio is the central case, but Uzuki's later crisis also demonstrates that having been publicly valued in the past does not guarantee present self-recognition.

Fourth, personal success is not simply “feeling happy.” It can include gaining a usable answer, choosing a path, discovering a limitation, or learning that a previous self-interpretation was incomplete.

Finally, artistic and institutional success should remain **additional axes**, not inflated universal categories. They matter where the work itself or organizational consequences are genuinely at issue.


# II. Episode 3 — performance begins as borrowed professional time

Episode 3 establishes the first major distinction between **being inside a professional structure** and **being able to author that structure**.

New Generations is explicitly judged not fully ready. The trainer's `正直 まだ早い` and the bounded assessment of their readiness are not erased when they are allowed to appear with Mika (`CG-PERF-0001`). The girls' participation is scaffolded by a mature performer, established choreography, existing stage logistics, rehearsal, and a limited backing role.

The sound audit sharpens the developmental distinction.

Immediately before entering, the girls perform the small `フライドチキーン` synchronization. It is not musically grand, but it gives them one shared breath/onset before they step into a much larger professional temporal field (`CG-PERF-0016`). Once `TOKIMEKIエスカレート` begins, Mika's performance supplies pulse, choreography, center logic, and an already-functioning relation to the audience (`CG-PERF-0017`).

Inside that structure, the rookies can succeed.

Then the song ends.

Mika transfers an open microphone and unstructured audience attention to them. Their speech fragments (`CG-PERF-0002`, `CG-VOICE-0020`). The contrast reveals a competence boundary:

> **They can inhabit public time that somebody else has already organized more successfully than they can yet organize public time themselves.**

That distinction becomes one of the series' deepest performance-development measures.

The episode does not treat the live as fake merely because its structure is borrowed. The experience is real enough that the girls later call it dreamlike (`CG-PERF-0003`). But the empty-auditorium aftermath also prevents immediate mastery fantasy. The performance has happened before they possess a stable conceptual language for what happened.

Performance therefore begins as **experience in advance of full interpretation**.

This is the first form of performance as epistemic action: the stage gives them evidence about a professional world they could not acquire by explanation alone.

# III. Episode 6 — recognition exists before the performer can interpret it

Episode 6 is the central success-axis episode because it dismantles the intuitive assumption that crowd size, audience recognition, technical execution, and personal meaning should move together.

## 1. Borrowed recognition becomes a dangerous reference class

Mio explicitly remembers the earlier Mika live as the standard against which she imagines New Generations' debut (`CG-PERF-0006`). That earlier performance placed New Generations inside an audience already assembled around an established idol. The rookies received the emotional force of scale without yet owning the audience relation that produced it.

By E06, Mio has transformed that experience into an expectation.

The mistake is not that she wants a large audience. Ambition remains legitimate throughout the series. The mistake is interpretive: she treats Mika's mature performance ecology as the normal baseline for a first rookie mini-live.

## 2. The actual debut is not socially empty

The sound evidence matters because it corrects any reading in which Mio collapses simply because nobody notices New Generations.

Audience members call her name and offer positive address (`CG-PERF-0020`). The stage is completed. The girls have become publicly recognizable enough for name-specific response.

So the performance contains real technical and recognitional success.

Mio still experiences personal failure (`CG-PERF-0004`).

That is not irrationality that the text asks us to dismiss. It is a mismatch between the evidence she receives and the reference frame through which she reads it.

The series' performance theory therefore acquires an important proposition:

> **Recognition is not self-interpreting.**

A person may be recognized and still be unable to use recognition as evidence of success.

## 3. LOVE LAIKA supplies a controlled contrast

LOVE LAIKA's adjacent first mini-live is technically successful and personally positive (`CG-PERF-0005`, `CG-PERF-0018`). Minami and Anastasia do not need the performance to prove an absolute career status. The experience is compatible with the scale and expectations they bring to it.

The juxtaposition makes audience size an insufficient explanation.

## 4. Episode 7 repairs interpretation, not history

The later repair does not retroactively turn E06 into a personal success for Mio. Producer's correction of `当然`, the photographic counterevidence to Mio's shame-dominated memory, and renewed group commitment make the event newly interpretable (`CG-PROD-0014`, `CG-MEDIA-0003`).

The anime does not rewrite the first debut as secretly perfect.

It teaches the characters how to read evidence more accurately the next time.

That distinction becomes decisive at E13.

# IV. Audience development — from scale to addressable relation

The audience is not a passive backdrop. It develops as a dramatic object alongside the performers.

The progression is not “small crowd -> bigger crowd.” It is a change in **what an audience can mean and what performers can do with it**.

## 1. Audience as overwhelming field — E03

At the beginning, audience sound primarily registers **scale**. It is a dense professional world that exceeds the rookies' interpretive fluency.

The crowd is there before New Generations knows how to address it.

## 2. Audience as recognition that can be misread — E06

Mio receives name-specific recognition (`CG-PERF-0020`) but cannot fit that recognition into her internal success model.

The audience is no longer anonymous, but recognitional evidence still fails to become personal meaning.

## 3. Audience as content utility — E09

Candy Island complicates audience response further. Chieri's panic and apology can generate laughter/points because an experienced MC turns the moment into successful television (`CG-PERF-0008`).

This is recognitional success of a kind, but it does not prove the experience is harmless or developmentally desirable.

Audience response can reward a moment that remains uncomfortable for the performer.

## 4. Audience as something performers can create — E10

Dekoration changes the model from receiving an audience to **forming one**.

The unit has the explicit objective `お客さんをもっと巻き込みたい`. In public space, `LET'S GO HAPPY!!` gathers attention; the girls then directly invite interested people to follow (`CG-PERF-0009`, `CG-PERF-0023`).

The song is not merely accompaniment. It intervenes causally in public space.

The crowd becomes an achievement of performer skill.

## 5. Audience as interaction grammar — E11 and E16

Asterisk and Nana show that audience participation can be structured through recognizable performer-specific calls.

In E11, Miku's `にゃー` call becomes a site through which Riina can participate without becoming Miku (`CG-PERF-0025`). In E16, the Usamin call changes the room's atmosphere and makes Nana's performer-owned persona socially operational (`CG-PERF-0031`).

Call-and-response is therefore not generic “fanservice.” It is a public grammar whose form depends on the performer's authored identity.

The crowd contributes to the performance, but does not own the identity that enables the call.

## 6. Audience as interpretable relation — E13

E13 is the decisive correction to E06.

After the storm, Producer warns New Generations that the audience has not fully returned. The girls are not allowed to discover reduced turnout by surprise. Mio then contextualizes the situation and chooses the people actually present. The performance receives repeated audible response (`CG-PERF-0011`, `CG-PERF-0028`).

The improvement is not that E13 finally produces recognition while E06 had none.

Recognition existed in E06.

What changes is Mio's ability to interpret recognition proportionately.

The audience becomes **addressable** rather than merely countable.

## 7. Audience as correspondent — fan letters after E13

Fan letters and surveys extend recognition beyond the duration of applause (`CG-REL-0020`). The audience can answer after the performance.

This matters because recognition becomes temporally durable and individually addressable. It leaves traces that performers can revisit rather than disappearing with the room's noise.

## 8. Audience as operational resource — E22

During the Autumn Live disruption, MC and call-response keep the crowd engaged while staff reorganize the running order (`CG-REL-0049`).

Here audience interaction becomes organizational slack.

The public relation literally creates time for backstage recovery.

## 9. Audience as support without authorship substitution — E24

At Uzuki's Christmas return, audience members call `卯月ちゃん` and encourage her after she refuses the offered prompt. They do not give her the sentence she needs to say.

The crowd can reopen relation without supplying identity.

This is a crucial limit on recognition:

> **Audience support can make speech possible without being entitled to author the content of the speech.**

## 10. Audience as evidence, not final judge — E25

The Ball's crowd remains materially present while adults debate what the institution should mean. Public response matters, but it does not settle the Producer/Mishiro dispute by plebiscite.

Audience approval is evidence among other evidence.

The mature audience trajectory is therefore:

> **scale -> nameable recognition -> usable reaction -> performer-created audience -> interaction grammar -> interpretable relation -> durable correspondence -> operational resource -> supportive public address.**

The permanent guardrail is equally important:

> **Loudness is not a truth meter, and public response never grants ownership over performer personhood.**

This conclusion is anchored by `CG-PERF-0053`.

# V. Performance as epistemic action — the stage can answer questions that speech cannot

One of the series' strongest mature patterns is that characters often perform **before** they possess a complete verbal answer.

Performance then generates evidence.

## 1. E08 — has Ranko actually been heard?

Producer's earlier conversation with Ranko can only claim understanding provisionally. The finished song is the harder test.

Ranko's performance retains her gothic symbolic system rather than converting it into ordinary language (`CG-PERF-0007`). The retrospective audio audit strengthens the claim: relational language such as `貴方と手を携え` and `新たな旋律` appears *inside* the symbolic vocabulary rather than replacing it (`CG-PERF-0021`).

Only after that object exists does `私の声を聴き取ってくれて` acquire its full force.

The performance tests whether interpretation survived professional translation.

## 2. E10 — can Dekoration reorganize public space?

The street performance is simultaneously solution and experiment. It demonstrates that the unit can convert recorded identity, live charisma, movement, music, and invitation into a following crowd (`CG-PERF-0023`).

The answer could not have been known with equal force from planning alone.

## 3. E11 — can Miku and Riina's difference become one professional object?

Before Asterisk is administratively ratified, the girls generate incompatible language, negotiate it, enter one another's audience grammar, and perform `ΦωΦver!!` (`CG-PERF-0010`, `CG-PERF-0024`, `CG-PERF-0026`).

The public work appears before the final name.

The unit is discovered partly by making something together.

## 4. E13 — can Mio experience recognition under a corrected reference frame?

Producer and peers can explain E06, but explanation alone cannot prove that Mio will be able to inhabit a smaller public relation differently.

The post-storm stage does that test (`CG-PERF-0011`, `CG-PERF-0028`).

Personal success emerges not from a magically maximal crowd but from a changed capacity to address the crowd that exists.

## 5. E15 — can Kaede's value claim be made perceptible?

Kaede's disagreement with Mishiro concerns what professional growth should preserve. Her chosen small venue is not merely described as meaningful; she performs there.

Her song continues across the remembered policy dispute and returns to the stage (`CG-PERF-0013`, `CG-PERF-0030`). The performance makes the history she is defending audiovisually present.

It does not logically prove Mishiro wrong, but it gives Kaede's position a form ordinary explanation could not supply.

## 6. E19 — can musical expansion coexist with unit loyalty?

Riina's conflict cannot be solved by choosing between “Asterisk” and “real rock” at the level of slogans.

Natsuki's performance gives her an embodied benchmark (`CG-PERF-0035`). Asterisk remains viable through Miku's mistake (`CG-PERF-0036`). The one-day Natsuki/Riina performance allows novice status to become invitation rather than exclusion while reusing Asterisk-associated material (`CG-PERF-0037`).

Riina learns through doing that musical expansion does not logically require relational repudiation.

## 7. E20 — `Trancing Pulse` before the decision

This is perhaps the clearest explicit performance experiment.

Nao and Karen sing first. Rin listens. Later she joins. Producer remains outside the room (`CG-PERF-0039`).

Rin's eventual position—`分からないから / それを確かめたい`—is not abstract curiosity. The rehearsal has supplied evidence about a possible professional relation that verbal argument could not produce in advance.

The stage/rehearsal becomes a way of knowing what deserves further trial.

## 8. E21 — acting as perspective practice

Mio's `秘密の花園` work expands the performance category beyond idol song.

The play's vocabulary of enclosure, outside possibility, stepping outward, and unseen treasures gives Mio a position from which to think about Rin's movement (`CG-PERF-0040`). Her own acting develops from earlier difficulty into sustained dramatic work, and she explicitly says the process helps her begin to understand Rin's feelings (`CG-PERF-0041`).

Performance here is not self-expression.

It is **practice in not being oneself** long enough to gain perspective.

## 9. E24 — stage before certainty

Uzuki cannot solve her crisis by first discovering a perfectly differentiated essence and then returning to performance.

She chooses to return while uncertainty remains. She arrives in ordinary school uniform (`CG-PERF-0046`), refuses the offered prompt, passes through silence, responds to public address, reclaims `頑張ります`, and only then enters `S(mile)ING!` (`CG-PERF-0047`).

The stage is not confirmation of a conclusion reached elsewhere.

It is where a still-incomplete self-definition becomes actionable.

## 10. E25 — performers as evidence that exceeds adult theories

The finale does not reduce the Ball to proof that Producer was right all along.

The performers' differentiated public work supplies evidence that both Producer and Mishiro must interpret. The event exceeds either adult's ability to derive all possible futures in advance.

Performance becomes epistemically plural: a single public event can support different institutional readings while still confronting each reader with realities they did not completely predict.

The mature principle is:

> **Performance in Cinderella Girls often produces knowledge by making a possibility livable before it is fully theorized.**

# VI. Diegetic, non-diegetic, and trans-diegetic music — causal claims require formal discipline

The completed sound audit imposes a major guardrail on literary interpretation.

Music is dramatically important, but importance does not mean causal omnipotence.

## 1. Diegetic performance can change the social situation

Some sound is materially produced inside the scene and directly alters what happens.

Examples include:

- E10's `LET'S GO HAPPY!!`, which attracts passersby and supports a direct invitation (`CG-PERF-0023`);
- E16's Usamin call, which changes the room's interactional state (`CG-PERF-0031`);
- E20's `Trancing Pulse` rehearsal, which supplies Rin with experiential evidence (`CG-PERF-0039`);
- E22's emergency MC/call-response, which buys backstage time (`CG-REL-0049`).

In these cases, sound is not merely “about” agency. It participates in the causal chain.

## 2. Non-diegetic music usually frames agency already underway

The opposite mistake is common: treating soundtrack entry as though the music grants courage or creates a decision.

The corpus repeatedly contradicts that model.

In E23, the sustained song field enters while Rin's testimony about Uzuki's smile is already happening (`CG-PERF-0045`, `CG-REL-0053`). The music reorganizes the emotional and thematic meaning of the testimony; it does not cause Rin to say it.

In E24, Uzuki has already chosen to return, arrived, refused the prompt, passed through silence, answered the crowd, and reclaimed `頑張ります` before `S(mile)ING!` begins (`CG-PERF-0047`).

The song confirms and extends enacted agency rather than supplying agency from outside.

## 3. Trans-diegetic continuation lets a performance become argument

The series also frequently begins a song in an identifiable performance space and then keeps it audible as the image crosses to another location or temporal layer.

This matters because the song remains historically anchored in a performer while its field organizes broader material.

### E04

E03 performance material becomes replayable media (`CG-MEDIA-0008`). A live is converted into an object that can be revisited.

### E15

Kaede's venue performance continues through the remembered Mishiro dispute and back to the live (`CG-PERF-0030`). The performance becomes an argument about the value of performance without the song becoming an omniscient narrator.

### E22

`Trancing Pulse` continues as the visual narrative crosses from Triad Primus to Mishiro/Producer, Fumika/Arisu, and backstage recovery (`CG-PERF-0044`). The new unit's musical identity is therefore embedded in the larger support system rather than acoustically isolated from it.

### E25

Active live sound remains present beneath institutional reflection. Adults theorize performers while performers remain materially audible.

The correct rule is:

> **A trans-diegetic song can organize relationships among scenes without converting every lyric into privileged psychological narration.**

This preserves both formal richness and evidentiary restraint.

# VII. Songs as public forms — unit identity becomes repeatable without becoming uniform

Songs do more than express emotion. They create **portable professional forms** that can travel across media, venues, rehearsals, memories, and later relationships.

## 1. `Memories` — unit identity survives private anxiety

LOVE LAIKA's E05 recording becomes a full public object in E06 (`CG-INST-0015`, `CG-PERF-0018`). The important point is not that anxiety disappears before the song can work.

Professional form can be coherent while private vulnerability remains present.

## 2. Ranko — collaboration enters a singular symbolic world

Ranko's song demonstrates that public form need not simplify personal specificity. The professional object is shareable precisely because collaborators learn how to preserve distinctions that matter (`CG-PERF-0021`).

## 3. `Happy×2Days` — unit identity moves across media

Candy Island's song accompanies fan encounter and is later explicitly promoted as the unit's debut single (`CG-PERF-0022`). The song becomes a product, an introduction device, a television topic, and an audience bridge.

Its spoken/comic material preserves member asymmetry rather than polishing the trio into one personality (`CG-REL-0029`).

## 4. `LET'S GO HAPPY!!` — a portable recording becomes live social action

Dekoration's music exists as environmental commercial media (`CG-MEDIA-0009`) and can then be redeployed live to gather an audience (`CG-PERF-0023`).

Recorded identity and performer agency reinforce rather than replace each other.

## 5. `ΦωΦver!!` — differentiated vocabularies become one object

Asterisk's song is especially important because it does not represent compromise as bland middle ground.

The finished object contains traces of both girls' preferred vocabularies (`CG-PERF-0024`). Riina can participate in Miku's call grammar without becoming Miku (`CG-PERF-0025`). Public performance precedes final administrative ratification (`CG-PERF-0026`).

The song therefore demonstrates a mature unit principle:

> **Shared form can be specific enough to preserve internal difference.**

## 6. `GOIN'!!!` — from bodily count to Project common time

E12 strips coordination to the physical mechanics of `せーのっ`, count, rope, breath, failure, and renewed onset after `ススメ☆オトメ` has represented linked dreams (`CG-PERF-0027`).

E13 then scales common timing publicly. `GOIN'!!!` integrates explicit count language into Project-scale performance while visible local identities remain differentiated (`CG-REL-0019`, `CG-REL-0033`).

The song is not significant because everyone becomes one.

It is significant because many people can act in common time without losing local form.

## 7. Kaede's venue song — professional history becomes audible

Kaede's performance holds past and present career meaning together (`CG-PERF-0013`, `CG-PERF-0030`). The song's value is partly historical: an early audience remains part of how she understands the significance of later work (`CG-REL-0025`).

## 8. `Trancing Pulse` — new identity inside old support

The rehearsal functions as pre-decision evidence in E20 (`CG-PERF-0039`). The public song in E22 then expands across backstage and institutional scenes (`CG-PERF-0044`).

The same musical object therefore performs two different functions:

- **before decision:** experimental evidence;
- **after decision:** public identity embedded in a broader system.

## 9. `流れ星キセキ` — mature New Generations retains uncertainty

The E25 song explicitly keeps `わからない` inside the trio's language while linking three distinct stars through correction, friendship, falling, care, and movement (`CG-PERF-0048`).

The song does not represent mature relation as solved certainty.

It represents continued motion under uncertainty.

## 10. `GOIN'!!!` -> `M@GIC☆` — common time becomes foundation rather than endpoint

The E25 reprise of `GOIN'!!!` bridges backstage/rally material into `M@GIC☆` (`CG-PERF-0049`). First-cour coordination is not discarded when the cast develops more differentiated careers.

It becomes infrastructure.

`M@GIC☆` can then articulate heart, effort, inner light, growth, and bonds as experienced meanings without monopolizing the definition of magic (`CG-CIND-0022`).

The mature collective is therefore not post-ensemble.

It is an ensemble whose common time has become durable enough to contain plural routes.

# VIII. `お願い！シンデレラ` — from observed possibility to inhabited public identity

The title song creates the most important full-mainline musical bookend.

## 1. Episode 1 — the idol world precedes the protagonists' membership in it

The opening shifts from suspended fairy-tale narration into a denser public performance field with `お願い！シンデレラ` (`CG-PERF-0014`, `CG-CIND-0009`).

At that point, Cinderella is something the central girls have not yet collectively become. The professional world already exists as spectacle, pulse, bodies, audience, and dream language.

The protagonists encounter a world whose grammar precedes them.

## 2. Episode 25 — the same world is now spoken from inside

Near the finale, narration progresses:

- `私達は夢をみてる`
- `私達は夢を生きてる`
- `私達は夢を信じている`

and arrives at:

- `こんにちはシンデレラガールズです`

(`CG-PERF-0050`).

Only then does `お願い！シンデレラ` return as final movement (`CG-PERF-0055`).

The relation to the title song has changed fundamentally.

In E01, it presents a public Cinderella world that the protagonists are being invited toward.

In E25, the cast can publicly name itself from inside that world after formation, rupture, differentiation, cross-unit development, institutional contest, uncertainty, return, and collective reconfiguration.

Yet the song still points forward.

Its recurrence does not imply circular stasis.

The series ends not with “the dream is complete” but with the condition under which dream can keep generating work.

The bookend can therefore be formulated as:

> **E01 turns narrated possibility into an observed idol spectacle; E25 turns accumulated lived experience into an inhabited plural public identity that still understands itself as moving.**

# IX. Smile and recognition — from selection shorthand to relationally evidenced public value

Document 03 owns the full evolution of Producer's smile criterion, but Document 07 owns the performance/recognition side of that development.

The central correction is that `笑顔` does not mature into a simple rule that smiling means success.

It becomes a question of **what kind of relation makes a smile meaningful**.

## 1. E01 — smile as underexplained selection claim

Producer says `笑顔です` about Uzuki and invokes smile in recruiting Rin (`CG-PROD-0001`). At this point, the criterion is real but underdetermined.

The viewer does not yet possess enough evidence to know whether it is recognition, projection, professional intuition, or some combination.

## 2. E02 — commanded smile fails as image production

Direct instruction to smile or be normal produces stiffness; relational play and movement precede the successful photographs (`CG-PROD-0003`).

The lesson is already incompatible with a facial-compliance theory.

## 3. E06 — social recognition does not guarantee a usable smile

Mio can be publicly recognized and still experience collapse (`CG-PERF-0004`, `CG-PERF-0020`). Recognition alone cannot mechanically generate personal success.

## 4. E13 — smile becomes connected to interpretable relation

New Generations knowingly addresses the reduced audience, receives response, and experiences the performance differently from E06 (`CG-PERF-0011`, `CG-PERF-0028`). Producer's later `いい笑顔` matters because it follows shared, interpretable experience rather than an instruction to display happiness (`CG-NG-0003`).

## 5. E16 — `Power of Smile` becomes explicit production language

Producer defines the event around performers drawing out smiles through their own individuality and fan relation (`CG-PROD-0034`).

The episode immediately complicates any naïve opposition between constructed persona and “real” smile: Nana's highly crafted Usamin form can produce a more fluent reciprocal relation than the externally imposed plain presentation (`CG-PERF-0031`).

So “real” cannot mean “unperformed.”

## 6. E17–E18 — smile as result of usable engagement

Rika's self-authored appropriation of her role produces fluent comic interaction (`CG-PERF-0032`). Chieri and Kanako's awkward footage can be broadcastable while they remain dissatisfied with their own interviewing (`CG-PERF-0033`). Their later grounded curiosity about Edo kiriko produces more credible representation (`CG-PERF-0034`).

Producer's formulation in E18—`笑顔を引き出して欲しかった`—works because Kanako identifies enjoyment as a condition: `楽しめなきゃ笑顔になれないよね` (`CG-PROD-0039`).

Smile is therefore downstream of relation, attention, curiosity, and usable action.

## 7. E22 — public strength can be observed without settling causal theory

An external executive recognizes that each Cinderella Project unit's individuality and strength is visible and asks whether this is `Power of Smile` (`CG-PERF-0042`). Mishiro accepts the strength of the material but reaches a different production conclusion.

Performance supplies shared evidence; interpretation remains contestable.

## 8. E23 — Uzuki's smile becomes relationally non-fungible

Uzuki dismisses smile as trivial because anyone can do it: `笑うなんて誰でも出来るもん`.

Rin refuses the abstraction. `誰でも出来るなんて言わないでよ` is followed by testimony that Uzuki's earlier smile made Rin step forward believing she might shine (`CG-REL-0053`).

The smile's value is not technical rarity.

It is historical causal force inside a particular relation.

The song field that enters during this testimony reframes smile as light across the unresolved breakdown (`CG-PERF-0045`) but does not create the testimony.

## 9. E24 — smile is enacted after choice rather than discovered as essence

Uzuki returns without possessing a final verbal theory of her unique self. She re-enters public relation, survives silence, speaks, and then performs `S(mile)ING!` (`CG-PERF-0047`).

The sequence prevents a mystical interpretation in which the true smile was hidden inside her waiting to be found.

The public act makes selfhood legible through use.

The mature smile argument is therefore:

> **Smile becomes meaningful not as a universal facial metric but as an emergent sign of non-alienated engagement whose value is established through particular histories of recognition, response, and causal relation.**

# X. Common time — professional maturity is differentiated coordination

The final sound audit identifies the largest longitudinal sonic-development pattern as a shift in who can author temporal structure.

This deserves a dedicated performance synthesis because timing is not merely musical technique. It becomes a model of professional social coordination.

## 1. Borrowed timing — E03

New Generations succeeds inside Mika's established pulse and choreography (`CG-PERF-0017`). When that structure stops speaking for them, public speech destabilizes (`CG-PERF-0002`).

They can follow common time before they can author it.

## 2. Negotiated unit time — E11

Asterisk does not reach coherence by eliminating difference. Miku and Riina carry different public grammars into the same song and audience relation (`CG-PERF-0024`, `CG-PERF-0025`).

Common time becomes negotiated.

## 3. Manufactured bodily time — E12

The rope/count exercise strips coordination to its physical basis. Togetherness is no longer something the soundtrack says on their behalf; bodies must generate onset, rhythm, adjustment, failure, and retry (`CG-PERF-0027`).

## 4. Project-scale time — E13

`GOIN'!!!` transforms the rehearsal's counted coordination into public Project rhythm (`CG-REL-0019`, `CG-REL-0033`).

The Project can now synchronize without erasing unit distinction.

## 5. Polyphonic common orientation — E15

Under one musical field, different members articulate resolve in distinct registers. The collective does not need a unison slogan to act together (`CG-VOICE-0022`).

## 6. Common time after divergence — E25

By the finale, the cast includes solo work, multiple unit commitments, acting, Krone, and different professional trajectories. `GOIN'!!!` can nevertheless return as a bridge into `M@GIC☆` (`CG-PERF-0049`).

The first-cour ensemble skill has survived individuation.

The mature performance principle is:

> **Professional togetherness is not everyone becoming the same performer; it is differentiated people becoming capable of entering, sustaining, and revising shared time.**

That conclusion is encoded in `CG-PERF-0054`.

# XI. Performance memory — successful public acts become reference classes for later action

A performance does not end when the stage ends. It can become memory, evidence, benchmark, professional history, or burden.

## 1. Mika's live as dangerous benchmark

E03 gives New Generations a valuable first experience, but E06 shows the hazard of misclassifying borrowed scale as owned norm (`CG-PERF-0006`).

Past success can distort future expectation if its conditions are forgotten.

## 2. E06 photographs as revision evidence

The debut can later be revisited through images that resist Mio's shame-dominated memory (`CG-MEDIA-0003`).

Performance history is therefore mediated and revisable.

## 3. Summer Fest as aspirational benchmark

By E14, newer idols cite Summer Fest as a goal and New Generations recognizes itself as `先輩` (`CG-REL-0021`, `CG-PERF-0012`).

The earlier rookies have become somebody else's reference class.

The cycle is structurally important:

> **performance produces memory; memory produces expectations; expectations can inspire or distort depending on whether conditions remain legible.**

## 4. Kaede's first venue as chosen historical continuity

Kaede deliberately returns to a place associated with early career history (`CG-PERF-0013`). She treats those fans as co-travelers and witnesses (`CG-REL-0025`).

Performance memory here resists a purely linear ladder model of professional growth.

## 5. First-cour songs as later infrastructure

`GOIN'!!!` is not nostalgically replayed in E25 merely to remind viewers of the past. Its recurrence functions as structural continuity into `M@GIC☆` (`CG-PERF-0049`).

A prior collective achievement becomes usable material for a later, more complex collective.

Performance history therefore has at least three possible functions:

- **misleading reference class**;
- **recoverable evidence**;
- **reusable professional infrastructure**.

The series' maturity lies partly in learning the difference.

# XII. Performance and institutions — public proof is real but never self-interpreting

Document 06 owns institutional philosophy, so this section stays deliberately narrow.

Performance matters to institutions because it converts hypothetical value into observable public evidence.

But observable evidence never interprets itself.

## 1. E15 — one performance, competing definitions of value

Kaede's small venue can be interpreted as insufficient scale or as preservation of meaningful professional history. The live gives her position material form without mathematically settling the dispute (`CG-PERF-0030`).

## 2. E22 — shared observation, divergent production conclusions

The executive observer can see that Cinderella Project's units possess distinct strengths (`CG-PERF-0042`). Mishiro agrees that the material has strength but concludes that it should be produced according to 346's established logic.

The same successful performance can therefore support different institutional inferences.

## 3. E22 — performance as bureaucratic survival test

The Autumn Live is not only entertainment; it is also a midterm survival/evaluation event for the Project (`CG-INST-0028`). The stage becomes both artistic/public encounter and institutional evidence.

The system's emergency recovery further demonstrates that performance competence includes backstage and audience-holding capacities that polished final product can conceal (`CG-REL-0049`).

## 4. E25 — public event exceeds adult theories

The Ball supplies evidence of plural professional viability without establishing that one adult possesses exhaustive predictive authority.

The performance specialist should therefore preserve this rule:

> **Public proof can falsify overly narrow predictions more easily than it can establish one complete philosophy of production.**

# XIV. MC, speech, and the edge of song

The series repeatedly places dramatic weight at the boundary between prepared musical structure and public speech.

## 1. E03 — the open microphone exposes an authorship gap

The girls perform competently inside `TOKIMEKIエスカレート`, then lose fluency when asked to produce public speech without the song's structure (`CG-PERF-0002`, `CG-VOICE-0020`).

## 2. E09 — expert MC can convert vulnerability into content

The program's experienced host makes Chieri's panic usable to the show (`CG-PERF-0008`). This establishes professional MC as interpretive labor performed on live vulnerability.

## 3. E11 — call before song creates shared grammar

Miku's `にゃー` interaction and Riina's participation establish an audience relation before the song begins (`CG-PERF-0025`). The threshold itself becomes part of the performance.

## 4. E22 — MC becomes emergency infrastructure

Audience holding under contingency gives staff time to reorganize (`CG-REL-0049`). Public speech is no longer merely personality display; it is operational competence.

## 5. E24 — public speech must precede the song

The most consequential boundary arrives when Uzuki refuses the prompt and must supply her own sentence before `S(mile)ING!` can begin (`CG-PERF-0047`).

The sequence reverses E03.

E03 shows girls who can sing inside supplied structure but cannot yet comfortably author public speech when the structure stops.

E24 shows a performer for whom the song **cannot ethically or dramatically begin until she has first authored speech**.

That longitudinal inversion is one of the series' most precise measures of maturation.

# XV. Recognition — being seen, being addressed, and being understood are different events

The performance corpus supports at least three levels of recognition.

## 1. Visibility

A performer is present before an audience, camera, producer, or institution.

Visibility is necessary for much idol work but does not guarantee meaningful recognition.

## 2. Addressability

The performer can be named, answered, called to, written to, or responded to as a particular public person.

Mio's name-specific E06 audience response (`CG-PERF-0020`), E13 fan letters (`CG-REL-0020`), and E24 audience calls to Uzuki all operate at this level.

## 3. Interpretive recognition

Someone understands enough of the performer's distinctions, history, or meaning for the response to become usable.

Ranko's `私の声を聴き取ってくれて` after a performance that preserves her symbolic language is a strong example (`CG-PERF-0021`). Rin's E23 testimony about Uzuki's smile is another (`CG-REL-0053`): the value of the smile is grounded not in generic attractiveness but in what it actually did in Rin's life.

The levels should not be collapsed.

One can be visible without being understood.

One can be addressed without personally accepting the meaning of the address.

One can be understood by a small audience more deeply than by a large but generic crowd.

The mature recognition thesis is therefore:

> **Recognition becomes developmentally powerful when public response is specific enough to be addressed and interpretable enough to enter the performer's own model of experience without claiming exhaustive knowledge of the person.**

# XVI. Performance and uncertainty — the anime refuses the fantasy of total pre-knowledge

Many performance scenes matter because the characters **do not know** the result in advance.

This uncertainty is not a flaw in the professional system; it is often the reason performance is necessary.

- E03 cannot prove readiness without exposure.
- E08 cannot prove successful translation without a completed professional object.
- E10 cannot know whether the improvised public performance will gather people until it is tried.
- E11 cannot know whether Miku/Riina difference is sustainable until the public form exists.
- E13 cannot know whether Mio's corrected audience interpretation will hold until she faces an actual audience again.
- E19 cannot know whether cross-boundary musical experience threatens Asterisk until the members experience it.
- E20 cannot know what Triad Primus means to Rin before she hears and joins the music.
- E21 cannot know what acting will teach Mio before she inhabits the role.
- E24 cannot know whether Uzuki can re-enter public relation before she returns.

This pattern aligns performance with the series' broader epistemology:

> **Some professional truths are practical truths. They become available only after someone enters the situation under conditions that preserve the possibility of revision.**

Document 09 owns the ethics of such risk. Document 07 owns the performance fact that experiment is sometimes epistemically indispensable.

# XVII. E26 — bounded supplementary confirmation: performance no longer has to carry every moment of identity

Episode 26 remains supplementary and does not replace E25 as the mainline ending.

Its value for the performance synthesis is modest but useful.

The mock historical production redirects recognizable member-specific idioms into fictional roles (`CG-PERF-0051`). Established style becomes portable professional material rather than an identity prison.

More importantly, the episode is not dominated by climactic live performance. It contains ordinary work, shoots, games, jokes, experiments, misreadings, and remembered spaces. The recurring musical grammar returns around a life that can now contain long stretches of mundane activity.

That is a meaningful postscript:

> **By the epilogue, performance has become one professional mode among several rather than the only place where the characters' identities can remain coherent.**

The series can end its mainline through performance and then show a world in which the performers continue living between performances.

# XX. Final specialist synthesis — public performance makes possibility answerable to reality

The 2015 Cinderella Girls anime begins with an idol performance already in progress.

The central girls do not create the professional world from nothing. They enter an existing field of songs, stages, experienced performers, audience expectations, cameras, staff, media circulation, and institutional opportunity. At first, much of that field knows how to function better than they do.

Episode 3 captures the asymmetry perfectly. New Generations can move inside Mika's already-authored stage grammar. They can borrow pulse, choreography, center, and audience relation. The moment that structure stops and an open microphone requires them to author public time themselves, their competence boundary becomes audible.

That is not failure in the final sense. It is information.

From that point forward, the series repeatedly uses performance to turn unknowns into evidence.

A first debut reveals that technical completion and public recognition can coexist with personal collapse. A television segment reveals that audience utility and performer comfort can diverge. A street song reveals that performers can make an audience rather than only receive one. A call-and-response reveals that a persona can become a reciprocal social technology. A jointly authored song reveals that different vocabularies can share one professional form. A reduced post-storm crowd reveals that recognition becomes useful when it can be interpreted rather than merely counted. Fan letters reveal that public relation can answer back after the room empties. A small venue reveals that professional value may reside in historical continuity as well as scale. An acting role reveals that performance can create perspective rather than simply display selfhood. A rehearsal reveals that a new unit can be investigated before it is chosen. A Christmas stage reveals that a person can re-enter public life before she possesses a final theory of herself.

The audience develops alongside this process.

At first it is scale: large, loud, professionally intimidating. Then it becomes recognition: a name shouted from the crowd. Then the series discovers that recognition can still be unusable if the performer misreads its meaning. Audience reaction becomes content, then call-response, then a crowd performers can actively form, then correspondence through letters and surveys, then operational slack during a disrupted live, then supportive address that deliberately stops short of supplying the performer's words.

The mature audience is neither anonymous market nor infallible moral judge.

It is a participant in a relation whose meaning still requires interpretation.

That is why the four success axes remain indispensable. Commercial reach, technical completion, social recognition, and personal experience can reinforce one another, but the anime repeatedly refuses to guarantee that they will. Artistic integrity and institutional usefulness add further dimensions when relevant. No one metric gets to define the whole event.

The songs themselves track a parallel development.

Early professional time is often supplied from outside: Mika's pulse, established choreography, recording structures, existing media. Later, performers increasingly create forms that preserve their differences: Ranko's symbolic world becomes shareable without normalization; Candy Island's public song contains internal asymmetry; Asterisk's song contains incompatible identity fields without neutralizing them; Dekoration turns recorded music into live audience formation; `Trancing Pulse` first becomes evidence for a possible future and later a public identity embedded inside an old support network.

At Project scale, counted bodily coordination in E12 becomes `GOIN'!!!` in E13. The cast learns how to share time. Crucially, it does not learn how to become one voice.

The second cour then stress-tests whether that shared time can survive individuation.

It can.

By E25, `流れ星キセキ` can make New Generations' continuing uncertainty audible rather than erase it. `GOIN'!!!` can return as infrastructure rather than nostalgic regression. `M@GIC☆` can articulate experiential meanings of magic without closing the metaphor. The final public self-introduction can move from dreaming to living to believing before the cast says `シンデレラガールズです`.

Then `お願い！シンデレラ` returns.

In Episode 1, that song belongs to a professional Cinderella world the protagonists are only beginning to enter. In Episode 25, the same broad public grammar is inhabited by people who have accumulated incompatible desires, different units, acting work, solo paths, institutional conflict, failures, recoveries, and relationships that no longer require synchronized development.

The recurrence therefore does not close a circle.

It changes the speaker position.

The world that was once observed as possibility can now be publicly inhabited without being treated as finished.

This is the anime's mature performance thesis:

> **Public performance makes possibility answerable to reality. It can expose unreadiness, generate recognition, test identity, create audiences, preserve memory, reorganize social space, supply evidence for future choices, and coordinate differentiated people in shared time. But it never reduces all value to crowd size, applause, technical polish, market visibility, or even the performer's immediate feeling. Its most important stages are not final verdicts. They are encounters in which people, audiences, and institutions learn what their prior theories could not completely know.**

Performance is therefore neither ornament nor reward scene.

It is one of the series' primary epistemic institutions.

It is where dreams acquire conditions, where recognition becomes specific, where failure acquires evidence, where identities become publicly usable, and where the possibility of a future becomes concrete enough to be chosen, rejected, revised, remembered, or tried again.
