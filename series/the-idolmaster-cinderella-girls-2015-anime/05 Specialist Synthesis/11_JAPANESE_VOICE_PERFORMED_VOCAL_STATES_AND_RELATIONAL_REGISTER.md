# 11 — Japanese Voice, Performed Vocal States, and Relational Register

## Scope, method, and evidentiary limits

This document asks the question reserved for it by the governing synthesis architecture:

> **How does voice construct identity, and how does the same voice change function across development?**

The answer requires three layers to remain distinct.

**Lexical voice** is the Japanese a character habitually chooses: first-person forms, address terms, politeness, sentence endings, catchphrases, theatrical language, professional vocabulary, fillers, self-correction, and recurrent formulations.

**Performed vocal state** is what the actor does with that language in a particular scene: projection, tempo, breath, fragmentation, hesitation, silence, laughter, constriction, force, turn-taking, and the capacity or inability to finish a thought.

**Relational register** is the way lexical and performed choices change according to the person addressed and the social task being performed: peer reassurance, conflict, family speech, executive speech, backstage coordination, MC, interview, confession, public self-presentation, or ordinary work.

The primary mainline evidence is Episodes 1–25. For this specialist pass, the earlier claim and episode corpus was re-grounded against the two user-supplied complete audio/subtitle archives. Bundle 1 contains E01–E12; Bundle 2 contains E13–E26. All 26 MP3s are full-program stereo 48 kHz tracks; both ZIPs pass CRC integrity. E01–E25 have matched Japanese ASS layers. E26 has original Japanese audio but an English ASS layer, so E26 is not used to make fine lexical claims.

The audio is a **mixed final program track**, not isolated voice stems. Accordingly, this document follows the v2 method's acoustic policy: direct source timing and established audited listening observations govern; lightweight amplitude/pitch probes are only corroborative and are never treated as isolated facts about an actor's voice. Numeric comparison across differently mixed scenes is especially weak evidence.

No attempt is made to turn voice acting into pseudo-laboratory phonetics. The goal is dramatic precision.

# I. Executive thesis — maturation is expanded vocal possibility, not normalization

The strongest full-series conclusion is:

> **Cinderella Girls usually represents vocal maturation not by making characters sound less marked, less stylized, lower-pitched, calmer, or more alike, but by allowing an established voice to carry more kinds of professional, emotional, relational, and reflective work.**

The voice that matures is generally the voice already there.

Mio does not become mature by losing her exuberance. Her exuberant social rhythm becomes something she can regulate, recover, and eventually place in the service of leadership and acting. (`CG-CHAR-0003`, `CG-VOICE-0001`, `CG-VOICE-0003`, `CG-VOICE-0012`)

Rin does not become mature by learning to speak with bright certainty. Her low, concise, qualified speech becomes increasingly capable of carrying curiosity, attachment, disagreement, and self-authored exploration while preserving epistemic caution. (`CG-CHAR-0032`, `CG-CHAR-0033`, `CG-VOICE-0013`, `CG-VOICE-0029`)

Uzuki does not become mature by abandoning `頑張ります`. The series instead subjects that phrase to the most severe pragmatic transformation in the cast: sincere aspiration becomes under-specified self-description, then self-pressure and conversational closure, then a phrase she can reclaim only after choosing what action it now refers to. (`CG-CHAR-0004`, `CG-CHAR-0034`, `CG-VOICE-0014`, `CG-VOICE-0017`, `CG-VOICE-0031`, `CG-VOICE-0032`, `CG-VOICE-0033`, `CG-VOICE-0035`)

Miku does not reveal a hidden authentic self by dropping `ニャ`. Seriousness can reduce the density of cat-speech ornamentation, but the cat-idol grammar remains genuinely hers. (`CG-CHAR-0005`, `CG-CHAR-0024`, `CG-VOICE-0002`, `CG-VOICE-0008`)

Ranko does not graduate from theatrical speech into plain speech. She learns to use theatrical, plain, vulnerable, collaborative, and grateful registers as tools inside one continuous identity. (`CG-REL-0009`, `CG-VOICE-0005`, `CG-VOICE-0011`, `CG-VOICE-0030`)

Kirari does not reveal seriousness by ceasing to be Kirari. Guilt can suppress buoyancy and characteristic markers, but E18 later lets a serious autobiographical account remain fully compatible with `ハピハピ`. (`CG-CHAR-0021`, `CG-VOICE-0007`, `CG-VOICE-0026`)

Nana is the decisive anti-binary case. Her tired private speech, ordinary relational speech, constrained "plain Abe Nana" professional speech, and high-energy Usamin performance are different authored vocal states. The supposedly plainer state is not automatically more fluent, honest, or real. (`CG-CHAR-0041`, `CG-VOICE-0023`, `CG-PERF-0031`)

The mature series therefore rejects a tempting but weak interpretive hierarchy:

> stylized voice = mask  
> plain voice = truth

Its stronger proposition is:

> **A voice is not authentic because it is unperformed. It becomes more fully owned when the speaker can use, suspend, intensify, soften, translate, or revise its established resources without being trapped inside one function.**

# II. Voice is identity-making before it is development

The anime establishes vocal difference very early as social information.

Uzuki's easiest public self-description is effort. Rin gives only the required factual minimum. Mio immediately creates rhythm, nicknames, future narration, and social motion. (`CG-CHAR-0003`, `CG-CHAR-0004`)

This asymmetry matters because later development does not erase it. E13's New Generations evidence is explicit: Mio carries controlled high-energy commitment, Rin retains qualified reality-testing, and Uzuki retains bright aspirational speech. (`CG-VOICE-0012`)

The ensemble uses the same principle beyond New Generations. Vocal identity is distributed across sharply marked resources:

- Miku's cat-idol grammar and `みく` self-reference;
- Riina's projected "rock" speech and softer family register;
- Ranko's gothic theatrical syntax;
- Kirari's buoyant `ハピハピ` lexicon;
- Anastasia's measured Japanese with Russian insertions;
- Nana's Usamin mythology and call structure;
- Chieri's breath-prone hesitant speech;
- Minami's polished composed diction;
- Producer's compressed formal politeness;
- Mishiro's compact executive declaratives.

The show does not treat these as superficial tags pasted over otherwise interchangeable speakers. Their later arcs frequently depend on the fact that the same verbal habits can be placed under new pressure.

This creates a useful interpretive rule:

> **Do not ask first whether a character stops sounding like her stereotype. Ask whether the stereotype-sized voice acquires person-sized range.**

# III. New Generations — three different relationships between voice and development

Document 02 remains the canonical home for New Generations as a relational system. Here the trio matters because they supply three different models of vocal maturation.

## 1. Uzuki — the same phrase can become readiness, evasion, and agency

Uzuki provides the series' richest longitudinal study of repeated language.

### 1.1 Early `頑張ります`: sincere readiness and available self-definition

In Episode 1, `島村卯月頑張ります` is neither empty nor defensive. It is the vocabulary of a girl whose idol desire is durable but whose professional self-conception remains broad. Her speech can readily name effort before it can specify what kind of idol she will become. (`CG-CHAR-0004`)

That is an important retrospective safeguard. Later crisis does **not** reveal that early Uzuki was always hiding behind a false phrase.

The phrase works because early developmental problems have concrete objects:

- attend lessons;
- enter a project;
- learn choreography;
- take the next opportunity;
- recover from embarrassment;
- keep moving.

Effort is a meaningful answer when the question is genuinely about readiness.

### 1.2 E14–E15: repetition becomes textually visible before it becomes pathological

By E14, the series itself begins noticing the repetition. Uzuki says she will work harder; Mio affectionately answers that she always says that; Uzuki still answers, in effect, that she will work hard. (`CG-CHAR-0034`, `CG-VOICE-0014`)

The scene is not yet a crisis. The crucial point is that **vocal sincerity and conceptual narrowness coexist**.

E15 increases the pressure. Asked what she thinks about the institutional crisis, Uzuki's bright established vocal identity remains intact, but her speech becomes filler-heavy and searches repeatedly for effort-language rather than a differentiated position. (`CG-CHAR-0037`, `CG-VOICE-0017`)

Thus the weakness is not "she no longer sounds cheerful enough." She still sounds recognizably Uzuki. The problem is that a formerly productive lexical resource is being asked to answer a question for which it has insufficient semantic resolution.

### 1.3 E21: relational fluency exceeds self-descriptive fluency

Episode 21 makes the asymmetry especially clear. Uzuki can tell peers that they should keep talking until they are satisfied. She can repeat `大丈夫`. She can articulate what is good about other people. Yet when the conversational object becomes Uzuki herself, her fluency collapses into:

> `え 私？`

> `私はあの…`

and eventually:

> `私の良い所って何でしょうか？`

before returning again to:

> `島村卯月 頑張ります`

(`CG-VOICE-0031`)

This is not general social incompetence. It is domain-specific lexical underdevelopment. Uzuki is already good at **relational speech**. She is much less able to produce a differentiated professional self-account.

### 1.4 E22: `頑張ります` narrows into recursive self-pressure

Episode 22 changes the phrase's function sharply. After Uzuki interprets herself as creating difficulty for other people, she cycles through:

> `私頑張ります`

> `いっぱい頑張ります`

> `皆頑張ってますから`

> `私頑張りますっ`

and then hears herself doing it:

> `何かがんばるばっかり言ってますね`

(`CG-VOICE-0032`)

This is one of the series' most important lexical-state transitions. The phrase is still sincere, but sincerity no longer guarantees usefulness.

Earlier, `頑張ります` pointed toward an identifiable task.

Here, effort has lost its object. The phrase becomes a way to answer anxiety with more effort before the underlying question has been named.

### 1.5 E23: a familiar voice becomes conversational closure

Episode 23 makes the change explicit enough that another character names it.

After implying that she does not yet belong at the ball, Uzuki returns immediately to:

> `島村卯月頑張ります`

> `いつかきっと キラキラしたアイドルに`

Rin interrupts:

> `ごまかさないでよっ`

(`CG-VOICE-0033`)

The important verb is functional, not moral. Uzuki is not lying. She genuinely wants to work hard. But the phrase now closes the conversation without answering the live questions of desire, value, belonging, or fear.

Her attempted smile self-description then fails inside her own speech:

> `私笑顔じゃないですか？`

followed by:

> `あれ？`

(`CG-VOICE-0034`)

The breakdown is therefore not merely visual. A public identity resource fails to provide evidence even to the speaker using it.

### 1.6 E24: silence precedes reclamation

Episode 24 refuses to solve this by giving Uzuki a better slogan.

Onstage she refuses the offered prompt. The program track then leaves an extended sparse interval. The existing audit identified approximately 4.93 seconds of near-silence after the refusal and another roughly 4.14-second low-level interval before her eventual speech, with the normal caveat that this is a mixed soundtrack rather than laboratory silence. (`CG-VOICE-0035`)

The audience supplies address:

> `卯月ちゃん`

and encouragement:

> `頑張れ`

but not the missing sentence.

Only after Uzuki has already chosen to return, arrived, gone onstage, refused supplied wording, and remained in the unresolved interval does she say:

> `島村卯月頑張ります`

The phrase is lexically unchanged.

Its pragmatic structure is not.

In E23 it substituted for a choice she could not articulate.

In E24 it follows a choice she has already made.

The object of effort has become present action rather than indefinitely deferred eligibility. (`CG-VOICE-0035`)

A new directional mixed-track probe of the exact `島村卯月頑張ります` cue supports, but does not establish, this difference. The E23 cue is materially less energetic in the final mix than the E24 stage cue; because the scenes have different ambience and mixing, the numbers are not treated as source-isolated voice measurements. The secure evidence remains the sequence of choice, silence, audience address, and renewed speech.

### 1.7 E25: stability after reclamation

Uzuki can say `島村卯月頑張ります` again in the finale without the narrative treating the recurrence as regression. The full-series lesson is therefore not that a mature Uzuki needs richer vocabulary at every moment.

It is:

> **A familiar phrase becomes developmentally dangerous when it replaces thought; it becomes usable again when it names action the speaker has actually authored.**

This is why Uzuki's vocal arc is not "catchphrase → breakdown → new catchphrase."

It is:

> **readiness formula → broad identity formula → recognized repetition → recursive self-pressure → defensive closure → silence → reclaimed commitment.**

## 2. Rin — epistemic caution becomes exploratory agency

Rin's development almost runs in the opposite direction.

She begins with resistance to unexplained interpretation. She wants evidence. She asks what Producer has actually seen and why she should accept his account of her. Her early low-output speech therefore already contains a moral-epistemic disposition: she does not readily perform certainty for other people's convenience.

The mature series does not punish this by making her brighter, more verbose, or more declaratively enthusiastic.

### 2.1 Qualified enjoyment is growth, not reluctance left behind

E13 gives:

> `楽しかった…と思う`

(`CG-CHAR-0032`)

The qualification matters. A weaker reading would treat `と思う` as residue that future development should eliminate.

E14 instead advances the same structure:

> `楽しくなる途中かな`

(`CG-CHAR-0033`, `CG-VOICE-0013`)

Rin can now positively locate herself in an ongoing process without pretending to possess final certainty.

The voice matures by becoming capable of **positive uncertainty**.

### 2.2 E20: `分からない` becomes a reason to investigate

The clearest statement is:

> `分からないから それを確かめたいの`

(`CG-VOICE-0029`)

Rin's concise `分からない` does not close the question. It opens an experiment.

This is crucial because the same episode gives Uzuki a superficially similar phrase:

> `わ 分かりません`

> `分かりません 私`

For Rin, not-knowing has become an epistemic condition of agency.

For Uzuki at that moment, not-knowing marks an inability to produce a position while being asked to mediate conflict.

Same semantic field; different pragmatic function. (`CG-VOICE-0029`)

### 2.3 Force does not erase Rin's baseline

By E23, Rin can sharply say:

> `ごまかさないでよっ`

The increased force is meaningful because it is not a conversion into Mio-style high-output social speech. Rin's intensity remains targeted. Her low, controlled baseline can now carry urgent relational insistence.

Thus Rin's arc is not reserve → openness in the generic sense.

It is:

> **evidentiary resistance → qualified positive experience → temporally qualified enjoyment → uncertainty as inquiry → forceful relational testimony.**

She becomes more committed without becoming epistemically careless.

## 3. Mio — social rhythm, its collapse, and regulated return

Mio begins as the trio's highest-output conversational engine. She generates nicknames, narrates the group's future, fills silence, and accelerates interaction. (`CG-CHAR-0003`)

That baseline makes her stress states unusually legible through **absence of rhythm**.

### 3.1 E03: silence is the anomaly

Before the first large stage, Rin notices that Mio has stopped talking:

> `さっきから全然喋ってないけど 大丈夫？`

(`CG-VOICE-0001`)

The retrospective audio audit strengthens this because Mio's silence occurs inside an already loud venue field. The pressure is not abstract. Audience scale is acoustically present while the character who normally generates speech stops generating it. (`CG-VOICE-0019`)

Once the rookies enter Mika's structured song, synchronization becomes possible. But after the song ends and Mika transfers open-microphone audience attention to them, speech fragments into pauses, fillers, and nonlexical vocalizations. (`CG-VOICE-0020`)

The episode therefore distinguishes two skills:

- performing inside a supplied temporal/vocal structure;
- spontaneously authoring public speech under unstructured attention.

### 3.2 E06: breakdown is rhythm failure, not simply "lower voice"

Mio's E06 crisis is marked by syntactic and prosodic fragmentation and the loss of her socially generative rhythm. (`CG-VOICE-0003`)

The methodologically important point is negative:

> the series does not need to give Mio a conventionally low, quiet "serious voice" to prove that she is devastated.

Her destabilization is audible in what happens to sequencing, turn-taking, and sentence formation.

### 3.3 Later Mio does not become less Mio

By E13 the trio's differentiated voices remain intact. (`CG-VOICE-0012`)

Later acting work expands Mio's repertoire further: an initially socially fluent girl can become awkward when asked to inhabit scripted dramatic speech, then learn to sustain a role. The change is important precisely because ordinary extroversion does not automatically equal performed dramatic competence.

Mio's mature leadership therefore rests on **regulated exuberance**, not its suppression. Her high-output voice can mobilize, joke, narrate, perform, reassure, and sometimes yield.

That is vocal maturity as role mobility.

# IV. Producer and Mishiro — authority through stable but differently scaled registers

Document 06 owns the institutional philosophy. Here the question is how authority becomes audible.

## 1. Producer — development without conversational casualization

Producer's vocal identity is unusually stable:

- low;
- formal;
- compressed;
- professionally polite;
- often economical in affective display.

His development does not require abandoning that identity. (`CG-VOICE-0004`)

### 1.1 E01: uncertainty inside professional form

Producer can say:

> `それは分かりません`

without filling uncertainty with false confidence.

The form is still formal. Epistemic humility does not require casual intimacy.

### 1.2 E07: admission and inclusive future speech

His major repair includes:

> `私は逃げていたのかもしれません`

and:

> `もう一度一緒に見つけに行きましょう`

(`CG-VOICE-0004`)

The change is pragmatic. The same compressed formal voice that previously managed information can now admit fault and place himself inside a shared future.

The joke/request that he try dropping polite speech is significant because the series does **not** need him to do so for relational development to become real. Formality remains part of his identity.

### 1.3 E15: the same voice can refuse upward

By E15 Producer's low formal register carries explicit moral disagreement toward a superior rather than only apology, information, or reassurance. (`CG-VOICE-0016`)

This is a major expansion of function without a new timbre.

The meaning of professionalism changes from obedience to accountable advocacy.

### 1.4 E22: mature authority can sound like a stop signal

When Uzuki's effort language becomes recursively self-pressuring, Producer's responsible vocal act is not inspiration but interruption:

> `今日はもう上がられた方が 良いかもしれません`

His concise voice now carries a limit.

### 1.5 E25: `いい笑顔です` moves from production criterion to recognition

After New Generations has chosen, separated, repaired, and performed, Producer says:

> `いい笑顔です`

(`CG-VOICE-0036`)

The phrase matters because its grammatical simplicity has not changed while its placement has.

Earlier smile language could precede explanation or become a production instruction.

Here it follows authorship.

Producer's mature voice is therefore not more emotional in a generic sense. It is more **answerable to timing**: when to interpret, when to admit uncertainty, when to explain, when to refuse, when to stop someone, and when to recognize what has already been done.

## 2. Mishiro — executive compactness without melodramatic villain voice

Mishiro's authority is constructed through compact declarative and directive speech. E14 gives formulations such as:

> `時間を無駄にしたくないもので`

> `期待している`

and finally:

> `白紙に戻す`

(`CG-VOICE-0015`)

The lack of overt emotional escalation is part of the force. She does not need to shout to make institutional consequence real.

This avoids a common caricature. The series does not vocalize Mishiro as a hysterical tyrant whose harshness is proven by theatrical cruelty. Her register presents policy as administratively self-evident.

That is why Producer's equally controlled refusal can oppose her without the scene becoming a simple loud/soft morality play.

The contrast is not:

> warm human voice vs cold machine voice.

It is closer to:

> **local-developmental professional form vs executive-strategic professional form.**

Both can remain controlled. Their conflict lies in what their language authorizes and prioritizes.

# V. Ranko — theatrical speech becomes more usable, not less true

Ranko is one of the clearest demonstrations that the series rejects a fake-persona/real-person binary.

Her early theatrical voice is genuinely hers. The developmental question is whether it can communicate across contexts.

## 1. E08: relationship changes inside theatrical language

Her designation of Producer changes from:

> `我が僕`

into:

> `我が友よ`

while the theatrical idiolect remains intact. (`CG-REL-0009`)

That is relational development **inside** persona.

The episode also permits direct vulnerable address and gratitude:

> `プロデューサー`

> `私の声を聴き取ってくれて`

without treating those moments as revelations that theatrical Ranko was fake. (`CG-VOICE-0005`)

The voice now performs more tasks.

## 2. E13: hesitant volunteer and fluent stage projection belong to one speaker

When Ranko volunteers for the substitute collaboration, her speech is visibly less fluent. Onstage, projected theatrical performance is much more available. (`CG-VOICE-0011`)

This is not evidence that the stage self is false and the hesitant speaker real.

It demonstrates task-specific competence.

Theatrical form is one of Ranko's strongest communicative technologies.

## 3. E21: plain speech becomes a tool when theatrical language stalls

Ranko begins to formulate her point through her established ornate register:

> `かの者達の旅路の…`

then stalls and moves toward:

> `私はいいことだと思うんです`

before explaining through personal experience. (`CG-VOICE-0030`)

The plain register is important because it is available **when useful**, not because it finally reveals a hidden true Ranko.

Her mature vocal identity is bilingual in a broad dramatic sense: she can use her authored theatrical language and comparatively plain interpersonal language without either invalidating the other.

# VI. Asterisk — disagreement becomes a professional vocal grammar

Miku and Riina develop partly by learning that shared professional form does not require lexical convergence.

## 1. Miku — persona density changes with task

Miku consciously curates the cat-idol register from early in the series. (`CG-CHAR-0005`)

E05's vulnerable confession reduces the density of cat-speech ornamentation while preserving `みく` self-reference and some `ニャ`. (`CG-VOICE-0002`)

That pattern matters because a simplistic reading would say:

> fewer cat markers = the real Miku appears.

E11 rejects that. Miku's serious professional register can be direct and comparatively de-ornamented:

> `プロデューサーが組ませてくれた意味 今納得しておきたい`

and:

> `李衣菜ちゃんに先にデビューして欲しいの`

yet she can return to:

> `皆元気ニャ`

without contradiction. (`CG-VOICE-0008`)

The cat grammar is professional craft and sincerely valued identity, not a lie disproven by private preferences. (`CG-CHAR-0024`)

## 2. Riina — public rock projection and family softness are relational states

E11 gives one of the cleanest professional/private contrasts in the series:

> `ロックはハートなんだってば`

versus a softer family call ending:

> `うんじゃあね お母さん`

(`CG-VOICE-0009`)

The contrast is real, but the series does not ask the viewer to select which register is "actually Riina."

E19 makes the developmental issue epistemic rather than tonal. In conversation with Natsuki, Riina's rock vocabulary encounters concrete practice questions. She hesitates and self-corrects:

> `えっと最近は UKとか？`

then later speaks candidly as a novice:

> `CDの真似してみたりするんだけど 難しくて`

and finally asserts:

> `私が自分で選んだことなんだから`

> `アスタリスクが私にとってのロックなの`

(`CG-CHAR-0048`, `CG-VOICE-0027`)

Riina becomes more credible not by sounding less "rock," but by allowing rock language to include ignorance, practice, difficulty, and choice.

## 3. Unit maturity is audible disagreement

By E12, Asterisk's developed MC competence can be redeployed for Cinderella Project work while Miku and Riina retain `ニャー` and `ロックンロール` markers. (`CG-REL-0032`)

The unit's professional success therefore does not require a merged voice.

Asterisk becomes usable because two incompatible idioms can coordinate.

# VII. Kirari, Chieri, Minami, Anastasia — marked voices gaining adult functions without normalization

## 1. Kirari — seriousness can remain `ハピハピ`

E10 demonstrates that stress can suppress characteristic markers. Under guilt Kirari's buoyancy and playful language recede. Recovery restores projection and ornament. (`CG-CHAR-0021`, `CG-VOICE-0007`)

That alone might still permit a "plain voice = serious truth" reading.

E18 closes that escape route.

Kirari gives a serious explanation of:

- her body size;
- clothing;
- work with Anzu;
- audience enjoyment;
- why she can smile;

while still speaking inside her recognizable lexical world:

> `可愛いー服着て`

> `可愛いー杏ちゃんと 可愛いーお仕事出来て`

> `とってもハピハピ`

(`CG-VOICE-0026`)

Her stylization is now capable of autobiographical seriousness.

That is a stronger maturation model than de-stylization.

## 2. Chieri — growth appears in clause structure while anxiety remains audible

Chieri's voice is high, hesitant, and breath-prone under pressure. E09 does not cure that identity. She can still say:

> `怖いけど 笑顔も自信無いけど`

and nevertheless complete:

> `一緒にやりたい`

(`CG-VOICE-0006`)

E18 develops the same principle. Early interview speech collapses into apologies, fragments, and ritual repetition around `カエルさん`. Later she can sustain longer self-motivated clauses:

> `だからこそ 素敵だなって思ったものを 紹介したいんです`

and concrete questions such as:

> `何を入れるお皿なんですか？`

(`CG-CHAR-0046`, `CG-VOICE-0025`)

The achievement is not vocal fearlessness.

It is increased **intentional syntax under continuing fear**.

## 3. Minami — stable polish gains directive, temporal, and personal functions

Minami's composed vocal identity is one of the series' most stable. It expands through function rather than personality replacement. (`CG-VOICE-0010`)

E12 is especially revealing. She can use delegated directive authority:

> `今日は私がまとめ役だから 私の指示に従ってちょうだい`

and later become the peer-requested timing anchor:

> `じゃあ掛け声よろしく みなみん`

> `せーのっ`

(`CG-VOICE-0021`)

This transition matters. Leadership is not only command. A trusted voice becomes a shared temporal reference against which other people coordinate.

By E21, Minami's stable composure can also carry explicit first-person adventure and desire rather than only responsibility for others. (`CG-CHAR-0052`)

## 4. Anastasia — mixed speech and measured tempo remain compatible with autonomy

Anastasia's development is similarly anti-normalizing.

In E20 she corrects Producer with:

> `ニェーット そうではなくて`

then states:

> `私 今 不安です`

> `分からないです`

and explains that she has not previously made such a decision alone. (`CG-VOICE-0028`)

Her mixed register does not have to become linguistically "more Japanese" or more conventionally assertive for her choice to become more autonomous.

The developmental marker is the ability to sustain longer causal self-explanation inside the same measured multilingual identity.

# VIII. Nana — the decisive case against "plain voice = authentic voice"

Episode 16 provides the series' clearest controlled test of vocal multiplicity.

Nana moves through at least four states. (`CG-VOICE-0023`)

## 1. Private bodily voice

In private she can sound tired and bodily:

> `はぁ 年々寝起きが辛くなる`

This state makes age, fatigue, and ordinary life audible.

## 2. Ordinary relational voice

Backstage and after work she can speak in less projected interpersonal language.

This state is not the opposite of Usamin. It is another social task.

## 3. Constrained "plain Abe Nana" professional voice

When the public event asks her to suppress the Usamin grammar, the result is not effortless naturalness. The professional interaction becomes less fluent and produces weaker audience recognition.

The lexical removal of persona therefore does not automatically free a truer performer.

## 4. Restored Usamin performance

When Nana returns to her authored public grammar, she can again deploy:

> `愛と希望を両耳にひっさげ ウサミン星よりやって来た`

> `さあ皆さん準備はいいですか`

> `いきますよー せーのっ`

The Usamin identity supplies a complete interactional technology:

- entrance mythology;
- projection;
- call cue;
- audience expectation;
- collective onset;
- shared response.

The audience does not own the persona, but it participates in making the persona socially real. (`CG-PERF-0031`)

## 5. The audio comparison

The original E16 audit used the mixed program track only directionally. It found the "plain" event-MC field substantially less energetic than the restored Usamin field, with the expected warning that crowd and music contaminate any numeric comparison.

A fresh probe against the newly supplied E16 MP3 reproduces the same direction: the restored Usamin section occupies a markedly higher-energy final mix than the plain section. Because these are not isolated stems, this is corroboration of the scene's already secure interactional evidence, not proof of a mathematically fixed Usamin pitch or loudness.

The decisive dramatic conclusion is therefore qualitative:

> **Nana's most heavily constructed voice is also one of her most fluent professional voices.**

Persona can be authored expertise.

# IX. Rika and authored fluency — the format can remain fixed while the voice becomes usable

Episode 17 offers a smaller but particularly clean experiment.

During the imposed kindergarten-costume rehearsal, Rika's introduction is repeatedly stopped for:

> `元気ないよ`

and:

> `もっと笑顔で`

Later, in the final broadcast, she can expand fluently into:

> `セクシーカリスマギャルの 城ヶ崎莉嘉でーす`

and sustain playful styling demonstration. (`CG-VOICE-0024`)

The professional medium has not disappeared.

The performance has not become "unconstructed."

What changes is Rika's relationship to the form: she finds a version she can inhabit.

Voice here is an index of **authorship within mediation**.

# X. Kaede and senior-idol range — quietness is compatible with authority

Kaede provides a useful senior comparison because her voice does not need high amplitude or aggressive syntax to carry authority.

E15 lets the same soft, composed range handle:

- joking private behavior;
- practical crowd management;
- warm MC;
- firm refusal.

(`CG-VOICE-0018`)

This matters for the series-wide model because it blocks another simplistic maturation code:

> authority = louder, harder, more masculine speech.

Kaede can refuse without becoming vocally combative.

Her professional authority resides partly in the fact that the same controlled voice is already socially credible across several contexts.

# XI. Relational register — people do not merely have voices; they have voices with someone

The most important vocal changes are often relational rather than purely individual.

## 1. Address terms can register changed relation without changed persona

Ranko's movement from `我が僕` to `我が友よ` is a compact example. The theatrical grammar stays stable while the relationship designation changes. (`CG-REL-0009`)

This is a recurring logic in the series: relational development can appear as **different use of the same idiolect**.

## 2. Nicknaming is Mio's social technology

Mio's rapid nicknaming is not simply comic noise. It reduces distance, establishes group rhythm, and converts administrative strangers into a social field. (`CG-CHAR-0003`)

The later significance of Mio going silent depends on this baseline.

## 3. Formality does not prevent intimacy

Producer's persistent polite speech is the strongest counterexample to the assumption that relational closeness must culminate in casual Japanese. (`CG-VOICE-0004`)

His relationship development occurs through the content and timing of formal speech:

- admitting uncertainty;
- admitting error;
- including himself in future work;
- yielding decisions;
- recognizing achievement.

## 4. Seriousness can reduce ornament without changing identity

Miku repeatedly shows that a marked persona can become temporarily less ornamented when the social task requires direct professional negotiation. (`CG-VOICE-0002`, `CG-VOICE-0008`)

Ranko shows the same principle through a different idiolect. (`CG-VOICE-0030`)

The useful concept is not "dropping the mask."

It is **register adaptation**.

## 5. Relational competence can precede self-definition

Uzuki is the strongest example. She can sustain reassurance and articulate other people's strengths before she can articulate her own professional specificity. (`CG-VOICE-0031`)

Thus conversational fluency should never be treated as one undifferentiated trait.

A person can be highly competent at:

- comforting;
- coordinating;
- joking;
- supporting;

and still be unable to answer:

> What do *you* want?

Voice analysis reveals that distinction more clearly than a trait label such as "shy" or "confident."

# XII. Stress states — what disappears, repeats, fragments, or falls silent

The series has several recurring vocal signs of destabilization, but none should be treated as a universal code.

## 1. Silence

Mio's E03 silence is meaningful because she normally fills social space. (`CG-VOICE-0001`, `CG-VOICE-0019`)

Uzuki's E24 silence is meaningful for a different reason: she has refused supplied wording and must decide whether any words will become hers. (`CG-VOICE-0035`)

Silence therefore has context-dependent functions.

## 2. Fragmentation

Mio's E06 breakdown and Uzuki's E20 inability to mediate both reduce ordinary sentence flow, but the developmental problems differ. (`CG-VOICE-0003`, `CG-VOICE-0029`)

Chieri's early E18 interview similarly fragments under pressure, yet her later speech becomes longer and more object-directed while nervousness remains. (`CG-VOICE-0025`)

Fragmentation is evidence of a speaker operating under pressure; it is not a diagnosis by itself.

## 3. Repetition

Uzuki's `頑張ります` becomes especially dangerous precisely because repetition can remain grammatically clean and emotionally sincere while semantic range narrows. (`CG-VOICE-0032`, `CG-VOICE-0033`)

This is different from fragmentation. A destabilized speaker may sound **more formulaic**, not necessarily less fluent.

## 4. Catchphrase suspension

Kirari's playful markers recede under guilt. (`CG-VOICE-0007`)

Miku's cat markers become less dense during vulnerable or serious professional speech. (`CG-VOICE-0002`, `CG-VOICE-0008`)

Neither suspension proves that the marked register was false.

## 5. Catchphrase persistence under seriousness

E18 Kirari is the inverse test: serious content can remain fully inside `ハピハピ`. (`CG-VOICE-0026`)

The function of a marker therefore has to be read scene by scene.

# XIII. Performance states — singing, MC, acting, and public speech are different vocal problems

Document 07 owns the full performance theory. The vocal contribution is that "public voice" is not one state.

## 1. E03 — structured song and open microphone

New Generations can enter a professionally supplied song structure after a synchronized pre-stage spoken onset, but the end of the song exposes a separate weakness when Mika hands them unstructured audience attention. (`CG-PERF-0016`, `CG-VOICE-0020`)

The anime therefore distinguishes:

- executing prepared vocal material;
- sustaining spontaneous MC;
- authoring public self-description.

## 2. Ranko — stage fluency can exceed conversational fluency

E13's contrast between hesitant volunteering and fluent projected performance demonstrates that public theatrical competence can be a real strength rather than compensatory falsity. (`CG-VOICE-0011`)

## 3. Asterisk — MC becomes unit competence

Asterisk's E11-developed interaction grammar becomes Project-level labor in E12. (`CG-REL-0032`)

Their marked voices remain differentiated while the unit gains professional reliability.

## 4. Minami — the voice as temporal infrastructure

Minami's `せーのっ` demonstrates that a leader's vocal function can become literally temporal: others coordinate their bodies against her count. (`CG-VOICE-0021`)

## 5. Nana — persona as audience-formation technology

Usamin is not merely a way Nana sounds. It is a repeatable performer-audience protocol. (`CG-VOICE-0023`, `CG-PERF-0031`)

## 6. Mio — acting expands the definition of "her voice"

Mio's acting work matters because it requires speech whose first-person referent is fictional. Her development therefore cannot be reduced to "speaking more honestly as herself."

A professional voice can become richer by learning to carry language that is deliberately not autobiographical.

This is another reason the series rejects the equation:

> unperformed = true.

# XV. Catchphrases and recurring formulations — a functional lifecycle

Recurring language is one of the anime's most efficient longitudinal devices. The mature question is not merely whether a phrase repeats, but what task it performs when it returns.

| Character / formulation | Early function | Stress or complication | Mature function |
|---|---|---|---|
| Uzuki — `頑張ります` | readiness, aspiration, effort | under-specification → recursion → conversational closure | reclaimed present-tense commitment after choice (`CG-VOICE-0032`–`0035`) |
| Rin — `分からない` / qualification | resistance to unsupported claims | can appear as uncertainty under relational conflict | uncertainty becomes reason to investigate (`CG-VOICE-0029`) |
| Miku — `ニャ`, `みく` | authored cat-idol identity | ornament density decreases in vulnerable/serious speech | serious professional competence and persona coexist (`CG-VOICE-0002`, `0008`) |
| Riina — `ロック` | aspirational self-brand | concrete practice exposes vagueness | can name novice labor and Asterisk as a chosen meaning of rock (`CG-VOICE-0027`) |
| Ranko — theatrical lexicon | primary expressive system | can stall when relational precision exceeds available formulation | theatrical + plain testimony become jointly usable (`CG-VOICE-0030`) |
| Kirari — `ハピハピ` | buoyant self/social language | guilt can suppress markers | serious autobiographical reasoning can remain inside the register (`CG-VOICE-0007`, `0026`) |
| Anastasia — Russian insertions | stable multilingual identity | uncertainty and independent choice | longer autonomous self-explanation without linguistic normalization (`CG-VOICE-0028`) |
| Nana — Usamin grammar | vocational myth + public interaction | "plain Nana" request reduces fluency/recognition | persona reclaimed as authored professional technology (`CG-VOICE-0023`) |
| Producer — `笑顔` language | recognition/selection criterion before full explanation | risks becoming an insufficient descriptor/instruction | `いい笑顔です` becomes recognition after authored performance (`CG-VOICE-0036`) |

The series' most sophisticated repeated-language device is therefore not a symbol but a **pragmatic history**.

Words accumulate prior uses.

A later utterance can sound identical and mean something developmentally different because the speaker's relation to the utterance has changed.

# XVI. Japanese lexical-profile reference — what remains recognizably stable

The longitudinal argument depends on stable baselines. The following profiles are deliberately functional rather than exhaustive linguistic inventories: they identify the recurring Japanese resources that the anime actually uses to make a character socially recognizable, then note what later development does with those resources.

## 1. Uzuki — polite standard speech organized around effort and reassurance

Uzuki's ordinary Japanese is comparatively unornamented. She relies on standard polite speech with adults and professional figures, `私` self-reference, frequent apology/acknowledgment, and the highly portable `頑張る` family of formulations.

The lack of a flamboyant idiolect is itself narratively important. Mio can brand herself socially almost on contact; Miku, Ranko, Kirari, Nana, and Riina possess conspicuous lexical signatures; Uzuki's easiest recurring signature is instead a **disposition toward effort**. (`CG-CHAR-0004`)

That is why her later crisis cannot be solved by telling her to "find a catchphrase." The problem is not insufficient surface distinctiveness. It is the increasingly painful comparison between a broadly prosocial, effort-oriented identity and peers whose professional specificities have become easier to name.

Her most reliable lexical resources remain useful in relationships—`大丈夫`, encouragement, agreement, doing one's best—long after they become insufficient for explaining what she individually wants. (`CG-VOICE-0031`)

## 2. Rin — brevity, qualification, and low-pressure sentence endings

Rin's Japanese tends toward lexical economy. She often resists ornamental explanation, uses plain speech with peers, and preserves qualification through constructions such as:

> `…と思う`

> `…かな`

> `分からない`

The resulting voice is not "emotionless." It is **epistemically conservative**. Rin avoids saying more than she believes the evidence warrants.

The longitudinal change is therefore unusually visible at the level of what her qualifications can now contain. `楽しかった…と思う` and `楽しくなる途中かな` are not weaker versions of an eventual unqualified `楽しい`. They are strong Rin-specific ways of owning positive experience. (`CG-CHAR-0032`, `CG-CHAR-0033`, `CG-VOICE-0013`)

By E20 the same verbal habit can generate action:

> `分からないから それを確かめたいの`

(`CG-VOICE-0029`)

Her mature speech preserves the right not to counterfeit certainty.

## 3. Mio — naming, momentum, and social conversion

Mio's lexical identity is built less around one catchphrase than around what she does to other people's names and to conversational tempo.

She generates nicknames, reframes uncertainty as an event, narrates what the group might become, and makes administrative relationships socially immediate. (`CG-CHAR-0003`)

That makes nicknaming a form of relational labor rather than mere comic characterization. `しまむー`, `しぶりん`, and similar formations reduce distance and put the group inside a shared informal rhythm.

Mio's later development therefore does not require more formal language. What changes is her capacity to regulate social momentum: knowing when to accelerate, when to interpret, when to let another person speak, and when a high-energy future narrative would become coercive rather than helpful.

## 4. Miku — self-naming and cat grammar as authored professional language

Miku's recurrent `みく` self-reference and `ニャ`/cat-related ornamentation make her one of the easiest speakers to identify from text alone. (`CG-CHAR-0005`)

The important full-series observation is that these markers are **gradient** rather than binary. Vulnerability and serious negotiation can reduce their density, but they are not discarded as childish residue. (`CG-VOICE-0002`, `CG-VOICE-0008`)

Miku's serious register is therefore best described as **lower-ornament Miku**, not "Miku without the persona."

That distinction is important because her professional authority eventually includes calling out poor conduct, negotiating unit identity, sustaining MC, and mentoring through direct criticism. The marked voice is not incompatible with seriousness; it has multiple intensity settings.

## 5. Riina — ordinary casual Japanese plus aspirational genre vocabulary

Riina's most conspicuous lexical marker is not an invented dialect but repeated orientation toward `ロック` as an evaluative word.

Early on, "rock" can operate as a flexible aspirational category: coolness, attitude, self-image, desired professional identity. Natsuki's questioning forces Riina to confront how much technical or genre specificity she can actually supply. (`CG-CHAR-0036`, `CG-CHAR-0048`, `CG-VOICE-0027`)

The mature development is not that she stops saying `ロック`. She becomes able to use the word **after admitting ignorance and practice difficulty**.

This is one of the series' cleanest cases of a catchword gaining semantic weight through contact with reality.

## 6. Ranko — deliberately authored theatrical Japanese with access to `私`

Ranko's lexicon includes elevated self-mythologizing forms such as `我`, `我が`, elaborate metaphor, and a fantasy-gothic register that translates ordinary situations into her own symbolic system.

The presence of comparatively plain `私` speech at vulnerable or explanatory moments does not create a binary identity. E08 and E21 make that especially clear. (`CG-VOICE-0005`, `CG-VOICE-0030`)

Ranko's development is best described as **increased translation bandwidth**. She does not abandon her symbolic language; she gains more ways to connect it to people who do not automatically inhabit the same code.

## 7. Kirari — lexical buoyancy as social method

Kirari's `ハピハピ`, elongated cute phrasing, and playful affect are not merely decorative markers. They help her turn social space toward encouragement and imaginative play.

E10 proves that these resources can recede under guilt. E18 proves that they can return inside serious self-explanation. (`CG-VOICE-0007`, `CG-VOICE-0026`)

The register therefore functions as a chosen social technology without exhausting the person who uses it.

## 8. Chieri — hesitation language and the development of object-directed clauses

Chieri's lexicon under stress contains apologies, fillers, incomplete starts, and recourse to a ritual object such as `カエルさん`. (`CG-VOICE-0025`)

Her development becomes especially visible when she starts producing longer clauses about the object in front of her rather than about her own failure to speak correctly.

The grammatical expansion is small but meaningful:

> from self-monitoring and ritual repetition

> toward description, curiosity, and question.

Her voice does not cease sounding anxious. Attention changes direction.

## 9. Minami — polished standard speech capable of becoming an anchor

Minami has relatively little flamboyant idiolect. Her recognizable resource is instead polished, orderly, socially legible speech.

That makes her well suited to tasks requiring people to understand what happens next. E12 develops this from consultation and directive speech into literal temporal anchoring through `せーのっ`. (`CG-VOICE-0010`, `CG-VOICE-0021`)

Her later first-person language of `冒険` matters because the same responsible register can begin to carry desire for herself rather than only competence on behalf of others. (`CG-CHAR-0052`)

## 10. Anastasia — multilingual continuity rather than linguistic assimilation

Anastasia's Japanese incorporates Russian lexical markers such as `ダー` and `ニェーット` while maintaining a measured, careful rhythm.

The series never makes better Japanese or reduced Russian marking a prerequisite for adult agency. In E20 she can correct Producer, state fear, state uncertainty, and describe the novelty of deciding alone while remaining unmistakably Anastasia. (`CG-VOICE-0028`)

This is important because it makes linguistic difference compatible with professional autonomy rather than treating maturation as assimilation toward a neutral cast norm.

## 11. Nana — mythology, call language, and ordinary bodily speech

Nana's Usamin vocabulary is unusually elaborate because it is both identity narration and audience protocol. The public voice contains origin mythology, recurring calls, heightened projection, and cues that tell the audience what kind of response the situation invites. (`CG-CHAR-0041`, `CG-VOICE-0023`, `CG-PERF-0031`)

Private tired speech demonstrates that this is not her only register. But plurality does not invalidate the public one.

The strongest lexical point is that **the most constructed register is also the most interactionally complete** in the E16 event context.

## 12. Producer — honorific distance as a durable ethical style

Producer's repeated surname-plus-`さん`, professional politeness, compact explanations, and restrained first-person speech preserve interpersonal distance even as relationships deepen.

That distance is not simply emotional deficiency. It is part of how he understands role responsibility.

The series can therefore deepen his relationships without converting him into a casual buddy-manager. (`CG-VOICE-0004`)

When his formal speech gains confession, refusal, uncertainty, or recognition, the form itself becomes evidence that intimacy and answerability do not require collapsed boundaries.

## 13. Mishiro — executive syntax and the language of irreversible consequence

Mishiro's speech is comparatively free of filler when she is acting institutionally. Short declaratives and directives can carry enormous consequences because the organization gives them force. (`CG-VOICE-0015`)

Her vocal authority therefore depends on **institutional backing plus syntactic economy**, not theatrical aggression.

This is why the phrase `白紙に戻す` lands with such force: almost nothing in the wording softens, negotiates, or narrates the decision.

# XVII. Naming and address as relational evidence

Japanese relational register is not limited to politeness levels. Names themselves can become evidence.

## 1. Mio's nicknames create social proximity

Mio converts formal names into immediately reusable social forms. (`CG-CHAR-0003`)

This is not a universal privilege. It works because Mio repeatedly reads the interactional room and makes informality feel participatory rather than imposed.

Her nicknaming therefore belongs to the same character system as her later leadership: she is unusually good at **making a social formation feel already underway**.

## 2. Ranko changes the relational noun without abandoning the register

`我が僕` → `我が友よ` is perhaps the purest series example of relational change encoded inside an idiolect. (`CG-REL-0009`)

The development would actually be less Ranko-specific if she merely switched to neutral standard Japanese.

## 3. Arisu's name boundary shows that intimacy can be negotiated explicitly

E22 gives a compact but important Project:Krone case. After Fumika's crisis, Arisu changes the permitted form of address:

> from `橘さん`

> to permission for `ありす`

and therefore `ありすちゃん`.

(`CG-REL-0050`)

This is not merely cute aftermath. A name boundary becomes a relationship boundary that Arisu herself revises.

The scene demonstrates an important principle for the whole document:

> **relational closeness is not inferred only from tone; characters can linguistically authorize a different relationship.**

## 4. Producer's persistent surnames preserve role structure

Producer's relationships become deeply consequential without widespread conversion to nickname intimacy. This reinforces the point made above: Japanese relational development is not a one-way staircase from formal to casual.

Different relationships mature through different grammars.

# XVIII. Targeted mixed-track acoustic corroboration

The v2 method explicitly warns against treating final-program audio as isolated-vocal laboratory material. The following probes were therefore run only to test **specific already-established dramatic contrasts**.

## 1. Exact Uzuki phrase: `島村卯月頑張ります`

The same wording was isolated at several mainline points in the newly supplied MP3s.

| Episode | Context | Cue duration | Approx. whole-cue RMS dBFS | Evidentiary use |
|---|---|---:|---:|---|
| E01 | early aspirational self-introduction | 2.25 s | -24.1 | baseline only |
| E21 | self-definition interview ending | 2.13 s | -25.0 | baseline only |
| E23 | defensive closure under NG confrontation | 2.13 s | -29.1 | directionally less energetic final mix |
| E24 | reclaimed stage commitment | 2.75 s | -23.0 | directionally more energetic public mix |
| E25 | post-recovery ordinary recurrence | 2.42 s | -27.2 | confirms no need for permanently heightened delivery |

These numbers are **not** used to claim that Uzuki literally changes vocal loudness by a fixed amount. E24 is a stage environment; the other scenes have different ambience, score, and mix treatment.

Their limited value is narrower: the source does not contradict the qualitative reading that E23 is a constricted conversational use while E24 is a newly projected public use. The much stronger evidence remains the surrounding dramaturgy and interlocutor response.

## 2. E16 plain Nana versus restored Usamin

A fresh directional probe of the supplied E16 program audio produced the same overall pattern as the original episode audit:

- "plain Nana" event passage: approximately **-27.8 dBFS** whole-segment RMS;
- restored Usamin event passage: approximately **-22.7 dBFS** whole-segment RMS.

The difference includes audience response, music, and stage mixing and therefore cannot be attributed solely to Nana's voice.

That contamination is actually part of the dramatic point: Usamin is an **interactional field**, not an isolated laryngeal setting. The restored grammar produces cues that the audience knows how to answer, and the final mix becomes correspondingly fuller. (`CG-VOICE-0023`, `CG-PERF-0031`)

## 3. What was deliberately not measured

No cross-character pitch ranking is used. No claim such as "mature Rin speaks X Hz lower" or "Uzuki's recovery raises pitch" is supportable from these mixed tracks.

The audio is most analytically valuable for:

- timing;
- pause structure;
- the presence or suspension of speech;
- whether a sentence is completed;
- interactional response;
- energy-field changes around MC/persona states;
- and verifying that repeated text occupies different performed contexts.

This is proportional acoustic rigor rather than false precision.

# XX. What the targeted audio re-audit adds

The dedicated E01–E26 audio/subtitle packages do not overturn the existing claim ledger. They strengthen the corpus by making several longitudinal distinctions easier to verify at source level.

## 1. Subtitle repetition can now be separated from performed repetition

The exact same text `島村卯月頑張ります` occurs at multiple mainline points, including E01, E21, E23, E24, and E25.

Text search alone could tempt an analyst to treat these as equivalent catchphrase repetitions.

The audiovisual chronology demonstrates otherwise. Their surrounding pause structure, conversational object, interlocutor response, and performance context change radically.

That is why the specialist conclusion is pragmatic rather than lexical-frequency based.

## 2. The E23/E24 Uzuki contrast is not just interpretive paraphrase

The E23 phrase occurs in a scene where Rin explicitly challenges the utterance as evasion.

The E24 phrase occurs only after a prolonged sparse interval in which nobody supplies Uzuki with wording.

A lightweight mixed-track energy probe of the exact cue is directionally consistent with stronger public projection in E24 than E23, but scene mixing prevents stronger numeric claims.

The decisive evidence is temporal architecture:

> challenged formula → collapse → choice → silence → audience address → self-authored formula.

## 3. Nana's persona contrast remains one of the clearest source-audible experiments

The newly supplied E16 file reproduces the already documented contrast between the thinner "plain Nana" event field and the more energetic, responsive Usamin field.

Again, music and crowd sound prevent isolated-vocal measurement.

But the source confirms that the scene is designed not merely as costume difference but as a difference in **interactional vocal fluency**.

## 4. Full-program audio matters for silence

Mio's E03 silence and Uzuki's E24 near-silence are not recoverable from dialogue text alone. Their meaning depends on what surrounds the absence:

- loud venue pressure in Mio's case;
- a refusal of supplied language and waiting audience in Uzuki's.

This is exactly why voice analysis requires the complete program track rather than subtitle extraction alone.

# XXI. Interpretive guardrails

The full-series voice analysis supports several explicit cautions.

## 1. Plain speech is not automatically true speech

Nana, Miku, Ranko, Kirari, and Riina all falsify that shortcut.

## 2. Stylization is not automatically alienation

A stylized voice may be:

- personally owned;
- professionally useful;
- relationally shared;
- emotionally protective;
- aesthetically authored;
- or all of these at once.

The correct question is what the speaker can do through it.

## 3. Catchphrase loss is not automatically growth

Loss can mark:

- guilt;
- fatigue;
- vulnerability;
- task adaptation;
- professional constraint.

Likewise, catchphrase persistence is not automatically stagnation.

## 4. High pitch is not childishness and lower pitch is not maturity

The mixed-track source does not support such a simple longitudinal claim, and the dramatic evidence repeatedly contradicts it.

## 5. Fluency is domain-specific

Uzuki can be fluent in reassurance and poor at self-definition.

Ranko can be fluent onstage and hesitant in spontaneous volunteering.

Mio can be socially fluent and initially awkward in scripted acting.

Rika can be energetic in one authored public frame and unusably flat in another imposed one.

## 6. Silence is not one symbol

Mio's silence, Uzuki's silence, conversational pauses, and performance silence perform different functions. (`CG-MOTIF-0039`)

## 7. Performed state is not mind-reading

A strained or fragmented delivery is evidence that an utterance is produced under pressure. It is not by itself proof of a hidden motive.

## 8. Singing voice does not erase speaking identity

Performance can expand what a character can communicate, but song lyrics should not automatically be treated as omniscient interior monologue. Document 07 retains the full song/performance argument.

# XXII. Longitudinal synthesis — from fixed markers to pragmatic repertoire

The series' voice architecture can be summarized as a sequence of increasing functional range.

## Stage 1 — identity is quickly legible

Early episodes make the cast distinguishable through high-salience vocal resources:

- effort;
- reserve;
- exuberance;
- cat language;
- gothic theater;
- rock aspiration;
- playful buoyancy;
- polished composure;
- multilingual speech.

This is efficient ensemble construction.

## Stage 2 — stress reveals that markers are not exhaustive

Characters encounter situations where established speech stops working smoothly:

- Mio stops talking;
- Miku de-ornaments;
- Kirari loses buoyancy;
- Chieri fragments;
- Ranko hesitates;
- Uzuki repeats rather than specifies.

The series does not conclude that the early voices were false.

It shows that every voice has a range problem.

## Stage 3 — professional experience expands the range

The same voices acquire new tasks:

- Minami can command and coordinate time;
- Asterisk can MC;
- Ranko can collaborate and testify;
- Riina can admit novice difficulty;
- Chieri can ask concrete questions under fear;
- Anastasia can reason autonomously;
- Rika can appropriate a professional frame;
- Mio can act;
- Producer can refuse upward.

## Stage 4 — mature collectivity preserves difference

E15's polyphony and E25's differentiated professional coexistence make the final point:

> **The cast does not mature into one shared adult voice.**

Professional common time does not require lexical sameness.

The strongest full-series voice claim, `CG-VOICE-0037` in the final sound audit's conceptual summary, is therefore better expressed as the governing proposition of this document:

> **Vocal maturation expands pragmatic repertoire rather than erasing marked voices.**

The series can make this claim because its most successful late states remain unmistakably character-specific.

# XXV. Final conclusion

The most useful way to describe voice in *THE IDOLM@STER CINDERELLA GIRLS* is not as a ladder from immature performance toward mature naturalness.

The series is far more interested in **capacity**.

Can a character's established voice carry:

- fear without becoming meaningless;
- professionalism without erasing personality;
- seriousness without abandoning stylization;
- intimacy without mandatory casualization;
- uncertainty without passivity;
- authority without shouting;
- persona without alienation;
- public performance without self-erasure;
- and development without sounding like everyone else?

For many characters, the answer gradually becomes yes.

That is why the strongest full-series vocal development is often subtle.

Miku still sounds like Miku.

Ranko still sounds like Ranko.

Kirari still sounds like Kirari.

Anastasia still sounds like Anastasia.

Producer remains formal.

Rin remains cautious.

Mio remains socially energetic.

Uzuki can still say:

> `島村卯月頑張ります`

The difference is that by the end, these voices are not merely recognizable.

They are **more usable**.

They can carry a larger portion of the person's life.

The mature formulation is therefore:

> **In Cinderella Girls, vocal maturation is the expansion of pragmatic repertoire inside a continuous person. Development does not require the marked voice to disappear; it requires that the voice stop being trapped in one role.**

And Uzuki provides the clearest final proof: the phrase that once became a way not to answer can become hers again after she has chosen what she is answering for.
