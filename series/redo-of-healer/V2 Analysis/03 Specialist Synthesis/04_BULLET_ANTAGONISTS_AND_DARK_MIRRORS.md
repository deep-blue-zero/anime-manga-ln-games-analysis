# Bullet, Antagonists, and Dark Mirrors — V2
## Predation, administration, corrupted offices, sacred hierarchy, and the failure to recognize another person’s jurisdiction over the self

**Series:** 『回復術士のやり直し』 / *Redo of Healer*  
**Primary scope:** Japanese light novels Volumes 1–10  
**Revision status:** V2 specialist synthesis, revised after Phase-1 audit and Phase-2 Japanese-primary re-entry  
**Primary evidence conventions:** Volume / chapter / XHTML locators refer to the frozen source corpus documented in the Phase-2 evidence ledgers.

**Content note:** This document necessarily discusses sexual predation, grooming, coercive conditioning, slavery, torture, and mass violence. It treats those subjects clinically and analytically rather than reconstructing explicit material.

---

# Executive thesis

The antagonists of *Redo of Healer* are most useful when they are not collapsed into a single category called “abusers” or “people who misuse power.”

They represent different ways power can fail to recognize another person as a person:

- **Blade** turns desire into permission.
- **Norn** turns categories and administrative necessity into permission.
- **Prohm Jioral** treats family and state as extensions of sovereign ownership.
- **Hakuou** shows how an office can deform the person who appears to command it.
- **Hiseki** combines real loyalty and public responsibility with a status hierarchy that makes some bodies insufficiently protected.
- **Faran and the Holy Emperor** convert species hierarchy into sacred and geopolitical jurisdiction.
- **Bullet** understands people with extraordinary accuracy while denying them the right to determine what their own lives are for.

Bullet is therefore the decisive antagonist.

He is not merely Keyaru’s worst first-timeline abuser, nor merely the final name on a revenge list. He is a former intelligence professional, priest and orphanage operator, Hero, strategist, teacher, cultic organizer, black-power researcher, and predator whose methods become part of the competence Keyaru later uses to survive.

The most important source statement arrives in Volume 4, when Keyaru acknowledges:

> `認めたくはないが、彼は俺の教師だ。`  
> He does not want to admit it, but Bullet is his teacher.

**Locator:** V4 — 第二十話「回復術士は夢を見る」 — `p-021.xhtml` ¶47 and surrounding passage.

That fact makes Bullet psychologically contaminating in a way Blade, Prohm, or Hakuou are not. Keyaru can kill an enemy and remove him from the world. He cannot kill the fact that some of his best survival methods were learned from a monster.

The V2 Bullet thesis is therefore:

> **Bullet can understand a person with extraordinary accuracy while still refusing to recognize that person’s right to author his own life.**

This is deeper than sadism.

Bullet can correctly identify what Keyaru fears, what he loves, what he will choose, what trauma produced him, and even—by Volume 10—what course of action may be necessary for Keyaru to survive. But knowledge never becomes a moral limit. It becomes leverage.

His idea of love is not simply false feeling. The more disturbing possibility is that his attachment is genuine **as attachment** while remaining radically defective **as recognition**:

> I know you.  
> I want you.  
> I understand what will save you.  
> Therefore I may decide for you.

That proposition survives his defeat, his regression, and his final death.

# I. Bullet — abuser, teacher, strategist, and anti-personhood

## 1. First-timeline significance: abuse by a supposed protector

Bullet enters Keyaru’s psychological history as one of the Hero-party adults who participates in the first timeline’s reduction of the Healing Hero to usable material.

That origin should remain explicit. Later sophistication does not make Bullet’s first function less fundamental.

The first volume itself states the abuse clinically as part of Keyaru's revenge memory, then gives the first-timeline Hero-party sequence in fuller form.

**Locators:** V1 — `p-013.xhtml` ¶42–43; V1 — 第零話「ケヤルが目覚めた日」 — `p-014.xhtml`, especially ¶214–269.

For Keyaru, Bullet belongs to the same traumatic system that teaches:

- Hero status does not protect bodily autonomy;
- adults occupying prestigious protective roles may be predators;
- dependency can be engineered and exploited;
- public usefulness can intensify vulnerability rather than reduce it;
- someone who understands one’s weakness may use that understanding to obtain access rather than provide safety.

Bullet’s priestly and orphanage roles make this especially corrosive. The social positions attached to him—Hero, priest, guardian, teacher—are roles that ordinarily promise protection, instruction, and care. Bullet converts precisely those forms of trust into access.

This is one reason “corruption of care” remains a better description than simple cruelty.

A battlefield sadist openly threatens. A corrupted caretaker can make dependence itself dangerous.

## 2. The dark teacher

Volume 4 changes Bullet from remembered predator into formative teacher.

**Locator:** V4 — 第二十話「回復術士は夢を見る」 — `p-021.xhtml` ¶35–62.

The source establishes his background in intelligence work and the fact that he passed techniques and knowledge to Keyaru. The relevant cluster includes:

- lie detection;
- friend/enemy classification;
- survival logic;
- psychological manipulation;
- conditioning methods;
- information discipline;
- suspicion of surfaces;
- contingency thinking.

Volume 5 makes the inheritance even more explicit. Keyaru calls Bullet both revenge target and teacher and studies the intelligence material Bullet left behind.

**Locator:** V5 — 第三話「回復術士は遺書を読み解く」 — `p-004.xhtml` ¶102–134, especially ¶114.

Volume 7 then shows Keyaru consciously reusing methods learned from Bullet:

**Locator:** V7 — 第二話「回復術士はペットを教育する」 — `p-003.xhtml` around ¶150.

The important point is not “Bullet secretly helped Keyaru and therefore deserves credit.”

The point is much darker:

> **survival knowledge can remain useful even when the relationship through which it was learned was monstrous.**

Keyaru’s competence is partly built from appropriated hostile knowledge. He survives by taking techniques that once made him vulnerable and turning them outward.

That makes Bullet a genuine dark mentor. Keyaru does not become Bullet, but some of Bullet’s cognitive machinery remains inside Keyaru’s way of reading the world.

## 3. Intelligence without recognition

Bullet’s most distinctive strength is not raw combat power.

It is that he understands the structure of choices.

He repeatedly asks questions like:

- What does the opponent value?
- What information can he see?
- What information does he think I can see?
- Which apparent objective is actually a decoy?
- What dependency can be turned into a constraint?
- What action will the target regard as unthinkable?
- What future state can be forced by narrowing the available choices?

This is why Keyaru gives him unusually high intellectual credit.

In Volume 7, while reconstructing Bullet’s likely plans, Keyaru admits that on a normal strategic field he cannot simply outread him; the experience gap is too large. He has to simulate Bullet’s thinking using years of first-timeline exposure and the knowledge acquired through healing.

**Locator:** V7 — 第十二話「回復術士はショートカットする」 — `p-013.xhtml` ¶9–34, especially the admission that ordinary strategic reading favors Bullet.

Even the aircraft does not make Bullet conceptually obsolete. Keyaru explicitly assumes Bullet is the one opponent likely to imagine attack modes others would fail to anticipate.

**Locator:** V7 — 第十五話「回復術士は爆撃する」 — `p-016.xhtml` ¶94–97.

This is the paradox at the center of their relationship:

> **the man Keyaru hates most is also one of the people whose intelligence he respects most.**

That is why Bullet is such a strong mirror for Ellen as well. Both can read systems, motives, and people with frightening accuracy. The difference is not simply intelligence versus empathy. Ellen can also be ruthless.

The difference is that Bullet treats accurate understanding as a claim to jurisdiction.

He knows the person, therefore he believes he may process the person.

## 4. His philosophy of persons: cultivation, preservation, replacement

Volume 4’s Bullet-focused chapter establishes that the “doll” language of Volume 8 is not an improvised insult added late in the series.

**Locator:** V4 — 第二十一話「【砲】の勇者は恋をする」 — `p-022.xhtml`, especially ¶62–72.

The chapter presents a recurring structure in Bullet’s relation to vulnerable boys:

> **selection → cultivation → idealized state → preservation → substitution**

The exact acts are less analytically important than the ontology beneath them.

Bullet wants a person fixed into the form he regards as most beautiful. Change is deterioration. Aging is loss. Independent development threatens the object he desires. The answer is to stop change and replace the individual with another suitable object when necessary.

This produces a radically anti-developmental idea of love:

> **the beloved should remain the form I chose.**

That is why Bullet’s later language about dolls and Keyaru’s Volume-8 argument about accumulated history fit together so precisely.

Keyaru’s household is morally compromised in origin, but the people inside it keep changing:

- Freia acquires relationships, military competence, and her own moral perceptions;
- Ellen reconstructs Norn’s strategic capacities and chooses Ellen;
- Eve remains sovereign;
- Setsuna retains a tribe-centered loyalty independent of romance;
- Kureha argues with Keyaru and develops heroic authority;
- Guren accumulates her own constraints and attachments.

Bullet’s preferred dependents are valuable insofar as they remain aligned to his authored form.

The contrast is therefore not “Keyaru respects autonomy and Bullet does not.” That would be false.

It is:

> **Keyaru increasingly values what his companions have become outside complete control; Bullet experiences deviation from authored form as defect.**

## 5. Bullet’s care is not always fake—and that makes him worse

A useful V2 correction is to avoid describing every helpful action by Bullet as merely camouflage.

Volume 5 complicates him. Kureha has reason to remember him as a capable, even admirable adult. Bullet warns her about Jioral’s black-power plan and leaves information that materially helps Keyaru’s side.

**Locators:** V5 — Prologue「【砲】の勇者の綺麗な部分」 — `p-001.xhtml`; V5 — 第三話「回復術士は遺書を読み解く」 — `p-004.xhtml` ¶81–120.

Keyaru himself grudgingly recognizes a split: outside Bullet’s predatory fixation, he can behave in ways conventionally classified as competent or benevolent.

This matters because Bullet’s thematic problem is not inability to produce care-like outcomes.

It is inability to let care be constrained by the recipient’s authorship.

He can:

- protect Kureha;
- train capable people;
- maintain an intelligence network;
- provide material comfort;
- correctly diagnose another person’s danger;
- in Volume 10, materially save Keyaru from a doomed course.

But his care repeatedly contains the hidden proposition:

> **because I care, I may decide what you must become.**

This is the same structure that makes certain paternalistic forms of rescue morally dangerous even when the rescuer is factually correct.

Bullet is therefore more interesting than a hypocrite whose kindness is always fraudulent. Some of the care may be real. It is simply not accompanied by respect for separateness.

## 6. Preserving himself under black transformation

Bullet’s relationship to black power reveals another form of the same personality.

When Prohm’s system attempts to convert him into an obedient black-power piece, Bullet immediately rejects becoming a “dirty doll” himself.

**Locator:** V4 — 第二十一話「【砲】の勇者は恋をする」 — `p-022.xhtml` ¶103–147.

This is an illuminating asymmetry.

Bullet strongly resists being processed by another person while treating the processing of others as acceptable when he is the author.

He anticipates the threat, prepares against it, preserves rationality, and later develops or applies methods that allow favored followers to retain consciousness under black enhancement. Volume 8 calls the process used for the “angel” retainers `聖別 / seibetsu`—consecration.

**Locator:** V8 — 第十九話「回復術士は見抜く」 — `p-020.xhtml` ¶23–69, especially ¶33–50.

Religion, surgery, black power, elite identity, and personal devotion fuse into one system.

The “angels” are not merely stronger monsters. Their ability to remain conscious becomes a sign of special status and closeness to Bullet.

He converts bodily modification into sacrament.

That is the mature form of his earlier caretaker corruption:

> **processing is not hidden from the ideology; processing becomes proof of favor.**

## 7. Taslam: ranged force and the concealed true form

Bullet’s Divine Armament, `神砲タスラム / Shinpō Taslam`, fits his character unusually well.

Its obvious function is distance. Bullet is at his best when he decides the geometry of engagement before the opponent can reach him: range, line of fire, suppression, timing, and the threat that an attack may be redirected toward someone the target must protect. Volume 5 repeatedly emphasizes his deliberate maintenance of advantageous distance, and Volumes 7–8 scale the same logic from duel range into strategic reach.

The weapon also has a concealed structure. Volume 10 reveals that the enormous cannon form is not the whole of Taslam: its core/true body is a handgun-like weapon hidden beneath the familiar external configuration.

**Locator:** V10 — Epilogue「回復術士のやり直し」 — `p-022.xhtml` ¶23–31.

As symbolism, the fit is strong but should remain labeled interpretation rather than system fact:

> **visible overwhelming force conceals a smaller decisive mechanism.**

That is Bullet’s strategic style. The public role is not necessarily the operative role; the apparent objective is not necessarily the real objective; the large weapon is not necessarily the final weapon. Taslam therefore formalizes the same habit that governs his intelligence work: make the opponent react to what is visible while the decisive structure remains hidden.

## 8. Volume 5: winning the objective rather than the duel

Bullet’s first major strategic victory over Keyaru comes through the Philosopher’s Stone.

**Locator:** V5 — 第十九話「回復術士は最後の復讐を誓う」 — `p-020.xhtml` ¶18–87.

Keyaru defeats Hakuou. Bullet allows the expensive part of the operation to happen, then:

- keeps Keyaru occupied at range;
- redirects pressure toward Eve, knowing Keyaru will defend her;
- uses black forces to seize the actual objective;
- leaves once the objective has been acquired.

The scene demonstrates Bullet’s governing strategic principle:

> **do not confuse defeating the opponent with winning the operation.**

Keyaru can be the superior improvisational combatant and still lose strategically if Bullet defines what counts as victory.

This becomes the model for Volumes 7–8.

## 9. Volume 7: the enemy who can outplan Keyaru

Volume 7 is the strongest demonstration that Bullet’s danger is systemic.

He exploits or penetrates:

- Granzbach’s political ambitions;
- intelligence networks with former subordinates and orphanage-trained personnel;
- misinformation;
- route prediction;
- layered military traps;
- black-monster escalation;
- diplomatic staging.

The depth of his institutional footprint is explicit. Keyaru notes that many Jioral intelligence personnel were trained by Bullet or by people Bullet trained, creating a network that cannot simply be purged without destroying the service itself.

**Locator:** V7 — 第二話「回復術士はペットを教育する」 — `p-003.xhtml` ¶15–24.

This is important politically as well as personally. Bullet scales intimacy into organization.

His model is not exactly Prohm’s top-down sovereignty. It is a **network of cultivated dependents** whose professional competence and personal loyalty overlap.

Keyaru eventually produces something structurally comparable through household-state trust networks, which is why Bullet remains a political mirror as well as a sexual/psychological one.

Volume 7 culminates in genuine strategic defeat. Keyaru cannot solve Bullet by superior force because Bullet repeatedly attacks:

- timing;
- logistics;
- information;
- diplomatic conditions;
- emotional priorities.

The series thereby gives Bullet a role few antagonists receive:

> **he proves Keyaru is not the smartest person simply because he is the protagonist with future knowledge.**

## 10. Volume 8: doll-play becomes the explicit philosophical conflict

Volume 8 converts the Bullet conflict from revenge into a test of personhood.

Ellen first proposes the cold reset solution: kill Eve, use the Stone, return to an earlier line, prevent Bullet from acquiring his advantage. Keyaru refuses because the present people beside him are not replaceable by same-shaped earlier versions.

Then Bullet independently tries to force essentially the same solution.

His trap is almost perfectly designed around Keyaru’s old weakness:

- isolate Keyaru;
- hold Setsuna as leverage;
- keep Keyaru starved of information;
- attack the world outside his sight;
- assume Keyaru’s women are dependent “meat dolls” who cannot act without him;
- create enough despair that Keyaru will choose a reset.

The key exchange occurs when Bullet learns that the women have continued fighting successfully without Keyaru.

**Locator:** V8 — 第十二話「回復術士はサインを送る」 — `p-013.xhtml` ¶14–15.

Bullet describes them as dolls. Keyaru answers that they think and move without him, are strong, and that he is not interested in `人形遊び / ningyō-asobi`—doll play.

The claim must be stress-tested because Keyaru himself has literally rewritten and conditioned people. V2 should not turn his self-description into acquittal.

But the plot then supplies evidence beyond his words.

During the counteroffensive:

- Ellen commands independently;
- Freia develops and deploys purification magic;
- Kureha fights strategically without Keyaru’s direction;
- Setsuna resists Bullet’s conditioning;
- Eve contributes sovereign power;
- Guren’s prior relationships and preparations become decisive;
- Casta acts as a political-military ally rather than a puppet.

Bullet’s mistake is therefore not simply underestimating combat statistics.

He misunderstands **what kind of social structure Keyaru has accidentally built**.

The great irony of the Bullet arc is:

> **Bullet tries to prove Keyaru’s companions are dolls and creates the conditions under which they prove they are not.**

## 11. Bullet discovers the first redo—and still cannot understand the refusal to repeat it

Near the Volume-8 climax, Bullet reconstructs Keyaru’s temporal history with extraordinary accuracy.

**Locator:** V8 — 第二十二話「回復術士は賭けにでる」 — `p-023.xhtml` ¶74–111.

He infers:

- Keyaru knew things no village boy should know;
- his hatred predated any offense Bullet had committed in the new line;
- healing must have been used to redo the world;
- the first timeline likely involved the Hero party’s abuse;
- the Sage Stone enabled the reset.

This may be Bullet’s purest feat of psychological intelligence.

He understands the causal history of Keyaru almost perfectly.

Then he makes the decisive error.

He assumes that because the world can be reset, present persons can be replaced.

Keyaru’s response is the heart of the arc:

> time can be rewound, but accumulated time with another person cannot simply be recreated as the same relationship.

The present Eve, Freia, Setsuna, Kureha, Guren, and others are not interchangeable with earlier same-shaped versions.

Bullet can understand **why Keyaru became Keyarga**.

He cannot understand **why Keyaru would choose another person’s continuity over strategic optimization**.

That is the boundary of his intelligence.

## 12. The twenty-year regression: punishment, metaphysics, and reversal

Keyaru’s Volume-8 victory over Bullet uses healing as individual temporal regression.

**Locator:** V8 — 第二十三話「回復術士は超越する」 — `p-024.xhtml` approximately ¶162–175.

The operation reverses approximately twenty years of Bullet’s personal development, stripping away:

- adult age;
- black enhancement;
- accumulated level;
- magic;
- Hero status/class development.

The memory-bearing subject remains.

Narratively, the punishment is exact because it reverses several Bullet structures at once:

- the processor is processed;
- the powerful adult becomes physically vulnerable;
- the Hero identity is stripped;
- the man who tries to force a global reset receives a localized reset;
- memory remains to register the loss.

The punishment is not ethically clean. It is explicitly designed as revenge, and Keyaru intends further suffering.

But it is thematically precise.

Most importantly, it does **not** cure Bullet’s governing problem.

His body is regressed.

His ontology of other people is not.

Volume 10 proves that distinction.

## 13. Volume 10: Keyaru no longer needs Bullet—and Bullet cannot tolerate it

Volume 10 first establishes Keyaru’s change before returning Bullet to the center.

**Locator:** V10 — 第八話「回復術士は招き入れる」 — `p-009.xhtml` ¶20–36.

Keyaru acknowledges that the old Keyarga would have subordinated the world to completing revenge. The present Keyaru no longer needs to be the person who kills Bullet. His desired life has become more important than the ritual completion of vengeance.

This creates a reversal in their relationship.

For years, Keyaru’s identity was organized partly around Bullet.

By Volume 10, Bullet is no longer necessary to Keyaru’s self-definition.

Bullet notices.

When he returns during the candidate catastrophe, he complains that Keyaru has become dull: the Keyaru who lived only to hate and kill him is gone.

This reaction is revealing. Bullet does not merely want Keyaru alive.

He wants to occupy the center of Keyaru’s attention.

To be hated absolutely is still to be granted exclusive importance.

His desire is possessive even when its emotional medium is murderous hatred.

## 14. Volume 10: salvation as autonomy violation

Bullet’s final act is the mature form of his entire philosophy.

**Locator:** V10 — Epilogue「回復術士のやり直し」 — `p-022.xhtml` ¶23–53.

The doomed timeline has left Keyaru in an impossible position. Ellen is reported dead. Eve is dying. Ragna and Lapis have been compelled by a world-system intervention. Keyaru nevertheless decides he would rather spend the remaining time loving the people still present than erase the timeline.

Bullet disagrees.

Crucially, Bullet may be **factually right** that Keyaru’s chosen path is catastrophically poor.

He then:

- threatens Eve’s heart;
- imposes a timed binary choice;
- explicitly rejects mere incapacitation as sufficient;
- forces Keyaru to kill him;
- knowingly causes another Hero-killing event;
- makes reset effectively unavoidable.

He calls the act love.

Keyaru later recognizes that Bullet’s intervention saved him in a consequential sense.

This must not be rewritten into redemption.

The final act is Bullet distilled:

> **I understand your situation better than you are willing to act on it. I know the future you should choose. Therefore I will remove your ability to choose otherwise.**

That is the same logic by which he has always treated beloved objects.

The outcome changes.

The method does not.

Bullet’s death is therefore simultaneously:

- useful;
- self-sacrificial in a narrow sense;
- emotionally attached;
- coercive;
- autonomy-negating;
- perfectly continuous with his prior character.

The mature reading should allow all six propositions to stand together.

## 15. Does Bullet love Keyaru?

A simplistic answer—“no, Bullet only wants a doll”—is less interesting than what Volume 10 permits.

Bullet appears genuinely attached to Keyaru in the sense that:

- Keyaru matters to him uniquely;
- he follows Keyaru across enormous changes;
- he studies him obsessively;
- he wants to be recognized by him;
- he is willing to die through Keyaru’s hand;
- he wants his intervention remembered as love.

The defect is not necessarily absence of feeling.

It is that Bullet cannot accept the beloved as a separate jurisdiction.

This produces a useful three-part distinction:

### Attachment
Bullet has it.

### Understanding
Bullet has a terrifying amount of it.

### Recognition of autonomy
This is where he fails.

That distinction explains why he can be more psychologically perceptive than many kinder characters while remaining one of the series’ most anti-personhood figures.

His final proposition is:

> **love authorizes override.**

The mature Keyaru, however inconsistently, has begun moving toward the opposite proposition:

> **love makes another person’s accumulated history harder to overwrite, not easier.**

That gap is narrow enough to preserve the mirror and large enough to make development meaningful.

# II. Blade — appetite licensed by power

Blade is a deliberately simpler antagonist than Bullet.

The Phase-2 corpus supports describing her dominant structure as:

> **appetite + entitlement + power**

Across the relevant Volume-3 material, refusal does not operate as a meaningful boundary in her permission structure. Existing relationships, family claims, or the other person’s wishes do not restrain desire when power can override them.

This produces a repeated pattern rather than one uniquely retaliatory target.

That makes Blade useful for a descriptive comparison with Keyaru.

Keyaru’s sexualized coercion is more often embedded in:

- revenge;
- punishment;
- possession;
- strategic manipulation;
- coercive incorporation.

Blade’s is more opportunistically appetite-driven.

This distinction is **not moral exoneration**. It does not establish that Keyaru possesses a robust consent ethic. It identifies different behavioral structures.

Blade’s failure mode is therefore clean:

> **desire becomes permission because power makes refusal defeasible.**

Kureha’s later inheritance of the Sword Hero role strengthens the contrast. Kureha is independently judged worthy by the Sword trial rather than merely appointed by Keyaru. The same heroic slot can therefore be occupied by:

- appetite without restraint;
- or disciplined strength experienced as duty.

Blade demonstrates that corruption belongs to the person, not automatically to the office of Sword Hero.

# III. Norn before Ellen — administrative violence

Norn is dangerous because she can turn violence into policy.

Her antagonist function is not the same as Ellen’s later personhood arc and should remain analytically separate.

**Locators:** V2 — 第十五話「回復術士は妹姫を追憶する」 — `p-016.xhtml` ¶148–175; V3 — 第十八話「回復術士はノルン姫の蛮行に心を痛める」 — `p-019.xhtml`; V3 — `p-021.xhtml` ¶64–80.

Norn’s defining capacity is to create categories through which destructive action becomes institutionally intelligible:

- brainwashed;
- heretic;
- contaminated;
- collaborator;
- population to be “saved.”

Once the category is accepted, contrary evidence can be absorbed into the category itself. Resistance proves corruption. Refusal proves brainwashing. Violence becomes rescue.

This is why Norn is not merely cruel.

She is a theorist and practitioner of **administrative moral closure**.

Her private wound complicates but does not excuse this structure. Keyaru’s memory access reveals a girl who develops strategic excellence partly because she cannot compete with Flare’s magical gifts and desperately wants recognition from her sister.

**Locator:** V3 — `p-022.xhtml` ¶53–70.

The important method is:

> **explain the emotional production of the strategist without laundering the strategist’s decisions.**

Norn’s failure mode is:

> **unmet need for recognition converted into indispensability, then indispensability converted into bureaucratic permission over other lives.**

# IV. Prohm Jioral — sovereign instrumentalization

Prohm makes the royal family and the Jioral state look like versions of the same structure.

**Locator:** V6 — 第十三話「回復術士は王を拒絶する」 — `p-014.xhtml` around ¶169–179.

When Prohm discusses his daughters and offers Keyaru a place at his side, he treats people as pieces whose value depends on usefulness. A lost piece ceases to matter when a superior piece is available.

The logic scales almost perfectly:

### Family
Daughters are assets.

### Heroes
Exceptional bodies are strategic resources.

### Subjects
Population is material to be ordered.

### Black power
Human bodies can be transformed into more useful instruments.

Volume 6 also gives Keyaru one of the series’ clearest explanation-without-excuse reflections: growing up under such a father helps explain what Flare and Norn became, but it does not forgive them.

**Locator:** V6 — 第十四話「回復術士は王と戦う」 — `p-015.xhtml`.

Prohm’s failure mode is therefore:

> **the ruler experiences family, office, and population as extensions of sovereign ownership.**

His importance as Keyaru mirror is obvious: Keyaru rejects being Prohm’s tool, then later constructs his own personal monarchy. Document 05 must determine how far Panakeia actually escapes Prohm’s instrumental grammar rather than merely replacing its beneficiary.

---

# V. Hakuou — culpable ruler inside a corruptive office

Hakuou is the antagonist who most clearly prevents a simple “bad person uses bad power” model.

**Locator:** V5 — 第十九話「回復術士は最後の復讐を誓う」 — `p-020.xhtml` approximately ¶134–149.

His recovered history establishes that:

- he was not always temperamentally identical to the later violent Demon King;
- he performed strength partly because political weakness endangered his people;
- he noticed himself becoming more aggressive and cruel;
- he feared losing the prior self;
- he investigated the hidden power behind the Demon King office;
- the black-god structure benefits from war and sacrificial death;
- he attempted partial resistance;
- the system responded by tightening control over him.

Hakuou is therefore neither innocent puppet nor ordinary tyrant.

He remains responsible for decisions and atrocities made while ruling.

But the field of agency is materially altered by a metaphysical office that pushes the occupant toward violence and increasingly uses the occupant as a vessel.

His failure mode is:

> **the office that promises sovereign power gradually turns the sovereign into infrastructure for the power behind the office.**

This makes him less a dark mirror of Keyaru’s appetite than a warning about institutional/metaphysical capture.

He matters especially to Eve: replacing Hakuou with a kinder person does not automatically remove the structural danger of Demon Kingship.

# VI. Hiseki — protector and predator in the same person

Hiseki is the antagonist/secondary figure V2 most needs to preserve as contradiction.

Flattening him in either direction damages the text.

Across Volumes 9–10, he is genuinely:

- a formidable Red Dragon warrior;
- politically perceptive;
- loyal to his people;
- capable of recognizing superior force;
- mentor and quasi-paternal support to Ragna;
- useful administrator/deputy in the emerging settlement.

**Locator:** V10 — 第二話「回復術士は求婚する」 — `p-003.xhtml` approximately ¶45–53.

His reasoning about duty is serious: if Keyaru means extermination, Hiseki’s obligation is to warn the tribe and spend his own life buying time. Keyaru recognizes the judgment as sound.

Yet Volume 9’s infiltration material also reveals a private status/species hierarchy under which a person he perceives as lower-status becomes sexually available to coercive entitlement.

The later loyal protector does not erase the earlier predator.

The earlier predator does not make the later protection fake.

Hiseki therefore demonstrates a recurring *Redo* problem:

> **a person may possess real virtues inside the circle he recognizes while denying full personhood outside that circle.**

That is precisely why he is a useful mirror for Keyaru.

His failure mode is:

> **bounded moral recognition: discipline, loyalty, and sacrifice for “ours,” paired with entitlement toward those placed below the protected category.**

His final opposition to compelled Ragna gives the tragedy real weight without retroactively purifying him.

# VII. Faran and the Holy Emperor — sacred hierarchy as world order

Faran differs from the other antagonists because the primary villain is an **institutional ontology** rather than a single appetite.

**Locators:** V9 — 第七話「回復術士は世界宗教に挑む」 — `p-008.xhtml` approximately ¶65–107; V9 — 第八話「回復術士は異議を唱える」 — `p-009.xhtml` approximately ¶62–67 and ¶119–144; V9 — 第九話「回復術士は世界会議を支配する」 — `p-010.xhtml` approximately ¶30–49.

Faran joins:

- human supremacy;
- religious authority;
- state power;
- slavery and discrimination;
- holy-war legitimacy;
- attempted international jurisdiction.

The Holy Emperor does not merely say his state prefers human rule.

He attempts to make a sectarian theological hierarchy function as the normative law of the international system:

> humans are uniquely favored;  
> nonhumans occupy inferior moral status;  
> coexistence becomes contamination;  
> war can become sacred obligation;  
> states that reject the hierarchy may be classified as enemies of humanity.

That is why Keyaru’s challenge matters. He refuses the equation:

> **Faran’s god = the world’s moral jurisdiction.**

But the resulting contrast is not “religion bad, secular Keyaru good.” Keyaru later considers religion useful social technology and contemplates building a miracle-backed counterreligion around genuine supernatural effects.

Faran’s failure mode is therefore:

> **sacred human hierarchy converted into political and legal authority over people who do not share the theology.**

Document 05 must carry the institutional comparison further, because Panakeia answers sacred supremacy not with procedural neutrality but with a different combination of pluralism, miracles, political management, and deterrent force.

# IX. Bullet and Keyaru — the decisive mirror

Bullet remains the most important antagonist because Keyaru cannot defeat him by claiming moral innocence.

The overlap is too extensive.

Both can:

- rewrite bodies;
- manipulate identity;
- exploit psychological knowledge;
- condition behavior;
- engineer dependency;
- use deception and information asymmetry;
- treat love possessively;
- distrust institutional morality;
- believe that extraordinary power creates unusual permissions.

The difference that emerges is narrower and therefore more credible.

Bullet’s governing logic remains:

> **if I can understand and control the beloved, I may author the beloved.**

Keyaru begins near that position. Freia and Ellen are the obvious evidence.

But accumulated experience creates friction against pure authorship:

- Eve remains valuable because she is self-willed;
- Freia becomes socially and morally thicker than the identity Keyaru installed;
- Ellen chooses Ellen while recovering Norn’s competence;
- Setsuna retains obligations to her tribe;
- Kureha retains justice claims Keyaru does not control;
- Ragna and Lapis remain loved even when their bodies attack him under compulsion;
- Guren becomes a continuity-bearing person whose growing power reduces her own freedom.

By Volume 8, Keyaru refuses to replace the current household with same-shaped earlier versions.

By Volume 10, he can give up the need to personally kill Bullet.

At the second redo, he asks others what **they** want history to become.

None of this turns Keyaru into a universal autonomy theorist. He remains controlling, coercive, and intensely particularistic.

But it establishes the central difference Bullet cannot cross:

> **Bullet’s love makes authorship over the other easier to justify. Keyaru’s maturing attachment increasingly makes rewriting the other harder to justify because the existing person has become historically irreplaceable.**

Bullet’s final death demonstrates the difference with unusual precision.

He is arguably right about the strategic need for a reset.

He saves Keyaru by refusing to let Keyaru choose.

Keyaru eventually resets by bringing other people’s wishes into the project of historical revision.

That contrast should not be overstated as autonomy versus domination.

It is better understood as:

> **exclusive authorship defended as love**  
> versus  
> **an unstable movement from exclusive authorship toward recognition of other authors.**

# Final antagonist thesis

*Redo of Healer* does not give Keyaru a clean gallery of moral opposites.

Its antagonists are more useful as **failure modes that remain partially available to him**:

- Blade’s equation of desire and permission;
- Norn’s use of categories to authorize harmful policy;
- Prohm’s concentration of people into a ruler’s personal inventory;
- Hakuou’s vulnerability to an office that consumes its occupant;
- Hiseki’s genuine virtue inside a bounded moral circle;
- Faran’s transformation of partial doctrine into universal jurisdiction;
- Bullet’s belief that understanding another person entitles one to determine that person’s fate.

Keyaru defeats all of them materially.

He does not simply escape their logics.

He borrows, reproduces, or remains vulnerable to several of them.

That is why Bullet is the final dark mirror.

> **Bullet does not fail to understand Keyaru. He fails to accept that understanding someone does not make that person yours.**

The deepest evidence of Keyaru’s development is not that he becomes unlike Bullet in every respect.

It is that the similarities become visible enough for one difference to matter:

> **another person’s accumulated life can acquire a claim against the author who has the power to rewrite it.**

That claim is weak, partial, relational, and inconsistently honored.

But Bullet never grants it at all.
