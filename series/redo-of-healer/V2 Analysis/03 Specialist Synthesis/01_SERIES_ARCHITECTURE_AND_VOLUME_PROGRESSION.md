# 『回復術士のやり直し』 Series Architecture and Volume Progression — V2
## How the governing problem changes from revenge-authorship to contested authorship of persons, institutions, and history

**Project:** *Redo of Healer* / 『回復術士のやり直し』  
**Scope:** Japanese light novels Volumes 1–10  
**Revision status:** V2  
**Primary function:** longitudinal causal architecture  
**Evidence rule:** original Japanese novels govern; Phase-2 source ledgers supply locators; Documents 02–08 govern specialist conclusions.  
**Anti-duplication rule:** this document explains *what each volume changes in the series' governing problem*. It does not replace the character, political, metaphysical, ethical, language, or evidence-ledger documents.

---

# Executive thesis

*Redo of Healer* begins with an unusually concentrated premise: a person whose body, pain, labor, sexuality, and Hero status have been treated as resources for others acquires the ability to redo history and decides that never being authored by other people again requires the power to author them first.

At the horizon of Volume 1, this can still look like the complete architecture of the work. Keyaru is victim; Keyarga is counter-author; healing becomes a means of bodily, mnemonic, and social reversal; revenge supplies the permission structure. The central question appears to be:

> **What does a person do when the power that once made him usable becomes the power with which he can dominate his abusers?**

Across ten volumes, that question repeatedly becomes insufficient.

The series expands in scale, but its more important development is **not** that fights become larger. The object of authorship changes. Keyarga first claims jurisdiction over bodies and identities. He then learns to manipulate public legitimacy. Human-demon coexistence destabilizes the moral categories used to justify war. Victory produces governments. Governments create succession, legitimacy, administrative, technological, and diplomatic problems. Companions acquired through asymmetric or coercive circumstances accumulate enough history to become irreplaceable persons. Revenge ends, but the habits built for revenge survive inside statecraft. Finally the higher rule architecture becomes implicated in bodily compulsion, anomalies in Hero/Demon King succession, constraints on divine beings, and historical revision.

The ten-volume movement is therefore better represented as:

> **bodily revenge and reversal**  
> **→ public legitimacy warfare**  
> **→ collapse of inherited moral categories**  
> **→ recovery of a buried constructive self and inheritance of wider conflicts**  
> **→ regime overthrow that exposes deeper systems**  
> **→ reconstruction and the separation of social, metaphysical, and ethical heroism**  
> **→ deterrence, technological state power, and strategic defeat**  
> **→ irreplaceability, distributed agency, and the moral cost of reset**  
> **→ post-revenge identity integration and authoritarian peacebuilding**  
> **→ world-system rupture, action without agency, and a consultative second redo**

The title concept changes correspondingly.

The first **やり直し / yarinaoshi** is primarily an act of unilateral revenge-authorship. Volume 8 makes redo morally costly by establishing that earlier same-shaped versions of loved people are not interchangeable with the present persons produced by accumulated history. Volume 10 therefore cannot simply repeat Volume 1. The second redo remains an extraordinary and unequal intervention into history, but Keyaru discloses the hidden timeline and asks others what *they* would want changed. The change is not equal collective control. It is the end of his **exclusive claim** to decide what revised history ought to be.

V2's architectural thesis is thus:

> **The series grows by forcing a revenge protagonist whose survival strategy is authorship-over-others to encounter increasingly complex forms of personhood and order that cannot be safely reduced to objects of his control.**

The unresolved endpoint is not whether Keyaru has become “good.” It is whether he can retain the strength that protected him from objectification while relinquishing the belief that strength gives him jurisdiction over the lives, identities, institutions, and histories of everyone he can affect.

# I. Volume 1 — A usable body becomes a sovereign weapon

## Governing question

> **If usefulness made Keyaru property, can absolute control make him a person again?**

Volume 1 establishes the first timeline and the moral grammar from which nearly every later problem grows. Keyaru's Healing Hero status makes him valuable, but value does not produce respect. It produces claims upon him. His pain is relevant only insofar as it obstructs healing output; drugs maintain availability; his body and Hero function are treated as resources of the royal order. When his memory and agency recover, the first reset is explicitly revenge-directed: the new timeline exists so that he can destroy the lives of those who destroyed his.

The structural importance of **healing** is already visible. It is not merely a restorative spell. Through contact with pain and bodily state, Keyaru gains access to information, experience, skills, and the body as something that can be revised. The same ability can restore, copy, redesign, disguise, degrade, and alter memory. Volume 6 will eventually state the governing principle as return toward an `あるべき姿`, but Volume 1 already establishes that healing is potentially a power of **state selection** rather than simple injury repair.

Keyarga is the defensive answer. The change of name and body is not yet explicitly called a `仮面` or `鎧`; those formulations come in Volumes 8 and 9. At this horizon, however, the functional logic is already clear: the person who was defined by others will now define the scene before anyone can define him.

Flare's reconstruction into Freia is the most extreme enactment of that reversal. Autobiographical memory is erased while knowledge and capacity remain. The scene establishes a central series contradiction before the novels possess the later language to analyze it: Keyaru can respond to being treated as editable material by treating another person as editable material.

Setsuna complicates any simple “revenge only against the guilty” reading. She is not a first-timeline perpetrator. She enters under slavery, illness, desperation, endangered community, and radical power asymmetry. Yet she also begins making choices that are not reducible to Keyaru's command. The later household-personhood problem is therefore present in embryonic form immediately: **real strengthening and real attachment can arise inside morally compromised founding conditions.**

Volume 1 also establishes the apple orchard as practical biography. At this point it should not yet be inflated into the full late-series nourishment thesis. The reader knows Keyaru as an apple farmer maintaining what his dead parents left behind. Only Volume 4 will explicitly turn apples, sweets, feeding, and happiness into a positive alternative dream, and Volume 10 will reveal the complete causal relation among orchard, bereavement, Hero aspiration, objectification, and revenge.

### Prospective horizon

At the end of Volume 1, the responsible reading is still that Keyarga is primarily a revenge identity who has built an asymmetric household around control, utility, desire, and protection. The text has **not yet** established that Freia's later subjectivity will be ethically meaningful, that Setsuna's loyalty will become self-directed, or that Keyarga will ever treat the current timeline as something too valuable to erase.

### Structural function

Volume 1:

- establishes the first reset as unilateral revenge-authorship;
- makes Jioral's treatment of the Hero an institutional form of objectification;
- turns healing into the series' master technology of bodily and identity authorship;
- creates Keyarga as a defensive reversal of helplessness;
- establishes the coercion/personhood problem through Freia and Setsuna;
- introduces the orchard as biography before its thematic meaning becomes explicit.

# II. Volume 2 — Revenge becomes a war over public reality

## Governing question

> **If institutions manufacture moral categories, is exposing their lies enough—or does counter-propaganda reproduce the same authorship problem?**

Volume 2 moves the conflict from the body to **legitimacy**. Keyarga's opponents are no longer only individual abusers; they belong to a state capable of deciding who appears as Hero, criminal, traitor, heretic, demon collaborator, or proper victim.

Kureha is the transition figure. Her central problem is not sadism but sincere justice attached to bad institutional information. She has believed that serving Jioral means serving justice. When confronted with evidence that the kingdom itself may be evil, she explicitly has to reconsider what justice is and what her sword should serve.

Keyaru's relationship to that sincerity is revealing. Privately, he acknowledges that the righteous interpretation Kureha places upon him is not an accurate description of his own motives. Publicly, however, he can use the vocabulary of justice as theater. This distinction becomes structurally important far beyond Volume 2: Keyaru becomes increasingly skilled at exposing hypocrisy while also learning how useful moral narratives are as instruments.

The public-execution sequence expands revenge into political spectacle. Jioral uses ceremony to authorize violence; Keyaru takes control of the ceremony and reverses the moral classification of victim and perpetrator. Freia-as-Flare becomes a political sign whose power depends upon the public image of the very identity Keyaru destroyed and reconstructed.

Norn introduces a more institutional mode of antagonism than Flare. Her danger is not simply personal cruelty. She can integrate logistics, intelligence, labeling, military necessity, and narrative management. Violence becomes easier to reproduce when the state can redescribe dissent as contamination and coercion as rescue.

The introduction of Branica at the end of this movement prepares the first major collapse of Jioral's ideological map. If humans and demons can coexist in ordinary social relations, the state narrative that species war is natural becomes politically contestable.

### Prospective horizon

Volume 2 should not be read backward as though Keyaru already possesses the later Panakeia peace program or a mature concept of coercion-sensitive culpability. He is learning that **truth, image, and legitimacy can be manipulated**, not yet that rulers ought to constrain themselves because persons possess independent claims against political authorship.

### Structural function

Volume 2:

- expands revenge into legitimacy warfare;
- establishes Keyaru as insurgent propagandist and political performer;
- gives Kureha a genuine justice problem rather than a mere conversion-to-Keyaru arc;
- introduces Norn's administrative violence;
- turns Freia's public Flare identity into a statecraft instrument;
- prepares the human-demon moral map for destabilization.

# III. Volume 3 — Branica replaces species morality with political contingency

## Governing question

> **If coexistence is already materially possible, what interests require war to remain morally inevitable?**

Branica is the first sustained counterexample to the political ontology on which Jioral depends. The city is not presented as a perfect egalitarian order. Instrumental relations remain; coexistence has costs and compromises. Its importance is more basic: humans and demons trade, eat, drink, and live beside one another. Coexistence exists as practice before it becomes Keyaru's doctrine.

At this horizon, Keyaru recognizes it partly as an intelligent arrangement and partly as something strategically useful. V2 should not project his Volume-9 peace orientation backward and pretend he already approaches coexistence as a principled universal good. The development is precisely that something initially legible as **useful and possible** later becomes part of the world he personally wants to preserve.

Eve destabilizes the Demon King category in parallel. She is not the monstrous sovereign Keyaru remembers killing in the first timeline; she is a hunted Black-Wing candidate embedded in tribal and succession politics. The office of Demon King begins separating from the moral essence of its holder.

This matters relationally as well. Keyaru wants to manipulate Eve's future, but her usefulness depends upon preserving her will, pride, power, and constituency rather than erasing them. She therefore becomes an early limit case for his authorship: he can manipulate a person without being able to reduce her completely to a derivative of himself.

Blade closes another portion of the first-timeline revenge structure and gives the series a useful antagonist distinction. Blade represents appetite plus entitlement plus superior force more directly than Keyaru does. That difference is descriptive rather than exculpatory, but it becomes important for the later ethical comparison among Blade, Bullet, and Keyaru's retaliatory/possessive forms of sexualized coercion.

Norn's campaign against Branica makes ideological violence systemic. Humans who reject the state narrative can be recoded as compromised, “saved” through killing, or rendered illegible as genuine dissenters. Her private wound regarding Flare also becomes visible through Keyaru's memory access, but the text does not ask the reader to convert causal explanation into forgiveness.

### Prospective horizon

At Volume 3's endpoint, the series has not yet established the black-god architecture or the full Demon King mechanism. What has changed is the **political map**: human, demon, Hero, and Demon King can no longer function as reliable moral categories by themselves.

### Structural function

Volume 3:

- establishes coexistence as material fact rather than utopian slogan;
- converts species morality into factional politics;
- introduces Eve as sovereign subject rather than monster-object;
- develops Norn as a manager of political reality;
- narrows the remaining personal revenge structure while widening the world it operates inside.

# IV. Volume 4 — The buried constructive self returns, and Bullet becomes inheritance

## Governing question

> **Was Keyarga built because the original Keyaru was false—or because the original Keyaru's goodness did not know how to protect itself?**

Volume 4 is the most important early correction to a reading of Keyarga as an empty revenge machine.

The apple/food motif becomes explicit positive desire. Keyaru remembers wanting to preserve his parents' orchard, make delicious apples and sweets, and make life happier for people living in a harsh world. The structure is important because it offers a form of efficacy opposite revenge:

> **make something → give it to another person → their happiness is the result.**

The volume then stages an internal conflict between the earlier Keyaru and Keyarga. The old self rejects what he has become. Keyarga's answer is not that the old dream was meaningless. It is that the old dream failed to include **Keyaru's own happiness and survival**. A powerless healer who only wants everyone else to be happy is precisely the kind of person the first timeline consumed.

This is the prehistory of the later `仮面` and `鎧` language. Volume 4 does not yet establish that Keyarga can be integrated or relinquished. It does show that Keyarga is a **solution to a defect Keyaru perceives in his earlier self**, not merely a name for sadism.

Bullet becomes equally important here because he is explicitly identified as Keyaru's teacher. His intelligence background, lie detection, enemy classification, conditioning methods, and survival philosophy have been transmitted into Keyaru's own cognitive toolkit. The deepest antagonist is therefore already structurally internal: defeating Bullet cannot mean simply purging all Bullet-derived capacities without also losing skills Keyaru uses to survive.

Bullet's own person-model is visible before the later “doll” confrontation. He selects, cultivates, preserves, and substitutes people according to his preferred ideal forms. His attachment can be real while still treating the beloved as material whose proper state he may determine.

Divine-beast rule constraints also begin mattering before Chronos. Caladrius and Guren introduce power whose use is bounded by costs, roles, and restrictions. Volume 10 will enlarge this into an explicit higher-rule architecture, but V2 should not describe that cosmology as appearing from nowhere.

### Prospective horizon

By Volume 4, the text supports a buried constructive Keyaru and a conflict between that self and Keyarga. It does **not yet** support the conclusion that the answer is simple return to childhood. Later volumes instead move toward integration: the competence of Keyarga plus recovered capacity for nourishment, vulnerability, and ordinary future.

### Structural function

Volume 4:

- makes nourishment/home an explicit pre-revenge positive telos;
- turns Keyaru/Keyarga into an internal developmental problem;
- establishes Bullet as literal teacher and contaminating inheritance;
- deepens Bullet's replacement ontology;
- prefigures rule-bound divine-beast cosmology.

# V. Volume 5 — Killing the ruler reveals that the ruler is not the whole system

## Governing question

> **What remains when a coalition destroys the sovereign it defined itself against?**

Volume 5 transforms both politics and metaphysics by making victory insufficient.

Kureha becomes Sword Hero through an independent qualification process. Keyaru enables access, but the Sword's judgment is hers to earn. This is an important correction to a simplistic state-appointment model of Hero succession: governments can manipulate vacancies, but they do not simply manufacture worthiness by decree.

Carol and Lapis complicate culpability. Lapis's “illness” is engineered poisoning disguised as medicine, making her body political leverage. Carol's collaboration occurs under manipulation and dependency. Keyaru genuinely saves Lapis while also using continuing medical control as leverage. The volume therefore repeats one of the series' most persistent patterns at a larger political scale: **rescue and instrumentalization can coexist in the same act.**

The assault on Hakuou's regime exposes the problem of mass violence committed for political liberation. Eve's insistence on seeing those who will die marks a different sovereign disposition from Keyaru's tendency to classify losses operationally. She does not simply refuse violence; she refuses to make its victims morally invisible.

Hakuou's defeat then reveals the deeper black-power layer. V1's formulation that the Demon King office was “designed to corrupt” was too teleological. The Japanese-primary evidence supports something more precise: Demon King power is structurally connected to a black system involving destructive pressure, sacrificial power, a deeper external source, and institutional succession. Hakuou is both culpable ruler and person acting under a genuinely corruptive office pressure.

Eve's accession does not automatically solve the polity. The anti-Hakuou coalition fragments; governing capacity becomes scarce; advisers, tribes, collaborators, and former enemies must be incorporated. Revenge intelligence becomes administrative intelligence.

Bullet's theft of the Philosopher's Stone supplies the volume's final lesson. Keyaru wins the direct fight against Hakuou while losing the larger objective to an opponent who designed the timing and structure of the operation. Bullet's threat is increasingly strategic rather than merely physical.

### Prospective horizon

Volume 5 establishes a deeper Demon King/black-power architecture but does not yet reveal its ultimate ontology. It also establishes Eve as ruler without proving that moral legitimacy automatically produces administrative capacity.

### Structural function

Volume 5:

- converts regime overthrow into a succession/governance problem;
- separates Kureha's Hero qualification from state appointment;
- complicates culpability through Carol/Lapis;
- embeds the Demon King office in a deeper corruptive system without resolving its design;
- makes Eve sovereign;
- demonstrates Bullet's objective-oriented strategic superiority.

# VI. Volume 6 — Heroism separates into social image, metaphysical function, and ethical judgment

## Governing question

> **What does it mean to be a “true Hero” when public heroism, world-system Herohood, and moral heroism are not the same thing?**

Volume 6 is the hinge at which the novels stop allowing “Hero” to function as one category.

Keyaru returns to Jioral as both destroyer and public rescuer. Saving Lanalitta and intervening in the crisis generate political capital. The useful public image of the Healing Hero becomes part of the successor regime's legitimacy before the old order is fully gone.

The central metaphysical clarification is explicit: `【回復】` returns something toward its `あるべき姿`, and Keyaru performs `概念の【回復】` on the Hero concept/function. This is not merely V2 terminology. The novel itself moves healing from body toward **role and concept**.

That produces three distinct forms of heroism:

- **social Hero** — the person publics recognize as protector or savior;
- **metaphysical Hero** — a bearer of the world's Hero slot/function, including restored anti-black capacity;
- **ethical hero** — a normative judgment the series never simply grants Keyaru because the first two categories apply.

The distinction is essential to the architecture. Keyaru can become more perfectly Heroic in the world-system sense without thereby becoming morally pure.

Prohm Jioral gives the volume an equally important psychological-political distinction. The text permits institutional and familial explanation for what Flare and Norn became while refusing to erase their or Prohm's culpability. Purification from black influence does not reveal an innocent man underneath. The state had already treated daughters, Heroes, and subjects as pieces.

New Jioral therefore cannot be built merely by removing corruption. It uses Freia-as-Flare for symbolic continuity, Ellen for administration, Keyaru for Hero legitimacy and deterrence, and selective narrative management to produce a successor order. Panakeia's later political theater grows out of techniques already visible here.

### Prospective horizon

Volume 6 establishes **conceptual healing** explicitly. It also already establishes Freia's self-understanding as Freia performing Flare and preserves deeper continuities such as magical disposition. Volume 10 should not be written as though it suddenly invents her separateness or her connection to the prior person.

### Structural function

Volume 6:

- explicitly defines healing as restoration toward an ought-state;
- separates social, metaphysical, and ethical heroism;
- demonstrates that removing corruptive power does not remove prior culpability;
- destroys old Jioral while preserving useful symbolic/institutional continuity;
- converts revenge victory into reconstruction.

# VII. Volume 7 — The builder becomes a deterrence-state actor and still loses the campaign

## Governing question

> **Can overwhelming personal capability compensate for an opponent who controls the structure of the problem?**

Volume 7 is simultaneously one of Keyaru's strongest demonstrations of competence and one of his clearest strategic defeats.

The aircraft is structurally important because it joins two sides of his identity. Keyaru genuinely enjoys calculation, design, iteration, and invention. The builder self is therefore broader than cooking. Yet the object almost immediately becomes a means of strategic bombing, mobility, surveillance, surprise, and deterrence. Constructive joy and coercive scale coexist in the same technology.

The political implications are explicit. Keyaru rejects a straightforward decapitation-conquest strategy because destroying rival rulers would make every neighboring state fear that it was next, generating balancing war until Jioral had conquered everyone. He instead prefers negotiation, public reassurance, and military superiority held in reserve.

This is a decisive bridge to Volume 9. Peace orientation does not suddenly appear after revenge. By Volume 7 Keyaru already understands that unconstrained victory can create a strategically worse political system.

Aircraft also begin ceasing to be purely personal magic. Later versions are designed for repeatability, ordinary-mage operation, alliance transfer, and expected reverse engineering. The important architectural shift is that one of Keyaru's exceptional capacities starts becoming **institutional technology**.

Bullet remains dangerous because he understands the significance of these innovations and designs around Keyaru's strengths. The conference appears to turn military superiority into diplomatic settlement: Freia-as-Flare provides public legitimacy, Ellen structures terms, and human-demon coexistence becomes public doctrine. Bullet then destroys the apparent victory and exposes a deeper black-monster system.

The lesson is not that Keyaru was incompetent. It is that tactical excellence, technology, coercion, and even good diplomatic framing do not guarantee control when the opponent determines the hidden objective and the timing of the crisis.

### Prospective horizon

Volume 7 supports the emergence of deterrence-plus-reassurance statecraft. It does not yet establish stable world peace or prove that Keyaru's personal control can be translated into durable institutions.

### Structural function

Volume 7:

- expands the builder identity into technological invention;
- creates airpower as strategic and later institutional capability;
- establishes explicit anti-conquest reasoning;
- makes human-demon peace a public diplomatic project;
- confirms Bullet as a strategic opponent capable of making Keyaru answer the wrong problem;
- prepares Volume 8's reliance on distributed agency.

# VIII. Volume 8 — Reset stops being an optimization tool because persons become histories

## Governing question

> **If an earlier version has the same body and potential, is it the same person?**

Volume 8 is the philosophical hinge of the first ten volumes.

Ellen can formulate the strategically cold solution: sacrifice the present arrangement, recover the necessary power, and reset. Keyaru refuses because another Eve, Freia, Setsuna, Kureha, or Guren produced by an earlier timeline would not simply be the present person again. The accumulated history of the relationship matters.

The line `やり直しは利かないな` therefore changes the title concept before Volume 10 changes it again. The reset that once meant liberation and revenge now threatens people whom Keyaru experiences as irreplaceable.

This is also where Keyarga becomes explicitly legible as performed identity. The narration describes him as an idealized `仮面` built because Keyaru hated the vulnerable self. Volume 9 will sharpen this into `鎧`, but the key architecture is already present: the revenge persona is something constructed for function.

The campaign against Bullet depends upon distributed action. Ellen commands. Freia develops and deploys large-scale magical solutions. Kureha fights as Sword Hero. Setsuna, Eve, Guren, and allied structures act beyond Keyaru's immediate hand. His own claim that the companions are not dolls is ethically imperfect given several coercive origins, but it identifies a real development: their usefulness increasingly depends upon **independent competence**, not perfect obedience.

Ellen's personhood develops in parallel. By Volume 8 she is deliberately reconstructing Norn's strategic capacities because those capacities are useful to her present self. Volume 9's later refusal to restore Norn fully is therefore not the choice of an ignorant amnesiac; it is the next stage of a self already integrating selected parts of the prior identity.

Bullet's defeat then turns healing into explicit individual time regression. The formal revenge is exact, but the deeper victory is earlier: Keyaru rejects Bullet's ontology of reproducible dolls.

Divine-beast restriction is also already present by this point through Caladrius/Guren material. Chronos later enlarges a known rule-bound architecture rather than inventing constraint ex nihilo.

### Prospective horizon

At the end of Volume 8, the original revenge list is effectively complete and the current household has become morally costly to erase. The text has **not yet** answered what Keyaru becomes without revenge. That becomes Volume 9's problem.

### Structural function

Volume 8:

- makes accumulated history part of personhood;
- transforms reset from easy optimization into moral loss;
- identifies Keyarga as an ideal-self mask;
- shows that victory depends on companions who act as agents;
- advances Ellen from constructed identity toward reflective integration;
- converts healing into individual temporal regression;
- ends the revenge arc while leaving the protagonist alive after its purpose.

# IX. Volume 9 — Post-revenge identity becomes post-revenge government

## Governing question

> **What does Keyaru build when revenge no longer supplies the reason to wake up?**

Volume 9 answers the identity question explicitly and the political question only provisionally.

Keyaru calls Keyarga his `鎧`. The armor existed because the earlier self was too vulnerable, too trusting, or too unwilling to inflict what revenge seemed to require. Revenge is now over; the armor is no longer necessary in the same totalizing form. But integration is not regression. Keyaru does not want to become the powerless village boy again. He wants the competence gained through Keyarga without living entirely inside Keyarga's defensive logic.

The positive side becomes clearer. Cooking is explicitly something he likes. He imagines a small restaurant and ordinary happy life. The apple dream also returns. Volume 10 will explain the full childhood causal history, but V2 should keep the chronology straight: **Volume 9 already knows the constructive dream; Volume 10 explains how bereavement redirected it into Hero aspiration.**

Ellen's identity reaches its clearest pre-reset articulation. She knows she was Norn, reconstructs Norn's strategic mind from traces, is offered fuller restoration, and refuses because she prefers Ellen and fears destroying the present self. “Self-owned” is too clean if it implies the original coercion disappears. The more precise formulation is **coercively created, later reflectively self-ratifying**.

Politics scale the same problem. Panakeia is founded through legal discontinuity, symbolic continuity, managed narrative, and concentrated competence. The monarchy debate distinguishes short-term performance from long-term durability. Kureha expresses a democratic/republican intuition against inherited elite rule. Ellen argues that current conditions reward competent concentrated government while acknowledging succession failure. Keyaru chooses the horizon in which he and Ellen can produce results.

The world conference then demonstrates **lawfare backed by deterrence**, not neutral rule-of-law politics. Ellen gets other states to recognize Panakeia and Jioral as separate when that benefits them, then uses the precedent against attempts to impose old liabilities. The argument matters because Keyaru also has the force to prevent hostile actors from simply ignoring it.

Religion becomes political technology. Keyaru rejects Faran's human-supremacist theology and false sacred hierarchy but not religion as a governing instrument. He considers miracle-backed counterreligion precisely because belief, solidarity, and legitimacy are politically useful.

Falbo creates one of the clearest ethical developments. Betrayal under hostage coercion becomes distinguishable from malicious betrayal. Keyaru's pardon remains conditional and particularistic, and the chapter quickly shows that punitive/objectifying habits persist. Development therefore means **more differentiated judgment**, not moral purification.

Eve's anti-retaliation policy offers the strongest alternative sovereign model. Keyaru recognizes her attempt to stop inherited hatred as strength, even though her thin governing class and imperfect subordinate control make the policy costly and fragile.

### Prospective horizon

Volume 9 already establishes aircraft proliferation as expected once designs are shared; Panakeia should not be imagined as an eternal technology monopoly. Eve's administrative limitations are also already visible. Volume 10 later tests these institutions but should not flatten their prior maturation into simple naivety.

### Structural function

Volume 9:

- turns `仮面` into explicit `鎧` and makes integration conscious;
- establishes post-revenge constructive desire before Volume 10 explains its origin;
- gives Ellen reflective self-ratification without cleansing coercive origin;
- abolishes Jioral and creates Panakeia;
- makes monarchy, succession, lawfare, religion, deterrence, and war economy explicit political problems;
- advances culpability analysis through Falbo;
- establishes Eve's mercy as a competing theory of sovereign strength.

# X. Volume 10 — The world system attacks agency and forces the second redo to answer Volume 8

## Governing question

> **Can trauma be prevented without treating the persons shaped by that trauma as disposable outcomes?**

Volume 10 first makes the post-revenge future more concrete, then makes that future impossible in the present timeline.

The Red Dragon conflict becomes a test of whether enemy categories can be revised. Ragna's `妾` is explicitly a performed chief-like register, while `私` marks the private self she has hidden beneath office. The marriage proposal begins inside a structurally coercive political situation: Keyaru holds overwhelming force and Ragna is willing to sacrifice herself for the tribe. Later vulnerability and personal desire make the relationship genuinely more reciprocal without retroactively transforming the origin into free bargaining.

The settlement matters because peace becomes material. Black Wings, Red Dragons, and potentially other displaced groups need water, farmland, housing, labor, tax policy, policing, delegation, and administration. Hiseki functions as a morally contradictory but competent mentor/deputy. The late peace project is no longer only diplomatic rhetoric; it is an attempt to construct conditions in which former enemies can live together.

Keyaru's identity integration is then behaviorally tested. He explicitly relinquishes the need to personally kill Bullet. He admits fear and loneliness. The orchard conversation does not invent a new desire but completes the causal biography already prepared by Volumes 1, 4, and 9:

> orchard and feeding people  
> → parents killed by monsters  
> → Hero aspiration as grief-response  
> → Jioral objectification  
> → Keyarga/revenge  
> → question of whether the current self is the self he wanted to become.

The answer is not restoration of childhood innocence. It is integration: keep competence, recover the capacity to nourish and build.

Chronos then enlarges the cosmology. Divine beasts are described as world-maintaining `駒`; opposing pieces exist; intervention is rule-bound; forbidden disclosure brings punishment. But V2 must preserve the endpoint's epistemic uncertainty. Chronos warns that Keyaru's Hero killing is connected to a balance violation, yet Ellen finds many historical Hero-on-Hero killings without comparable catastrophe, and Bullet claims an even larger kill history. The simple rule “Hero kills Hero → world breaks” is therefore textually inadequate.

The seven Hero deaths and seven Hero-marked Demon King candidates are a confirmed numerical correlation. Special causal relevance is a strong inference. The exact mechanism remains unresolved. Ellen's idea that the hidden preparation may occur “outside” ordinary world causality is explicitly a hypothesis, not omniscient explanation. Literal external players, simulation, metafictional authors, or a fully specified higher deity should not be smuggled into the architecture as fact.

The crisis then produces the purest separation of **action and agency** in the series. Lapis and Ragna can remain consciously loyal, loving, and resistant while their bodies are compelled to attack. Keyaru refuses to reinterpret their behavior as chosen betrayal. This is a genuine expansion beyond the early revenge grammar, where enemy action often functioned as evidence of enemy identity.

He also refuses an immediate reset even when escape is theoretically available. Volume 8's commitment to the present persons survives the Volume-10 catastrophe until the timeline becomes effectively unsalvageable.

Ellen's apparent death and Bullet's final intervention force the endpoint. Ellen's death is reported and overwhelmingly implied, not corpse-verified by Keyaru. Bullet's “help” has a beneficial consequence while reproducing his defining pathology: he overrides Keyaru's decision because he believes he understands what Keyaru needs better than Keyaru does.

Only after catastrophic loss does Keyaru accept another world-scale `回帰の【回復】`. This time he reveals the hidden history and asks others what they want changed.

Setsuna prioritizes saving her tribe before enslavement even if that changes the causal path to Keyaru. Freia asks him to reach Flare before the destruction that produced Freia, treating the prior Flare as a suffering person rather than merely raw material for her own existence. Kureha asks for an ordinary beginning grounded in gratitude and training rather than deception. Eve imagines saving the Black Wings herself. Guren expects to retain continuity more reliably than the others, while general memory retention remains probabilistic.

The second redo is therefore **procedurally different** without becoming equal authorship. Keyaru still possesses exceptional information and decision power; Guren supplies indispensable temporal function; the exact landing point is not shown. The change is that other people now possess acknowledged claims over what history should prevent, preserve, or allow them to do for themselves.

### Prospective horizon

The Volume-10 endpoint deliberately leaves the higher causality, exact rewind point, memory distribution, Hero-replacement mechanism, and future identity consequences unresolved. V2 should preserve those unknowns rather than allowing thematic confidence to masquerade as metaphysical certainty.

### Structural function

Volume 10:

- materializes peace as settlement and administration;
- behaviorally tests Keyaru's post-revenge integration;
- completes the orchard/bereavement/Hero/revenge causal biography;
- enlarges the divine-beast system into explicit higher rule architecture;
- separates action from moral agency through bodily compulsion;
- preserves Volume 8's anti-replaceability principle until catastrophe becomes overwhelming;
- makes Bullet's final act the strongest beneficial-outcome/autonomy-violation case;
- transforms the second redo from sole historical authorship into **consultative revision with shared claims but unequal power**.

# XI. Major retrospective revisions across the ten volumes

The mature architecture depends upon later volumes revising earlier interpretations without erasing what the earlier volume actually established.

## 1. Volume 3 revises Volume 1's moral geography

Early human/Hero versus demon language can initially appear ontological. Branica and Eve reveal it as politically unstable. Species and office do not reliably determine moral status.

## 2. Volume 4 revises the reading of Keyarga as empty revenge machinery

The apple/sweets dream and the old-Keyaru/Keyarga confrontation show that a constructive self survives. Keyarga's defense is not “kindness was fake”; it is “kindness that excludes my own survival is insufficient.”

## 3. Volume 5 revises victory

Killing a ruler does not eliminate the office, power system, coalition fracture, succession problem, or administrative burden that made the ruler politically significant.

## 4. Volume 6 revises Heroism and healing

Heroism divides into public image, metaphysical world-function, and ethical evaluation. Healing becomes explicit restoration toward an `あるべき姿`, opening the question of who defines the proper state.

## 5. Volume 8 revises ownership and redo

Possessive language remains real, but current companions are no longer treated as reproducible units. Accumulated history creates obligations that conflict with optimization.

## 6. Volume 9 revises Keyarga and constructed identity

Keyarga becomes `鎧`, not the whole self. Ellen's chosen Ellen identity demonstrates that later reflective ratification can be morally significant without legitimating coercive creation.

## 7. Volume 10 revises the cause of Keyaru's Hero dream

The orchard dream predates Hero ambition. Heroism begins as a grief-mediated redirection after parental loss. Revenge is therefore not the only thing covering the constructive self; heroic aspiration itself was already a response to trauma.

## 8. Volume 10 revises the ethics of the title

The first redo asks what Keyaru wants to do to history. The second asks what people produced by that history want changed—even when their answers threaten the causal conditions that brought them into Keyaru's life.

# XII. The series' changing governing problem

The ten-volume architecture can be compressed into five major problem regimes.

## Regime 1 — Volumes 1–2: authorship through reversal

Keyaru's problem is external domination. The solution is to become the person who determines bodies, identities, stories, and punishments before others can determine him.

## Regime 2 — Volumes 3–4: inherited categories fail

Human/demon morality collapses; ordinary coexistence becomes visible; the old constructive Keyaru reappears; Bullet is revealed as both enemy and teacher. Keyarga's identity can no longer be explained only by hatred.

## Regime 3 — Volumes 5–7: victory becomes governance and strategy

Killing tyrants reveals systems. Heroism splits into multiple meanings. Reconstruction requires symbols and administrators. Technology and deterrence make Keyaru a state actor. Bullet demonstrates that superior force does not equal strategic control.

## Regime 4 — Volumes 8–9: revenge ends but history has become valuable

The household is no longer replaceable. Keyarga becomes mask/armor. Ellen chooses Ellen. Panakeia attempts to scale a protected household into political order. The ethical problem shifts from “may I destroy my enemies?” toward “what obligations do I owe people and institutions whose existence now depends partly on me?”

## Regime 5 — Volume 10: authorship itself becomes the crisis

The world-system can compel action, alter succession, constrain divine beings, and force historical revision. Keyaru's final problem is no longer simply whether he can control the world. It is whether any actor—including Keyaru—should possess unilateral jurisdiction over the state persons and history **ought** to take.

# XIII. Final structural thesis

Through Volume 10, *Redo of Healer* is neither a completed redemption narrative nor merely revenge fiction that happened to accumulate political lore.

Its most coherent architecture is a long experiment in **authorship under trauma**.

Jioral authors Keyaru as a Healing Hero whose usefulness gives institutions claims over his body.

Keyarga responds by authoring bodies, names, memories, punishments, and relationships before anyone can reduce him to an object again.

That defensive authorship succeeds often enough to become dangerous. It produces attachments inside coercive structures. Those attachments accumulate histories that Keyaru does not fully control. The people around him develop skills, loyalties, constituencies, roles, and desires that exceed the functions for which he originally acquired or manipulated them.

Politics scales the same problem. Jioral treats people as state resources. Eve attempts to rule through mercy without always possessing the administrative capacity to enforce it. Panakeia changes protected membership and political ends while retaining personalism, managed narrative, concentrated coercion, and founder dependence. Keyaru can build a materially better order without thereby proving that his power has become impersonal or procedurally legitimate.

Metaphysics then universalizes the question. Healing itself is restoration toward an ought-state. Hero and Demon King offices place persons inside world functions. Black power can overwrite will. Divine beasts are powerful because they are pieces inside rules that also constrain them. Time regression can restore earlier states while destroying accumulated history.

The mature series therefore asks a question much larger than the one with which it begins:

> **Can a person escape objectification without turning the power to define “what ought to be” into a new right to author everyone else?**

Volume 8 supplies the decisive limit: a present person cannot be replaced merely by reproducing the same body in an earlier history.

Volume 9 supplies the developmental answer: Keyarga can become armor rather than destiny.

Volume 10 supplies the unresolved historical test: if the next timeline prevents the traumas that produced Freia, Ellen, Setsuna's dependency, Eve's losses, and Keyaru's own revenge identity, can the new history respect those persons rather than treating them as outcomes he is entitled to recover?

The second redo is ethically more mature because Keyaru no longer claims exclusive interpretive ownership of the future. It is still profoundly paternalistic because he remains one of the few people capable of performing the intervention at all.

That tension is the correct Volume-10 endpoint.

> **The first redo is the victim seizing the pen. The second asks whether he can keep writing while recognizing that the other people on the page are also authors of lives he does not own.**

# XIV. V2 changes from V1

This revision preserves the V1 macro-architecture but makes several substantive corrections.

1. **The nourishment chronology is corrected.** The orchard is V1 biography, V4 explicit positive dream, V9 post-revenge future, and V10 causal autobiography—not a motif invented by Volume 10.
2. **Keyarga's identity progression is demonstrated.** V4 internal conflict → V8 `仮面` → V9 `鎧` → V10 behavioral integration.
3. **Freia and Ellen are not treated as simple late revelations.** Freia's independent self-understanding and deeper continuity are visible before Volume 10; Ellen begins deliberately integrating Norn before her V9 reflective choice.
4. **The Demon King system is made less teleological.** Corruptive pressure and black-system embedding are textual; ultimate design remains unresolved.
5. **Conceptual healing is upgraded from analytical shorthand to explicit text.** `概念の【回復】` belongs to Volume 6's own metaphysical vocabulary.
6. **Aircraft are institutional technology, not a one-off superweapon.** Reproducibility, export, and expected diffusion matter to the political progression.
7. **Chronos is treated epistemically.** Higher rule architecture is textual; the simple Hero-killing explanation is contradicted by history; literal external players and exact seven-to-seven mechanics remain unresolved.
8. **The second redo is no longer called simply collaborative.** The more accurate formulation is consultative/partial co-authorship: others acquire claims over revision while operative power remains unequal.
9. **The architecture now preserves prospective truth.** Later volumes revise earlier interpretations without making earlier ambiguity disappear retroactively.
