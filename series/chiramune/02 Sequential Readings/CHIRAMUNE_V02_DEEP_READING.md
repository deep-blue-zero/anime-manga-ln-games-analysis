# Chiramune Volume 02 deep reading

## Control statement

This is a prospective Volume 02 reading anchored to the frozen Volume 01 state. No Volume 03 or later narrative evidence has been used. The Japanese EPUB was independently re-hashed before analysis and exactly matched the locked source SHA-256. Locator syntax such as `[p-0032:048]` refers to the extracted EPUB resource and paragraph marker retained in the local reading workspace.

Volume 02 is not primarily a second demonstration of Saku Chitose's social competence. It takes the competencies established in Volume 01 and asks what happens when two people who are both highly skilled at self-presentation attempt intimacy, when one person's authored identity encounters trauma that cannot be solved by ordinary social optimization, and when Saku's intervention ethic is forced to distinguish **helping someone**, **acting for someone**, and **acting with someone after they have chosen to ask for help**.

The result is simultaneously a romance volume, a Yuzuki Nanase character reconstruction, and a severe stress test of Saku's ethics.

# II. Formal architecture: false love, unnamed feeling, named love

The prologue and epilogue establish the volume's governing formal opposition.

The prologue calls this a story of **偽物の恋**: feelings can begin as admiration, idealization, or delusion, and the premature act of naming them "love" can become permission to stop knowing the actual person. When reality diverges from the imagined object, the name of love can become an excuse to injure the person one claims to love.

The epilogue deliberately reverses the formulation: this is a story of **本物の恋**. The difference is not that all projection has disappeared. Rather, a name can become legitimate when it functions as a reason to move toward the other person instead of an excuse to possess them. The volume's antagonist structure makes the distinction concrete:

- Tomoya Naruse loves an imagined Yuzuki and converts his fantasy into entitlement, surveillance, manipulation, and coercion.
- Yuzuki begins with an idealized image of Saku as her unique mirror, then discovers that he differs from her, disappoints her assumptions, frightens her, behaves absurdly, exposes his limits, and remains difficult to classify. Her attraction strengthens through that disconfirmation rather than despite it.
- Saku experiences increasingly unmistakable attachment but resists naming it. At the festival and again near the ending, he explicitly says it is still too early to give the feeling a name `[p-0026:070-075]`, `[p-0034:261-262]`.

**INFERENCE:** The volume does not simply endorse "name your feelings" or "do not name your feelings." It contrasts names used to freeze another person inside one's story with names used to authorize one's own movement toward a person who remains independent.

# III. Yuzuki's performed self is adaptive authorship, not hidden falseness

Volume 01 identified Yuzuki as socially perceptive and strategically composed. Volume 02 moves inside that system.

In Chapter 1 she tells Saku that she has deliberately tuned behavior, speech, and character presentation to make her exceptional status socially sustainable. Beauty and competence produce admiration, but also envy and unwanted claims. Her solution is to become so consistently competent and appropriately responsive that comparison itself becomes difficult: rather than be the nearby girl others resent, she aims to become the person whose excellence is treated as a given.

Her Chapter 4 focalization makes the point even more explicit. There is no singular origin trauma explaining the persona. She rejects the easy story that a damaged earlier self was transformed by one dramatic incident. She says she simply encountered small social problems and solved them one by one, while also working harder so that others would have less legitimate ground to criticize her `[p-0032:002-013]`.

This matters because the later Yanagishita trauma must not be retroactively made the origin of "the mask." The chronology is the opposite: **the mask was already a successful self-authored adaptation, and the trauma exposed a domain in which that adaptation failed.**

Her problem is therefore not "discover the real girl beneath a fake persona." It is that a repertoire optimized for reading and managing social expectations contains no answer when the other person refuses the shared rules of social interaction.

# IV. Saku and Yuzuki begin as mirrors and become legible through difference

The fake-dating contract begins because each recognizes the other's performance.

Yuzuki can ask Saku for help precisely because he notices that she wears a mask. Showing vulnerability to someone who believes the polished performance is effortless would feel like unilateral self-destruction. Saku, in turn, sees in Yuzuki another person who draws lines, calibrates disclosure, and treats social behavior as authored form.

At first they are intoxicated by recognition. Their exchanges are repeatedly described in theatrical terms: scripts, acting, stage, mask, deliberately dropping a line. The intimacy comes not from abandoning performance entirely but from **mutual literacy in performance**.

The decisive revision occurs later. Yuzuki's own focalization says Saku is not actually her double:

> she can avoid obstacles; he can avoid them too, but instead often runs into them, gets scraped up, and breaks through anyway `[p-0032:045-055]`.

Haru is the first person Yuzuki describes as brighter than herself: direct, physically committed, and capable of continuing even when the optimal route disappears. Saku becomes the second. This produces a major correction to the early "same kind of person" model.

**V02 finding:** Saku and Yuzuki share tools, not governing orientation. Both are socially perceptive and self-authored. Yuzuki's default is optimization and controlled distance; Saku's is aestheticized commitment that can override optimization.

# V. The stalker plot is fundamentally about jurisdiction over Yuzuki

The surface mystery asks who is stalking Yuzuki. The deeper conflict asks who gets to define what Yuzuki is and what her vulnerability means.

Three male positions form a useful contrast:

1. **Yanagishita:** explicit ownership. He speaks as if Yuzuki was and should again be "his," and treats force as sufficient proof of jurisdiction.
2. **Tomoya:** benevolent-seeming fantasy ownership. He imagines a wounded Yuzuki whose true needs he already understands, then manufactures danger and weakness so that he can occupy the rescuer role.
3. **Saku:** materially better because he repeatedly acknowledges that Yuzuki's problem belongs to Yuzuki and waits for her request before claiming the right to intervene more deeply; nevertheless, he still repeatedly exceeds the authority actually granted to him.

That third point prevents the volume from being flattened into hero versus villains. Saku's intentions and responsiveness are qualitatively different from Yanagishita and Tomoya, but good intent does not erase agency problems.

# VI. The intervention ethic changes: from unilateral rescue to requested alliance

Several V02 scenes show Saku learning from Volume 01.

When Yuzuki is exhausted after harassment, Saku stops himself from asking the empty question "Are you okay?" because he knows she would perform being okay. More importantly, he explicitly reasons that she has asked him to play boyfriend and bodyguard—not to solve her interior life—and that crossing that line without invitation would be self-satisfaction `[p-0027:274-285]`.

When Yua and Yuko confront him for taking physical risks, he recognizes that his calculation had omitted the people who would be hurt if he were injured. Yuko's correction is not simply "do not take risks." It is that if the same dangerous act must occur, doing it because one is protecting someone and promising to return is morally and relationally different from treating oneself as a disposable optimization variable.

Later, when Yuzuki finally cries "help me" `[p-0029:024-038]`, Saku narratively marks a jurisdictional transition. After she discloses her trauma and explicitly thanks him as her hero, he states: this had been Yuzuki's problem; because she chose to rely on him and step forward, "from here on, it is our problem" `[p-0031:101-106]`.

This is a real developmental gain from V01: Saku is becoming more attentive to consent, requested assistance, and shared responsibility.

It is not a completed ethical correction.

# VII. Counterevidence: Saku's reenactment is successful in-story and still coercive

The apartment scene is the most ethically difficult intervention so far.

Yuzuki, overwhelmed and frightened, moves toward physical intimacy. Saku abruptly converts the situation into a controlled reenactment of coercion: he restrains her, frightens her, strikes her lightly, invokes the prior aggressor's logic, and continues until anger breaks through fear and she physically resists `[p-0029:055-p-0031:010]`.

The intended lesson is explicit: physical pain is not identical to psychic surrender; she should keep thinking, searching for action, and refusing another person's claim over her mind. Yuzuki understands that lesson, laughs afterward, discloses the buried incident, and explicitly credits Saku with helping her change her relation to the memory `[p-0031:031-105]`.

Those source facts matter. It would be inaccurate to rewrite the scene as if Yuzuki experienced only violation or as if the narrative presented the intervention as a failure.

But the opposite flattening is equally untenable.

**VALUE JUDGMENT / RIVAL READING:** Saku undertakes an intense exposure-like reenactment without prospective consent, while Yuzuki is acutely distressed and has just sought safety in his home. He intentionally triggers terror to force a state transition. The fact that he predicts her resilience correctly and that she later values the result does not retroactively create informed consent. A wrong prediction could have compounded the trauma substantially; Saku himself acknowledges that a school counselor could regard the method as extreme and that it might have made her unable to smile `[p-0031:077-082]`.

This scene therefore updates the agency ledger in both directions:

- **STRENGTHEN:** Saku recognizes that help should wait for the recipient's own decision to seek it.
- **COUNTEREVIDENCE:** once invited in, he can still interpret broad permission as authority to choose highly coercive means on the recipient's behalf.

The distinction between **consent to help** and **consent to method** must remain explicit going forward.

# VIII. Relational accountability: Yua and Yuko reject Saku's self-disposability

Volume 01 showed Saku taking responsibility for outcomes. Volume 02 asks whether that is actually responsibility when he treats his own body as expendable.

After he deliberately confronts danger, Yua and Yuko object not because they deny Yuzuki's need but because Saku's model erases their stake in him. If he is hurt, the cost is not his alone. Yua asks that if danger truly is unavoidable, he at least allow them to consciously share the burden of waiting and worrying.

This is a crucial modification of Saku's autonomy rhetoric:

> self-authorship does not imply that one's own body and risk belong only to oneself once relationships of care exist.

Saku later partially honors this correction. Before the final operation he explains the plan to the group; Yuko and Yua object, but he proceeds only after the danger is disclosed and responsibilities are distributed. Kazuki is selected to record evidence precisely because Saku trusts him to continue the assigned task even while Saku is being beaten `[p-0034:006-010]`.

The plan remains reckless, but it is no longer solitary heroics hidden from everyone affected.

# IX. Yuzuki's growth is not "become less feminine" or "become more like Saku"

Yuzuki's development is easy to misdescribe as learning to fight back. The text supports something more specific.

Her trauma was not simply "a man was stronger than me." Her Chapter 4 narration says the deeper terror was discovering that when her normal social weapons failed, she seemed to possess no inner principle that remained hers under coercion `[p-0032:015-023]`.

Her answer is therefore to build a **jurisdictional core**:

- physical domination does not establish ownership;
- fear does not determine assent;
- pain does not compel identification with the aggressor's story;
- another person's suffering can matter enough to give her a reason to keep acting;
- she can choose whom she loves and what that love means.

Her confrontation with Yanagishita is a test of this. She plans exits, monitors visibility, anticipates the possibility of calling for help, and states explicitly that even rape would not make her "his" `[p-0032:068-135]`. When Saku is beaten, her resolve nearly collapses because the cost has shifted onto someone she values. She then converts concern for him into action: escape, seek help, return.

The point is not that fear vanishes. The narration repeatedly says she remains afraid. The change is that fear no longer exhausts the space of available action.

# X. The cost of Saku's hero role becomes visible

Saku's confrontation with Yanagishita is staged as heroic, but the volume repeatedly undercuts any reading of effortless invulnerability.

His successful plan depends on accepting a beating long enough for Kazuki to record unambiguous evidence. Yuzuki sees him curled up, weak, and apparently helpless. The later reversal reveals that Saku has been controlling the tactical frame, but it does not make the pain unreal.

His final threat to Yanagishita is also darker than his public clown/hero persona. Once he has legal and evidentiary leverage, he says he will use every available means to destroy Yanagishita if Yuzuki is targeted again. The text presents this as the terror of someone who understands rules well enough to weaponize them.

**INFERENCE:** V02 clarifies that Saku's governing ideal is not conventional niceness. "Beauty" can include intimidation, manipulation, violence under a defensible rule structure, and deliberate willingness to be harmed. His ethic is procedural and relational rather than pacifist.

**Counterpoint:** this also strengthens the concern that Saku can aestheticize extremity. Because suffering can be folded into a beautiful heroic story, his aesthetic code may make self-endangerment easier to justify than it should be.

# XI. Tomoya is Kenta's deliberate negative parallel

Tomoya Naruse is not simply "another unpopular boy." The volume makes him a negative control for V01's Kenta arc.

Both begin from perceived distance between themselves and a desired social world. Both are tempted by shortcuts. But Kenta eventually allows his fantasies to be challenged, accepts embarrassment, practices ordinary interaction, and acts while knowing he may fail. Tomoya protects the fantasy instead.

Saku repeatedly gives Tomoya advice that would require him to encounter Yuzuki as a person rather than as an ideal. Tomoya ignores it. He surveils her, recruits Yanagishita's aggression, interprets suffering as romantic opportunity, steals what he thinks is compromising material, and finally speaks as though coercion is rescue.

Saku's verdict—Tomoya never reached the starting line—echoes V01. The "start line" is not popularity. It is willingness to enter a reciprocal world where the other person can contradict the story one has written for them.

This yields a stronger series-level hypothesis:

> **HYPOTHESIS:** Chiramune's social competence ethic is less about rank than about tolerating contact with independent minds. Incompetence becomes morally dangerous when a person protects self-serving fantasy by denying the other's jurisdiction over their own identity.

# XII. Haru and Yuzuki: directness versus optimization

Haru is not only a friend or rival heroine. She is structurally important to Yuzuki's development.

Yuzuki reveals that she chose Fujishi after losing a middle-school basketball game to Haru's team. Her team was technically better; Haru's relentless directness changed the emotional energy of the match and produced an upset. Yuzuki's fascination is precisely with something her optimized system does not generate: forward movement that remains meaningful even when it is not elegant or statistically ideal `[p-0032:024-035]`.

Their arcade competition repeats the difference in miniature. Haru attacks directly and accepts inefficiency; Yuzuki waits for the clean line. Haru accuses Yuzuki of always standing one step outside and warns her not to regret losing because she refused to commit `[p-0024:116-156]`.

The friendship therefore has a corrective function similar to Haru's role with Saku in V01. Haru pressures highly self-controlled people toward embodied commitment.

# XIII. Ordinary life is where fake dating becomes actual intimacy

The volume's strongest romantic evidence is not the staged public couple performance. It is the accumulation of ordinary scenes in which performance becomes porous:

- eating ramen with Haru;
- a festival date in yukata;
- goldfish scooping and childhood memories;
- sharing an apple candy and food utensils;
- Saku cooking improvised Echizen soba at home;
- Yuzuki wearing his shirt and glasses;
- domestic knowledge embedded in Saku's shampoo, dryer, kitchen habits, books, radio, and sparse apartment;
- the mutual awareness that friends' objects and advice already inhabit Saku's supposedly solitary home.

These scenes matter because they give Yuzuki access to a Saku who is neither class leader nor intervention architect. Conversely, Saku sees a Yuzuki who is childish about goldfish, jealous, frightened, sexually playful, exhausted, apologetic, and capable of ugly accusation.

The romance becomes more credible as the glamorous symmetry between two "perfect" people deteriorates.

# XIV. The festival and Ramune motif: beauty under impermanence

At the festival Saku experiences sadness because the exact moment cannot be preserved `[p-0026:070-073]`. This is one of V02's clearest apertures into emotion before Saku can organize it into a role.

The pair drink Ramune, remove the marbles, and look through them at an inverted festival world. They tell each other that the other is beautiful through the marble `[p-0026:086-098]`.

The V01 hypothesis of the marble as "beautiful enclosure" remains relevant, but V02 expands the motif:

- the marble is no longer only trapped inside the bottle; it can be removed and used as a lens;
- perception through the marble transforms and inverts the ordinary world;
- mutual beauty is mediated rather than directly declared;
- the scene's emotional power depends on knowing the festival heat will dissipate.

**INFERENCE:** Ramune increasingly functions as a model of perception and bounded intimacy: a small transparent medium through which the world can become strange and beautiful, while never becoming permanent.

This should remain open for V03+ rather than frozen into a single-symbol interpretation.

# XV. Asuka becomes concrete enough to threaten Saku's idealization

Asuka's role changes substantially.

Yuzuki initially experiences Asuka as proof that her supposed unique access to Saku is not unique. She had taken pride in being perhaps the only person who understood his hidden structure. Seeing Asuka speak to him from an even deeper register produces a "small defeat"—not yet simply jealousy, but injury to the identity claim that special understanding makes Yuzuki special.

Saku then learns that Asuka is unsure whether to remain in Fukui or leave for Tokyo. This turns the idealized white-dress figure into a person with a banal, concrete decision. Saku experiences simultaneous disappointment and relief that her fantasy of Tokyo is as ordinary as his own `[p-0031:484-505]`.

Yet he still cannot fully meet her. When she asks whether he would come if she wanted him to, he evades. She tells him that the version of her suited to the white dress is probably not one he can love `[p-0031:515-539]`.

**INFERENCE:** Asuka's specialness may partly depend on maintained distance. The more she becomes a person who can choose, leave, need, and change, the more Saku must either relinquish the ideal or enter a reciprocal relationship.

# XVI. Saku's home complicates the myth of solitary self-sufficiency

Yuzuki's overnight stay reveals Saku's private environment: sparse furniture, books, radio, no television or computer, practical cooking competence, and a home full of small traces of friends—shampoo recommended by Yua, a dryer from Yuko, habits and objects embedded in a relational network.

Asuka's later diagnosis is therefore exact: Saku lives as though there is only himself, yet other people are everywhere in the structure of his life. Conversely, he seems always surrounded by people, yet often treats final responsibility as solely his own.

The apartment is ordinary-life evidence against a simple "lonely popular boy" cliché. He is genuinely relational. His isolation is better understood as **jurisdictional isolation**: he readily gives care and accepts objects, humor, companionship, and practical help, but resists making another person co-author of his direction.

# XVII. Relationship-state updates

## Saku → Yuzuki

**FACT:** attraction and attachment become substantial. He experiences jealousy/specialness injury, domestic/sexual attraction, protective concern, sadness at separation, and the urge to preserve physical proximity.

**FACT:** he explicitly refuses to name this as love within V02.

**INFERENCE:** his refusal is not absence of feeling but a principled/defensive commitment not to convert partial knowledge into a totalizing romantic label too early.

## Yuzuki → Saku

By the end of her focalized chapter, **FACT:** Yuzuki has consciously named her feeling as romantic love. She explicitly frames Tomoya as a trigger that helped her recognize her longing for Saku and ends by kissing Saku on the cheek on his birthday.

Her love is not purely disillusioned realism: she still aestheticizes Saku as beautiful and heroic. But unlike Tomoya, she increasingly knows traits that violate her original model and is attracted through those violations.

## Saku ↔ Haru

The relationship remains physically easy and teasing in a way distinct from Yuzuki. Saku tells Haru that certain intimate care—massaging her legs—is "only you" `[p-0024:001-015]`. Haru acts as a directness-corrective to both Saku and Yuzuki.

## Saku ↔ Yua / Yuko

Their roles become moral rather than merely affectionate. They assert standing to object to Saku's self-endangerment. Yuko's jealousy remains open and overt, but she also helps regulate group conflict and later participates in Saku's plan despite opposing its danger.

## Saku ↔ Asuka

Greater mutual disclosure, greater threat of separation, and persistent evasion. The relationship becomes more concrete but not more secure.

# XVIII. Social-status findings

V02 confirms that high status can become permission for punishment as well as insulation from it.

When Saku and Yuzuki publicly become a couple, anonymous school-board hostility intensifies. Yuzuki's connection to Saku recategorizes her: admiration can flip into socially licensed attack once she is interpreted as having claimed a scarce high-status romantic object.

This complicates the V01 model that status provides accumulated forgiveness. It does—but status also amplifies visibility, envy, interpretive policing, and the scale of reputational consequences.

Yuzuki's self-fashioning was built precisely around this problem. She attempts to move herself outside ordinary comparison, but romance drags her back inside a competitive frame.

# XIX. Rival readings and counterevidence audit

### Rival reading A — Saku's methods prove that benevolent manipulation is justified when the manipulator is competent

**Rejected as too strong.** The volume rewards several manipulations, but also repeatedly has Yua, Yuko, Haru, Yuzuki, and the narrative consequences expose the costs of Saku's unilateral control. His success rate does not convert competence into general authority.

### Rival reading B — Yuzuki's development is simply trauma recovery through a strong male protector

**Rejected as reductive.** Saku is materially important, but Yuzuki's decisive Chapter 4 movement is self-authored. She rejects passive rescue, chooses confrontation, builds contingency plans, articulates bodily and psychological jurisdiction, and later defeats Tomoya's fantasy without needing Saku to speak for her.

### Rival reading C — the fake relationship simply becomes real dating

**Rejected within V02.** The fake relationship formally ends. Yuzuki names love; Saku does not. The endpoint is greater intimacy plus greater asymmetry, not an established mutual couple.

### Rival reading D — Yuzuki and Saku are perfect mirrors

**Explicitly rejected by Yuzuki's focalization.** They share social tools, but differ in how they move when optimization fails.

### Rival reading E — Tomoya is merely socially awkward and therefore pathologized by a popular-person narrative

**Rejected.** His wrongdoing is not awkwardness. It is surveillance, deliberate endangerment, theft of private material, coercive fantasy, and refusal to update his model when Yuzuki contradicts it. Kenta serves as the internal counterexample showing that low status and awkwardness do not determine this trajectory.

# XX. V02 revisions to the V01 model

## PRESERVE

- Saku's social competence is real.
- self-fashioning is not equivalent to falseness.
- Saku's authored identity is both genuine and constraining.
- social status is real but not a morality score.
- Saku's intervention ethic contains paternalistic risk.
- Kenta's V01 endpoint was genuine incorporation rather than cosmetic conversion.

## STRENGTHEN

- Saku struggles to receive relational claims on himself.
- friends are active correctives, not satellites.
- Saku's hero role encourages self-endangerment.
- performance can be a route to intimacy when both parties understand its authored nature.
- ordinary life is central evidence for relationship development.

## REVISE

- Saku/Yuzuki "same type" → **shared tools, divergent governing orientation**.
- Ramune marble as enclosure → **broader motif of mediated perception, bounded beauty, inversion, and impermanence**.
- intervention ethics → distinguish **permission to help** from **permission to choose method**.
- Saku's self-sufficiency → better described as **jurisdictional isolation amid genuine relational embeddedness**.

## OPEN

- baseball departure and the formation of Saku's current code;
- family divorce and current family arrangement beyond the fact of his solitary residence;
- whether Saku will name romantic attachment and how he handles competing bonds;
- whether Yuzuki's newly named love survives ordinary disappointment rather than crisis intensity;
- whether Asuka leaves Fukui and what her movement does to Saku;
- whether Saku can accept being helped without converting help into another role he controls.

# XXII. V02 freeze statement

Within the Volume 02 boundary, the strongest integrated conclusion is:

> **Volume 02 treats intimacy as a test of whether self-authored people can remain authors when another person becomes important. Yuzuki's growth is the acquisition of a core that coercion cannot own; Saku's growth is partial recognition that care creates reciprocal claims on the helper. Their romance becomes credible not because their polished selves match, but because their initial fantasy of perfect similarity breaks and they continue moving toward one another anyway.**

The largest unresolved contradiction is Saku's ethics of intervention. V02 makes him more consent-aware at the level of *whether* to enter another person's problem, while simultaneously showing that once he believes entry is authorized, he can choose frighteningly coercive methods. That contradiction must remain live into V03 rather than being resolved by the fact that this particular intervention succeeded.
