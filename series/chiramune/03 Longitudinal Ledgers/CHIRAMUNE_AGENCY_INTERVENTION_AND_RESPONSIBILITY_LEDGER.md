# Chiramune agency, intervention, and responsibility ledger

## Responsibility

Track when one character tries to change another person's situation, what authority or leverage makes the intervention possible, how consent/refusal operates, who is treated as responsible for outcomes, and where help crosses into paternalism or instrumentalization.

V01 justifies an independent ledger because its central plot repeatedly returns to the same ethical structure under different stakes.

## V01 governing tension

Saku's default helping model is **high-agency intervention**. He does not merely offer emotional support; he diagnoses, plans, selects social instruments, engineers circumstances, and accepts retaliation. His best restraint is an insistence that the recipient eventually own the story. His recurring failure mode is forgetting that principle while trying to guarantee a beautiful outcome.

## Responsibility model across V01

### Stage 1 — responsibility as heroic burden

Saku's initial maxim is effectively: if he accepts a reliance claim, he must exceed expectations. This produces extreme follow-through but also makes every outcome a referendum on whether he remains Chitose Saku.

### Stage 2 — responsibility as control

When Kenta approaches the confrontation, Saku thinks that because he pushed Kenta forward he must produce the successful result. He recognizes, disturbingly, that Kenta may be serving Saku's need to preserve the self-image of someone who never says "can't" or "impossible" `[part0028:p086-p090]`.

### Stage 3 — responsibility redistributed to the agent

Haru's coach/player analogy corrects the model: instruction does not erase the player's authorship. Saku sees that claiming all failure would also claim all success.

### Stage 4 — recipient preference enters the model

Kenta's soccer criticism adds a different correction: even an intervention designed to include can be unwanted. Saku's own criterion—trust grounded in mutual understanding—requires him to treat Kenta's experience as relevant rather than invoke benevolent intention.

### Stage 5 — partial commitment with load limits

At Starbucks, Saku is still willing to intervene forcefully, but afterward he articulates a finite carrying capacity. The ethical endpoint is not nonintervention. It is **bounded commitment to chosen people without pretending to own their entire life**.

## Instrumentalization ledger

V01 repeatedly permits one person to be used as a means while rejecting total reduction to a means.

- Saku uses Yua's low-threat presentation, but rejects using her romantic availability as bait.
- He uses Yuko's bluntness, but she knows why she is there and acts according to her own values.
- He says he is using Kenta to satisfy his own aesthetic, while explicitly allowing Kenta to use him in return.
- Kenta initially uses Saku as external authority; the intervention succeeds when Kenta can act without direct control.

**Current inference:** the V01 ethical distinction is less "never instrumentalize" than **preserve the other person's independent subjecthood while entering reciprocal uses openly enough that refusal and divergence remain possible**.

## Hard counterevidence against an uncomplicated heroic reading

1. Kenta did not initiate the original intervention.
2. Window-breaking is intentionally invasive even with parental permission.
3. Public disclosure of Kenta's embarrassing reason is not clearly consented to.
4. Saku's social advantage lets him define what counts as acceptable inclusion.
5. His Starbucks retaliation is humiliating and physically intimidating.
6. He admits that deeply hurting the aggressors would not by itself trouble him.

## Hard counterevidence against a purely manipulative reading

1. Saku invests substantial private time that brings little status payoff.
2. He reads Kenta's interests rather than treating them as beneath him.
3. He rejects the easiest manipulative route when it would exploit Yua's affection.
4. He admits fault when Kenta identifies a real blind spot.
5. He actively terminates the dependency-producing mentor role.
6. He continues ordinary friendship after the "project" no longer supplies a dramatic challenge.

## V02+ watch conditions

- a recipient explicitly refuses Saku's help;
- Saku's desired beautiful outcome conflicts with another person's informed choice;
- he cannot fix an outcome despite accepting responsibility;
- someone exploits his inability to refuse reliance;
- friendship obligation conflicts with impartial fairness;
- he learns to support without designing the entire route.

Volume 02 does not remove Saku's interventionist temperament. It forces a more precise three-stage distinction:

1. **another person's problem remains theirs**;
2. **their request for help can authorize shared responsibility**;
3. **authorization to help does not automatically authorize every method the helper chooses**.

Saku makes genuine progress on stages 1–2 and remains dangerously underdeveloped on stage 3.

## Relational standing over self-risk

V02 adds a category missing from the V01 model: **other people may acquire legitimate standing over how Saku treats himself**, not because they own him but because relationships create foreseeable shared costs.

Yua and Yuko object that an injury to Saku is not merely "his" consequence. Saku's later promise to consult them does not surrender self-authorship; it recognizes that autonomous action can still impose burdens on others.

This should remain distinct from paternalism. The claim is not "friends may veto Saku's body." It is that a model of responsibility that counts only the acting subject's costs is descriptively incomplete once care is reciprocal.

## Consent-to-help versus consent-to-method

The apartment scene requires a standing warning in this ledger.

**FACT:** Yuzuki explicitly asks Saku to help her and later endorses the result of his intervention.

**FACT:** she does not prospectively consent to being restrained, frightened, slapped, or having elements of her prior coercion reenacted.

**VALUE JUDGMENT:** retrospective gratitude cannot by itself validate the method. The scene is evidence that Saku can correctly recognize *when* he has been invited into another person's problem while still overclaiming authority over *how* to solve it.

Future readings should test whether he learns this distinction or whether successful outcomes reinforce his confidence in extreme methods.

## V02 revision state

- V01 claim "Saku often appropriates responsibility" → **REVISE:** he now explicitly recognizes recipient jurisdiction, but remains prone to method-level overreach.
- V01 claim "Saku accepts retaliation personally" → **REVISE:** he increasingly recognizes friends' standing in his risk decisions.
- V01 claim "beautiful outcome can tempt paternalism" → **STRENGTHEN**.
- New live question: can Saku let another person choose not merely the goal, but the method by which he helps?

## Process authorship rule

V03 supports a four-part intervention test:

1. **standing** — why may the helper enter?
2. **goal jurisdiction** — who selects the desired outcome?
3. **method jurisdiction** — who knows, authorizes, and may refuse the procedure?
4. **consequence ownership** — who bears ordinary failure, debt, and aftermath?

Saku improves most at stages 2 and 4. He accepts Tokyo as Asuka's choice and her refusal of his lifelong guarantee. His failures cluster at stage 3: disclosure, surveillance, physical shocks, and unsafe secrecy. V03 therefore rejects any claim that respecting the final answer is enough.

## V03 revision state

- permission to help versus permission to choose method → **STRENGTHEN** with multiple new counterexamples.
- relational standing over Saku's self-risk → **PRESERVE**; the Tokyo journey again distributes consequences beyond him.
- Saku can receive another person's method → **STRENGTHEN but scope-limit** to Asuka's consensual editing/witness.
- successful intervention as ethical vindication → **REJECT in strong form**.
- New live question: can Saku make procedural consent ordinary rather than exceptional when urgency, romance, or accurate prediction tempts control?

## Collective-process rule after V04

The V03 four-part intervention test now applies to groups:

1. agreement on a goal does not imply agreement on method;
2. positional authority does not confer authority over another person's inner effort;
3. withheld governance information can make apparently voluntary participation less informed;
4. repair requires more than a successful outcome—it must reopen voice, role differentiation, and refusal.

Haru's intervention with Saku works because her pressure terminates in his deliberation. Her initial basketball leadership fails because the pressure is designed to terminate in compliance.
