Spoilers for all three seasons of *Love Live! Superstar!!*.

Keke Tang and Wien Margarete are almost perfect opposites in how they enter *Superstar!!*.

Keke arrives as **faith**.

Wien arrives as **judgment**.

Keke looks at school idols and sees salvation, beauty, possibility, and a dream worth crossing national borders for. Wien looks at school idols and sees amateurism, inferiority, and a lower stage she can use to claw her way back toward elite musical legitimacy. Keke hears Kanon’s voice and thinks: *this must reach people*. Wien hears Kanon’s voice and thinks: *prove yourself*. That contrast matters because the whole series is about whether singing is primarily a form of proof or a form of connection.

Their arcs eventually bend toward each other. Keke has to learn that a dream is not less real because its first form ends. Wien has to learn that a song is not less serious because it is made through shared feeling rather than solitary excellence. Together, they mark two poles of *Superstar!!*’s central argument:

> **A dream must be loved intensely enough to begin, but not frozen so tightly that it cannot change form.**
> **Talent must be taken seriously enough to sharpen the song, but not worshiped so completely that it kills the heart of singing.**

# Keke Tang: the believer whose dream has a deadline

Keke is the character who makes *Superstar!!* happen.

Not Kanon, even though Kanon is the protagonist. Not Chisato, even though Chisato anchors Kanon emotionally. Not Ren, even though Ren connects the story to Yuigaoka’s founding. Keke is the spark. She is the one who sees Kanon before Kanon can see herself. She is the girl who runs toward the dream without embarrassment, fear, or social calculation.

The earlier Kanon analysis called Keke “the protagonist who wasn’t,” and that remains one of the best ways to understand her. She initially looks like the standard *Love Live* lead: school-idol evangelist, dream-bearer, recruiter, emotional engine, the girl who refuses to let the dream die. But the story gives the formal protagonist role to Kanon instead, creating a stronger structure: Keke has the faith, Kanon has the wound, and Liella! is born when faith reaches wound.

That is Keke’s first great function. She externalizes the franchise’s dream-logic. She is the person who can say, without irony, that school idols matter. Kanon’s story tests whether that belief can actually save someone.

## Keke as a person

Keke is loud, direct, impulsive, theatrical, affectionate, stubborn, and often hilarious. Her speech style, her idol worship, her extreme reactions, her Sunny Passion fixation, her anti-Sumire outbursts, and her tendency to overcommit all make her the most overtly comic member of the original five.

But the comedy is deceptive. Keke is not simply the “genki foreign girl” archetype. Her intensity is rooted in distance, risk, and limited permission. She is from Shanghai. She came to Japan because school idols meant something to her deeply enough to leave home. Unlike the girls who encounter school idols inside their own school environment, Keke’s love begins across distance. She loves school idols before she can live inside that world.

That gives her dream a strange mixture of purity and desperation. It is pure because she loves school idols with almost religious sincerity. It is desperate because she has come from another country to chase something that may not last.

The series repeatedly frames her as someone whose dream is conditional. In Season 1, she has to make the dream real. In Season 2, family pressure and results hover over whether she can remain in Japan. In Season 3, the question changes: not “will she be allowed to stay?” but “what does she do with this love after high school ends?” The full-series synthesis notes that Keke’s family pressure evolves instead of disappearing, and that Keke’s Shanghai stage is one of the places where character psychology becomes performance structure.

That is the key to Keke: she often sounds like the most certain person in the room, but her dream is one of the least secure.

## Keke’s faith

Keke believes in school idols before the story proves them.

That is important. She does not need Yuigaoka’s school-idol past. She does not need a winning record. She does not need Kanon to already be confident. She hears Kanon sing and immediately recognizes something precious. Season 1 is built around “a failed voice becomes a shared beginning,” and Keke’s first role is to reopen Kanon’s road by treating her voice as worth hearing before Kanon can believe that herself.

This makes Keke’s faith active, not passive. She does not merely admire school idols. She translates admiration into action:

She recruits Kanon.
She writes lyrics.
She researches school idols.
She makes costumes.
She follows Sunny Passion.
She pushes against Ren’s institutional opposition.
She keeps the dream legible when Kanon wants to retreat.

She is the one who says, essentially: this beautiful thing exists, and therefore we must move toward it.

The weakness of this faith is that Keke can become absolutist. She sometimes mistakes her own pure vision of school idols for the only legitimate form of the dream. That is why her conflict with Sumire matters so much. Sumire approaches performance through show business, stage experience, ambition, and worldly hunger. Keke initially reads that as impure. Sumire reads Keke as naïve. Both are wrong, and both are right.

Keke’s idol-faith needs Sumire’s showbiz realism. Sumire’s showbiz hunger needs Keke’s sincerity.

## Keke as performer

Keke is not initially the strongest physical performer. Her stamina is poor, and her enthusiasm often outruns her body. That is part of her charm, but also part of her thematic position. She is not the person who proves school idols through technical excellence. She proves them through belief.

Her strongest performance contributions are:

- costume-making
- lyric imagination
- idol knowledge
- emotional ignition
- stage sincerity
- group morale
- symbolic center moments

Keke is not the “best singer” like Kanon or the dancer like Chisato. She is the girl who knows what school idols are supposed to mean before the group knows how to embody it.

That makes her a kind of internal audience. She is both performer and fan. She never stops being a fan, even after becoming a champion. The point is not that she outgrows fandom; it is that her fandom becomes performance, then becomes future.

Her Shanghai performance in Season 3 is the clearest expression of this. Shanghai returns Keke to her origin: she left home as a school-idol admirer and returns as someone transformed by that love. The stage translates her Japanese school-idol life back into her family and hometown context.

That is why the Shanghai arc works. It is not just an international episode. It answers the question: can the girl who came from Shanghai to chase school idols bring that transformed self home?

The answer is yes.

## Keke and Kanon

Keke’s relationship with Kanon is the founding relationship of Liella!.

Keke hears Kanon before Kanon can hear herself. She turns Kanon’s voice from a source of shame back into a source of possibility. Kanon’s early singing is frightening because it exposes her, and the “I can sing because I’m not alone” motif begins with support from people like Keke.

Their relationship begins asymmetrically:

> Keke believes.
> Kanon doubts.
> Keke pulls.
> Kanon resists.
> Keke hears the singer Kanon thinks has already ended.

But the relationship does not remain that way. Kanon eventually gives Keke’s dream executable form. Keke has passion, lyrics, and faith; Kanon turns those feelings into song. The earlier analysis described Kanon’s first major creative act as taking Keke’s lyrics and emotional force and making them singable.

Then Season 3 reverses the original rescue. Keke decides school idols are a beautiful but temporary youth dream, and Kanon returns Keke’s own philosophy to her: something you love that much does not simply reach “the end of the road.”

That is one of the cleanest full-circle moments in *Superstar!!*.

Season 1 Keke tells Kanon, in effect:

> Do not let failure end singing.

Season 3 Kanon tells Keke:

> Do not let gratitude, duty, and graduation end the thing that changed you.

Their bond is therefore not simply “Keke saved Kanon.” It is reciprocal across time. Keke gives Kanon the courage to begin again. Kanon gives Keke permission to let the dream continue beyond its first form.

## Keke and Sumire

Keke’s relationship with Sumire is arguably her richest non-Kanon relationship.

At first, they clash because they represent incompatible vocabularies of performance. Keke speaks school-idol purity. Sumire speaks showbiz ambition. Keke sees Sumire as opportunistic and unserious. Sumire sees Keke as delusional, abrasive, and fanatical. Their bickering is comic, but the emotional logic is serious.

Sumire’s center arc reveals the pain of wanting to be chosen, and being trusted as center by Keke matters because it is recognition without pity. Keke is the harshest judge of school-idol sincerity in the group. If she chooses Sumire, it means something. It means she is not simply humoring Sumire. She believes Sumire can carry the stage.

Then Season 2 turns their hostile intimacy into open care. Keke hides her family pressure. Sumire is the one who sees the danger, worries about Keke, and is willing to become the villain by suggesting a second-years-only performance if it might keep Keke in Japan. That is a bad solution, but it comes from love.

Keke’s “I hate you, but I love you” response captures their relationship better than a clean confession of friendship ever could. They do not become soft. They become honest within their abrasive grammar.

By Season 3, Sumire can say plainly that she wants to keep seeing Keke perform. That is growth for both of them. Sumire no longer has to hide care entirely behind arrogance; Keke no longer has to treat school idols as something that must end at graduation.

Their final path together — Keke continuing toward performance and helping Sumire’s stage future — is exactly right. Keke began as the fan who wanted school idols to exist. She ends as someone who can help produce another performer’s future.

## Keke’s family and Shanghai

Keke’s family conflict is often easy to flatten into “parents versus dream,” but *Superstar!!* is more careful than that. Her parents are not depicted as villains. They worry, but they also allowed her to go to Japan. Her sister Mengmeng wants her to keep singing. Keke’s problem is not oppression in the simple sense. It is gratitude.

She thinks:

> My parents let me chase this dream.
> I should repay them by becoming stable, reassuring, and dutiful.
> Therefore, perhaps school idols should end with high school.

This is emotionally mature and emotionally wrong.

It is mature because she understands that other people have supported her. It is wrong because she converts gratitude into self-denial.

That is why the Shanghai stage is so important. Keke is not simply performing for an audience. She is allowing her family and hometown to see the person school idols made her. The Shanghai stage translates her school-idol life back into the place she came from.

The core message is not “ignore your family and chase your dream.” It is subtler:

> The people who love you may not be reassured by watching you abandon what made you truly happy.

Keke’s eventual future — returning to Shanghai for a while, attending college, and preparing to return to the stage — is the correct synthesis. She does not reject family, home, or adulthood. She rejects the false idea that adulthood requires killing the love that school idols awakened.

## Keke’s completed arc

Keke begins as the girl who loves school idols from afar.

She becomes the girl who makes Liella! possible.

She becomes the girl whose dream is endangered by family pressure.

She becomes the girl who helps Sumire believe she can be center.

She becomes the girl who tries to treat school idols as “the end of youth.”

She ends as someone who understands that the high-school form of the dream ends, but the love behind it does not.

The series’ larger thesis says a failed or ended form of a dream does not prove the feeling behind it was false, and Keke is one of the clearest embodiments of that idea. Her graduation does not invalidate her love of performing; dreams change form and do not have to be discarded because their first form ends.

Keke’s final meaning is not simply “follow your dream.” It is:

> **The dream that saved you in youth can become an adult future, but only if you stop treating gratitude, time, and endings as reasons to lie about what you love.**

# Wien Margarete: the singer who mistook proof for song

Wien enters *Superstar!!* as the most direct ideological threat Liella! faces.

Sunny Passion are rivals, but they share Liella!’s basic worldview. They believe in school idols, rootedness, community, and mutual inspiration. Wien does not. She treats Love Live as a lesser stage, school idols as amateurs, and singing as proof of solitary superiority. The earlier synthesis identifies her as the strongest ideological foil to Liella!: solitary excellence, elite selection, singing as proof, against Liella!’s belief that people’s feelings become song through connection.

Wien’s question to the series is simple:

> Is school idolhood actually artistically serious, or is it just amateur sentimentality?

That is a dangerous question because she is not untalented, not stupid, and not merely mean. She is extraordinary. Her performances have force. Her criticism has bite. If she were obviously inferior, her critique would not matter. But she is good enough that Liella! has to answer her seriously.

## Wien as a person

Wien is proud, severe, blunt, impatient, socially awkward, and allergic to pity. Her default mode is contempt because contempt protects her from humiliation. She looks down on school idols because the alternative would be admitting that the school-idol world might contain something she lacks.

Her wound is elite failure.

She comes from a musical family. She expected to follow a serious path. She failed to enter the Vienna school she wanted. Love Live becomes, for her, not a dream but a route back toward the “real” dream. She does not initially value school idols as themselves. She instrumentalizes them.

That is why losing to Liella! hurts so badly. She was not merely beaten in a contest. She was beaten by the very kind of performer she had dismissed as inferior. If Love Live is worthless, then losing there should not matter. But it does matter, because she needed the result to restore her own legitimacy.

Her arrogance is therefore not just personality. It is a defense against the possibility that her hierarchy of value is wrong.

## Wien as anti-school-idol

Wien’s early worldview is:

> Singing is proof.
> Talent should be recognized.
> The superior singer should win.
> If the superior singer loses, the judging system is illegitimate.

That is why she rejects Love Live when she loses. She cannot yet imagine a valid criterion of performance beyond her own superiority.

Liella!’s answer is not “talent does not matter.” That would be too easy and too false. Wien’s talent matters enormously. Her voice, stage presence, and intensity are real. What the show rejects is the idea that talent alone exhausts the meaning of song.

The Kanon analysis summarized their contrast clearly:

> Wien sings to prove.
> Kanon sings to reach.

That is the entire Wien problem.

She does love singing. But by the time she enters the story, love has been buried under proof, status, hierarchy, family expectation, and injury. Her arc is not learning to sing. It is remembering why singing mattered before it became evidence.

## The warmth before proof

Season 3 gives Wien the crucial memory: singing once warmed her loneliness. Before singing became an elite ladder, it was consolation. Before it became proof of superiority, it was something that filled an empty space.

That memory is the crack in her armor.

Kanon’s role is important here. She does not try to beat Wien’s musical worldview by arguing that school idols are technically superior. She asks a more dangerous question:

> When did singing make your heart sparkle?

That question bypasses Wien’s hierarchy. It asks for origin, not ranking. It makes Wien look behind the elite musical system and remember the emotional beginning of the thing she now uses as a weapon.

The series’ Season 3 analysis names this directly: Kanon teaches Wien that singing began as warmth before it became proof.

Wien’s first genuine breakthrough is not a sentimental declaration that she loves Liella!. It is smaller and more believable: she has fun singing again.

That is the beginning.

## Wien as performer

Wien’s performance style is completely different from Liella!’s early style.

She is vertical, solitary, sharp, theatrical, elite-coded. Her songs create a world around her, but it is a world she dominates alone. Her stage presence says: look at me, recognize me, measure me, admit me.

This is why she is such an effective rival. She exposes the potential softness in Liella!’s worldview. If school idols are only about friendship, then a performer like Wien can dismiss them. If Liella! cannot prove that shared feeling can produce something artistically serious, then Wien’s critique stands.

But the story does not leave Wien outside forever. Her gifts are eventually absorbed into Liella!, and that is where her arc becomes especially interesting.

Once inside the group, Wien does not become the warmest member. She becomes an internal source of seriousness, objectivity, and formation discipline. The full-series synthesis describes Wien and Tomari as external critiques of school idolhood: Wien says school idols lack elite artistic legitimacy, and once she joins, that critique becomes a strength — she brings seriousness and objectivity.

That is exactly right.

Liella! does not defeat Wien by making her soft. It defeats her by making her useful without erasing her severity.

## Wien and responsibility

The decisive integration moment for Wien is not ordinary friendship. It is responsibility.

When Kanon and the others try too hard to make her comfortable, Wien resists. She does not want to be handled gently. She hates forced small talk and social accommodation because it feels like pity. What works is giving her formation responsibility. She is asked to use her eye, her standards, her bluntness.

This is good writing because it respects Wien’s personality. Some characters are integrated through warmth. Wien is integrated through seriousness. She needs to know she is not being included as charity. She needs to know Liella! needs something only she can provide.

Her early critiques of Kinako and Natsumi are harsh, but they are not meaningless cruelty. They are performance judgments. The difference is that Liella! has matured enough to receive harshness without interpreting it as rejection. Kinako and Natsumi ask her how to improve. Wien, in turn, learns from their humility.

This is the moment where her severity begins serving the group instead of isolating her above it.

## Skill and heart

Wien’s mature insight is that skill matters, but heart matters too.

That sounds like a simple idol-anime moral, but for Wien it is a major internal transformation. She is not renouncing skill. She is refusing the false separation between technical excellence and emotional alignment.

The earlier synthesis puts it well: Wien’s growth is not becoming generically nice; it is learning that skill and heart are both necessary, and her seriousness becomes useful once it serves the group rather than isolating her above it.

That is why she can remain recognizably Wien at the end. She is still blunt. Still proud. Still technically severe. But her severity now serves Liella!’s shared song. She does not stand above the group as a judge. She stands inside it as a sharpening force.

## Wien and Kanon

Wien’s relationship with Kanon is the series’ most important singer-to-singer relationship.

Keke hears Kanon’s voice and believes in it.
Wien hears Kanon’s voice and challenges it.

Both matter.

Kanon and Wien are mirrors because both failed elite musical pathways. Kanon failed to enter Yuigaoka’s music course. Wien failed the Vienna route. But their responses diverge.

Kanon’s failure breaks her open to relational song.
Wien’s failure hardens her into proof-based song.

Kanon learns:

> I can sing because I am not alone.

Wien initially insists:

> I must sing so no one can deny me.

That is why Kanon is the only person who can really teach Wien. Kanon understands musical failure from the inside, but she also embodies an alternate answer. She is living proof that a failed elite path does not invalidate singing.

The final inversion is beautiful:

> Kanon leaves Yuigaoka because Yuigaoka restored her dream.
> Wien stays at Yuigaoka because Yuigaoka restored hers.

Vienna is the dream beyond Yuigaoka for Kanon, but for Wien, Vienna is the place she must temporarily refuse because Yuigaoka has become the more necessary site of growth.

That is not a contradiction. It is the clearest evidence that they needed opposite movements.

## Wien and Tomari

Wien and Tomari are paired because they attack school idolhood from different angles.

Wien says school idols lack artistic legitimacy.
Tomari says school idols lack practical, measurable value.

Wien’s critique is aesthetic and hierarchical.
Tomari’s critique is economic and instrumental.

Together, they make Season 3 sharper. They prevent Liella! from becoming a closed system of self-congratulation. If school idols matter, the series has to answer not only the fan’s question — “isn’t this wonderful?” — but the skeptic’s question:

> Is it artistically serious?
> Is it worth the time?
> Does it produce anything real?

Wien’s eventual answer is not verbal argument. It is participation. She becomes part of the six-member future Liella!, adding vocal and technical intensity. Tomari adds analysis and sober care.

That is a strong resolution. The critiques are not erased. They are domesticated into strengths.

## Wien’s completed arc

Wien begins as a singer who uses Love Live as a stepping-stone back to elite legitimacy.

She becomes a rival who cannot accept defeat.

She becomes a student forced to learn from Kanon.

She becomes part of TomaKanoTe, where rivalry becomes honest rather than contemptuous.

She joins Liella!, where her objectivity becomes useful.

She chooses to stay at Yuigaoka rather than immediately return to Vienna.

She ends as one of the future pillars of Liella!, bringing a seriousness the group needs.

Her final meaning is not “the arrogant girl becomes nice.” That would be too small.

Her arc is:

> **The singer who used music as proof rediscovers music as warmth, then learns to place excellence in service of shared song.**

# Keke and Wien as opposites

Keke and Wien are two different foreign arrivals who force Yuigaoka to define what school idols are.

Keke comes from Shanghai with love.
Wien comes from Vienna with contempt.

Keke sees school idols from afar and thinks they are worth crossing the world for.
Wien sees school idols as a lower world she can use to repair a failed elite path.

Keke’s question is:

> Can this dream I love become real?

Wien’s question is:

> Can this amateur form possibly be worthy of serious song?

Keke’s danger is over-idealization.
Wien’s danger is contempt.

Keke believes so intensely that she risks turning the dream into a sacred, time-limited youth object.
Wien judges so intensely that she risks turning singing into a weapon against her own loneliness.

Keke starts with connection and must learn future.
Wien starts with proof and must learn connection.

## Faith versus proof

Keke’s first movement toward Kanon is faith. She does not ask Kanon to prove herself. She hears Kanon and believes.

Wien’s first movement toward Kanon is proof. She asks Kanon to sing, to demonstrate, to justify the reputation surrounding her.

This makes them two different answers to Kanon’s wounded voice.

Keke says:

> Your voice already matters because it moved me.

Wien says:

> Your voice must prove it deserves recognition.

Kanon needs Keke first. Without Keke, the story does not begin. But Liella! eventually needs Wien too. Without Wien, the story’s idea of song may remain too protected from critique. Keke makes school idols lovable. Wien forces school idols to become rigorous.

## Shanghai and Vienna

Their places also matter.

Shanghai is Keke’s origin. It is where her family, language, and pre-Liella! self exist. Returning there lets the story ask whether school idols are merely a Japanese high-school dream she borrowed, or whether the transformed Keke can be seen by her home. The answer is that her love can travel back with her.

Vienna is Wien’s injured destination. It is not simply “home” in the same emotional way. It is the elite musical future she failed to enter and still desires. Yuigaoka becomes necessary because it gives her something Vienna, as she imagined it, could not: a way to sing that is not only proof.

Keke’s movement is:

> Shanghai → Yuigaoka → Shanghai transformed → stage future.

Wien’s movement is:

> Vienna wound → Yuigaoka contempt → Yuigaoka belonging → future Liella!.

They cross in opposite directions. Keke’s school-idol dream begins abroad and becomes real in Japan. Wien’s elite musical dream begins abroad, breaks, and is reinterpreted through Japanese school idolhood.

## Family and duty

Both are tied to family, but in different ways.

Keke’s family conflict is about gratitude and reassurance. She wants to repay parents who let her chase her dream. Her sister sees that the dream should continue. Her danger is self-denial disguised as filial maturity.

Wien’s family conflict is about comparison and legitimacy. Her sisters and musical family represent a path she expected to join. Her failure injures her identity. Her danger is superiority disguised as artistic seriousness.

Keke’s family problem says:

> I should stop wanting so much because others supported me.

Wien’s family problem says:

> I must become undeniable because failure made me lesser.

Both require correction. Keke must keep wanting honestly. Wien must stop making worth depend on superiority.

## Their relationships to Liella!

Keke is one of Liella!’s birth parents.

Wien is one of Liella!’s late-stage challengers.

Keke gives the original group its dream-language. Without Keke, Kanon’s voice may remain buried. Without Keke, school idols do not become the form through which Yuigaoka’s first generation begins.

Wien gives the inherited group its technical edge and critique. Without Wien, future Liella! may remain warm but less sharpened. She enters late, but she changes what Liella! can become after the founders leave.

The full-series synthesis describes Wien and Tomari as late-stage challengers who make inherited Liella! sharper. Keke, by contrast, is part of the original formation: one of the blocked selves the first Liella! resolves, and the person whose faith creates the pair from which everything grows.

So their roles are temporally opposite:

> Keke is the beginning.
> Wien is the sharpening before inheritance.

# Keke and Wien as performers

Keke’s performance is faith made visible.
Wien’s performance is excellence under pressure.

Keke is most powerful when the stage expresses love: love of school idols, love of Kanon’s voice, love of Sunny Passion, love of Liella!, love of Shanghai, love of the dream she almost lets end.

Wien is most powerful when the stage expresses seriousness: ambition, skill, injury, refusal, rivalry, discipline, and eventually the resolve to make Liella! better because she is part of it.

Keke’s weakness as a performer is that her passion can outrun her body, technique, or future-plan. Wien’s weakness is that her technique can outrun her heart.

Keke needs form.
Wien needs warmth.

Kanon gives both, in different ways.

To Keke, Kanon gives executable song and later permission for the dream to continue.

To Wien, Kanon gives the memory that singing began before proof.

# Their writing as characters

Keke could easily have remained comic relief: foreign idol fangirl, odd speech, Sunny Passion obsession, anti-Sumire gags. What saves her is that her comedy is attached to a real structure: distance, permission, family, time, and the terror that the thing she loves most might have to end.

Wien could easily have remained a shallow arrogant rival. What saves her is that her arrogance is attached to a real structure: elite musical failure, family expectation, loneliness, and the need to turn singing into proof because proof feels safer than vulnerability.

Both characters work because their surface exaggerations are not random.

Keke’s extremity is dream as survival.
Wien’s extremity is pride as armor.

The series is also at its strongest when their psychology becomes performance. Keke’s Shanghai stage and Wien’s formation planning are explicitly examples of the series tying character psychology to performance structure. That is why their arcs land. They are not just explained through dialogue. They become stages, centers, formations, songs, rival structures, and future roles.

# Final synthesis

Keke Tang and Wien Margarete are two of *Superstar!!*’s most important non-Kanon characters because they define the boundaries of what school idols mean.

Keke proves that school idols are worth loving before the story has earned that belief.

Wien forces the story to earn that belief against a serious critique.

Keke says:

> This dream is beautiful enough to cross the world for.

Wien says:

> If this dream is real, prove it can stand beside true musical excellence.

Liella! needs both.

Without Keke, there is no beginning. Kanon’s wounded voice remains unheard, and school idols do not become the form through which Yuigaoka’s first generation finds itself.

Without Wien, there is no final sharpening. Liella! might remain warm, but it would not have to answer whether warmth, amateur youth, and shared feeling can produce a song with the seriousness of elite art.

Keke’s final arc says:

> A school-idol dream does not have to die when youth ends.

Wien’s final arc says:

> A serious singer does not become lesser by singing with others.

Together, they complete one of *Superstar!!*’s deepest claims:

> **A song is not made real by purity alone, and it is not made great by excellence alone.**
> **It becomes alive when love, skill, pain, rivalry, gratitude, and future all find a form they can share.**
