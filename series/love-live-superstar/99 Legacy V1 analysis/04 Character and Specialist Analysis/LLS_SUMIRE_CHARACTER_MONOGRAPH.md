Spoilers for all three seasons of *Love Live! Superstar!!*.

Sumire Heanna is one of *Superstar!!*’s best examples of a character whose surface joke is also her wound.

On the surface, she is the “Galaxy!” girl: theatrical, vain, showbiz-obsessed, dramatic, easily offended, and desperate to be treated as special. She wants the center, wants attention, wants a stage, wants recognition, and often talks as if fame is simply her natural destination.

But the joke works because it is not empty. Sumire is not a girl who believes she is destined for stardom because life has always rewarded her. She is a girl who keeps insisting she is destined for stardom because life has repeatedly told her the opposite.

Her core wound is simple:

> **Sumire wants to be seen, but has spent her life being placed in the background.**

That makes her one of the cleanest “blocked self-expression” characters in the original five. In the full-series structure, the original Liella! members each resolve a distorted relation to performance: Kanon’s blocked voice, Keke’s distant dream, Chisato’s usefulness-bound support, Ren’s misread inheritance, and Sumire’s denied visibility.

# Sumire as a person

Sumire is proud, theatrical, ambitious, defensive, perceptive, funny, and much more worldly than most of Liella!. She has a strong instinct for presentation: camera angles, audience reaction, costumes, stage image, show-business logic, and the gap between amateur sincerity and public-facing performance.

She is also prickly because she has learned to expect rejection.

That is the key to her personality. Sumire’s arrogance is not simple narcissism. It is armor against humiliation.

She has been auditioning since childhood. She has worked in show business. She understands sets, cameras, timing, posing, and the strange indignities of entertainment work. But instead of becoming a star, she becomes the extra, the background girl, the child performer who gets shoved into novelty roles, the “giant isopod” rather than the heroine. Her life has trained her to believe that no matter how hard she reaches for center stage, someone else will be chosen.

So she develops a persona that says:

> I am already special.
> I am already a galaxy-level talent.
> If others fail to recognize me, that is their blindness.

But underneath that is something more painful:

> If I stop declaring my own specialness, maybe no one else will.

That is why Sumire’s comedy often has a slightly desperate undertone. Her grandiosity is not purely delusional. It is self-maintenance.

# The meaning of “Galaxy”

“Galaxy” is one of those catchphrases that looks like a gag first and a character thesis second.

It is absurd, flashy, and self-branding. It sounds like someone trying to create a marketable identity out of sheer willpower. But that is exactly Sumire. She knows that show business rewards memorable images. She knows that being ordinary is death. “Galaxy” is a personal brand, a defense mechanism, and a prayer.

It says:

> I am not background.
> I am not forgettable.
> I am cosmic, dazzling, impossible to ignore.

The tragedy is that she says this because she has been ignored so often.

That is why Sumire should not be read as merely vain. Vanity implies she is overvaluing an already secure self-image. Sumire’s self-image is not secure. Her theatricality is a scaffold around insecurity.

# Sumire’s central wound: being chosen

Sumire’s strongest arc is her center arc.

When the others consider who should stand at the center, Sumire wants it badly — but wanting it and being offered it are not the same thing. Wanting the center lets her preserve the fantasy that she deserves it. Being given the center risks exposing whether she can actually bear it.

That is why she panics when external comments suggest someone else should be center. She does not fight with the confidence she performs. She folds. She accepts the rejection almost too quickly because it confirms the story she already believes:

> In the end, it will not be me.

This is what makes Keke’s trust matter so much. Keke is the harshest judge of school-idol sincerity in the group; if Keke chooses Sumire, it cannot be dismissed as empty pity. The prior Keke/Sumire analysis put it well: Sumire’s center arc reveals the pain of wanting to be chosen, and being trusted by Keke matters because it is recognition without pity.

That distinction is everything.

Sumire does not need someone to say, “You can be center because we feel sorry for you.” That would be unbearable.

She needs someone to say:

> I believe you can carry this stage.
> I am entrusting something precious to you.
> Do not run away from the thing you claim to want.

Keke gives her that. Not softly, not neatly, but sincerely.

# Sumire as performer

Sumire is Liella!’s showbiz realist.

Kanon gives voice.
Keke gives school-idol faith.
Chisato gives movement.
Ren gives tradition and institutional legitimacy.
Sumire gives theatrical self-presentation.

That is her crucial contribution. She understands that performance is not only sincerity. It is also image, staging, costume, framing, impact, and audience perception. She has lived in the world where performance is evaluated, packaged, and rejected. That makes her valuable because Liella! can sometimes be too innocent.

Keke loves school idols almost religiously. Kanon interprets feelings into song. Chisato arranges motion. Ren brings formal discipline. But Sumire understands the entertainment industry’s colder reality: people watch, judge, compare, comment, ignore, and move on.

This is why she is often the first to notice practical danger. She smells Natsumi’s revenue scheme faster than the others because she understands contracts, production costs, and exploitation. Kanon’s generosity is beautiful, but Sumire knows that sincerity can be used by people who understand the machinery of attention.

That makes Sumire one of Liella!’s most important counterweights. She is not the most idealistic member; she is the one who knows the stage has teeth.

# Sumire and Keke: incompatible vocabularies, necessary bond

Sumire’s richest relationship is with Keke.

They clash because they speak different languages of performance.

Keke speaks the language of school-idol purity: love, dream, admiration, effort, belief.

Sumire speaks the language of show business: opportunity, recognition, presentation, center position, visibility, career.

Keke initially sees Sumire as opportunistic and unserious. Sumire sees Keke as naïve, fanatical, and abrasive. The earlier analysis captured this as a clash between incompatible vocabularies: Keke’s school-idol purity versus Sumire’s showbiz ambition, with both girls partly wrong and partly right.

The deeper truth is:

> **Keke’s idol faith needs Sumire’s showbiz realism.**
> **Sumire’s showbiz hunger needs Keke’s sincerity.**

Without Keke, Sumire risks treating school idols as merely another route to fame. Without Sumire, Keke risks treating school idols as too pure, too sealed off from the realities of performance and public reception.

Their bickering is therefore not just comic relief. It is ideological friction.

Keke asks Sumire:

> Do you really love school idols, or do you just want a stage?

Sumire asks Keke:

> Can love alone survive the realities of being watched, judged, and needing results?

Over time, they answer each other.

## Hostile intimacy

Keke and Sumire’s care language is abrasive. They do not become soft friends who calmly validate each other. They insult, argue, provoke, deny, and then do the exact thing the other needs.

Season 2 makes this explicit. Keke hides her family pressure; Sumire sees the danger. Sumire is willing to become the bad guy by suggesting a second-years-only strategy if that might help keep Keke in Japan. It is a bad plan, but it is born from care. The prior analysis described this as “hostile intimacy” becoming open care: Keke hides the pressure, Sumire worries, and Sumire’s ugly proposal comes from love rather than cruelty.

Keke’s “I hate you, but I love you” sentiment works because it preserves their grammar. Their relationship is not cleaned up into bland affection. They remain themselves.

This is why the pair is so compelling. Sumire, who is terrified of not being chosen, is chosen by the girl who most cares about school-idol sincerity. Keke, whose dream is fragile and time-limited, is protected by the girl who understands showbiz harshness and refuses to let her disappear.

By Season 3, Sumire can say plainly that she wants to keep seeing Keke perform. That is a huge step. She no longer has to hide all care behind arrogance, and Keke no longer has to treat school idols as something that must end at graduation. Their final path — Keke continuing toward performance while helping Sumire’s stage future — is exactly the right endpoint for them.

# Sumire and Kanon

Sumire’s relationship with Kanon is less emotionally explosive than Keke/Sumire, but it is structurally important.

Kanon is the one who first gives Sumire a dignified invitation. She does not pity her. She does not simply flatter her. She gives Sumire a challenge:

> If you want the center, take it from me.

This matters because Sumire needs competition more than comfort. Comfort risks feeling like condescension. Competition acknowledges her as someone with pride.

Kanon’s broader leadership style is interpretive: she reads the wound in each person and offers the form of encouragement that person can actually accept. For Sumire, that means challenge, not pity. The Kanon analysis explicitly identifies this: “Sumire cannot accept pity, so Kanon gives her rivalry.”

That is why Kanon’s recruitment of Sumire works. She does not ask Sumire to become humbler before joining. She gives Sumire a place where wanting the spotlight is not shameful, as long as that desire becomes part of the group rather than a substitute for it.

Kanon sees that Sumire’s ambition is not poison. It is wounded energy that needs a healthier stage.

# Sumire and Chisato

Sumire and Chisato are not the most foregrounded pair, but they reveal an important contrast.

Chisato is disciplined, group-oriented, and bodily expressive. Sumire is image-oriented, self-branding, and theatrically visible. Chisato supports through structure; Sumire pushes through friction.

Both are practical in different ways. Chisato understands practice, choreography, pacing, and physical development. Sumire understands stage image, entertainment logic, and public reception. Together, they keep Liella! from being merely emotionally sincere.

If Kanon and Keke make Liella! possible as a dream, Chisato and Sumire help make it workable as performance.

# Sumire and Ren

Sumire and Ren also form a useful contrast within the original five.

Ren comes from formal refinement: piano, figure skating, school prestige, institutional memory, elegance.

Sumire comes from entertainment labor: auditions, child-showbiz disappointment, costumes, cameras, center hunger.

Ren gives Liella! legitimacy. Sumire gives Liella! showbiz tactility.

They both know performance is serious, but they know different worlds of seriousness. Ren’s seriousness is tradition and discipline. Sumire’s is exposure and rejection.

This makes Sumire especially important after Ren joins. Liella! cannot only be a sentimental school club, nor can it only be a refined music tradition. Sumire keeps the group aware that it is also an idol unit in front of audiences.

# Sumire and the first-years

Season 2 makes Sumire look more senior than she initially appears.

The first-years view the original five as intimidatingly exceptional, and Sumire is explicitly part of that aura: the “showbiz veteran” among the admired seniors. This is ironic because Sumire herself spent years feeling unchosen. From the first-years’ perspective, she is impressive. From her own perspective, she is still the girl who could not land the role she wanted.

That gap matters. It shows how success changes social meaning. Sumire’s old wounds do not vanish, but from below she already looks like someone who belongs onstage. That is part of Season 2’s central irony: Liella! was born as a place where wounded girls could begin again, but success makes it look like a place only extraordinary girls can enter.

Sumire can therefore function as both senior and warning. She proves that the impressive seniors were not always secure. Behind the “showbiz veteran” label is a girl who still fears not being chosen.

# Sumire as comic character

Sumire is very funny because she has no chill.

She overposes, overbrands, overreacts, and tries to turn every situation into a stage. Her confidence often collapses the instant she encounters actual rejection. Her “Galaxy!” persona is big enough to fill a room but brittle enough to crack under a single bad comment.

The comedy is sharp because it exposes contradiction:

- She wants attention but fears being judged.
- She claims superiority but is highly sensitive to dismissal.
- She acts worldly but is often emotionally transparent.
- She mocks Keke’s naïveté but is moved by Keke’s sincerity.
- She treats school idols as opportunity but slowly becomes one of the people most protective of the dream.

Sumire is a comic character whose jokes do not float above the drama. They express the drama.

Her catchphrase, poses, and vanity are all part of the same structure:

> **If the world will not assign me a role, I will perform one loudly enough that it has to notice.**

# Sumire as realist

Sumire’s realism is one of her most underrated traits.

She is often vain, but she is not stupid. She understands performance labor. She understands that audiences are not automatically kind. She understands that public response matters. She understands that contracts, production, and money can structure what happens onstage.

That makes her different from Keke. Keke is the believer. Sumire is the believer’s irritant: the person who keeps reminding the group that the dream must survive contact with reality.

This is why she reacts so strongly to Natsumi’s scheme. Sumire recognizes exploitation because show business has trained her to see it. Kanon’s instinct is to search for the wound beneath Natsumi’s behavior. That is valuable. But Sumire’s suspicion is also valuable. Without her, Liella! is too easy to take advantage of.

Sumire’s role is therefore not cynicism. It is protective realism.

# Sumire’s Season 1 arc

Season 1 introduces Sumire as an outsider to the initial Kanon/Keke dream. She sees school idols partly as a route back toward public recognition. That makes Keke distrust her. But the series gradually reveals that Sumire’s hunger for the stage is not shallow.

Her center conflict is the heart of her Season 1 material. She wants to shine, but she has learned not to trust any offer of visibility. Kanon’s challenge and Keke’s eventual trust let her transform ambition from defensive vanity into group contribution.

Her Season 1 endpoint is not “Sumire stops wanting attention.” That would be the wrong lesson.

Her endpoint is:

> Sumire can want the center without that desire isolating her from the group.

That is much better.

# Sumire’s Season 2 arc

Season 2 gives Sumire her strongest emotional role through Keke.

Keke’s family pressure makes the question of winning urgent. Sumire sees what Keke hides. She tries to protect Keke in a flawed but deeply revealing way. Her second-years-only proposal is morally wrong because it would compromise Liella!’s wholeness, but it is emotionally right in what it reveals: Sumire is willing to be disliked if it might keep Keke’s dream alive.

This is Sumire’s maturity. The girl who wanted to be admired becomes willing to be the bad guy.

That is a significant transformation.

She still wants recognition. She still has showbiz hunger. But she is now capable of subordinating her image to someone else’s future. That does not erase her ambition; it deepens it.

# Sumire’s Season 3 arc

Season 3 gives Sumire less explicit central focus than Kanon, Keke, Wien, Tomari, Kinako, or Shiki, but her ending is important.

By the finale, Sumire has a stage future. Agency attention and auditions pay off her long history of denied visibility. She can say, in effect, that her time onstage is not over.

This matters because *Superstar!!*’s final thesis is that dreams change form. The high-school school-idol form ends, but the feeling behind it does not. Sumire’s showbiz dream existed before Liella!, was wounded before Liella!, was reshaped by Liella!, and then continues after Liella!.

She does not graduate into vague possibility. She graduates into a renewed performance path.

That is exactly what she needed.

Sumire’s ending says:

> The girl who kept being placed in the background will keep standing on stages.

# Sumire and the series’ central themes

Sumire embodies several of *Superstar!!*’s major themes.

## 1. Failure does not invalidate desire

Sumire has failed repeatedly in show business. But the series’ broader answer is that a failed form of a dream does not prove the feeling behind it was false. This principle applies to Kanon’s failed audition, Ren’s mother’s failed school-idol club, Natsumi’s failed childhood dreams, Keke’s graduation, and Sumire’s denied center-stage recognition.

Sumire’s desire to be seen is not false just because it has been frustrated.

## 2. Recognition must not be pity

Sumire’s arc is built around the difference between being pitied and being trusted. She can accept challenge. She can accept rivalry. She can accept harshness. She cannot accept being treated as a charity case.

That is why Keke’s trust matters. It is not gentle, but it is real.

## 3. Ambition can be communal

Sumire begins with an individualistic ambition: she wants the stage for herself. But Liella! teaches her that ambition does not have to disappear into group harmony. It can become part of group strength.

This is one of *Superstar!!*’s subtler virtues. It does not punish her for wanting attention. It purifies the desire by placing it in relation.

## 4. Performance is not only sincerity

Sumire keeps the show honest about idolhood as entertainment. School idols are sincere, but they are still performers. They need impact, image, presentation, and stagecraft. Sumire prevents sincerity from becoming amateur self-satisfaction.

# Sumire’s future with Keke

Sumire and Keke’s future is one of the most satisfying post-graduation arrangements because it preserves their dynamic while changing its form.

Keke continues toward performance rather than treating school idols as the end of youth. Sumire pursues a stage career. Keke, who began as the fan who wanted school idols to exist, becomes someone who can help produce or support another performer’s future.

That is beautiful because it completes their exchange:

Keke gave Sumire sincerity.
Sumire gave Keke realism and stubborn protection.
After graduation, Keke can help Sumire’s stage future while continuing her own.

They do not need to become a conventional soft pair. Their affection remains sharp. But their futures are meaningfully intertwined.

# Sumire’s literary function

Sumire’s literary function is to bring show-business injury into the school-idol dream.

Kanon’s injury is musical failure.
Keke’s injury is distance and time-limited dream.
Chisato’s injury is usefulness-bound support.
Ren’s injury is inherited grief.
Sumire’s injury is professional invisibility.

That makes her distinct. She is the original member who already knows something about entertainment before Liella!. She is not approaching performance with innocent wonder. She is approaching it with scars from having tried and not been chosen.

This gives the series an important tension:

> School idols are beautiful because they are amateur, sincere, and school-bound.
> But stages are also places of selection, rejection, image, and public judgment.

Sumire stands at that fault line.

She is the one who knows that “I want to shine” is both a dream and a wound.

# Sumire’s completed character profile

Sumire Heanna begins as a girl who loudly insists she is special because she is terrified no one will ever choose her.

She joins Liella! partly because school idols look like a route back to visibility.

She clashes with Keke because Keke’s school-idol purity exposes the opportunism in her motive, while Sumire’s showbiz realism exposes the naïveté in Keke’s.

She is offered the center and nearly retreats because being chosen is more frightening than wanting to be chosen.

She learns that recognition without pity is possible.

She becomes one of Liella!’s practical realists, able to see entertainment-world danger and public-image stakes.

She protects Keke, badly but sincerely, when Keke’s dream is threatened.

She learns to say care more directly.

She graduates into a stage future rather than leaving performance behind.

Her final meaning is:

> **Sumire is not the vain girl who learns humility.**
> **She is the unseen girl who learns that wanting to be seen is not shameful, provided the spotlight becomes something she can share, protect, and answer with real performance.**

That is why she works. The series does not cure her by making her stop wanting the center. It lets her want it more honestly.

# Final synthesis

Sumire is Liella!’s hunger for visibility.

Not Kanon’s recovered voice.
Not Keke’s school-idol faith.
Not Chisato’s embodied support.
Not Ren’s institutional memory.
Not Kinako’s ordinary courage.
Not Wien’s elite seriousness.

Sumire is the girl who knows what it feels like to stand near the stage and still be unseen.

That makes her ambition necessary. Liella! would be poorer if everyone were content to shine only through gentle collective warmth. Sumire brings the uncomfortable, human desire to be chosen — and the series treats that desire as something worth saving rather than something to be scolded out of her.

Her arc says:

> **The wish to be seen can be vain, defensive, and ridiculous.**
> **It can also be wounded, brave, and artistically necessary.**

Sumire Heanna is all of those things.
