# S1E10 Audio-Integrated V1 Revisit

# *Love Live! Superstar!!* — Season 1, Episode 10

## “チェケラッ!!” — Audio-integrated analysis and cumulative character voice profiles

The bundle is complete and internally consistent: approximately 23 minutes and 42 seconds of stereo audio, an original Japanese subtitle track with 402 cues, dense frame coverage, and clear visual reconstruction of the rap exercises, Sumire’s private rehearsal, the center dispute, Keke’s confrontation with her, and “ノンフィクション!!”

This is the most important Sumire episode of Season 1, but the complete audio reveals that it is almost equally important for Keke. Its central question is not simply **who should be Liella!’s center?** It is:

> **Can Sumire accept recognition when her entire performing life has taught her that she will eventually be replaced?**

The episode distinguishes three desires that Sumire herself has fused together:

1.  wanting attention;

2.  wanting to perform;

3.  trusting that someone has genuinely chosen her.


She has no difficulty with the first two. The third terrifies her.

The vocal performances make that distinction much clearer than the dialogue alone. Sumire is musically and verbally fluent whenever she is *performing a role*. Her voice destabilizes when the role becomes a sincere offer of recognition. Keke, meanwhile, spends most of the episode speaking through irritation because direct admiration would expose how seriously she has begun to believe in Sumire.

The result is one of the first major instances of what will become their characteristic **hostile intimacy**.

---

# I. The episode’s core thesis: Sumire wants the center but does not believe she can remain there

Sumire’s apparent contradiction is introduced almost immediately.

When the group needs someone who can rap, she demonstrates effortless competence. She can improvise, rhyme, control rhythm, present herself, and make the other girls respond. She is clearly the best candidate.

But when Kanon tells her that the rap section places her at the center, Sumire’s speech changes completely:

> 「ちょちょ…ちょっと待って」
> 「えーと、つかぬことをお聞きしますが…」
> 「この位置というのはもしかして…」

The woman-of-the-world show-business persona disappears. Her words become fragmented, excessively cautious, and strangely formal. She knows what “center” means, but she needs someone else to state it.

When Kanon confirms it, Sumire tries to recover her pride:

> 「だ…だって私よ」
> 「私がセンターでいいったらいいの」

But even that boast contains a revealing stammer. The literal meaning is confident; the delivery is disbelief trying to dress itself as arrogance.

This is the key distinction:

> **Sumire knows how to demand the center when the demand remains hypothetical.
> She does not know how to receive the center when someone actually entrusts it to her.**

Wanting the role preserves fantasy. Accepting it creates the risk of losing it again.

---

# II. The rap assignment as a vocal diagnostic for the entire group

The comedy at the start of the episode is unusually efficient because rap exposes each girl’s instinctive relationship to voice.

## Kanon: verbal fluency interrupted by self-consciousness

Kanon begins:

> 「Hey Yo 私の名前は澁谷かのん」
> 「澁谷といっても渋谷は苦手」

She immediately runs out of words.

This is funny, but it continues the voice model established in Episode 1. Kanon is naturally musical, emotionally perceptive, and capable of writing lyrics, but improvisational self-display still makes her conscious of herself as an object being watched.

Her problem here is not the same as her earlier inability to sing. She can now perform. But rap asks her to produce an assertive public self in real time—to say *who she is* rhythmically and without protective reflection. She stalls after two lines.

Her vocal energy rises during the playful opening, then abruptly contracts into:

> 「こ…言葉が…」
> 「出てこない」

The delivery echoes her earlier audition blockage in comic miniature. Kanon has recovered song more readily than she has recovered spontaneous self-promotion.

That contrast becomes important beside Sumire, for whom self-presentation is almost automatic.

## Chisato: speech becomes body

Chisato begins well:

> 「Hey Yo 私千砂都　嵐千砂都」
> 「生まれはこの辺　特技はダンス」

Her delivery is cheerful and rhythmically comfortable. She has encountered rap through dance training and approaches it without Kanon’s self-consciousness or Ren’s intellectual uncertainty.

But then she forgets to continue rapping because she becomes absorbed in dancing.

Her voice helps sell that this is not incompetence. She sounds delighted once her body takes over. The verbal task becomes secondary to movement because Chisato’s most natural expressive system is physical.

Her explanation—

> 「ダンス始まっちゃうと、ついそっちに夢中になっちゃうんだよね」

—is casual, smiling, and entirely unashamed. She knows what kind of performer she is.

The scene further refines Chisato as **body-first rather than word-first**. She does not distrust language, but movement is where concentration becomes pleasure.

## Ren: language as cultivated form

Ren initially panics:

> 「わ…わたくしは無理です」
> 「何も知らないのですから」

She then produces an elegant tanka:

> 「秋あかね　歌にいざよう　葉月恋」
> 「想いはいまだ十六夜なり」

Her voice remains poised and ceremonially serious even though she has completely misunderstood the assignment. Ren’s comedy comes from the mismatch between her composure and her error.

She does not fail because she lacks language. She fails because she possesses too much inherited language. Asked to improvise street-derived rhythm, she retreats to classical literary form, layered wordplay, seasonal imagery, and an explanation of how 「いざよう」 relates to 「十六夜」.

Even when Keke protests that she was told to rhyme, Ren explains the mechanics of the poem as though the intellectual justification should resolve the disagreement.

This further establishes her early vocal identity:

> **Ren responds to uncertainty by increasing formality, structure, and explanation.**

She is most comfortable when expression has rules, lineage, and interpretable craft.

## Keke: enthusiasm exceeding her available Japanese form

Keke’s attempt shifts into Chinese. This recalls her Episode 1 introduction, where emotional intensity also exceeded the Japanese she could immediately organize.

Her voice is not deficient. It is overflowing. The group’s concern is practical: a Japanese competition audience may not follow the required section. But the moment reinforces that Keke’s first expressive instinct is not to restrain feeling for formal compatibility.

## Sumire: immediate performance authorship

Sumire’s improvised rap is qualitatively different:

> 「Hey Yo お見知りおきに自己紹介」
> 「でも結女のみんなにしとこうかい」
> 「私の名前は平安名すみれ」
> 「AB型の神社の娘」

Her diction becomes crisp, rhythmic, forward, and controlled. Unlike Kanon, she does not hesitate over public self-definition. Unlike Chisato, she does not abandon speech for movement. Unlike Ren, she does not need prior formal rules. Unlike Keke, she keeps the performance comprehensible to the immediate audience.

The ease in her voice confirms that her show-business history is not empty boasting. She has real adaptive stage competence.

Her statement afterward is accurate:

> 「これでも小さい頃からショウビジネスの世界で場数は踏んでいるの」
> 「アドリブだったら負けないわ」

Sumire’s particular talent is not that she surpasses every other member in a single measurable category. It is **performative synthesis**:

-   timing;

-   presentation;

-   improvisation;

-   camera awareness;

-   verbal agility;

-   dance competency;

-   vocal competency;

-   and the ability to construct a public image instantly.


That will become the episode’s argument for why she should be center.

# III. Sumire’s vocal duality: presenter and frightened candidate

The greatest contribution of the audio is the contrast between two Sumire voices.

## The presenter

This Sumire is lower, smoother, more deliberate, and rhythmically composed. It appears when she:

-   introduces herself;

-   improvises the rap;

-   invokes her show-business history;

-   dismisses the Love Live preliminary as beneath her;

-   says 「誰だと思ってるの」;

-   performs “ノンフィクション!!”


This voice projects the self she wants the world to accept.

## The candidate

This voice becomes higher, breathier, faster, and less syntactically stable. It appears when she:

-   learns she has been placed at center;

-   hears public criticism of her center performance;

-   asks whether the others chose her out of pity;

-   admits that she is always replaced;

-   receives Keke’s crown;

-   hears Keke call her by name.


The candidate voice belongs to the child performer who repeatedly waited to be selected and repeatedly saw the role go elsewhere.

Sumire’s theatricality is therefore not the opposite of sincerity. It is the form she uses to contain a much more vulnerable desire.

> **“Galaxy” Sumire is not fake.
> She is the voice Sumire built so the repeatedly unchosen girl could keep auditioning.**

---

# IV. Keke’s initial opposition: artistic severity, personal prejudice, and fear

Keke’s first response to Sumire as center is blunt:

> 「無理デス」

She argues that Sumire has lower potential than Kanon or Chisato and that Love Live’s first challenge is too important to entrust to her. Her Japanese becomes particularly clipped under hostility. The stylized 「デス」 endings, normally part of her enthusiastic charm, become percussive.

Keke’s objection has three layers.

## 1\. She is sincerely protective of Love Live

Keke treats Love Live as sacred. The word is not rhetorical for her. School idols transformed her sense of possibility, brought her to Japan, and created the context in which she met Kanon. Entrusting a crucial position is therefore an ethical judgment, not a casual production choice.

Her standards are harsh because the object matters enormously.

## 2\. She still distrusts Sumire’s motive

Keke has not fully forgiven Sumire for initially treating school idols as another show-business opportunity. Her hostility repeatedly takes the form:

> You care about the spotlight.
> I care about school idols.
> Therefore I cannot assume your ambition deserves the spotlight.

This is unfair, but it is not arbitrary.

## 3\. Sumire matters enough to threaten Keke’s judgment

Keke protests too much. Her attention to Sumire is already unusually intense. She watches her, judges her, notices her practice, designs for her body, and becomes emotionally invested in whether she lives up to the role.

Indifference would be quieter.

Keke’s severity is partly the inability to admit that Sumire has become important to her own school-idol dream.

---

# V. Kanon’s developing leadership voice

Kanon’s most important contribution is not the initial center decision itself. It is her interpretation of Sumire.

When Keke remains skeptical, Kanon explains:

> Sumire often says she wants to stand in front, but when a real opportunity appears, she passes it to Kanon, Chisato, or Keke.
> She probably lacks confidence because of what happened before.
> If the five of them genuinely want to win, every member must be capable of feeling like the center.
> If Sumire becomes serious, she may display a power no one else can match.

Kanon’s delivery is quiet and reflective rather than inspirational. She does not sentimentalize Sumire’s childhood failures. She gives a practical psychological reading.

This continues the leadership profile established in Episode 1:

> **Kanon detects the contradiction between what a person says she wants and what she permits herself to receive.**

In Episode 1, she could defend Keke more forcefully than she could defend her own dream. By Episode 10, that relational courage has matured into genuine interpretive leadership.

She recognizes:

-   Sumire’s demand for attention is real;

-   her avoidance of actual center responsibility is also real;

-   neither cancels the other;

-   giving her the center is not charity;

-   it is a developmental decision that the group needs.


Kanon’s phrase that everyone needs the feeling of being center also anticipates *Superstar!!*’s broader rejection of a group organized permanently around one irreplaceable protagonist.

Even in Season 1, Kanon is imagining Liella! as a group with distributable centrality.

---

# VI. Chisato and Ren diagnose Sumire from different directions

The later conversation about Sumire’s abilities further clarifies Chisato and Ren’s voices.

## Ren: balanced categorization

Ren says Sumire’s singing and dancing are both at a high level, but she may not rank first in any single category.

Her delivery is measured, complete, and evaluative. She sounds almost like a judge delivering a technical assessment. But unlike her institutional voice in Episode 1, the judgment is not exclusionary. She is trying to understand what kind of excellence Sumire possesses.

Ren’s thinking proceeds by categories:

-   singing: Kanon;

-   dancing: Chisato;

-   elegance: Ren;

-   brightness: Keke;

-   Sumire: competence across all of them.


Ren then reaches the painful conclusion:

> That breadth may be precisely why Sumire’s wishes have not been granted.

She is legible as highly capable but not easily marketable as *the single best example* of one quality.

## Chisato: holistic performer judgment

Chisato agrees with the category analysis but draws the opposite practical conclusion:

> 「だからこそ、すみれちゃんがセンターやるべきだと思う」
> “That is exactly why I think Sumire should be center.”

Chisato hears Sumire’s lack of a singular category not as absence, but as integrated stage capacity. Her dancer’s perception is holistic. She understands that center presence is not necessarily reducible to isolated vocal or choreographic superiority.

This is an important addition to Chisato’s performance philosophy:

> **The person who organizes the stage’s attention need not be the person who wins every technical subcategory.**

Sumire can hold the center because she knows how performance works as a total event.

---

# VII. Private rehearsal: the show-business boast becomes labor

The episode’s most important silent evidence is Sumire practicing alone.

She runs. She repeats choreography. She drills the rap. She performs vocal warmups:

> 「あ、え、い、う、え、お、あ、お」
> 「か、け、き、く、け、こ、か、こ」

This is not glamorous. It is professional maintenance.

The delivery is stripped of “Galaxy” theatricality. Her voice is rhythmic, repetitive, and task-oriented. She sounds like someone trained to understand that the performer’s public ease is produced by private repetition.

The accompanying memories all revolve around variations of:

> 「私が…」

She remembers wanting the role, expecting the role, and watching it go to someone else:

-   the village person is important too;

-   Sumire will play the protagonist’s friend;

-   the grand prize goes to someone else.


The episode does not portray her as a talentless narcissist imagining that effort guarantees entitlement. Instead, it shows a capable child whose repeated near-selection created a very specific wound:

> **She is good enough to remain in the industry, but never secure enough to believe the foreground will remain hers.**

That is more psychologically corrosive than simple rejection. Total failure might have encouraged her to leave. Repeated proximity keeps the desire alive while teaching her not to trust fulfillment.

The private rehearsal proves that her ambition is not merely verbal. She works hardest precisely when no audience is there to validate her.

---

# VIII. Keke’s costume: care translated into insult

Keke brings Sumire a specially modified center costume.

Her spoken language remains abrasive:

> 「いいから、とっとと着るデス」
> 「オーラのないあなたでもセンターで戦えるよう、他の人とは少し形を変えてやりマシタ」

The insult and the gift arrive together.

This is the beginning of Keke and Sumire’s stable care grammar:

-   affection is encoded as criticism;

-   recognition is expressed through labor;

-   vulnerability is displaced into craft;

-   gratitude is initially difficult for both.


Keke does not say:

> I believe in you.
> I spent hours considering how to make you look like Liella!’s center.
> I wanted the costume to reveal the presence I now think you possess.

She says the opposite on the surface: even someone with no aura needs special help.

But costume-making is authorship. Keke has observed Sumire’s proportions, performance style, movement, coloring, and role, then changed the design so the group’s visual grammar points toward her.

The clothes say what Keke cannot yet say.

When the others praise the costume, Keke’s delivery collapses into:

> 「ク…可可は別に」

The initial consonant catches. Her voice becomes smaller and less forceful. This is Keke’s version of embarrassment: the person whose feelings usually overflow becomes inarticulate when care is correctly identified as care.

---

# IX. Public feedback reactivates the original wound

The test video receives positive reactions to the song but repeated suggestions that Kanon or Ren should replace Sumire as center.

This sequence matters because the commenters are not malicious. They praise the performance. They think they are giving practical strategic advice.

That makes the wound more credible.

Sumire is not rejected as incompetent. She is told:

> The performance is good.
> You are good.
> But someone else would be more appropriate in the most visible position.

This is the exact pattern of her childhood.

Her immediate response is controlled:

> 「そんなの決まっているでしょ」
> 「この学校のスクールアイドルなんだから、みんなの意見に従うのが当然でしょ」

She sounds almost reasonable. Then she activates the show-business persona:

> She has walked through the professional entertainment world.
> A preliminary round of an amateur contest is beneath her.
> She will save her appearance for the final.

The content grows grander as the emotional truth becomes less sustainable.

The lower, dismissive register is a retreat strategy. If the role is beneath her, losing it cannot humiliate her.

Keke refuses the performance:

> 「何逃げようとしてるデスカ」

That word—**逃げる**, to run away—is exact. Sumire is not making a disinterested strategic decision. She is withdrawing before the group can officially repeat her childhood pattern.

Then the constructed voice breaks:

> 「無理よ」
> 「どうせ最後はいつも私じゃなくなるんだから」

The second line is the episode’s emotional key.

Her delivery is no longer polished. The volume rises, the phrasing tightens, and the grievance sounds younger than her usual public voice. The sentence uses 「いつも」—always. She is not speaking only about the video or Liella!. Every prior replacement is present.

Sumire’s real belief is:

> **Even when people initially select me, the choice will not survive comparison.**

---

# X. The overheard call: Sumire recognizes Keke’s deadline

Sumire overhears Keke speaking with her family and understands that Keke may have to return to Shanghai if Liella! cannot produce results.

The phone conversation is staged through distance, partial access, and Sumire’s expression. She learns something Keke has not entrusted to the group.

This changes Sumire’s apparent withdrawal. Her argument becomes:

> If winning is necessary for Keke to remain, then the group should choose the center with the highest public approval.

She weaponizes her own insecurity in the name of Keke’s future.

This is not yet healthy selflessness. It is Sumire accepting her oldest negative belief because it can now be framed as useful:

> I am replaceable.
> The group needs to win.
> Keke needs the group to win.
> Therefore letting myself be replaced is care.

That logic foreshadows her more destructive protective proposal in Season 2. Sumire’s care often takes the form of volunteering to occupy the disliked or sacrificial position.

It also reveals how closely she is already watching Keke.

---

# XI. The courtyard confrontation: pity versus trust

When Sumire returns, the group tells her that they have decided she should remain center.

Her first interpretation is pity:

> 「私がかわいそうだから？」
> 「頑張っているのにいつもセンターになれないから？」

The questions come quickly and sharply. She does not permit the others time to explain because she is trying to destroy the offer before she becomes dependent on it.

Sumire can accept:

-   competition;

-   criticism;

-   rejection;

-   challenge;

-   even hostility.


She cannot accept compassionate recognition if she suspects that the recognition is compensatory.

For her, pity would mean:

> We agree that you are not the best, but we are giving you the role because your history is sad.

That would turn her greatest desire into humiliation.

Keke answers with the sentence Sumire needs:

> 「可可は同情なんかで衣装を作ったりはしません」

Keke’s delivery is forceful, but unlike her earlier criticism, it is not scattered or petulant. Her voice becomes concentrated. She is defending the seriousness of her own judgment.

The argument escalates because Sumire still cannot trust it. She threatens to quit rather than accept the role under false premises. Keke accuses her of betraying her own school-idol desire. Sumire finally reveals that she heard the phone call:

> 「あんた、絶対勝たなきゃいけないんでしょ」

The abrasive 「あんた」 carries concern without gentleness. Sumire cannot say, “I am afraid you will be taken away.”

Keke’s reply is crucial:

> The need to win is precisely why Sumire should be center.

Sumire keeps producing alternate explanations:

-   Kanon recommended her;

-   Keke reluctantly followed;

-   Keke saw her practicing and felt sorry for her;

-   Keke became stubborn;

-   Keke created the costume because she had already committed.


Sumire is not simply arguing. She is exhausting every explanation except genuine artistic trust.

Keke finally states it directly:

> 「可可があなたに任せたのは、あなたがふさわしいと思ったからデス」
> 「練習を見て、その歌声を聴いて、Liella!のセンターにふさわしいと思ったからデス」
> 「それだけの力があなたにはあると思ったからデス」

This is recognition in Keke’s own highest moral vocabulary.

Keke does not say Sumire deserves a chance because of effort. She says she evaluated the effort and performance and concluded that Sumire has the power to represent Liella!.

Because Keke treats Love Live as sacred, this judgment cannot be casual.

That is why Keke is the person whose recognition Sumire can eventually accept. Praise from Kanon might be interpreted as kindness. Praise from Chisato might be interpreted as group support. Keke has spent most of the episode insisting that Love Live cannot be entrusted lightly.

When *that* judge selects Sumire, the selection acquires weight.

---

# XII. 「想いの全て」: costume as confession

Keke extends the crown and says:

> 「だから受け取りなさい」
> 「私が想いの全てを込めて」
> 「あなたのために作ったのですから」

The phrase 「想いの全て」—all of my feelings—is strikingly intimate.

Within the literal context, it refers to Keke’s commitment to Love Live, Liella!, the costume, and Sumire’s center performance. But the direction and delivery make the emotional surplus impossible to miss.

Keke has placed:

-   her artistic judgment;

-   her competitive hope;

-   her fear of returning home;

-   her school-idol devotion;

-   her labor;

-   and her newly acknowledged trust in Sumire


inside the object she is offering.

The crown is not a consolation prize. It is a materialized vote of confidence from the person most reluctant to give it.

Kanon then appears with the complete costume and repeats the group’s choice:

> 「センターのあなたのために」

The plural group decision matters, but Keke’s individual testimony is what makes it emotionally receivable.

---

# XIII. The chase and fountain: intimacy through physical farce

Sumire cannot simply take the crown and say thank you. She grabs it and runs. Keke chases her.

The scene returns their relationship to comedy just as it risks becoming too emotionally explicit. But the comedy does not erase the confession. It gives both girls a familiar language in which to survive it.

Sumire says:

> 「届いて…届いて」

Her private wish is finally voiced without defensive argument. She wants the performance, song, and herself to reach others.

Keke tackles her into the bushes while trying to recover the crown. Their physical closeness is chaotic rather than sentimental, fitting their relationship perfectly.

Then Keke calls:

> 「すみれ」

No surname. No 「この人」. No contemptuous distance. No honorific.

Sumire immediately notices:

> 「初めて名前呼んだわね」

This response proves how carefully she has tracked Keke’s language toward her. Beneath the bickering, forms of address matter enormously.

Keke tries to dismiss the significance:

> 「そんなことはどうでもいいデス」

It is plainly not unimportant. The denial is the point.

Keke then returns to the safe language of performance standards:

> 「Liella!のセンターとして、恥ずかしくないステージにしてクダサイ」

Sumire can answer in her restored public voice:

> 「当然でしょ」
> 「誰だと思ってるの」

The final boast is no longer pure compensation. It has been authorized by trust.

# XIV. “ノンフィクション!!” as Sumire’s performance argument

The song is exceptionally well chosen for Sumire.

Its lyrical voice says:

> I want to smile more.
> I want to be more honest.
> I want to shine now.
> I will prove that nothing is impossible.
> I will break your poker face.
> I will captivate you.
> This is not fantasy; it is nonfiction.

This is Sumire’s character architecture in song.

## Performance rather than confession

Sumire cannot ordinarily say:

> I want to be seen.
> I am afraid my desire is ridiculous.
> I need your recognition.
> I want to prove that the role can really remain mine.

The song transforms those admissions into theatrical promises directed at the audience.

Her singing voice is brighter and more forward than the vulnerable speaking voice heard earlier. The stage does not erase her insecurity; it gives the insecurity usable form.

The line:

> 「証明してあげるわ」
> “I’ll prove it to you”

is particularly Sumire-like. She does not ask the audience to reassure her. She converts the need for reassurance into a challenge.

## The center as orchestration of attention

The staging does not merely place Sumire in front continuously. It repeatedly lets the others frame, answer, flank, and redirect attention toward her.

That fulfills Kanon’s “everyone as center” idea. Sumire’s centerhood does not reduce the others to backing dancers. The group makes her visible, and her show-business presence organizes their collective energy.

The costume’s modified structure works similarly. Sumire is differentiated, but recognizably part of the same visual system.

## The visual world as Sumire’s authored fantasy made real

The performance uses amusement-park, card, crown, purple, gold, and theatrical illusion imagery. Sumire’s self-branding has often sounded grander than the material world around her. Here the group temporarily constructs a world large enough for her voice.

That is why “nonfiction” matters.

The stage is fantastical, but the feeling is real. The person she has insisted exists—the captivating center, the professional entertainer, the girl who can make the audience follow her—is no longer merely a defensive claim.

For the duration of the song, the performance proves it.

---

# XV. The post-performance exchange: gratitude becomes name

After the song, Sumire says:

> 「ありがとう、可可」

The delivery is simple. She does not decorate the gratitude with show-business language or hide it inside a joke.

Keke replies:

> 「すみれ」

The name exchange is immediately disrupted by the others piling onto them, preserving the show’s comic ensemble tone. But the relational threshold has been crossed.

Before this episode, Keke and Sumire largely relate through categories:

-   opportunist;

-   fanatic;

-   show-business girl;

-   school-idol obsessive;

-   irritating rival;

-   “this person.”


Afterward, they increasingly relate as specific people whose vulnerabilities the other understands.

Their speech remains hostile. Their bond does not become conventionally gentle. But the hostility is now carried by trust.

# XVI. Cumulative voice profile: Heanna Sumire

Episode 10 substantially revises and deepens the provisional profile from Episode 1.

## Established vocal modes

### The public-show-business voice

Controlled, relatively low-set, rhythmically intentional, and often decorated by confident feminine endings such as 「〜わ」「〜の」「〜でしょ」.

It is used for:

-   self-presentation;

-   boasts;

-   technical performance claims;

-   dismissal of criticism;

-   the “Galaxy” identity;

-   stage command.


This voice is authored, but not false. It is Sumire’s professionally constructed mode of agency.

### The improviser

Fast, crisp, rhythmically agile, and verbally inventive. Her spontaneous rap proves real performance intelligence. She can read a task and produce presentable material immediately.

### The private worker

Repetitive, disciplined, stripped of theatrical affect. Her warmups and rehearsal voice reveal professional habit beneath the boasting.

### The threatened candidate

Higher, less stable, fragmented, and emotionally younger. It appears when:

-   she is genuinely chosen;

-   she expects replacement;

-   she suspects pity;

-   she receives direct recognition.


### The defensive aristocrat of show business

Lower and colder than the ordinary public voice. It appears when she calls Love Live an amateur competition or claims the preliminary is beneath her.

This is not confidence. It is strategic devaluation.

### The accepted center

Bright, expansive, playful, and more secure. Her final 「誰だと思ってるの」 resembles earlier boasts, but now rests on a real group decision rather than self-generated compensation.

## Personality refinement

Sumire is not simply attention-seeking. She is **selection-sensitive**.

She needs to be chosen, but previous experience has taught her that choice is provisional. Consequently, she may sabotage the offer, reinterpret it as pity, or pretend she never valued it.

Her ambition and fear are therefore the same structure viewed from opposite directions:

> She demands visibility because she has repeatedly been denied it.
> She distrusts visibility because she has repeatedly lost it.

## Performance philosophy

Sumire treats performance as:

-   craft;

-   adaptability;

-   total presentation;

-   command of audience attention;

-   professional labor;

-   proof that a role can become real through enactment.


Her mature idolhood will not require abandoning theatricality. It requires allowing theatricality to communicate rather than conceal desire.

## Updated core thesis

> **Sumire is the performer who can make any role look convincing except the role of a girl who believes she has truly been chosen. Episode 10 begins teaching her that recognition can be trust rather than pity.**

---

# XVII. Cumulative voice profile: Tang Keke

Episode 10 adds a much sharper side of Keke than Episode 1.

## Conviction can become severity

Keke’s emotional openness is not always warm. Because school idols are sacred to her, enthusiasm readily becomes judgment. Her clipped 「デス」 endings can sound accusatory and absolute when she believes someone is treating the dream lightly.

## Craft is one of her deepest care languages

Keke’s costume work reveals that her love is not only verbal enthusiasm. She observes, designs, modifies, and labors privately. Clothing allows her to communicate judgments she cannot speak cleanly.

She gives Sumire a distinct center silhouette before she can admit that she sees Sumire as center.

## Embarrassment reverses her normal energy

Keke is usually fast and expansive. When her care is recognized, she stammers and contracts:

> 「ク…可可は別に」

This is an important contrast. She is not embarrassed by loving school idols publicly. She is embarrassed by being seen loving a particular person.

## She needs moral certainty before giving recognition

Keke’s trust carries unusual force because she does not offer it cheaply. She argues against Sumire, observes her training, evaluates her voice, and only then concludes that Sumire is suitable.

This can make Keke cruel, but it also means her eventual praise feels earned.

## Forms of address as emotional index

Calling her 「すみれ」 is a major vocal event. Keke’s hostility has crossed into personal attachment. The subsequent denial only confirms the significance.

## Updated core thesis

> **Keke’s voice is faith sharpened into judgment. When she finally says that Sumire is worthy, the statement functions as both artistic verdict and reluctant confession of attachment.**

---

# XVIII. Cumulative voice profile: Shibuya Kanon

Episode 10 shows how far Kanon has developed since the premiere.

## She is no longer merely the person whose voice needs rescue

Kanon now uses voice to interpret and develop others. Her strongest dialogue is not a speech to Sumire, but a quiet explanation to Keke of why Sumire avoids the center and why the group needs her to accept it.

## Her leadership remains non-theatrical

Kanon does not dominate conversations through volume or authority. She uses:

-   careful observation;

-   ordinary language;

-   low-pressure questions;

-   practical explanations;

-   confidence in another person’s latent ability.


She makes the consequential decision—Sumire should be center—sound natural rather than heroic.

## Her own weakness becomes empathy rather than identity

When Keke argues that Sumire’s past proves she is unsuitable, Kanon replies:

> 「それ言ったら私だって歌えなかったよ」

Kanon can now use her wound as evidence against determinism. She no longer treats the failed past as an authoritative prediction of the future.

That is substantial growth from Episode 1.

## She already practices distributed leadership

Her statement that all five need the feeling of being center anticipates the mature Kanon who eventually creates space for Liella! to exist without depending completely on her.

## Updated core thesis

> **Kanon’s recovered voice is becoming an interpretive instrument: she now identifies where another person’s past has been mistaken for destiny and creates a role that lets the person contradict it.**

# XIX. Cumulative voice profile: Arashi Chisato

## Body-first expressiveness is confirmed

Her rap turns into dance because physical movement is not simply a technical specialty; it is where attention and joy naturally converge.

## She evaluates whole-stage competence

Chisato understands why Sumire should center despite not being the strongest singer or dancer. She perceives integrated performance capacity rather than ranking isolated traits.

## Her soft voice can deliver firm artistic judgment

When she says Sumire should be center, the tone remains gentle, but the conclusion is not tentative. Chisato’s softness should not be confused with uncertainty.

## Updated core thesis

> **Chisato hears performance through the body and the total stage. She recognizes Sumire as a center because she understands that presence is an arrangement of many competencies, not a single ranked statistic.**

# XX. Cumulative voice profile: Hazuki Ren

## Her cultivated register remains central to her comedy

The rap exercise shows how completely Ren inhabits a literary, refined, rule-seeking vocal world. Even playful improvisation becomes classical poetry and technical explanation.

## Her judgment is analytic rather than instinctive

Ren identifies categories, compares abilities, and constructs a balanced description of Sumire. This is her growing form of group usefulness: the institutional evaluator becomes an internal source of fair assessment.

## She has become less exclusionary

In Episode 1, her formal voice announced why school idols did not belong. Here the same composure helps her articulate why Sumire does belong at a high performance level.

The vocal system has not changed; its ethical application has.

## Updated core thesis

> **Ren continues to translate uncertainty into cultivated form, but she is learning to use structure for recognition rather than prohibition.**

# XXI. Keke and Sumire: the birth of hostile intimacy

Episode 10 establishes nearly the full grammar of their relationship.

They understand one another through antagonism because straightforward softness would be intolerable to both.

Keke fears that Sumire’s ambition lacks sacred sincerity.
Sumire fears that Keke’s idealism lacks practical recognition of her labor.

Keke insults while creating a costume.
Sumire mocks while protecting Keke’s future.
Keke chases Sumire to force trust upon her.
Sumire runs because accepting the trust matters too much.
Keke calls Sumire by name and immediately denies its significance.
Sumire thanks Keke plainly only after proving herself onstage.

Their attachment is not built by discovering that they were secretly identical. It is built by each girl recognizing that the other possesses something she needs.

Sumire needs a judge whose recognition cannot be confused with pity.

Keke needs a performer whose worldly ambition can carry school-idol sincerity into actual stage command.

The center decision binds those needs together.

# XXII. What this episode changes in the larger interpretation

## Sumire’s vanity is more wounded than narcissistic

Her central anxiety is not that others fail to praise her enough. It is that praise and selection will be withdrawn once comparison begins.

## Keke’s hostility is already a form of attachment

She pays extraordinary attention to Sumire because Sumire’s relationship to school idols matters personally to her. Costume labor and direct artistic judgment reveal care long before her language becomes friendly.

## Liella!’s center philosophy is distributive from its first season

Kanon’s “everyone must feel like center” idea is not empty ensemble rhetoric. The group tests it by allowing its most insecure candidate to hold actual central responsibility.

## Recognition must be specific

Generic reassurance would not resolve Sumire’s wound. Keke gives specific evidence:

-   she saw the practice;

-   she heard the voice;

-   she evaluated the performance;

-   she made the costume;

-   she believes Sumire possesses the necessary power.


That is why the recognition works.

## Art can make a defensive persona truthful

“Galaxy” is initially partly armor. “ノンフィクション!!” does not strip that armor away. It creates conditions in which the grand theatrical persona expresses something real.

Sumire does not become authentic by becoming plain.

She becomes authentic by receiving a stage on which theatricality and desire finally align.

---

# Final synthesis

Episode 10 is not a story in which Sumire learns humility.

It is almost the opposite.

Sumire already knows how easily she can be displaced. She already knows other members surpass her in individual categories. She already knows audiences may prefer someone else. Beneath the boasts, she has internalized too much humility of the destructive kind: the expectation that when the consequential choice arrives, she should step aside.

The episode teaches her something harder:

> **She may be allowed to believe that she is worthy of the position she wants.**

Kanon identifies the wound.
Chisato recognizes the integrated performer.
Ren articulates the technical case.
Keke makes the judgment emotionally binding.
Sumire turns that judgment into performance.

The episode’s center is therefore not merely the center of a formation. It is the place where trust becomes visible.

Sumire’s arc can be condensed into three vocal moments:

> 「私がセンターでいいったらいいの」
> bravado unable to believe the offer;

> 「どうせ最後はいつも私じゃなくなるんだから」
> the child performer’s actual expectation;

> 「当然でしょ。誰だと思ってるの」
> the familiar boast, now supported by people who have deliberately chosen her.

The words at the end resemble the old Sumire.

The voice means something new.
