There is another unsubtitled **「ふん」 at approximately 11:21.5–11:22**, after Kanon says:

The progression within that conversation is therefore:

> avoid Kanon
> → grudgingly listen
> → defend hierarchy
> → encounter an incomprehensible relational theory of song
> → huff
> → verbally dismiss
> → leave.

That second instance should be added to the running Wien vocalization log.

# I. Wien’s 「ふん」 audit in Season 3, Episode 2

The complete audio reveals at least **two strong unsubtitled examples**, plus one more ambiguous exhalation that I would not yet count definitively.

## Approximately 10:38.7 — compliment-deflection 「ふん」

The sequence is:

> Kanon: 「いい、すっごいかわいい」
> “Good—really cute.”

> Wien: 「本当？」
> “Really?”

Then comes a brief unsubtitled **「ふん」**, followed by:

> 「かのんがうるさいから、アイドルっぽくしてみただけ」
> “I only tried acting idol-like because you were being so annoying.”

This is one of the clearest examples yet of the huff functioning as a **tsun transition**.

Wien’s spontaneous 「本当？」 escapes before her defensive system catches up. For a moment she simply wants to know whether Kanon genuinely thinks she is cute. The huff then repairs the exposure, allowing her to reframe the behavior as reluctant compliance rather than pleasure in Kanon’s praise.

The structure is:

> involuntary receptivity
> → realization that she sounded pleased
> → 「ふん」
> → retroactive denial.

This is not contempt. It is embarrassment management.

## Approximately 11:55.8–11:56.1 — processing/closure 「ふん」

Kanon has explained that school-idol performance needs all of its elements because the audience comes wanting to smile, become happier, take one another’s hands, and enjoy the event together.

Wien summarizes:

> 「だから、全部必要だと？」
> “So you’re saying all of it is necessary?”

Kanon answers:

> 「そうそう」
> “Exactly.”

A short unsubtitled huff follows before the scene moves on.

This huff is quieter and less combative. Wien has not fully accepted Kanon’s philosophy, but she has processed enough of it to summarize it accurately. The sound functions as:

> “I hear what you are saying.”
> “I am not going to admit that it makes sense.”
> “This conversation is over before you interpret my understanding as agreement.”

It is a **reluctant-comprehension 「ふん」**.

## Updated huff taxonomy

Wien’s 「ふん」 is not one interchangeable catchphrase. It already has several functions:

The common grammar is:

> **Wien has been affected.
> She refuses to give the other person clean verbal access to that fact.
> The huff seals the leak.**

This episode is particularly important because the sound is shifting away from pure superiority. It is becoming the audible punctuation of attachment.

# II. Core thesis of the episode

Season 3, Episode 2 is about **recovering the non-instrumental beginning of art**.

All three members of the temporary group enter with distorted relationships to performance:

-   **Wien** treats song as proof of talent, rank, and belonging.

-   **Tomari** treats activity as something whose utility, profit, and future return must be demonstrated.

-   **Kanon** understands performance relationally, but must build a viable group with two people who distrust that philosophy for different reasons.


The episode’s central movement is:

> Wien remembers that she originally loved song because it transformed loneliness into warmth.

> Tomari witnesses people choosing to perform even when success has become mathematically impossible.

> Kanon demonstrates that song can possess value before victory, profit, qualification, or institutional recognition.

The episode therefore asks:

> **What remains worth doing when the desired result is no longer available?**

Its answer is not abstractly “follow your dreams.”

It is:

> The act itself may create connection, joy, courage, and a new beginning—even when it cannot secure the measurable outcome for which it was undertaken.

That is why the climax requires the trio to sing **after** the one goal attached to the live has effectively disappeared.

# III. The opening dispute: Kanon enters a club that Wien thinks belongs to her

Kanon submits an application to Wien’s newly formed school-idol club.

Wien’s response is immediate:

> 「私、認めないから」
> “I’m not accepting this.”

Kanon points to the recruitment notice:

> 「誰でも歓迎」
> “Everyone welcome.”

> 「学年、経験、問いません」
> “No restrictions on year or experience.”

The comedy is legally and psychologically precise. Wien has created the formal appearance of an open club but imagines it as an extension of herself. Kanon treats the language literally.

Wien answers:

> 「当然でしょ。Liella!は私にとって敵なのよ」
> “Of course. Liella! is my enemy.”

Then imagines Kanon as a spy who might secretly send information to Liella!.

Her use of **敵 / enemy** is revealing. She does not say “rival.” A rival can sharpen and coexist with you. An enemy threatens identity and future.

Wien still interprets Kanon through Season 2’s zero-sum structure:

-   Kanon defeated her;

-   Kanon received the Vienna offer;

-   Kanon possesses the relational quality her family wants Wien to learn;

-   therefore Kanon’s presence inside Wien’s group risks annexation or humiliation.


Kanon’s desired relationship is entirely different. She wants:

> two clubs that challenge one another;
> a place where she can sing beside Wien without dissolving either group;
> a new structure rather than a conquest.

This is the first major conflict of the episode:

> **Kanon treats boundaries as places across which relation can be built.
> Wien treats boundaries as defenses against losing authorship.**

# IV. Tomari’s entrance: emotional conflict translated into contract law

Tomari intervenes:

> 「契約において、後から書き足した事項は通常無効とされます」
> “In a contract, provisions added afterward are normally considered invalid.”

She concludes that Kanon has the stronger claim because the posted recruitment conditions did not exclude Liella! members.

This is an extremely efficient vocal introduction.

Tomari’s voice is:

-   controlled;

-   evenly paced;

-   relatively low in emotional volatility;

-   formally constructed;

-   oriented toward rules, efficiency, and objective adjudication.


She does not ask what Kanon or Wien feels. She identifies:

-   an offer;

-   stated terms;

-   a later attempt to alter those terms;

-   the legally stronger position.


Her language makes an emotionally chaotic interaction administratively legible.

Wien and Kanon then discover that neither of them knows who Tomari is. Tomari calmly states that she wants to examine school-idol activity and intends to submit an application.

Her:

> 「私のことはお構いなく」
> “Please pay me no mind.”

is comically impossible. She has entered a two-person conflict, adjudicated it, and joined the club. Her attempt to classify herself as irrelevant reveals one of her defining contradictions:

> **Tomari insists on being an observer even while her actions repeatedly alter what she observes.**

# V. Tomari’s voice: formality without Ren’s institutional warmth

Tomari’s speech can superficially resemble Ren’s because both often use complete formal constructions. But their vocal functions are different.

## Ren

Ren’s formality comes from:

-   upbringing;

-   institutional responsibility;

-   social decorum;

-   belief in inherited structures.


Even when severe, Ren sounds like someone occupying a recognized office.

## Tomari

Tomari’s formality comes from:

-   analytic distance;

-   risk control;

-   reluctance to expose personal motivation;

-   preference for statements that can be justified impersonally.


She sounds less like an institution’s representative and more like an auditor.

Her recurrent phrases include:

-   「お構いなく」

-   「必要と判断すれば」

-   「無駄な時間は過ごしたくない」

-   「現在収支計算を行っています」

-   「不可能と判断されます」

-   「したがって」


The connectors matter. Tomari’s speech constantly produces logical chains:

> condition
> → calculation
> → conclusion
> → recommended action.

Her defense is not emotional coldness in the simple sense. It is **proceduralization**. By converting feeling into analysis, she can act without admitting what the issue means to her personally.

# VI. Liella!’s reaction: Keke experiences Kanon’s choice as betrayal

Keke is furious that Kanon has joined the rival club:

> 「不満デス」
> “I am dissatisfied.”

She asks why everyone permitted it and insists Kanon has been deceived.

The line:

> 「Liella!は敵、ルラルラルララ」
> “Liella! is the enemy…”

becomes a comic imagined mind-control chant. Natsumi immediately sees viral potential in the scenario.

Keke’s exaggeration is comic, but the emotional basis is serious.

Keke was the person who recovered Kanon’s voice and brought her into school idols. Kanon choosing to sing in another formation activates a possessive fear beneath Keke’s usual faith:

> Is the voice that founded Liella! still ours?

Sumire answers with practical confidence:

> Kanon may be extremely kind, but she is not as simple as Keke.

Chisato gives the deeper interpretation:

> Kanon said she wanted Liella! and Wien’s club to challenge one another.
> Chisato understands that feeling.

Her voice remains calm because she recognizes continuity rather than abandonment. Kanon has not rejected Liella!. She is attempting to create the productive external relationship the group could not have while Wien remained only a solitary enemy.

Shiki observes:

> 「千砂都さんは一貫してますね」
> “Chisato-san is consistent.”

That is exactly right.

Chisato’s mature care repeatedly refuses possession:

-   Kanon may sing alone.

-   Kanon may study abroad.

-   Kanon may join another club temporarily.

-   Kanon’s growth is not betrayal merely because it creates distance.

# VII. Separate practice spaces: rivalry without forced coexistence

Kanon and Chisato arrange for the two clubs to practice separately so they do not repeatedly collide.

The decision is relationally intelligent. Kanon does not demand that Wien prove maturity by immediately rehearsing beside Liella!. She acknowledges that rivalry requires space.

She then introduces a festival opportunity. Wien initially assumes the goal is to enter the same event as Liella! and prove herself superior:

> 「格が上だってところを見せつけてやろうって話ね」
> “So we show everyone that we are the higher-class group.”

Kanon says no. Only one group from the school is normally invited. Their club must first earn the remaining guest slot through a remote rookie live and receive at least ten thousand positive evaluations.

Wien’s anger is understandable:

> She won the event last year.
> She defeated Liella! there.
> She overwhelmed other school idols.
> Yet she is neither invited nor automatically recognized.

The episode does not present her grievance as wholly irrational. Institutions do not preserve prestige indefinitely. Her past excellence has not transferred automatically into legitimacy for a new group.

Wien wants accumulated achievement to function as capital.

The festival instead demands that the new formation produce a new relationship with an audience.

---

# VIII. “The purpose is for the local audience to have fun”

Kanon explains that the festival’s main goal is:

> 「地元のお客さんに楽しんでもらうこと」
> “To let the local audience enjoy themselves.”

This sentence redirects Wien away from rank.

The event is not principally designed to determine the objectively greatest singer. It exists to create an experience for people.

Tomari interprets the statement economically:

> performers receive benefit because audiences watch them.

Kanon corrects her:

> no money is being collected;
> nothing is being sold;
> the activity produces no direct profit.

Tomari then confirms the conclusion she came to test:

> school idols continue despite receiving no profit and no useful future qualification.

Her tone contains neither admiration nor mockery at first. She is verifying an apparently irrational phenomenon.

This is the episode’s second major philosophical conflict:

> **Wien asks whether the activity proves superiority.
> Tomari asks whether it produces utility.
> Kanon asks whether it makes people happy.**

# IX. Wien’s anger at Tomari: she recognizes the devaluation immediately

Tomari’s detached questioning irritates Wien:

> 「いいかげんにして」
> “Enough.”

> 「あんたは何なの。何が目的なの」
> “What are you? What is your objective?”

Wien’s anger is partly territorial, but it also reveals that she is more artistically invested than Tomari.

Wien may criticize school idols, yet she recognizes that Tomari is evaluating music as an inefficient allocation of time. To Wien, this is a more fundamental offense than Kanon’s relational philosophy.

Wien believes art should establish value.

Tomari questions whether the entire practice has value at all.

Tomari answers only:

> 「お構いなく」
> “Pay me no mind.”

The phrase infuriates Wien because Tomari is simultaneously:

-   evaluating her;

-   withholding her own motive;

-   refusing reciprocal exposure.


Wien wants to confront a person.

Tomari keeps offering a process.

---

# X. Tomari’s physical ability and selective participation

Tomari says she will join practice only if she judges it necessary and does not want to waste time.

She then demonstrates unexpectedly strong core stability despite having done little of the normal training.

Her calm:

> 「体幹には自信があります」
> “I am confident in my core strength.”

contrasts with the others’ surprise.

Tomari’s competence is important because the episode does not make her utilitarianism the result of laziness or incapacity. She is physically disciplined and capable of following a technical demand.

Her problem is one of valuation:

> What goal justifies the labor?

She can work intensely when the causal relation between effort and outcome is legible. School idols trouble her because the output is emotional, shared, temporary, and difficult to price.

# XI. The revelation that Tomari is Natsumi’s sister

Tomari submits her formal application:

> 「鬼塚冬毬」

Natsumi’s response disintegrates:

> 「ととと……冬毬？」
> “T-T-Tomari?”

This is one of Natsumi’s strongest persona breaks since the bear sequence.

Her OniNatsu voice is normally:

-   high;

-   fast;

-   branded;

-   rhetorically prepared.


Here the family name and given name interrupt the brand before she can construct a response.

Natsumi then attempts an obviously absurd denial:

> perhaps they merely share a surname;
> it is a tremendous Oni-coincidence;
> she knows nothing.

Shiki states flatly:

> 「夏美ちゃんは嘘つき」
> “Natsumi-chan is a liar.”

The contrast is perfect. Natsumi creates language to obscure. Shiki removes every unnecessary word.

# XII. Tomari’s stated motive: verify the promise that changed Natsumi

Tomari explains that Natsumi once told her:

> school idols offer a special dream more important than earning money or preparing for the future.

Tomari wants to determine whether school-idol activity really is what Natsumi claims.

This is crucial. Tomari is not investigating a random cultural practice.

She is investigating a change in her sister.

Natsumi used to share Tomari’s worldview:

-   future security;

-   money;

-   measurable return;

-   avoidance of reckless dreams.


School idols altered Natsumi’s priorities.

Tomari therefore treats the activity as a possible source of harm, irrationality, or self-deception.

She joins the group without Natsumi because she wants a control condition:

> examine school idols without the sister whose testimony is under review.

That is analytically coherent and emotionally revealing. Tomari distrusts Natsumi’s conclusion because she cares about its consequences for Natsumi.

# XIII. Natsumi’s voice when Tomari appears

Natsumi tries to stop Tomari’s explanation:

> 「冬毬、もういい」
> “Tomari, that’s enough.”

Her delivery drops some of the OniNatsu theatricality. It sounds less like a creator managing a public segment and more like an older sister trying to prevent exposure.

After Tomari leaves, Natsumi explains:

> Tomari is even more obsessed with money than she is;
> Tomari thinks activities without profit should not be pursued;
> she once supported Natsumi’s video work;
> since Natsumi became a school idol, Tomari has looked down on her.

Natsumi’s usual comic term **マニー** remains, but the emotional register is different. Her voice is lower and less playful. The branding vocabulary can no longer fully disguise family shame.

The key line is:

> 「私を軽蔑しているんですの」
> “She despises me.”

That is Natsumi’s interpretation—not necessarily the whole truth.

Tomari’s later statement reveals fear rather than simple contempt:

> 「スクールアイドルは、きっと姉者を傷つける」
> “School idols will surely hurt my sister.”

The sisters misread each other symmetrically.

Natsumi hears:

> You are foolish for doing something unprofitable.

Tomari means:

> Dreams hurt you before, and I do not trust this new one not to hurt you again.

# XIV. Wien tries to “act idol-like”

During rehearsal, Kanon praises Wien:

> 「いい、すっごいかわいい」
> “Good—really cute.”

Wien spontaneously asks:

> 「本当？」
> “Really?”

The upward, receptive delivery is striking. There is none of her usual superiority in it. She sounds like someone who genuinely wants confirmation.

Then comes the unsubtitled 「ふん」 and the defense:

> 「かのんがうるさいから、アイドルっぽくしてみただけ」
> “I only tried acting idol-like because you kept pestering me.”

The scene clarifies that Wien is not incapable of cute presentation. She is uncomfortable allowing the presentation to be interpreted as personal enjoyment.

She then says:

> 「本番はクールに決めるわよ」
> “I’m doing it cool in the actual performance.”

Her attachment to “cool” is partly aesthetic, but also defensive. Coolness preserves distance and control. Cuteness invites the audience—and Kanon—to read vulnerability.

The huff is the hinge between those states.

# XV. Why Wien dislikes the multiplicity of school-idol performance

Wien asks why they must do so many things:

-   singing;

-   dancing;

-   costumes;

-   audience presentation.


Her previous artistic model isolates song as the principal object. Everything else can appear ornamental or dilutive.

Kanon answers:

> It is wonderful simply to feel that something is enjoyable and loved.

Then she asks:

> 「マルガレーテちゃんは、どうして歌が好きになったの？」
> “Why did you come to love singing?”

Wien begins with family genealogy:

> her family has been musical for generations.

Kanon interrupts:

> not the institutional explanation;
> the pure moment when her heart first sparkled.

This is a highly characteristic Kanon intervention. She distinguishes:

-   inherited identity;

-   professional destination;

-   social expectation;


from:

-   the first experience that made the art emotionally necessary.


Wien has learned to narrate music through family and academy. Kanon asks for the pre-hierarchical origin.

# XVI. Wien’s childhood memory: song as warmth during loneliness

Wien hesitates:

> 「そんなの……」
> “Something like that…”

Then tells the story.

Her family was away for approximately a week. She was lonely and crying. A song played. Although she should still have been lonely, she became warm and regained energy.

This is the most important new fact about Wien’s artistic identity.

Her original relation to music was not:

-   outperform others;

-   enter Vienna;

-   stand beside her sister;

-   prove family legitimacy.


It was:

> **sound entered loneliness and changed its emotional temperature.**

That is almost identical to the function Kanon believes school-idol performance should have for an audience.

Wien’s mature philosophy has become estranged from her own first encounter with song.

She now treats singing as proof.

The child experienced it as companionship.

The softness of her delivery matters. Her pace slows, the declarative sharpness recedes, and she allows causal ambiguity:

> she does not fully explain why it made her warm;
> she simply reports that it did.

For someone normally obsessed with standards, this is an unusually non-analytic memory.

# XVII. Kanon connects Wien’s origin to school-idol performance

Kanon asks:

> 「幸せな気持ちになれるのが、歌の魅力だと思わない？」
> “Don’t you think the appeal of song is that it can make someone feel happy?”

She explains that people come to school-idol lives wanting to smile. Therefore performers think about:

-   how to make them happier;

-   how to take one another’s hands;

-   how to enjoy the event together.


Wien summarizes:

> 「だから、全部必要だと？」
> “So all of it is necessary?”

The multiplicity she previously regarded as dilution now receives a coherent purpose.

Dance, costume, gesture, audience address, group synchrony, and staging are not distractions from song. They are tools for recreating the emotional transformation Wien once received alone.

Kanon’s argument is not:

> school idols are technically more complex.

It is:

> **school idols deliberately build the conditions in which warmth can pass between people.**

The likely 「ふん」 after Kanon’s confirmation indicates that Wien has understood more than she wishes to admit.

# XVIII. Cohabitation: Wien’s defensive voice enters domestic space

Kanon discovers that Wien has moved into the Shibuya family home while her father is abroad.

Wien has already helped at the café and says:

> 「世話になる以上、相手が誰であろうと、お返しするのは私のモットー」
> “If I am being cared for, repaying the other person—whoever it is—is my principle.”

This adds a significant trait.

Wien does not want to owe people.

Debt creates asymmetry and dependency. She therefore repays practical support quickly so that emotional access cannot be claimed through obligation.

Kanon calls it surprising.

Wien sharply replies:

> 「なに」
> “What?”

Then warns:

> she did not come to become friends;
> Kanon must not misunderstand.

Kanon’s casual:

> 「はーい」
> “Okay.”

defuses the declaration without honoring its emotional pretense.

Wien’s domestic tsun structure is becoming established:

> perform care
> → insist it has no relational meaning
> → become annoyed when Kanon refuses to take the denial seriously.

# XIX. Childhood in Japan and the shadow of Wien’s sister

Wien explains that she lived in Japan when she was young and moved to Vienna when her older sister advanced in her education.

Kanon says:

> 「お姉ちゃん、すごい人だもんね」
> “Your sister is amazing.”

Wien becomes silent.

Kanon’s silence in response is careful rather than pitying, but Wien immediately attacks it:

> 「黙らないでよ」
> “Don’t go quiet.”

> 「みじめな気持ちにさせたいの？」
> “Are you trying to make me feel miserable?”

This confirms that pity remains her most sensitive trigger.

Even well-intentioned silence can feel like a hierarchy:

> successful Kanon observes failed younger sister with sympathy.

Wien prefers argument because argument preserves parity. Compassion risks making her the object.

Kanon has to learn that responding to Wien requires neither aggressive challenge nor visibly cautious sympathy. Wien needs ordinary collaboration.

That is why Kanon changes the subject by asking her to write a song together.

# XX. Collaborative authorship as the real invitation

Kanon proposes:

> Kanon, Wien, and Tomari should pool ideas;
> write about their present feelings;
> and make one song.

Wien warns that she knows nothing about trends in school-idol music.

Kanon answers:

> 「それでいい」
> “That’s fine.”

And:

> 「新しいものを作らなきゃ意味がないって、私も思っているから」
> “I also think there is no meaning unless we make something new.”

This is the invitation Wien can accept.

Kanon does not ask her to imitate Liella!.

She does not ask her to soften her artistic standards.

She asks Wien to help author something that does not yet exist.

This preserves Wien’s need for seriousness and individuality while redirecting them toward cooperation.

The temporary unit will not succeed by absorbing Wien into Kanon’s established style. Its value lies in translating their differences into a shared work.

---

# XXI. Natsumi asks Tomari for seriousness, not agreement

Natsumi tells Tomari:

> school idols do not produce money;
> nevertheless, if Tomari is going to do it, she should do it properly.

She wants Kanon and Wien to feel:

> Tomari’s presence mattered;
> she was not engaging halfheartedly.

This is major growth for Natsumi.

Season 2 Natsumi initially approached Liella! through monetization and content extraction. She now defends the dignity of unprofitable effort.

But she does so in her own voice:

> not through abstract artistic idealism;
> through standards, responsibility, and the refusal of half measures.

Tomari later says privately:

> 「スクールアイドルは、きっと姉者を傷つける」
> “School idols will surely hurt my sister.”

Her delivery is quiet and emotionally constrained. This is the first point where the utilitarian argument reveals its wound.

Tomari is not only afraid that school idols waste time.

She is afraid that Natsumi has again become vulnerable to a dream.

---

# XXII. Tomari as producer: utility begins transforming into care

Tomari constructs the promotional profile:

-   the previous Love Live champion center;

-   the shocking solo performer Wien Margarete;

-   a collaboration likely to attract interest.


She calculates that five out of every anticipated viewers must approve for them to reach the target.

Her media and numerical skills resemble Natsumi’s, but her style differs.

Natsumi’s creator voice is:

-   flashy;

-   improvisational;

-   emotionally manipulative;

-   brand-centered.


Tomari’s is:

-   sober;

-   predictive;

-   conversion-oriented;

-   system-centered.


Tomari initially treats the performers as variables inside a campaign. But the work also makes her part of the group. She must:

-   understand their appeal;

-   design positioning;

-   anticipate audience behavior;

-   coordinate production;

-   choose what story to tell about them.


Like Natsumi before her, she begins discovering people through the act of trying to market them.

---

# XXIII. Wien’s center reaction: desire escaping before pride

Kanon tells Wien to begin the lyrics because:

> 「センターはマルガレーテちゃんでしょ」
> “You’re the center, aren’t you?”

Wien stammers:

> 「そ……それは……」

Then regains control:

> 「いいわ」
> 「やるわよ、センター」
> “Fine. I’ll do it. The center.”

This mirrors Sumire in Season 1, Episode 10.

Both girls loudly claim exceptional status but become destabilized when someone actually entrusts them with the center.

The reasons differ.

Sumire fears the role will be withdrawn.

Wien fears that visible eagerness will reveal how much she wants recognition from Kanon and an audience she has previously condemned.

The center is not difficult for her technically.

It is difficult relationally.

Accepting means allowing other people’s trust to matter.

---

# XXIV. Tomari’s demands expose the limits of pure optimization

Tomari proposes ambitious choreography, attention-grabbing presentation, and even an outfit designed to maximize profit.

Wien and Kanon both recoil at some of the suggestions.

Tomari then proposes a custom payment system for the live.

Kanon immediately says:

> 「ダメ」
> “No.”

Tomari answers:

> 「手厳しい」
> “Strict.”

The exchange is comic, but philosophically important.

Tomari assumes audience attention should be converted into measurable return. Kanon insists that the event’s value cannot be privatized in that way.

Tomari is learning that production does not merely optimize outcomes. It is bounded by the ethical meaning of the event.

# XXV. The seniors watching from afar

Liella! follows the rookie live with intense interest.

Mei is visibly excited, which Shiki identifies:

> 「一番ワクワクしているのはメイ」
> “Mei is the most excited.”

Mei immediately denies it in her rough voice.

The pattern from Season 2, Episode 4 remains, but the object has changed. She no longer hides love of school idols in general; she hides the degree of excitement created by this specific rival collaboration.

Natsumi notes that Tomari is gradually receiving attention. Kinako asks whether Tomari may have begun to like school idols.

Natsumi says she does not know, but insists Tomari is not someone swept away by a temporary feeling.

The line is protective and admiring. Natsumi believes Tomari’s eventual decision will be serious precisely because Tomari resists emotional momentum.

Then Natsumi falls quiet. She is afraid of what Tomari may discover—or of the possibility that Tomari will confirm her belief that Natsumi’s dream is foolish.

# XXVI. Wien’s pre-stage voice: denial can no longer hide nervousness cleanly

Before the live, Kanon asks:

> 「緊張してる？」
> “Are you nervous?”

Wien answers:

> 「誰が」
> “Who is?”

Kanon says:

> 「かわいいよ」
> “You’re cute.”

This is not merely teasing. Kanon has learned that direct praise destabilizes Wien’s armor without humiliating her.

Wien does not launch into a long hierarchy-based denial. The performance is too close, and her attention is already on the stage.

Her tsun behavior is becoming shorter because Kanon no longer treats the surface rejection as authoritative.

---

# XXVII. The audience does not arrive

The projected audience count fails catastrophically. They cannot realistically obtain ten thousand positive reactions.

Natsumi explains that critical comments connected to Wien’s conduct in the previous Tokyo tournament have suppressed interest.

The consequences of Season 2 have become material.

Wien had said the audience and stage were worthless. Now that audience has exercised its freedom not to watch her.

The episode does not frame this as crude cancellation or mob injustice alone. Wien herself recognizes the connection:

> 「そういうことね」
> “So that’s what it is.”

Then:

> 「笑顔になりたいと思っていた場所で、不快な思いをさせたんだものね」
> “At a place where people came wanting to smile, I made them feel unpleasant.”

This is a major development.

In Season 2, Wien treated audience judgment as something that should accurately register her excellence.

Here she considers what **she did to the audience’s experience**.

That is the first real movement from audience-as-judge toward audience-as-person.

---

# XXVIII. 「この顔を見ているだけでも腹が立つ人がいる」

Wien says:

> 「私のこと、嫌いにもなる」
> “Of course they would come to dislike me.”

And:

> 「この顔を見ているだけでも、腹が立つ人がいるってことよね」
> “There are people who become angry merely from seeing my face.”

Her voice loses much of its habitual projection. The conclusion sounds resigned rather than theatrical.

This is not healthy accountability yet. Wien moves rapidly from:

> I harmed people’s experience

to:

> my very face has become hateful.

The first is specific and reparable.

The second is global self-condemnation.

Her earlier superiority protected her from shame. Once she admits wrongdoing, she has little emotional middle ground between invulnerability and self-rejection.

Tomari supplies the analytic conclusion:

> ten thousand approvals are now impossible;
> therefore there is no need to sing.

This is mathematically correct.

It is also the precise moment at which the episode tests whether mathematics exhausts value.

---

# XXIX. Kanon’s decisive answer: sing after success becomes impossible

Kanon says:

> 「歌おう」
> “Let’s sing.”

Tomari has just demonstrated that the performance cannot accomplish its formal goal.

Wien assumes the event is over.

Kanon insists that they sing anyway.

She reminds Wien:

> song has the power to move people’s hearts;
> if they lose the willingness to challenge before even considering the result, nothing can begin;
> they have accumulated work every day for this moment.

Then:

> 「マルガレーテちゃんが本当に歌が好きなら、その力を信じて」
> “If you truly love singing, believe in that power.”

Kanon does not ask Wien to believe:

-   that they will reach ten thousand;

-   that the audience will forgive her;

-   that the festival will admit them;

-   that success is secretly guaranteed.


She asks her to believe in song while the desired outcome remains unavailable.

This is the direct answer to both Wien and Tomari:

> **Art does not receive all of its value retroactively from winning or profit.**

# XXX. 「このステージから」「ここから？」

Kanon says:

> 「私たちの歌で、みんなの心を動かそうよ」
> “Let’s move everyone’s hearts with our song.”

> 「このステージから」
> “From this stage.”

Wien repeats:

> 「ここから？」
> “From here?”

Kanon answers:

> 「うん、ここから」
> “Yes. From here.”

The exchange condenses the whole episode.

Wien sees the stage as a failed preliminary venue:

-   insufficient viewers;

-   no path to the guest slot;

-   damaged reputation;

-   a dead end.


Kanon sees it as a point of origin.

“This stage” need not be validated by what comes after. It can be where a different relationship with singing begins.

The repetition of **ここから / from here** converts failure from an ending into a location.

---

# XXXI. The live address: direct responsibility to the audience

Kanon opens:

> 「今日は、私たちのライブを見に来てくれてありがとう」
> “Thank you for coming to see our live today.”

She then expands the address:

> everyone who came;
> everyone the voice reaches now;
> may the song reach all of them.

The speech is simple and audience-facing. It contains none of the sensational promotional language Tomari used to attract viewers and none of Wien’s former demand to be recognized.

The trio begins by acknowledging the people who are actually present rather than lamenting those who are absent.

This is Kanon’s performance ethics in practice:

> Do not treat a small audience as failed scale.
> Treat each listener as the reason the song is being sung.

---

# XXXII. “Bubble Rise”: the trio’s shared argument

The song’s imagery is perfectly suited to the group.

A bubble is:

-   fragile;

-   temporary;

-   light;

-   easily broken;

-   yet capable of rising and reflecting light.


That makes it an image of dreams without guarantees.

## Wien’s argument within the song

> 「打ち砕かれ Crying」
> “Shattered, crying.”

> 「それでも消えない Wishing」
> “Even so, the wish does not disappear.”

Wien’s academy dream has been shattered. Her public reputation has been damaged. Her philosophy of performance has been challenged.

The song does not tell her that these injuries were unreal.

It says the persistence of desire after injury is itself strength.

## Natsumi–Tomari’s argument

> 「叶えたいって駄々をこねてたいよ」
> “I want to keep stubbornly insisting that I want it to come true.”

Tomari distrusts dreams because they expose Natsumi to pain and provide no reliable return.

The lyric openly embraces the irrationality:

> desire may resemble childish stubbornness;
> that does not make it worthless.

## Kanon’s argument

> 「もしずっと届かなくても」
> “Even if it never reaches…”

> 「足掻くの、恥ずかしくなんてない」
> “There is nothing shameful about struggling.”

This is the core philosophy she has learned since Episode 1.

A failed result does not retroactively humiliate sincere effort.

## Shared conclusion

> 「光に手を伸ばそう」
> “Let us reach toward the light.”

The three girls do not share one reason for reaching:

-   Kanon reaches through connection.

-   Wien reaches through wounded ambition.

-   Tomari reaches through skeptical inquiry.


The stage lets those motives coexist without resolving them into sameness.

---

# XXXIII. The staging: three separate colors becoming one upward movement

The performance retains distinct visual identities:

-   Kanon’s warmth;

-   Wien’s cool, commanding presence;

-   Tomari’s controlled green-toned reserve.


They are not costumed as indistinguishable copies. The temporary group’s meaning depends on visible difference.

The choreography repeatedly organizes them into:

-   separate lines;

-   intersecting paths;

-   upward gestures;

-   shared reaches toward the same light.


The bubble imagery transforms fragility into movement. Hope is not represented as a solid monument. It survives by continuing to rise after repeated rupture.

Wien’s center position matters because she now stands at the front of a song whose thesis contradicts her Season 2 worldview:

> strength is not only arriving above others;
> strength can be refusing to stop reaching after being visibly broken.

---

# XXXIV. 「歌、久しぶりに楽しいって思えた」

Afterward, Wien says:

> 「ねえ、かのん」
> “Hey, Kanon.”

The use of **かのん** without the distancing surname or hostile 「あんた」 is significant.

Then:

> 「歌、久しぶりに楽しいって思えた」
> “For the first time in a long while, I thought singing was fun.”

This is the emotional culmination of her Season 2 arc.

The line does not say:

-   she won;

-   she proved her talent;

-   she convinced the audience;

-   she entered the festival;

-   she moved closer to Vienna.


It says she enjoyed singing.

The delivery is quieter than her public performance voice and lacks the forceful edges of her normal declarations. She is not making an artistic claim. She is confiding an experience to Kanon.

The phrase **久しぶりに / for the first time in a long while** confirms that the joy existed before the series began but had been obscured by:

-   family comparison;

-   examination failure;

-   proof conditions;

-   rivalry;

-   resentment;

-   and public disgrace.


Kanon answers:

> 「その気持ち、ずっと持ってようね」
> “Let’s keep that feeling with us.”

She does not claim credit for restoring it. She invites Wien to preserve it together.

---

# XXXV. Tomari’s final resistance

Tomari says:

> 「価値のあるものとは思えませんが」
> “I still do not consider it something of value.”

Kanon answers:

> 「いつか分かるよ」
> “Someday you’ll understand.”

The exchange preserves Tomari’s autonomy.

The episode does not force her into immediate sentimental conversion. She has witnessed:

-   a mathematically futile performance;

-   an emotionally meaningful performance;

-   Wien recovering joy;

-   Kanon choosing action after the formal incentive disappeared.


But witnessing does not yet mean accepting.

Tomari’s sentence also contains a small opening. She says she **does not think** it is valuable, not that the observation has conclusively proved worthlessness.

Kanon likewise does not lecture her further. She trusts experiential accumulation.

# XXXVI. Cumulative voice profile: Wien Margarete

This episode transforms the running profile substantially.

## 1\. Enemy-making voice

Early in the episode, Wien’s speech remains categorical:

-   「敵は敵」

-   「ダメったらダメ」

-   「あっち行って」


Short imperatives and repeated negatives let her preserve a simple world in which Kanon remains external.

## 2\. Competitive grievance voice

When discussing the festival, she speaks in longer, faster bursts. Her anger is not mere vanity; it reflects the belief that previous achievement should retain institutional force.

## 3\. Compliment-receptive voice

The spontaneous:

> 「本当？」

after Kanon calls her cute is open, high, and almost childlike. It reveals how strongly she values Kanon’s gaze.

The following 「ふん」 and denial repair the exposure.

## 4\. Origin-story voice

Her childhood memory is slower, quieter, and less adorned by judgment. She allows loneliness to be stated directly:

> 「私、寂しくて泣いてたの」

This is one of her least defended utterances so far.

## 5\. Domestic defensive voice

At Kanon’s home she performs helpfulness, repays hospitality, and then insists that none of it signifies friendship.

Her tsun structure has moved from rivalry into daily life.

## 6\. Center-eagerness leakage

The stammer after Kanon names her center shows that being entrusted matters more than she wants to admit.

## 7\. Shame voice

When she understands why viewers stayed away, her voice becomes quieter and more global in its self-condemnation:

> perhaps people hate even seeing her face.

She has not yet learned proportionate accountability.

## 8\. Recovered enjoyment voice

> 「歌、久しぶりに楽しいって思えた」

This is the most important new vocal mode: low-pressure, personal, addressed specifically to Kanon, and free from immediate hierarchy.

## Updated personality thesis

> **Wien is not learning to become less serious about art. She is recovering the reason seriousness mattered before art became a court in which she had to prove her right to belong.**

## Updated 「ふん」 thesis

> **Her huff is evolving from a weapon of superiority into a tiny border-control mechanism around intimacy. The more Kanon reaches her, the more often 「ふん」 means “I felt that, and you are not allowed to make me say so.”**

# XXXVII. Initial comprehensive voice profile: Onitsuka Tomari

## Vocal baseline

Tomari’s voice is:

-   restrained;

-   formally articulated;

-   low in affective ornament;

-   economical;

-   explanatory rather than expressive.


Unlike Shiki’s highly compressed fragments, Tomari often speaks in complete causal structures. Shiki removes language because the fact seems sufficient. Tomari supplies enough language to make the conclusion procedurally defensible.

## Characteristic linguistic habits

She favors:

-   conditional statements;

-   formal vocabulary;

-   cost-benefit language;

-   explicit conclusions;

-   low-emotion honorific constructions;

-   phrases that depersonalize her own motives.


Examples:

> 「必要と判断すれば」
> “If I judge it necessary…”

> 「無駄な時間は過ごしたくない」
> “I do not want to waste time.”

> 「不可能と判断されます」
> “It is judged impossible.”

> 「したがって」
> “Therefore…”

## Emotional concealment

Tomari rarely lies flamboyantly. Instead, she states only the portion of motive that can survive analytic scrutiny.

She says she wants to examine school idols.

She does not initially say:

> I am afraid they will hurt my sister.

That private sentence reveals the feeling underneath the investigation.

## Care language

Her care appears through:

-   research;

-   risk assessment;

-   monitoring Natsumi’s choices;

-   constructing plans;

-   refusing to let temporary emotion dictate long-term action.


The problem is that she can mistake risk prevention for respect.

## Relationship to art

At this stage, Tomari regards artistic activity as suspect when it produces:

-   no profit;

-   no credential;

-   no durable return;

-   no high probability of success.


She can recognize functional excellence but not yet experiential value.

## Initial core thesis

> **Tomari is protective love translated into utility analysis. She distrusts dreams because she has watched Natsumi be injured by them, and she treats emotional distance as the most reliable form of care.**

---

# XXXVIII. Cumulative voice profile: Shibuya Kanon

## Open-club voice

Kanon uses the literal wording of Wien’s recruitment notice rather than escalating emotionally. Her leadership works through gentle persistence.

## Artist-mentor voice

She asks Wien for the original moment of artistic wonder rather than accepting a résumé-like explanation.

This is a refined version of the interpretive skill seen with Mei, Shiki, and Natsumi:

> Kanon searches beneath the defensive explanation for the experience that made desire meaningful.

## Embodied teaching

She does not only explain school-idol multiplicity. She makes Wien:

-   practice cute presentation;

-   collaborate on writing;

-   center;

-   address an audience;

-   perform despite futility.


Kanon teaches through participation rather than lecture.

## Ethical firmness

She rejects Tomari’s payment system instantly and rejects the conclusion that no achievable result means no reason to sing.

## Performance philosophy

This episode gives perhaps Kanon’s clearest practical formulation:

> song changes emotional states;
> the whole performance is designed to amplify that transformation;
> the audience consists of people seeking connection;
> therefore every element can serve happiness.

## Updated core thesis

> **Kanon’s mature gift is not merely singing warmly. It is creating circumstances in which another person can encounter her own buried reason for singing.**

---

# XXXIX. Cumulative voice profile: Onitsuka Natsumi

## Family-induced persona fracture

Tomari’s appearance causes immediate stammering. Natsumi’s creator identity is least stable around the person who knows her before and beneath OniNatsu.

## Revised relationship to utility

Natsumi now tells Tomari that although school idols do not produce money, they deserve serious commitment.

That is a profound reversal from her introduction.

## Older-sister insecurity

She believes Tomari despises her. The language suggests shame that her younger sister considers her irrational, unserious, or vulnerable.

## Mature standard

Natsumi does not demand that Tomari love school idols. She asks her not to do them halfheartedly.

This is Natsumi’s own performance ethic:

> a dream may be unprofitable, but once entered, it should be treated as real.

## Updated core thesis

> **Natsumi has moved from monetizing other people’s dreams to defending the dignity of her own, but Tomari’s gaze still reactivates her fear that dreaming makes her foolish.**

# XL. Cumulative voice profile: Arashi Chisato

Chisato’s contribution is brief but structurally important.

She trusts Kanon’s decision to join the rival club because she understands Kanon’s desire for reciprocal challenge.

Her voice does not carry Keke’s abandonment fear. She has already practiced releasing Kanon toward growth.

Shiki’s observation that Chisato is consistent is accurate at every level:

> Chisato does not require Kanon to remain in one role, one group configuration, or one physical position in order to preserve their bond.

## Updated core thesis

> **Chisato’s faith in Kanon has become stable enough that difference, distance, and temporary separation no longer read as threats to intimacy.**

# XLI. Cumulative voice profile: Tang Keke

Keke’s anger shows that her relationship to Kanon remains more possessive than Chisato’s.

She fears deception, magical brainwashing, and betrayal in exaggerated comic forms. Beneath the comedy is the conviction that Kanon’s voice belongs at the heart of Liella!.

Yet Keke does not sabotage Kanon’s choice. She complains, investigates, and eventually watches.

This represents growth from emotional ownership toward reluctant trust.

Her familiar hostility toward Sumire is also partially displaced: Natsumi now joins the group’s irritation economy, and Keke can observe rather than monopolize that role.

# XLII. Cumulative voice profile: Mei and Shiki

## Mei

Mei’s excitement about the rival live is immediately audible and immediately denied. Her rough voice still conceals enthusiasm, but the concealment is now socially playful rather than identity-protective.

## Shiki

Shiki names Mei’s excitement without teasing emphasis:

Her observational voice has become an accepted form of intimacy. Mei no longer experiences every exposure as a threat to belonging.

# XLIII. Relationship grammar

## Kanon and Wien

Their relationship has moved from ideological confrontation to practical intimacy.

Kanon now:

-   praises Wien’s cuteness;

-   asks about childhood;

-   shares a home with her;

-   invites collaboration;

-   entrusts her with center;

-   challenges her shame;

-   refuses to abandon a futile performance;

-   receives her private confession of enjoyment.


Wien now:

-   helps Kanon’s family;

-   allows personal questioning;

-   exposes loneliness;

-   accepts praise badly rather than rejecting it entirely;

-   writes and performs with Kanon;

-   calls her 「かのん」;

-   tells her how singing felt.


Their core grammar is:

> Kanon asks the question beneath Wien’s judgment.
> Wien resists, huffs, attacks, or denies.
> Kanon does not punish the defense.
> Wien eventually gives a smaller, truer answer.

## Natsumi and Tomari

Their bond is love distorted by anticipatory protection.

Natsumi wants Tomari to respect her dream.

Tomari wants Natsumi not to be destroyed by another dream.

Each experiences the other’s concern as contempt.

The episode begins making their shared trait clear:

> both convert vulnerability into practical language.

Natsumi uses branding and money.

Tomari uses analysis and utility.

## The temporary trio

Kanon supplies relational purpose.
Wien supplies artistic intensity and the wounded center.
Tomari supplies structure, promotion, and skeptical resistance.

They are not naturally harmonious. That is why the group has analytical value. Its song is not the expression of preexisting unity; it is a device through which three incompatible theories of value briefly cooperate.

# XLIV. Visual and formal structure

The episode repeatedly moves characters across thresholds.

Kanon crosses from Liella! into the new club.

Tomari crosses from observer into applicant.

Wien crosses from solitary artist into collaborative center.

Natsumi’s private family relationship crosses into public group knowledge.

Wien crosses into Kanon’s home.

The trio crosses from no audience to performance.

The most important visual boundary is the screen.

Tomari initially understands the live through:

-   site views;

-   predicted conversions;

-   numerical approval;

-   payment possibilities.


Wien initially understands the audience through:

-   judgment;

-   reputation;

-   rejection.


Kanon addresses the people on the other side of the screen directly:

> thank you for coming;
> may our voice reach you.

The live converts metrics back into listeners.

# Final synthesis

Season 3, Episode 2 is the first episode in which Wien begins living inside Kanon’s philosophy rather than merely arguing against it.

She does not fully adopt that philosophy.

She still:

-   resists practice;

-   insists on coolness;

-   bristles at pity;

-   treats center recognition defensively;

-   dismisses warmth with 「ふん」;

-   and depends heavily on achievement.


But the episode restores the forgotten origin of her art.

Wien once loved song because it entered an empty home and made loneliness warm.

Everything that followed—family hierarchy, elite education, failed admission, rivalry, public disgrace—buried that memory beneath proof.

Kanon asks her to perform after proof has become impossible.

That is why the live matters.

No ten-thousand-like target can be reached.
No festival slot is secured.
No academy accepts her.
No family hierarchy is overturned.
No public shame is erased.

Yet Wien sings and says:

> 「歌、久しぶりに楽しいって思えた」

That sentence is the episode’s victory.

Tomari still cannot price it.

Wien cannot fully explain it.

Kanon does not need either of them to agree in advance.

The episode’s final answer is:

> **A performance can fail as a strategy and succeed as an encounter.**

And Wien’s recurring 「ふん」 is one of the smallest but clearest ways we hear that encounter taking hold. Each huff says she has not surrendered her pride—but the need to defend it now arises because Kanon has reached something behind it.
