# S3E01-E02 V1 Deep Reading

Season 3 starts from a very interesting contradiction:

> Season 2 taught everyone to let Kanon go.
> Season 3 asks what happens when Kanon physically stays, but cannot emotionally return to the old arrangement.

That is the key. Kanon is back at Yuigaoka, but she is **not back in Liella!** — at least not immediately. That choice is not a gimmick. It is the only way the Season 2 finale’s emotional work can remain meaningful after the study-abroad cancellation.

If Kanon had simply said, “Never mind, I’m back!” and returned to Liella!, Season 2’s farewell would have been retroactively cheapened. Instead, Season 3 creates a strange but productive compromise:

> Kanon stays at Yuigaoka, but preserves the separation everyone had already accepted by becoming Liella!’s rival.

That is why Episodes 1–2 feel structurally necessary rather than merely chaotic.

# Episode 1: Kanon returns, but cannot go back

Episode 1 begins with the immediate fallout of the study-abroad cancellation. Kanon and Wien Margarete are both told that their Vienna path is postponed. The Vienna academy, through Wien’s family, wants Wien to spend one more year at Yuigaoka learning about singing from Kanon as a school idol. If Wien can win — or achieve equivalent results — her transfer may be approved.

This puts both girls in humiliating positions.

For Kanon, the humiliation is social and emotional. Everyone already said goodbye. Liella! had agreed to continue without her. She had emotionally accepted departure. Returning would make the grand farewell feel false.

For Wien, the humiliation is hierarchical. She lost to Liella!, failed to secure her own Vienna path, and now has to learn from Kanon at “a school nobody’s heard of.” Worse, she is being told that the school-idol world she despises may be the route back to the elite music world she values.

So the episode begins with two girls who cannot simply walk back into the previous order.

Kanon cannot comfortably return to Liella!.
Wien cannot accept joining Liella!.
Both need a third structure.

That structure becomes the new school idol club.

## Liella! without Kanon

The scenes of Liella! practicing without Kanon are very important. They are not merely waiting for her to come back. They have intensified their practice to fill the hole she left. Chisato and Keke have built a harder schedule; the others talk about winning again; they imagine Kanon giving her all overseas, and they want their own effort to motivate her.

This means Season 2’s ending worked. Liella! really did begin reorganizing itself around Kanon’s absence.

That is exactly why Kanon cannot easily return. If she returns too quickly, everyone’s feelings regress. The “we’ll do our best apart” promise collapses into “actually, we never had to change.”

Kanon sees this. Her hesitation is not only embarrassment. It is ethical sensitivity.

She recognizes that Liella! has already begun growing in the shape of her absence.

## Wien’s new club

Wien creating a new school idol club is childish, proud, and necessary.

Her language is still combative: Liella! is the enemy, she wants to beat them, she cannot sing with them, she needs victory to get back to Vienna. But her refusal is emotionally coherent. If she joins Liella!, she may get closer to winning, but she would not resolve the humiliation of having lost to them. She needs to defeat Liella! because Liella! represents the wound she cannot swallow.

That makes her very different from earlier rivals like Sunny Passion. SunnyPa were mature rivals who could respect and nurture Liella!. Wien is a rival who must first learn what rivalry even means inside the school-idol world.

Her new club is therefore less a practical organization than a psychological stance:

> “I will not be absorbed by the group that defeated me. I will stand across from them until I can accept myself again.”

## Chisato’s response to Kanon

The night scene between Kanon and Chisato is one of the episode’s emotional anchors.

Chisato says what she truly feels: she wants Kanon to come back. That matters. She does not pretend to be above loneliness. She does not instantly sanctify Kanon’s indecision. She wants her friend back.

But then she gives Kanon space:

> “There’s no need to rush.”
> “Keep going down the path you believe in.”
> “Welcome home.”

That is Chisato’s Season 2 maturity carried into Season 3. She once loved Kanon by standing beside her. Then she loved Kanon by urging her to leave. Now she loves Kanon by welcoming her home without forcing her to return to the old shape.

That is a subtle but important distinction:

> Chisato welcomes Kanon home as a person, not as Liella!’s missing part.

This is why she can tolerate Kanon joining Wien. It hurts, but she understands the emotional logic.

## Kanon’s decision

Kanon’s decision not to return to Liella! initially looks shocking, but it follows from her entire arc.

She tells Liella! that when she imagined herself in Vienna, she felt she had to keep growing so she would not lose to them. That mindset motivated her. If she simply rejoins Liella!, both her feelings and Liella!’s feelings may return to what they were before.

This is one of the most mature things Kanon has said. She recognizes that relationships can regress if structure does not change.

So she chooses rivalry as a form of care.

Her idea is not “I reject Liella!” It is:

> If we compete as two groups, we can push each other to improve.
> One day, we can come together again as something even better.

That is the Season 3 thesis in embryo.

Kanon is preserving the meaning of separation within the fact of reunion. She cannot go to Vienna, so she creates a symbolic Vienna-like distance inside Yuigaoka.

That is clever writing.

# Episode 2: the new club becomes real

Episode 2 tests whether the new club can be more than Wien’s resentment and Kanon’s good intentions.

At first, Wien refuses even Kanon’s application. This is funny because her recruitment flyer says everyone is welcome regardless of age or experience, and Shiki-like logic immediately makes Kanon’s application valid. But the deeper point is that Wien wants a club only on her own terms. She wants the status of opposition without the vulnerability of collaboration.

Then Tomari Onitsuka enters.

Tomari changes the dynamic completely.

## Tomari as the anti-dream analyst

Tomari is Natsumi’s younger sister, but she is not simply “another Natsumi.” If anything, she is a more severe version of Natsumi’s old worldview.

Natsumi used to treat attention as money and dreams as unreliable. But Natsumi was still theatrical, impulsive, and emotionally porous. Tomari is colder. She thinks in terms of efficiency, profit, qualifications, time, predictive analytics, viewership, and rational expenditure.

Her basic question is:

> Why do school idols matter if they do not produce money, qualifications, or future utility?

That makes her a strong addition because she attacks the Love Live ethos from a different direction than Wien.

Wien says: school idols are amateurs compared to true musical excellence.
Tomari says: school idols are irrational because they produce no practical return.

Wien attacks school idols aesthetically and hierarchically.
Tomari attacks them economically and instrumentally.

Kanon now has to answer both.

## Tomari and Natsumi

Tomari’s relationship with Natsumi is also more emotionally charged than it first appears. She says Natsumi once told her that being a school idol mattered more than money or future preparation because it gave her a dream. That means Natsumi’s Season 2 transformation was not private; it affected Tomari, even if Tomari rejected it.

Tomari’s harshest line is that school idols will do nothing but hurt Natsumi. This suggests she is not only contemptuous. She may be protective.

Her logic seems to be:

> Natsumi used to chase metrics and money.
> Now she is chasing something unprofitable and emotionally vulnerable.
> Therefore, school idols are dangerous.

Tomari is not simply greedy. Like Natsumi before her, she may be using rationality as protection against disappointment. But unlike Natsumi, she has not yet been moved from inside. She is observing from the outside, trying to calculate value.

That makes her perfect for Kanon and Wien’s new club. She joins not because she loves school idols, but because she wants to determine their true nature.

## Kanon and Wien: “what made your heart sparkle?”

The best Kanon/Wien scene in Episode 2 is not about technique. Kanon does not ask Wien about musical training, vocal range, competitions, or elite pedigree. She asks:

> What made you fall in love with singing?
> When did singing make your heart sparkle?

This is exactly the question Wien does not know how to answer at first. Her reflex is to explain that everyone in her family is a musician. Kanon rejects that answer because it explains social background, not love.

Eventually Wien remembers being lonely when her family was away, hearing a song, and feeling warmth spread through her. That memory is crucial because it reveals that Wien’s love of singing did not begin as superiority. It began as consolation.

Before singing became proof, it was warmth.

That is Kanon’s point. She is trying to return Wien to the pre-hierarchical origin of her own love.

This is the same thing Kanon has done for herself and others across the series. She finds the place where a dream became distorted by fear, shame, utility, or status, then asks what it meant before distortion.

For Wien, singing has become:

> I must prove I deserve the elite path.

Kanon tries to recover:

> Singing once comforted me when I was lonely.

That is the first real crack in Wien’s worldview.

## Wien living with Kanon

The reveal that Wien is staying at Kanon’s house is comic, but it also matters structurally. It forces the elite rival into Kanon’s ordinary domestic world: café, family, futons, tatami, casual meals, Kanon’s mother, Aria, daily life.

This is almost the opposite of Wien’s preferred self-image. She wants to be treated as a serious, exceptional, Vienna-bound singer. Instead, she becomes a difficult guest in Kanon’s home.

That domestic proximity matters because Kanon’s strength has always come from ordinary warmth. Her home life is not dramatic trauma; it is stable, teasing, practical, and humane. Wien needs exposure to that because her own musical identity is bound up with family hierarchy, absence, and comparison to her sisters.

Wien may need to learn not only school-idol singing, but ordinary belonging.

# The performance problem: results become impossible

The most important late Episode 2 turn is the like-count crisis.

The new club needs ten thousand likes to earn the guest slot, but the viewer numbers are too low. The negative comments are linked to Wien’s previous outburst at Love Live. This is a major consequence. Wien cannot simply start over as though her contempt for the audience did not happen.

She publicly rejected the value of Love Live. Now the audience does not want to see her.

That is fair in a way the show does not overstate. Wien hurt the social bond between performer and audience. If her song is to reach people now, she must face that damage.

Tomari’s conclusion is rational: if ten thousand likes are impossible, there is no need to sing.

Wien’s initial reaction is despairing: of course people hate her; of course they cannot stand seeing her.

Kanon’s answer is the heart of the episode:

> Let’s sing.
> Forget results.
> Nothing will begin if we lose the will to face the challenges ahead of us.
> If you love singing, believe in its power.

This is one of the purest Kanon moments in the entire series.

She is not denying metrics. The metrics are bad. She is not denying consequences. Wien’s past behavior has consequences. She is saying that the song must exist before it can change anything.

That directly counters both Wien and Tomari.

Against Wien:

> Singing is not only proof of superiority.

Against Tomari:

> Singing is not only worthwhile if it produces measurable gain.

Against both:

> Singing begins with the will to reach someone.

## “I hope our song reaches you”

The performance is therefore not primarily a competitive entry. It is an act of re-opening communication.

Wien, Kanon, and Tomari perform to a weak stream, a damaged audience, and impossible numerical odds. That makes the performance more meaningful, not less. They are singing without guarantee of reward.

This is exactly what Tomari does not yet understand and Wien has forgotten.

The repeated line — “I hope our song reaches you” — is the whole point. It is not “I hope we win.” It is not “I hope we prove we are better than Liella!” It is not “I hope we optimize viewer conversion.” It is “I hope this reaches someone.”

That is the foundational school-idol ethic rebuilt from zero.

Afterward, Wien says it has been so long since she last had fun singing. That is a major breakthrough. It means Kanon succeeded in returning her, if only briefly, to the original warmth beneath the elite wound.

Tomari’s response — that this probably does not have much value — is also perfect. She has seen something, but cannot interpret it yet. Kanon says she will understand someday. That is likely Tomari’s future arc.

# Kanon Shibuya: updated profile through S3E2

Kanon’s Season 3 arc is already distinct from Seasons 1 and 2.

Season 1 Kanon recovered her voice.
Season 2 Kanon learned that her voice could go beyond Yuigaoka.
Season 3 Kanon must decide what to do when leaving is postponed but returning would be regression.

Her answer is deeply characteristic:

> Create a structure where everyone can keep growing.

That is Kanon’s most mature leadership yet. She is no longer merely interpreting the feelings of existing members. She is designing emotional distance as a developmental tool.

## Kanon as teacher

The Vienna academy’s request sounds absurd at first — Wien must learn singing from Kanon as a school idol — but it is actually accurate. Kanon has become a teacher of what singing means after failure, loneliness, and public pressure.

She cannot teach Wien better vocal technique in the elite-conservatory sense. That is not the point. She can teach Wien what singing becomes when it is no longer only proof of worth.

Kanon’s pedagogy is not lecture-based. It is experiential:

-   ask what made singing sparkle

-   write a song together

-   stand onstage despite bad odds

-   sing to reach people rather than to validate oneself

-   notice whether singing feels fun again


This is very Sakuta-like in structure, actually: Kanon helps not by overpowering the other person’s problem, but by creating the situation in which the person can feel the truth directly.

## Kanon’s relation to Liella!

Kanon’s decision hurts Liella!, especially Kinako and Keke, but it is not betrayal. It is care through rivalry.

The most important line is that one day they can come together as one team. Kanon has not abandoned the nine-member identity. She is postponing reunion so that reunion can mean more.

This is a much more complicated form of love than simply staying with the people who want you.

Kanon is saying:

> If I return now, I may comfort everyone but weaken the growth that separation created.
> If I stand apart now, I may hurt everyone but help us become stronger.

That is a hard choice, and it shows how far she has come from the girl who once hid from her own voice.

# Chisato: updated profile

Chisato’s response remains one of the best emotional threads in Season 3.

She wants Kanon back. She says that clearly. But she does not force her. This makes her different from Keke, whose panic is more openly possessive and comedic, and different from Kinako, whose sadness is kouhai-like and sincere.

Chisato understands Kanon’s logic because she has already released her once. Her “welcome home” is therefore deeply layered:

> You are home.
> You are loved.
> You are not obligated to return to the old shape.
> I will wait while you choose the path you believe in.

That is a mature attachment model. Chisato has become the person who can hold the contradiction: wanting Kanon beside her while honoring Kanon’s need to stand elsewhere.

# Wien Margarete: updated profile

Wien is much more interesting now than she was as a pure antagonist.

Her core wound remains elite failure. She was supposed to follow her sisters and family into the Vienna musical world. Instead, she failed, tried to use Love Live as a route back, lost to Liella!, and was then forced to learn from Kanon.

Her pride is therefore not ornamental. It is defensive survival.

If she admits Liella! has something she lacks, then she must admit the elite framework that has structured her identity is incomplete. That is terrifying.

Episode 2 gives us a softer origin: singing once warmed her loneliness. That matters because it shows that her love of singing is older than her contempt. She is not fake. She is frozen around a corrupted definition of value.

Her first breakthrough is not “I like school idols now.” It is smaller and better:

> “It’s been so long since I last had fun singing.”

That is the beginning of her recovery.

# Tomari Onitsuka: running profile

Tomari is the major new conceptual addition.

She is precise, rational, profit-oriented, and dismissive of activities that do not produce measurable future value. She sees school idols as an irrational investment of time. Her presence forces the show to articulate why school idols matter even when they do not produce money, credentials, or immediate results.

Her surface role:

-   Natsumi’s younger sister

-   analytic observer

-   profit/efficiency thinker

-   joins the new club to study school idols

-   helps with metrics, profile-building, and audience strategy


Her deeper function:

> **Tomari represents the worldview that all activity must justify itself through measurable utility.**

That makes her a sharper foil than Natsumi was. Natsumi wanted money because she had failed at dreams and needed something concrete. Tomari seems to distrust dream itself as inefficient or harmful. Her line that school idols will hurt Natsumi suggests that her rationalism may be protective, not merely cold.

She may be afraid that dreams create emotional loss without sufficient return.

Kanon’s eventual task with Tomari will be different from her task with Wien.

Wien needs to rediscover the warmth inside singing.
Tomari needs to discover that some value exists before profit, qualification, or measurable return.

# Liella! after S3E2

Liella! is in a strange but productive position.

They are reigning champions.
They are now third-years, second-years, and a new school with all three classes.
They are recruiting new members.
They have public admiration.
But their original center is standing across from them in another club.

This creates a new problem:

> Can Liella! remain Liella! when Kanon is nearby but unavailable?

That is emotionally different from Season 2’s question. In Season 2, Kanon was going to leave, and Liella! had to imagine continuation across distance. In Season 3, Kanon is right there, but the group must not collapse back into dependence.

That may be even harder.

Kinako’s reaction is especially important. She is happy Kanon is back, then devastated that Kanon is not coming back to Liella!. As the kouhai who idolized Kanon-senpai, she embodies the emotional cost of Kanon’s choice. For Kinako, this is not an elegant developmental strategy. It is loneliness.

That makes the rivalry ethically complicated. Kanon’s logic is sound, but it hurts people who love her.

# Season 3 thesis so far

The emerging Season 3 thesis is:

> **A bond that survived separation must now survive proximity without regression.**

Kanon could not go to Vienna.
Wien could not return to Vienna.
Liella! could not simply continue without Kanon.
Kanon could not simply rejoin Liella!.

So Season 3 creates a third form:

> They will be together at Yuigaoka, but apart as school idols.

This lets the story test whether connection means constant unity or whether true connection can include rivalry, distance, and competing paths.

The new club’s first performance gives the answer in miniature. It does not achieve the metric goal. It does not solve Wien. It does not convince Tomari. But it does something more basic: it lets three very different girls sing toward someone.

That is where everything begins again.

Season 1 began with Kanon learning to sing because she was not alone.
Season 3 begins with Kanon teaching Wien and Tomari that singing can matter even when victory, profit, and recognition are not guaranteed.

That is an elegant continuation. Kanon is no longer merely the recovered singer or Liella!’s center. She is now the person who can carry the meaning of school idols into people who do not yet believe in it.
