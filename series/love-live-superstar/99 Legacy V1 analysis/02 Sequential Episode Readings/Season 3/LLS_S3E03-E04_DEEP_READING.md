# S3E03-E04 V1 Deep Reading

Episodes 3–4 are very useful because they prove Season 3 is not simply going to be “Kanon’s rival club versus Liella!” in a mechanical way. These episodes show the **benefit and cost of Kanon’s separation**.

The benefit: Liella! is forced to define itself without Kanon as center.

The cost: several members, especially the second-years, now have to confront whether their own dreams are large enough to survive after the third-years graduate.

The emerging Season 3 question becomes sharper:

> **If Liella! is no longer only the group Kanon founded, what kind of centers, dreams, and futures can exist inside it?**

Episode 3 answers this through Shiki. Episode 4 answers it through Natsumi and Tomari.

# Episode 3: Shiki, or the center who does not stand out

Episode 3 begins with the immediate social consequence of Kanon’s new group. Kanon, Wien, and Tomari’s remote performance has drawn attention. People at school are talking about them. The second-years start imagining nightmare branding like “Newella!” — a “new Liella!” replacing the original. It is funny, but emotionally revealing.

Liella! is not only competing with an external rival. They are competing with a version of their own origin.

Kanon was the founder-center of Liella!, and now she is outside the group, creating another school idol identity inside the same school. That destabilizes Liella!’s self-image. The question is not only “can we beat Kanon’s group?” but:

> **Can Liella! prove that it has become more than Kanon’s presence?**

Chisato understands this better than anyone. She says she expected this would happen and allowed it because having someone aiming for even greater heights nearby would push Liella! to grow. That is an important continuation of Season 2: Chisato has accepted Kanon’s separation not as abandonment, but as pressure that can mature both sides.

## Post-victory anxiety

Chisato’s speech about feeling uneasy after winning Love Live is one of the episode’s most important moments. She asks: after becoming the best school idols, was there still a greater happiness? Was there still something higher to aim for?

This is a real problem after a championship season. Season 1 ended with hunger after loss. Season 2 ended with victory. Season 3 now has to answer what comes after victory without turning the characters into complacent champions.

Kanon’s rival group provides the answer:

> Liella! can keep growing because Kanon is no longer safely inside it.

That is why the rival structure works. It gives Liella! a new horizon. They are not simply defending a title; they are trying to discover what Liella! looks like when Kanon is absent and present at the same time.

## Choosing a new center

The debate over center position is the heart of Episode 3. At first, Chisato seems like the obvious replacement. She is a third-year, club president, dance leader, emotionally closest to Kanon, and someone who can stand opposite Kanon on equal terms.

But the episode makes a more interesting choice: the new Liella! should be centered on someone who has not been center before. That means one of the second-years. And Shiki becomes the choice.

This is excellent because Shiki is the least obvious center imaginable.

Sumire wanted the center because she had always been denied visibility.
Ren could be center through elegance and classical poise.
Natsumi could be center through charisma, attention, and performance-as-media.
Kanon could be center through voice and emotional gravity.

Shiki is almost the inverse of all that. She is quiet, deadpan, strange, bug-obsessed, scientifically minded, emotionally understated, and uncomfortable in direct attention.

So making Shiki center raises the episode’s real question:

> **Does “center” have to mean the person who naturally draws everyone’s gaze?**

The answer is no.

## Shiki’s wound

Shiki’s discomfort is not just stage fright. It is rooted in her self-image. She says she never stood out in class and assumed it would always stay that way. Then she met Mei and began thinking that maybe someone could notice her.

That is the key: Shiki does not want to be a center in the normal sense, but part of her does want to be seen. She just does not believe she can survive direct visibility.

Her comparison to Kanon is especially revealing. She sees Kanon practicing and concludes that a center should draw people in. Then she looks at Sumire, Ren, and Natsumi as prior centers and sees people who can command attention. Against that standard, she concludes: “It definitely shouldn’t be me.”

This is very consistent with *Superstar!!*’s broader psychology. Characters often do not reject the dream itself; they reject the version of the dream they think is required of them.

Shiki does not reject being important.
She rejects being the kind of center she thinks a center must be.

## Chisato’s leadership crisis

This episode is also quietly about Chisato as leader. When Shiki runs away, Chisato blames herself and says she cannot keep things going like Kanon did. That is important because Chisato became club president precisely because Kanon stepped away. Now she is discovering that leadership without Kanon means carrying emotional uncertainty directly.

But the resolution is not that Chisato becomes Kanon 2.0. It is better than that. Chisato finds her own answer.

Her breakthrough is the “center that doesn’t stand out” idea. She says school idols exist precisely because all kinds of people are there. It is fine to have all kinds of centers, even centers who do not stand out.

That is one of the clearest philosophical statements of Season 3 so far.

Kanon’s Season 3 move was to create productive distance.
Chisato’s Season 3 move is to create productive multiplicity.

She is saying:

> Liella! does not need another Kanon-shaped center.
> Liella! needs a center only Shiki can be.

That is mature leadership.

## White as Shiki’s color

The “white” metaphor is a little simple, but it works beautifully for Shiki.

White by itself is clear and may not stand out. But in a sea of colors, it can shine the brightest. That describes Shiki very well. She is not the most flamboyant member, but her quietness can structure the performance differently. She becomes the negative space that lets the group’s other colors appear.

This is a very good refinement of Liella!’s “connected colors” idea from Season 1. Originally, Liella! meant different-colored lights connecting. Episode 3 now says that even the color that seems least visible can be central if the group is arranged around it properly.

That is what makes Shiki’s center concept strong:

> Shiki does not need to become charismatic in the usual way.
> The group needs to discover how her quietness can become luminous.

## Mei and Shiki

The emotional climax belongs to Mei and Shiki, and it is excellent.

Mei tells Shiki:

> If it is too bright, I will be your shade.
> If you cannot draw people in, I will draw them in with you.
> You do not have to stand out. You can just be yourself.

That is the most Mei-Shiki version of love imaginable. Mei is also embarrassed by visibility, but she is willing to take the attention Shiki cannot bear. Their relationship has evolved from hidden desire and avoidance into mutual adaptation.

Earlier, Mei and Shiki could only try school idols because they were together. Now they can even redefine the center position together. Shiki becomes center not by becoming less Shiki, but because Mei stands beside her as shade, shield, and amplifier.

That is a wonderful relational payoff.

## Episode 3’s main thematic point

Episode 3 is not just “Shiki gets confidence.” It is more specific:

> **Liella! proves it can generate a new form of center that Kanon could never have embodied.**

That is crucial for Season 3. If Kanon’s absence only leaves a hole, then Liella! is incomplete. But if Kanon’s absence forces Liella! to invent a new center logic, then the separation is doing what Kanon intended.

A Shiki-centered Liella! is not weaker because it lacks Kanon. It is different because Kanon is absent.

That is the first real proof that Kanon’s rival-club decision can work.

# Episode 4: Natsumi, Tomari, and dreams after repeated failure

Episode 4 shifts from Shiki’s center to Natsumi and Tomari’s unresolved sister drama. This is a strong follow-up because Episode 3 ended by asking what kind of center the second-years can create. Episode 4 asks what kind of future the second-years are secretly imagining.

The episode begins by showing that Shiki’s performance was well received. Natsumi is thrilled, doing recap content and feeding the attention economy as usual. But the Love Live problem now becomes unavoidable: each school can only send one group.

This makes the intra-Yuigaoka rivalry real.

Yuigaoka students like both groups. Some still support Liella! because Liella! has been Yuigaoka’s star since the beginning. But Kanon is in the other group, and Wien/Tomari are part of Yuigaoka now too. The school is emotionally divided without being hostile.

That is exactly the Season 3 situation:

> The rival group is not an enemy.
> But only one group can represent the school.

Kanon says she does not want the groups to drag each other down; she wants them to push each other higher and create the best song possible at Yuigaoka. This remains her Season 3 thesis.

Wien is still irritated by that softness, but she is now participating in it whether she likes it or not.

## Tomari’s challenge to Natsumi

Tomari’s question to Natsumi is direct:

> Do you truly intend to continue being a school idol?

Natsumi says yes. She says after seeing Shiki sing, she realized she can still shine brighter and find an even brighter light. This is significant because Natsumi’s school-idol dream is no longer vague. She wants to continue, improve, and eventually take on a central role.

Tomari’s response is cold:

> Then I will show you that pursuing dreams is pointless.

This is harsh, but Episode 4 finally explains it. Tomari is not primarily anti-idol because she is greedy, efficient, or purely rational. She is anti-dream because she watched Natsumi break herself over and over again.

## The trip to Ushiku

The whole sequence of chasing Natsumi and Tomari to Ushiku is comedic, but it does important work. It reveals the Onitsuka sisters’ actual life context. They commute a long way from Ibaraki; their home is small enough that nine visitors make it absurdly cramped; Natsumi chose Yuigaoka partly because Tomari thought a Tokyo school would be better for her LTuber career.

This matters because it makes Natsumi’s hustle feel less like a gag and more like a life strategy. She was not simply a city influencer playing at monetization. She is commuting from outside Tokyo, trying to build an online career, and organizing herself around opportunity.

Tomari helped build that route. She was not always opposing Natsumi. She was trying to help Natsumi find a practical path after dreams failed.

## Natsumi’s old failed dreams

The flashbacks are very important. Young Natsumi tried to become an Olympic athlete, a Nobel Prize-winning scientist, a model, and more. Each dream collapsed. The repeated failures gradually changed her. She stopped wanting cake, stopped cheerfully moving to the next dream, and eventually concluded that she had no talent.

Her decisive line is:

> “I’m not cut out to chase after my dreams. Chasing after my dreams is just pointless.”

That is the origin of her money philosophy.

In Season 2, we knew Natsumi had failed at many dreams and retreated into monetization. Episode 4 shows how Tomari internalized that process. Tomari was not just watching Natsumi become money-obsessed. She watched her beloved sister’s smile disappear through repeated disappointment.

So when Natsumi eventually said:

> Money will never betray me. What matters is reality, not dreams.

Tomari absorbed that as doctrine. For Tomari, “dreams are pointless” is not an abstract worldview. It is a protective conclusion drawn from Natsumi’s pain.

This makes Tomari much more sympathetic.

## Tomari’s talent argument against Kanon

Tomari’s argument with Kanon is one of the most philosophically important scenes in Season 3 so far.

Kanon says that having a dream gives people unexpected strength and lets them overcome difficulty. Tomari counters:

> That is because you have the talent to make your dreams come true.

This is a strong challenge because it attacks a possible weakness in *Love Live*’s usual optimism. It asks whether “follow your dream” is only convincing when spoken by someone who actually succeeded.

From Tomari’s perspective, Kanon’s message is biased by victory. Kanon is an exceptional singer who won Love Live. Of course she believes dreams can come true. But Natsumi tried many dreams and failed. What does Kanon’s optimism mean to someone like that?

This is a better critique than Wien’s. Wien challenges school idols from elite pride. Tomari challenges them from protective realism.

And the show does not have Kanon instantly refute her. Kanon hesitates, because Tomari’s point is not stupid.

## Natsumi’s new dream

Natsumi then does something important: she accepts part of Tomari’s argument. She admits that before Liella!, she did fail at all her dreams. But then she says that standing onstage at the Love Live finals changed her. She wants to sing there again, this time as center. She wants to become someone everyone can rely on after Kanon and the others graduate.

This is huge for Natsumi.

Her dream is no longer “money,” “views,” or even “be a school idol” generally. It is specific, relational, and future-oriented:

> She wants to become the center of the next Liella! and help carry the group after the third-years leave.

That means Natsumi has moved from external monetizer to future pillar.

This also reveals something important about the second-years. Kinako, Mei, Shiki, and Natsumi have privately shared a dream they were too embarrassed to tell the seniors: after the third-years graduate, they want to win and become reliable successors.

This connects Episode 4 directly to Episode 3. Shiki’s center was not only about Shiki. It was part of the second-years learning that Liella!’s future can have their colors at the center.

## Tomari’s cruelty and protectiveness

Tomari tells Natsumi that her dream will surely never come true. That is cruel. But the episode immediately reveals the emotional source:

> She does not want Natsumi to be hurt anymore.

Tomari loves Natsumi’s smile. She saw Natsumi regain confidence when she started making money. To Tomari, school idols are dangerous because they put Natsumi’s smile at risk again.

This changes the meaning of Tomari’s rationalism. It is not simply utility-maximization. It is anti-grief. She would rather Natsumi live safely in measurable, practical success than gamble her heart on another dream.

That makes her very different from Wien. Wien’s pride says, “I must not fail because my worth depends on elite success.” Tomari’s protectiveness says, “I do not want the person I love to suffer because dreams are unreliable.”

## The second-years answer Tomari

The second-years’ answer is the episode’s emotional payoff.

They do not deny that dreams hurt. In fact, they say the opposite. Mei says she has never had huge failures like Natsumi, but she still frets and feels like a loser. Kinako says that making dreams come true probably requires going through pain before smiling. Shiki brings in the phrase:

> No rain, no rainbow.

This is a good answer because it does not return to naïve optimism. It does not say, “Dreams always come true if you try hard.” It says:

> Pain is part of what makes the eventual smile meaningful.

That matters because Tomari’s worldview is based on avoiding pain. The second-years answer that a life organized only around avoiding pain may also avoid the rainbow.

## Natsumi and Tomari at the end

The ending is lovely because Natsumi does not reject Tomari’s love. She thanks her for loving her smile. That is exactly what Tomari needed to hear: her protectiveness is understood.

But Natsumi also says that when she became a school idol, she learned what it means to smile from the bottom of her heart. She believes the day will come when she can smile more wonderfully than money could ever make her smile.

That is the true rebuttal to Natsumi’s old worldview.

Money made her smile because it gave her control, confidence, and measurable progress. School idols make her smile because they return her to vulnerable aspiration. That kind of smile is riskier, but deeper.

Her request to Tomari is not “stop worrying.” It is:

> Keep watching me as I wipe away the tears I once shed here and aim for the most amazing smile I can.

That is a beautiful reframing. The place where Natsumi’s dreams failed becomes the place where she restates her dream.

And then she signs it with “OniNatsu” and “Song for all,” which fuses her streaming persona with Liella!’s ethic. OniNatsu is no longer a shield against dreams. It is now part of how she will carry the dream.

# Kanon Shibuya: updated through S3E4

Kanon is less central emotionally in these two episodes, but her philosophy is everywhere.

Episode 3 proves that Liella! can develop a center that is not Kanon-shaped. That is exactly what her separation was supposed to make possible.

Episode 4 shows Kanon encountering a critique she cannot easily solve: Tomari’s argument that dreams are only persuasive to people with talent. Kanon does not defeat Tomari with a speech. Instead, the second-years and Natsumi answer from lived experience.

That is important for Kanon’s Season 3 role.

In Season 1, Kanon often had to be the emotional answer.
In Season 2, Kanon distributed agency more.
In Season 3, Kanon’s best work may be creating conditions where other people answer questions she cannot answer alone.

Tomari’s challenge is not really for Kanon to solve. It is for Natsumi and the second-years to solve, because they are the ones who will inherit Liella! after Kanon graduates.

That is a major sign of narrative maturation.

# Chisato: updated through S3E4

Chisato’s leadership is the hidden spine of Episode 3.

She doubts herself because she cannot manage things like Kanon did, but then she finds a solution Kanon probably would not have found in the same way: the “center that does not stand out.” That is Chisato’s leadership at its best. She does not force Shiki into an existing role. She changes the role to fit Shiki.

This is a very good continuation of her Season 2 presidency. Chisato’s job is not simply to train bodies. It is to arrange the group so that each body, each personality, each rhythm can become expressive.

She is becoming less “Kanon’s support” and more “Liella!’s choreographer of possibility.”

# Shiki and Mei: updated through S3E4

Shiki’s center episode gives Mei and Shiki one of the strongest relational arcs in the series.

Shiki wants to be seen but fears direct light. Mei wants Shiki seen so badly that she is willing to take attention herself, even though she hates standing out too. This is a very specific kind of mutual love: Mei does not drag Shiki into spotlight alone; she offers shade.

Their bond now has a creative function inside Liella!:

> Mei can draw out Shiki’s hidden desire.
> Shiki can give Mei something worth courageously exposing.

That makes them more than a cute pair. Their relationship generates a performance concept.

# Natsumi: updated through S3E4

Natsumi receives her strongest material since Season 2 Episode 6.

Her old money obsession now looks even more tragic. It was not merely comic greed or influencer-brain. It was the worldview of someone who failed so often that she needed a substitute for dreams. Money was safe because it did not ask her to believe in herself as an athlete, scientist, model, or idol. It simply accumulated.

But Natsumi has now developed one of the clearest post-Kanon dreams among the second-years:

> She wants to stand at the Love Live finals again, this time as center, and become someone everyone can rely on after the seniors graduate.

That is a huge change. The girl who once extracted revenue from Liella! now wants to carry Liella!’s future.

# Tomari: updated through S3E4

Tomari becomes much more emotionally legible.

Her anti-dream stance is not cold utilitarianism alone. It is protective devotion twisted into pessimism. She saw Natsumi’s dreams hurt her, then saw money give Natsumi back confidence. So she concluded that dreams are dangerous and money/reality are safer.

Her core wound is therefore not personal failure, but **witnessed failure**.

She was the younger sister watching the older sister repeatedly break. That gave her a worldview:

> Dreams make the people I love suffer.
> Reality protects them.
> Therefore, dreams are pointless.

That is why Natsumi’s smile matters so much to her. She does not want Natsumi to succeed abstractly. She wants Natsumi to keep smiling.

This makes her eventual integration likely to require a different kind of answer than Wien’s. Wien needs to rediscover the warmth of singing. Tomari needs to see that a dream can protect a smile, not only threaten it.

# Liella! and the second-years

The second-years are now becoming the emotional future of the series.

Kinako, Mei, Shiki, and Natsumi are no longer simply the kouhai who joined an already-great group. They have a dream that the seniors do not fully know: after Kanon, Keke, Chisato, Sumire, and Ren graduate, they want to continue Liella!, become reliable, and win.

That is important because it answers the question Season 2 raised at the end:

> Can Liella! continue after Kanon?

Season 3 is starting to say:

> Yes, but not as a copy of Kanon’s Liella!. It must become the second-years’ Liella!, with Shiki’s white, Natsumi’s rainbow, Mei’s courage, and Kinako’s sincerity.

That is the real narrative work of Episodes 3–4.

# Updated Season 3 thesis

After Episodes 1–2, I said Season 3 was about a bond surviving proximity without regression.

After Episodes 3–4, I would refine that:

> **Season 3 is about inheritance without imitation.**

Kanon stays close but outside Liella! so that Liella! cannot simply revert to Kanon-centered identity. Episode 3 proves this by creating a center who does not stand out. Episode 4 proves it by giving the second-years their own dream for the post-senior future.

Kanon’s separation is already doing what it was supposed to do.

Liella! is not becoming weaker without Kanon.
It is discovering colors Kanon’s presence may have unintentionally overshadowed.

That is the strongest development of these two episodes. The group is no longer asking, “How do we get Kanon back?” It is beginning to ask:

> **What can we become because Kanon is no longer standing in the center?**
