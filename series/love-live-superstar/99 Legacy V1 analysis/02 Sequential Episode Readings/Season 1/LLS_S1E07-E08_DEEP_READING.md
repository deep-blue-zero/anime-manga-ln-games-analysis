# S1E07-E08 V1 Deep Reading

Episodes 7–8 are the **Ren Hazuki resolution arc**, but they also transform the meaning of Yuigaoka itself. Up to this point, the school was mostly the setting that restricted or enabled the school idol club. After these two episodes, Yuigaoka becomes an inherited emotional project: a school founded to recover a lost place where music once connected people.

The major shift is:

> **School idols stop being merely Kanon and Keke’s activity. They become the hidden origin of Yuigaoka’s founding dream.**

That is a very strong midseason pivot. It retroactively makes Ren’s opposition tragic rather than merely antagonistic.

# Episode 7: Ren as antagonist, but not villain

Episode 7 begins with the afterglow of Chisato joining, then immediately pivots into school governance. Chisato has transferred from the music course to the general course so she can pursue the same goal as the others. That alone is a big social move: the group is no longer just operating within school boundaries; its members are now crossing the music/general-course divide.

Then the student council election appears, and Keke panics because Ren is the only candidate. Keke’s logic is simple: if Ren becomes student council president, the school idol club is doomed. This leads to the very funny but thematically revealing attempt to make someone else run — preferably Kanon, because Kanon is the natural emotional center, though she absolutely refuses.

Sumire becomes the candidate almost by accident and ego. This works comedically, but it also shows where Sumire still is as a person: she is attracted to status, visibility, and public recognition, even if she is not suited to the responsibilities of office. Her campaign is pure Sumire — showbiz confidence, self-branding, gimmicks, and the catastrophic takoyaki bribery incident. She even ends up with negative votes due to the penalty, which is a perfect gag for her: she wants center-stage legitimacy and instead gets institutional humiliation.

But the episode’s serious point is that Ren wins because she is the only candidate who seems to understand the weight of the school. She campaigns on unity between the music and general courses, and the girls themselves admit that Ren being elected is probably the better outcome. This is important: the show does not pretend Keke’s paranoia is an adequate political theory. Ren is severe and anti-school-idol, but she is also disciplined, serious, and suited to responsibility.

Kanon’s response is the key. She does not want to recall Ren immediately, and she resists reducing Ren to “enemy.” She says there must be a reason Ren is acting this way. This is one of Kanon’s most consistent traits now:

> **Kanon’s leadership begins where ordinary group resentment stops.**

The others see obstruction. Kanon sees motive.

## The first real Ren reveal

The visit to Ren’s home is staged almost as comic gothic mansion material: enormous house, no servants, the giant dog Chibi, the girls hiding and panicking. But the comedy breaks into loneliness.

Ren is dismissing a longtime servant because she no longer has the money to keep her employed. Her father is overseas, her mother is dead, and the large house is mostly empty. The scale of the house makes the loneliness sharper. It is not cozy domestic warmth like Kanon’s home. It is inheritance without people.

That scene changes Ren entirely.

Until now, Ren seemed like the privileged founder’s daughter protecting institutional prestige from above. Episode 7 reveals that she is actually someone trying to hold together the remnants of her mother’s dream with almost no emotional or financial support. Her formality is not just personality. It is armor.

Ren’s motive becomes:

> **The school is the last living thing her mother left behind.**

That is why she is desperate enough to break her campaign promise and center the school festival around the music course. It is not snobbery alone. She believes the music course is the school’s strongest asset, and she thinks showcasing it may save Yuigaoka by attracting applicants.

The tragedy is that she tries to save a school built around connection by deepening the division inside it.

# Episode 8: the misread inheritance

Episode 8 opens by making Ren’s family history explicit. Her mother wanted to recreate, on the site of Jinguu Music Academy, a new school that would bring people together. Her father opposed the project and left. Her mother pushed forward, overworked herself, and died two years before the present. Ren refused to leave for overseas because she wanted to build up the school her mother founded.

That makes Ren’s position emotionally coherent:

> Her mother died trying to create Yuigaoka.
> Yuigaoka now lacks applicants and money.
> Ren feels personally responsible for preventing the school from dying.
> Anything that seems to threaten Yuigaoka becomes intolerable.

This includes school idols.

The key reveal is that Ren once wanted to become a school idol herself. She wanted to liven up the school using the thing her mother loved. But when she searched for records of her mother’s school idol activities, she found nothing. From that absence, she inferred regret: perhaps her mother had erased those memories because becoming a school idol failed to save Jinguu Music Academy.

So Ren’s anti-idol stance is not hatred. It is a grief-based misinterpretation.

Her logic is:

> My mother tried school idols and failed.
> She left no records.
> Therefore, maybe school idols became painful memories.
> If I repeat that path, I may betray her.

This is a very *Love Live* kind of tragedy: the child misreads the parent’s unfinished dream and tries to honor it by suppressing the very thing the parent treasured.

## Kanon as interpreter of memory

Kanon’s role in Episode 8 is excellent. She does not defeat Ren in a debate. She investigates the emotional evidence.

Everyone else is stuck on the present conflict: music course versus general course, school festival participation, anti-idol policy, petition, cancellation threat. Kanon keeps focusing on the inconsistency: she saw the photo of Ren’s mother smiling, and she cannot believe those memories were regretted.

That intuition matters. Kanon trusts the emotional truth of a smile. That is very consistent with her character: she is musically and emotionally perceptive before she is politically strategic.

When she searches the archives and then realizes the old school idol clubroom key may matter, the plot becomes a literal memory retrieval. The girls find the diary that preserves the Jinguu Music Academy school idol club’s history. That diary reframes everything:

The old school idol club failed to save Jinguu from closure, but it succeeded in connecting the students. They sang, dreamed, hurt, and struggled together. Ren’s mother did not found Yuigaoka because school idols were a shameful failure. She founded Yuigaoka because that experience was precious enough to revive in another form.

That is the thematic correction Ren needed:

> **Failure does not mean the memory was worthless.**

This connects directly back to Kanon. Kanon also began the series by believing a failed dream meant the end of the road. Now she helps Ren understand that her mother’s failed school idol effort still mattered. Kanon is uniquely suited to deliver this truth because she has already lived it.

## The assembly scene

Kanon speaking at the school assembly is a huge character moment. She is still visibly uncomfortable being stared at. The subtitles even preserve her anxious reaction to everyone looking at her. But she steps forward anyway.

This is not singing, but it is another use of voice. Earlier, Kanon’s voice failed in the audition room. In Episode 3, she reclaimed singing through the light of others. In Episode 8, she uses her voice as public testimony. She speaks not to perform musically, but to reconnect a fractured institution with its own forgotten memory.

That is a major expansion of her “voice” theme:

1.  Voice as wounded singing.

2.  Voice as song creation.

3.  Voice as relational performance.

4.  Voice as invitation to others.

5.  Now, voice as public witness.


She becomes the person who can stand before the school and say: this is what your shared history actually means.

## Ren’s breakdown and release

Ren’s reaction to the diary and uniform is one of the emotional high points so far. She realizes that what she thought was erased out of regret was actually preserved elsewhere. Her mother’s school idol memories were not shameful. They were treasured.

Her line that school idols are her mother’s most precious memories is the collapse of the false inheritance she had been carrying.

This is why Ren’s joining works. It is not a sudden conversion. It is the restoration of the desire she had originally suppressed. She wanted to be a school idol, but fear of betraying her mother turned that desire into prohibition. Once the memory is corrected, the desire can return.

The group does not invite her merely because she is talented. They invite her because school idolhood is the form through which she can finally participate in her mother’s dream rather than guarding it from the outside.

# Ren Hazuki: running profile

Ren is now one of the more psychologically legible characters in the early cast.

Her surface traits are discipline, formality, elegance, seriousness, and institutional responsibility. But these are shaped by loneliness and grief. She is not simply a strict rich girl. She is a teenage girl trying to act like the custodian of a dead parent’s unfinished project.

Her core wound:

> **Ren inherits her mother’s dream but misreads its emotional meaning.**

Her core fear:

> If Yuigaoka fails, her mother’s sacrifice was meaningless.

Her core mistake:

> She tries to protect connection by controlling it from above.

Her arc in Episodes 7–8 is not “strict girl learns to have fun.” It is more specific:

> **Ren learns that her mother’s dream was not school prestige, music-course hierarchy, or institutional survival alone. It was connection through music.**

That is why the music/general-course conflict is central. Ren’s plan privileges the music course because it is the school’s obvious selling point. But her mother’s actual dream was a school where people are connected through music, not a school where the “elite” course shines while everyone else assists.

Ren’s tragedy is that she was protecting the wrong part of the inheritance.

## Ren’s personality after the reveal

Ren remains serious, but Episode 8 starts puncturing her stiffness. She is shy when invited, embarrassed by her own past obstruction, and surprisingly capable in dance because of figure skating. The others immediately begin pulling her into their informal culture: “Ren-chan,” “Ren-ren,” center position, joint preparation, group chant.

The funniest and most revealing detail is the headmistress. She knew Ren’s mother had been a school idol but kept quiet because Ren’s mother wanted Ren to make her own choice. The headmistress’s real personality also briefly drops the formal mask. This matters because it places Ren inside a network of adults who were trying — clumsily — to preserve her autonomy. Ren thought the absence of records meant erasure. In reality, the truth had been withheld so she could choose freely.

That makes the episode more complex. The adults’ silence contributed to Ren’s misunderstanding, but it was not malicious. It was an attempt to avoid forcing inheritance onto her.

# Kanon Shibuya: updated profile through Episode 8

Kanon’s leadership deepens again. Episodes 4 and 6 already showed her adapting to other people’s wounds. Episode 8 now shows her doing that at institutional scale.

With Sumire, Kanon recognized the wound of being unseen.

With Chisato, she recognized the wound of needing to prove usefulness.

With Ren, she recognizes the wound of misread legacy.

Her leadership pattern is now clear:

> **Kanon does not simply recruit people. She finds the place where their dream has become blocked and gives it a new interpretation.**

For Ren, the blocked dream is inheritance. Kanon does not tell Ren, “Your mother would have wanted this,” in a shallow way. She finds the evidence that Ren’s mother’s dream was broader and warmer than Ren allowed herself to believe.

That is a much more mature protagonist function than “cheerful center girl.” Kanon is becoming the interpreter of the group’s emotional truths.

## Kanon’s relation to public pressure

Another important detail: Kanon still does not like standing before a crowd. Her assembly anxiety is real. She is not magically cured of stage discomfort. But the story has changed the conditions under which she can face it.

At first, judgment froze her.

Now, if speaking serves someone else’s recovery or a shared dream, she can bear the gaze.

That is an important distinction:

> Kanon is not fearless.
> She has found reasons stronger than fear.

This makes her much more believable than a protagonist whose insecurity simply evaporates.

## Kanon and Ren

Kanon and Ren now have a very specific relationship. Kanon is the person who refused to accept Ren’s hostility at face value. She sought Ren’s reason, witnessed Ren’s loneliness, protected her family secret, then helped uncover the truth of her mother’s memories.

Ren, in turn, sees Kanon as strong. But Kanon rejects that directly: she says she is not strong; she just loves the feeling of a new beginning.

That exchange is very revealing. Ren’s life has been defined by preservation: preserve mother’s school, preserve tradition, preserve prestige, preserve memory, preserve the institution. Kanon’s emotional orientation is toward restart, rebeginning, reopening. That is exactly what Ren needs.

Ren preserves. Kanon begins again.

Their relationship therefore adds a new axis to the group:

> **Ren gives Kanon institutional seriousness.
> Kanon gives Ren permission to treat memory as a beginning rather than a burden.**

# The group after Episode 8: Liella’s conceptual completion

With Ren joining, the five-member original unit is now conceptually complete.

Each member represents a different blocked relation to performance:

-   **Kanon** — voice blocked by failure and judgment.

-   **Keke** — dream blocked by distance, family expectation, and impractical idealism.

-   **Sumire** — visibility blocked by repeated showbiz failure.

-   **Chisato** — movement blocked by the belief that she must become strong alone before belonging.

-   **Ren** — inheritance blocked by grief and misread memory.


This is a very strong construction. The group is not just “five girls with different colors.” Each girl brings a different wound into music, and school idolhood becomes the medium that reinterprets those wounds.

The five also fill performance/philosophical roles:

-   Kanon: voice, composition, emotional center.

-   Keke: ignition, idol faith, dream-language.

-   Sumire: showbiz awareness, ambition, friction.

-   Chisato: movement, discipline, embodied confidence.

-   Ren: tradition, institutional legitimacy, classical refinement.


That last point matters. Ren’s joining does not just add another talented member. It resolves the legitimacy problem. The founder’s daughter, student council president, and guardian of school tradition is now inside the school idol club. School idols are no longer an activity happening against Yuigaoka. They become one expression of Yuigaoka’s deepest origin.

# Music course versus general course

Episodes 7–8 also make the school’s internal social structure much sharper.

The music course/general course divide is not just a background gimmick. It is a miniature class/status system inside the school. The music course represents prestige, excellence, institutional branding, and public appeal. The general course represents ordinary students who still want to participate in the school’s identity.

Ren’s failed festival plan reveals the danger of treating prestige as survival strategy. If Yuigaoka saves itself by showcasing only the music course, it may attract attention, but it betrays the school’s purpose as a place of connection.

The diary provides the corrective:

> A school is not saved merely by prestige.
> A school is saved, if it is saved at all, by people feeling that they built something together.

That aligns perfectly with *Love Live*’s larger DNA, but *Superstar!!* gives it a slightly different flavor. This is not an old school facing closure yet; it is a new school failing to become a living community. The problem is not only survival but identity formation.

# The “Song for me / Song for you / Song for all” chant

The final chant is a concise statement of the whole arc:

> Song for me.
> Song for you.
> Song for all.

This is almost the thesis of the first eight episodes.

Kanon’s singing begins as **song for me**: her personal dream, wound, and recovery.

Keke turns it into **song for you**: singing because someone else believes and is moved.

Ren’s arc turns it into **song for all**: music as a way to connect the entire school, including the living and the dead, the general course and music course, the current students and the old Jinguu students.

That progression is why Episodes 7–8 feel like more than a recruitment arc. They connect the personal, interpersonal, and institutional meanings of music.

# Updated thematic trajectory through Episode 8

The season so far now forms a clean escalation:

Episodes 1–2: Kanon’s voice can begin again.
Episode 3: Kanon can sing because she is not alone.
Episode 4: Kanon can invite another failed performer into the group.
Episodes 5–6: Kanon and Chisato learn mutual dependence is not weakness.
Episodes 7–8: Kanon helps Ren reinterpret inherited grief, and school idols become Yuigaoka’s forgotten origin.

The deeper theme is now:

> **Music is not legitimate because it is prestigious, technically elite, or institutionally approved. Music is legitimate when it connects people across failure, grief, status, and time.**

That is the strongest *Superstar!!* thesis so far. It is why Ren’s arc matters: the person most committed to protecting Yuigaoka has to learn that the school’s real legacy was never hierarchy. It was connection.
