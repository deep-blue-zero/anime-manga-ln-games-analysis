# S1E09-E10 V1 Deep Reading

Episodes 9–10 are a very strong pair because they move *Superstar!!* out of the “formation arc” and into the question of **what this group actually is once all five girls are present**.

Up through Episode 8, the structure was member-by-member integration. Episode 9 asks: now that the five are together, what is their name, image, song, and collective identity? Episode 10 then asks: once that identity exists, can the group trust one member to stand at the center even when public feedback says otherwise?

The short version:

> **Episode 9 gives Liella! a name.
> Episode 10 teaches Liella! to trust that name against external judgment.**

# Episode 9: naming the blank canvas

Episode 9 begins with Love Live finally becoming a concrete goal. The tournament is not just an abstract dream anymore; the entry period begins, the finals will be at Jinguu Stadium, and the scale of the contest becomes clear. This matters because the group has just become complete, but completion does not automatically mean identity.

The girls have five members, but they do not yet have:

-   a group name

-   a clear self-image

-   a unified song concept

-   an audience-facing identity

-   a shared answer to “what are we?”


That is why the group-name problem is not merely a comedy device. The joke names — Chisato’s circle obsession, Sumire’s showbiz logic, Ren’s awkward suggestions, Keke’s intensity — all reveal that each girl still sees the group through her own symbolic vocabulary. They are together, but they do not yet know what their togetherness means.

The most important line is Ren’s diagnosis: the five of them are not cohesive yet, and they did not originally come together to achieve one common goal. That sounds negative, but it is actually the episode’s key insight. Liella! is not like a unit formed around one preexisting concept. It was assembled through a sequence of blocked dreams:

Kanon’s failed voice.
Keke’s school-idol longing.
Sumire’s thwarted stardom.
Chisato’s desire to stand beside Kanon.
Ren’s misread inheritance.

So the group’s lack of a single obvious “brand” is not bad writing. It is the natural result of how they formed.

## The livestream comedy

The livestream section is funny, but it does useful character work.

Ren’s confusion about livestreaming is especially good because it shows how sheltered and institution-bound she still is. She is now part of the group, but she has not yet absorbed school-idol media culture. She can lead a school assembly, play piano, figure skate, and carry inherited institutional responsibility, but she has to learn ordinary fan-facing digital performance from the others.

Kanon initially trying to hide behind the camera is also very consistent. She is much stronger now than she was in Episode 1, but she still instinctively avoids being the exposed face of public address when there is no song to organize her. Ren calling her out — “Are you not going to be in-frame?” — is a nice reversal: Ren, who was once outside the school-idol emotional world, now pushes Kanon to appear as part of the group.

The livestream also reveals something important about school idols in this setting: they are not just performers. They are media entities. They have to present themselves, talk to viewers, solicit engagement, respond to comments, and become socially legible. This is where *Superstar!!* briefly acknowledges the idol-as-public-interface side of the genre.

## Kanon’s lyric crisis

Kanon’s writer’s block is the serious core of the episode.

She can compose. She can write. She has already made music from Keke’s feelings. But now she is being asked to write something that represents all five of them and Yuigaoka itself. That is a much harder task.

Her problem is not lack of skill. It is interpretive weight.

She is being asked to convert a still-forming group into language and music before the group fully understands itself. The others pressure her, then accidentally reveal the problem: everyone has a different answer to what the group is. “Galaxy,” “circle,” “excellent,” “strongest,” “blank canvas” — none of them quite captures it.

Then the key insight arrives: their “blankness” is the point.

They are not ultra-talented stars in a cleanly unified sense. They are not ordinary girls overcoming limits in exactly the same way. They are a new group from a new school, with different colors, different origins, different wounds, and no settled history.

That is why “Liella!” works.

The name is built around the idea of connection. Kanon links it to Yuigaoka’s founding idea — “connect you and I” — and then extends it into the image of different-colored lights joining to make colors none of them could make alone.

That is the cleanest conceptual statement of the group so far:

> **Liella! is not a single color. It is the act of connecting colors.**

This is why the name lands emotionally. It does not impose a fake unity over them. It describes the process by which their differences become expressive.

## Kanon’s role in Episode 9

Episode 9 makes Kanon the group’s interpreter again.

She does not just write a song or pick a name. She listens to the incoherence of the group and discovers that the incoherence itself can be meaningful. That is a very Kanon kind of solution.

Her leadership pattern continues:

-   With Sumire, she reinterpreted failure as a place to compete.

-   With Chisato, she reinterpreted dependence as mutual strength.

-   With Ren, she reinterpreted inherited grief as a beginning.

-   With Liella!, she reinterprets lack of cohesion as creative possibility.


That is a strong protagonist function. Kanon’s gift is not merely singing. It is **turning emotional confusion into singable form**.

# Episode 10: Sumire, Keke, and the meaning of center

Episode 10 is framed around the Love Live preliminary theme: rap. This is initially comic, but it is actually a clever way to force Sumire into the center.

Rap, in this episode, represents improvisation, self-presentation, rhythm, boldness, and public confidence. Kanon freezes comedically. Chisato turns it into dance. Ren writes a tanka. Keke becomes chaotic. Sumire, however, can actually do it. Her showbiz experience, ad-lib ability, and instinct for performance finally become directly useful.

This is important because Sumire has always claimed showbiz authority, but the story often made that authority ridiculous. Episode 10 lets it become real.

She is not the best singer, not the best dancer, not the most elegant, and not the strongest idol believer. But she has a little bit of everything: stage experience, quickness, presentation, comic timing, and hunger. The group realizes that this broad, unstable mixture may be exactly why she should be center.

That is a genuinely good choice.

## Kanon’s argument: the past is the past

Kanon’s defense of Sumire is one of the episode’s key moments. When Keke objects that Sumire has never been center before, Kanon answers by comparing it to herself: she could not sing at first either. What matters is not the past, but what comes next.

That is Kanon’s philosophy in miniature.

She is not naïvely ignoring the past. Her whole character is shaped by the past. But she refuses to let the past function as destiny. This is the same logic she used for herself, Ren, and Chisato. Episode 10 applies it to Sumire:

> The fact that Sumire was never chosen as center before does not prove she cannot become center now.

This is very consistent with *Superstar!!*’s broader “new beginning” theme. Liella! is a group of girls whose earlier categories failed them. Kanon failed as music-course singer. Sumire failed as showbiz center. Ren failed as guardian of her mother’s dream. Chisato misread strength as solitary proof. Keke fled into school idols because that dream gave her something she could not find at home. Liella! exists to reopen those categories.

## Keke’s hidden desperation

Episode 10 also changes Keke substantially.

Until now, Keke’s school-idol passion has often been comic, ecstatic, and obsessive. She is the girl who worships Love Live, adores Sunny Passion, and throws herself into the dream with near-religious intensity. But the phone call reveals another layer: her family expects results, and if she does not produce them, she may have to return home.

That makes her zeal more complicated.

Keke is not only a fan chasing a dream. She is someone living under a conditional reprieve. Her school-idol life in Japan is not guaranteed. Her “we have to win” energy suddenly has personal stakes.

This recontextualizes her harshness toward Sumire. Keke is not merely being stubborn or petty. She believes deeply in school idols, but she is also afraid. If Liella! fails, the dream that brought her to Japan may be taken away. That explains why she is so intense about the center, the costume, and the promise to grasp the light together at Love Live.

The strongest part is that she does **not** tell the others. She does not want them to worry about her while they are aiming to be school idols. That is classic idol-anime self-sacrifice, but here it also exposes Keke’s pride. She wants the dream to remain pure and shared, not become a rescue operation for her personal circumstances.

## Keke and Sumire: hostile intimacy begins becoming real

Episode 10 is the first truly important Keke/Sumire episode.

Their bickering has existed for a while: Keke treats Sumire as unserious and showbiz-corrupted; Sumire needles Keke’s idol fanaticism and resents her sharp tongue. But this episode reveals that their conflict works because each sees something real in the other.

Sumire thinks Keke is dismissing her out of pity or prejudice. Keke thinks Sumire is running away from the very thing she claims to want. Both are partly right and partly wrong.

Sumire’s wound is very clear now:

> She wants the center more than anyone, but being offered the center scares her because she has learned to expect rejection.

When public comments say someone else should be center, Sumire immediately accepts the rejection before it can hurt her more deeply. She performs arrogance — “I’ll debut as center in the finals” — but emotionally, she has already concluded: “In the end, it won’t be me.”

This is the same fatalism Kanon had in Episode 1, but expressed through vanity rather than withdrawal.

Keke’s response is not gentle, but it is sincere. She made Sumire’s costume specifically for center. She watched her train. She heard her sing. She does not entrust the Love Live center position lightly. Her argument is:

> I am not choosing you because I pity you.
> I am choosing you because I believe you can carry what matters most to me.

That is the real breakthrough.

For Sumire, being center out of pity would be humiliation. Being center because Keke — the harshest, most school-idol-devoted member — genuinely believes in her is different. It means the person most likely to distrust her is the one who recognizes her.

The costume toss/catch/fall sequence is visually and emotionally strong because it literalizes the relationship: Keke throws her faith at Sumire; Sumire rejects it; Keke nearly breaks herself trying to protect it; Sumire finally reaches back.

And then Keke calls her “Sumire” rather than “Sumire-chan” or some more distancing form. That small naming change matters. It marks a shift from comedic hostility to genuine, if abrasive, intimacy.

## Public opinion versus internal trust

The episode’s deeper conflict is not only “should Sumire be center?” It is:

> Who gets to decide what Liella! is?

The group uploads a recording, and viewers praise the song but question Sumire as center. This is important because it puts fan/audience judgment into the story for the first time as a destabilizing force.

Liella! are school idols. They need audiences. They need votes. They need engagement. But if they simply obey every external judgment, their internal trust collapses.

Sumire herself says they are “this school’s idols,” so they should go with what people say. That sounds pragmatic, but it is also self-erasure. She uses public opinion to confirm her worst fear.

Keke rejects that. Kanon also rejects it more quietly. The group’s answer becomes:

> Audience response matters, but it cannot replace our own belief in each other.

That is a very important maturation point. Episode 9 established Liella! as connected lights. Episode 10 tests whether one light can be dimmed by outside opinion. The answer is no: if they chose Sumire, they must stand by that choice.

# Kanon Shibuya: updated profile through Episode 10

Kanon is less overtly central in Episode 10 than in the Ren and Chisato arcs, but her leadership keeps evolving.

In Episode 9, she functions as the **name-giver**: the person who can interpret the group’s blankness and turn it into identity. In Episode 10, she functions more as the **condition-setter**: the one who creates the conceptual space for Sumire to become center, then nudges Keke toward supporting her.

Kanon’s most important Episode 10 line is not a grand speech. It is her argument that Sumire’s lack of past center experience does not matter because Kanon herself also began from inability. That shows how thoroughly Kanon’s own wound has become her leadership philosophy.

Her core belief now looks like this:

> **A failed past is not evidence against a future self.**

This belief lets her see Sumire differently from how Sumire sees herself.

## Kanon as adaptive leader

By now, Kanon’s leadership is impressively adaptive:

-   Keke needed someone to give practical form to her dream.

-   Sumire first needed a dignified challenge, then later a group that would trust her despite public doubt.

-   Chisato needed reassurance that usefulness was not the condition for belonging.

-   Ren needed help recovering the true meaning of her mother’s memory.

-   Liella! needed an identity that did not erase their differences.


Kanon does not dominate the group. She interprets it.

That makes her a more subtle protagonist than the usual “leader girl.” She often lets other people act: Keke makes the costume, Chisato handles choreography, Ren handles institutional matters, Sumire takes the center. Kanon’s role is to articulate why those choices matter.

## Kanon’s remaining limitation

Kanon still tends to rely on emotional intuition rather than hard confrontation. In Episode 10, the crucial direct confrontation is Keke and Sumire, not Kanon and Sumire. Kanon sets things up, but she does not force the resolution herself.

That is not a flaw in the writing. It is actually important: Kanon cannot be the answer to every wound. If Liella! is truly a group of connected lights, then Keke has to be the one who reaches Sumire here. Kanon’s leadership matures by allowing other relationships to carry the emotional climax.

# Keke Tang: updated profile

Keke’s profile becomes much sharper after Episode 10.

Her school-idol passion is not fake, but it is now inseparable from urgency. She came to Japan for this dream, and her family situation means she may not be allowed to continue unless she proves results. That explains why she can be funny and extreme, but also suddenly severe.

Keke’s core contradiction:

> She believes school idols are joyous dreams, but she is pursuing that dream under the threat of losing it.

This makes her harder-edged than she initially seemed. She is not just a catalyst for Kanon. She has her own hidden pressure.

Her relationship with Sumire also reveals that Keke’s harshness can become care. She does not comfort Sumire in a soft way. She confronts her, challenges her, and builds a costume for her. Like Kanon with Sumire in Episode 4, Keke understands that pity would insult Sumire. So she gives Sumire something better than pity: trust with stakes.

# Sumire Heanna: updated profile

Sumire’s arc advances enormously here.

Episode 4 established her as the girl who has always been pushed into background roles. Episode 10 reveals the deeper consequence: she does not simply want the center; she distrusts any offer of the center because she assumes it must be fake, pitying, or temporary.

Her arrogance is protective theater.

She says “Galaxy,” talks about showbiz, and acts like she is destined for stardom, but when the center position is actually given to her, she panics. She is more comfortable desiring the center than accepting it. Desire lets her preserve fantasy; acceptance risks humiliation.

That is why the audience comments wound her so quickly. They confirm the story she already believes:

> No matter how hard I work, in the end it won’t be me.

Keke breaks that story because Keke is not easily sentimental. If Keke says Sumire should be center, it carries weight precisely because Keke is so demanding about school idols.

Sumire’s new profile:

> **Sumire is not merely vain. She is a performer whose repeated failure to be chosen has made recognition feel suspicious. She wants the spotlight, but fears that being handed it will expose her as someone who never truly deserved it.**

That is a much stronger characterization than “comic wannabe star.”

# Liella! after Episodes 9–10

The group now has two critical forms of identity:

1.  **Conceptual identity** — Liella! as connected lights / different colors / blank-canvas possibility.

2.  **Internal trust** — willingness to stand by Sumire as center despite audience doubt.


This is the first time Liella! feels like an actual unit rather than a completed recruitment set.

Episode 9 gives them language.
Episode 10 gives them loyalty.

The phrase “connected lights” becomes more than a pretty name because Episode 10 tests it. If they are truly connected lights, then Sumire’s weaker confidence cannot be abandoned just because outside viewers prefer Kanon or Ren. The group has to make a color together that no individual audience poll would predict.

# Thematic trajectory through Episode 10

The season now has a very coherent development:

Episodes 1–2: Kanon’s voice can begin again.
Episode 3: Kanon can sing because she is not alone.
Episode 4: Sumire is invited into a stage where failure can be challenged.
Episodes 5–6: Chisato learns mutual dependence is not weakness.
Episodes 7–8: Ren learns inherited memory can become a beginning.
Episode 9: the five girls become Liella!, a group defined by connected differences.
Episode 10: Liella! proves that internal trust matters more than external first impressions.

The deeper season thesis is becoming:

> **A group is not formed when everyone joins.
> A group is formed when its members learn how to interpret, trust, and protect one another’s unfinished selves.**

That is exactly what Episode 10 does for Sumire. She is unfinished, insecure, and not obviously the best center. But Liella! chooses her not because she is already perfect, and not because they pity her. They choose her because becoming Liella! means giving each member a chance to become the version of herself that the past denied.
