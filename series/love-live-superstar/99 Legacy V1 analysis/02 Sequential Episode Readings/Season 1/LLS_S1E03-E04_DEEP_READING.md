# S1E03-E04 V1 Deep Reading

Episodes 3 and 4 are where *Superstar!!* starts becoming much more structurally interesting. Episodes 1–2 were about **Kanon recovering the possibility of singing**. Episodes 3–4 are about something harder: **what happens when recovered possibility has to survive public pressure, failure, comparison, and other people’s wounds**.

The early thesis is no longer simply “Kanon can sing again.” It becomes:

> **Kanon’s gift becomes reliable only when she stops experiencing the stage as a place of judgment and begins experiencing it as a place of shared support.**

That matters because it changes how I read her. She is not merely “a shy singer gaining confidence.” She is someone whose selfhood is reorganized by discovering that performance does not have to mean standing alone before an evaluating world.

# Episode 3: the first live, or Kanon’s fear meets an audience

Episode 3 is the payoff to the first two episodes, but it deliberately withholds the clean victory one might expect. Kanon and Keke do not win the festival. They win the **Newcomer Special Award**, while Sunny Passion takes first. That is an important choice because the episode’s emotional victory is not competitive. It is existential.

Kanon’s problem initially looks like stage fright, so everyone tries stage-fright solutions: exposing her to people through takoyaki work, giving her cute outfits, making her perform in front of others, trying to psych her up. These scenes are funny, but they also clarify that her issue is not merely unfamiliarity with attention. She can tolerate attention in some forms. What breaks her is the feeling that her voice must prove something decisive.

The festival requirement makes this worse. They must take first to continue as school idols, and then Sunny Passion enters at the last minute. For Keke, Sunny Passion are idols of near-sacred importance — the group that inspired her to come to Japan. For Kanon, their entry transforms the performance from “try your best” into “you will fail Keke’s dream because of your weakness.”

That is the crucial psychological pivot. Kanon’s fear is no longer only “I can’t sing.” It becomes:

> **If I can’t sing, I will destroy Keke’s dream.**

That is why her breakdown with Keke is so important. Kanon tries to remove herself from the equation, saying Keke would be better off without her. Keke rejects that completely. She tells Kanon that Kanon’s singing stole her heart, that Kanon is already a star in her eyes, and that performing beside Kanon is one of her dreams too. This reframes Kanon from “burden” into “beloved partner.”

The naming shift — Keke moving from “Kanon-san” toward just “Kanon” — is small but important. It makes the pair less recruiter-and-recruited, less fan-and-star, and more two girls forming a real unit. The episode’s emotional center is not the later performance; it is Kanon allowing Keke’s belief to become something she can borrow.

The live itself is beautifully constructed because Kanon’s breakthrough comes from an accident, not perfect preparation. Sumire, not yet introduced properly, accidentally triggers the blackout/technical disruption. The audience responds with penlights, turning the dark venue into a field of light. Kanon sees the crowd not as judgmental faces but as supportive stars.

That is why she can sing.

Her line, “I can sing because I’m not alone,” is the first real solution to her wound. The audition room was isolation: one girl, one voice, one judgment. The festival stage becomes relation: Keke behind her, Chisato watching, family and classmates cheering, strangers raising lights, even Ren and Sumire witnessing from outside the emotional circle.

The performance of “Tiny Stars” therefore works as more than a debut song. It is Kanon’s new theory of performance:

> **A stage is not where I am judged alone.
> A stage is where other people’s light lets me sing.**

# Episode 4: Sumire and the question of “center”

Episode 4 shifts from Kanon’s stage trauma to Sumire’s visibility trauma. This is a smart progression because Kanon’s newly recovered confidence is immediately tested against someone else’s version of the same wound.

The episode begins with success that is not quite success. Kanon and Keke did not fulfill Ren’s original condition, but the headmistress recognizes the value of what they accomplished: they won an award, gained attention, and proved that school idols can bring prestige rather than disgrace. They are allowed to operate as a school idol association, get a clubroom, and use the rooftop.

Ren, however, remains opposed. Her position has evolved slightly: she no longer simply blocks them procedurally, but she still tells Kanon that if she pursued almost anything other than school idols, Ren could support her. This is important. Ren is not anti-Kanon. She is anti-school-idol. She treats school idols as a dangerous form of music because they are amateur, public-facing, and tied to reputation in ways she distrusts.

The headmistress’s comment to Ren — that taking a place away from people who want to work hard is wrong, and that Ren’s mother might agree — hints that Ren’s opposition is emotionally inherited. She is not simply the strict student council type. She is guarding a legacy, probably without fully understanding what that legacy actually demands.

Sumire then enters as the episode’s comic antagonist and hidden mirror.

At first she looks like a vain showbiz girl who sees school idols as a shortcut to fame. She watches Love Live footage, sees Sunny Passion’s massive follower count, and decides school idols might be her route to public recognition. She is technically talented: she can pick up dance quickly, has showbiz experience, understands performance language, and is physically far ahead of Keke. But her motive is not yet aligned with the group’s ideal.

The center vote exposes her wound. Keke and Chisato both choose Kanon as the center, not because Kanon is objectively the most technically skilled, but because she is the emotional origin of the group. Sumire cannot accept that. From her point of view, it is another repetition of the same humiliation she has experienced all her life: she works, auditions, performs, and still becomes the forgettable extra.

Her backstory gives the episode its weight. She has been auditioning since childhood, trying to become a star, but always ends up as background material — even reduced to the absurd “giant isopod” bit. Her fixation on center position is not simple ego. It is the desire to finally stand in the place she has always been denied.

That makes Sumire’s fatalism very close to Kanon’s earlier despair:

Kanon thought, “I can’t sing when it matters; this is the real me.”

Sumire thinks, “No matter how much I try, I’ll never shine in the center; this is the star I was born as.”

Episode 4 works because Kanon recognizes that parallel. Chisato and Keke both help her see it, but Kanon is the one who can speak to Sumire because Kanon has just escaped the same mental trap.

The recruitment scene in the rain is the key Kanon moment. She does not simply pity Sumire. She does not offer a soft “you can shine too” speech. Instead, she reframes Sumire as needed:

> We need your showbiz knowledge and experience.
> If you want the center, take it from me.
> Competition will help our group grow.

That is much more interesting than ordinary comfort. Kanon gives Sumire a place, but not as charity. She offers her a contest, a challenge, and a reason to stay without surrendering pride.

This tells us a lot about Kanon’s emerging leadership style.

She does not lead by domination.
She does not lead by charisma alone.
She leads by recognizing the wound someone is hiding and converting it into a role within the group.

# Kanon Shibuya: updated running profile through Episode 4

Kanon is now clearly the emotional center of the story, but not in the usual idol-protagonist way. She is not the most relentlessly cheerful, not the most ambitious, not the most institutionally powerful, and not the most technically invulnerable. She becomes the center because she understands failure from inside.

Her defining psychological structure so far is:

> **Kanon is a girl who lost faith in her own voice, then learned to help others by recognizing the same loss of faith in them.**

Episodes 1–2 made her a recovered singer. Episode 3 makes her a performer. Episode 4 begins making her a leader.

## Personality refinement

Kanon is more emotionally intelligent than she initially appears. Her self-deprecating, embarrassed, avoidant manner can obscure how sharp she is about other people. She reads Sumire’s center fixation correctly. She understands that Keke’s dream is not abstract idol worship but a life-direction born from emotional deprivation. She understands that Chisato’s refusal to join is not rejection but sincerity. She senses that Ren’s opposition is not simply procedural, even if she cannot yet name its source.

She is also surprisingly willing to confront people once she believes confrontation is necessary. She argued with Ren in Episode 1. She refuses to reduce school idols to an inferior form of music in Episode 4. And with Sumire, she directly challenges her: if you want the center, come take it.

So Kanon is not timid in a simple sense. She is afraid on behalf of her own wounded dream, but she can be quite brave when defending the dreams of others.

## The stage breakthrough

Kanon’s performance anxiety is not “fixed” in a simple mechanical way. It is transformed by relationship. She can sing at the festival because the audience becomes supportive light rather than faceless judgment.

This gives Kanon a very *Love Live* kind of psychology: individual expression is only possible through collective support. She needs the crowd, Keke, Chisato, and the social field around her. That does not make her weak. It means her strength is relational.

Her core theme remains **voice**, but now that theme has expanded:

1.  **Voice as trauma** — the voice that failed her in the audition.

2.  **Voice as craft** — the voice that writes and sings “Tiny Stars.”

3.  **Voice as relation** — the voice that appears when she feels supported.

4.  **Voice as leadership** — the voice that calls Sumire back from self-erasure.


## Kanon and Keke

Their relationship deepens enormously in Episode 3.

Keke is no longer just the catalyst who drags Kanon into school idols. She becomes the person who refuses to let Kanon define herself as a burden. Her willingness to sing alone if necessary is both touching and slightly dangerous: Keke will overextend herself for the dream and for Kanon. She is not merely a genki enthusiast; she has a self-sacrificial streak under the comedy.

Kanon responds by trying to protect Keke from being dragged down. This creates the first real tension in their bond:

-   Keke wants Kanon beside her even if Kanon cannot sing.

-   Kanon fears that standing beside Keke while unable to sing is selfish.

-   The solution is not one sacrificing for the other, but both choosing the stage together.


After Episode 3, the pair feels much more equal. Keke gave Kanon belief; Kanon gave Keke music; together they create the first real school idol performance of the series.

## Kanon and Chisato

Chisato’s role is still supportive but increasingly complex. She clearly loves Kanon and wants to help her, but Episode 3 makes an important point: Chisato refuses to join the school idols casually because dance is her own central commitment. She will not enter Kanon’s dream half-heartedly just to make Kanon happy.

That gives Chisato more depth than the standard childhood-friend helper. She is gentle, but she has her own axis of seriousness. She supports Kanon precisely because she knows what it means to commit to something.

Her best function so far is not solving Kanon’s problems for her. It is quietly holding the emotional space around Kanon so Kanon can make decisions without being abandoned.

## Kanon and Sumire

This is the most important new relationship from Episode 4.

Sumire mirrors Kanon through failure. Kanon failed at singing under judgment; Sumire failed at being seen despite constant effort. Both convert repeated failure into fatalism. Both say, in different ways, “This is just what I am.”

Kanon’s recruitment of Sumire is therefore the first time Kanon uses her own wound constructively. She recognizes that Sumire does not need pity. Sumire needs a stage where her desire to be seen can become productive rather than bitter.

The line “If you want to be the center, then take it from me” is excellent because it gives Sumire dignity. Kanon does not say, “You can be center because I feel sorry for you.” She says, “Compete with me.” That is exactly the kind of invitation Sumire can accept without humiliation.

Their future dynamic is already visible:

> Kanon gives Sumire a place where striving does not have to be shameful.
> Sumire gives Kanon pressure, showbiz knowledge, and a rival-like energy that prevents the group from becoming too soft.

## Kanon and Ren

Ren remains the unresolved ideological opponent. But Episode 4 makes her less flat. The headmistress’s references to Ren’s mother suggest that Ren’s hostility to school idols may be rooted in a distorted inheritance. She may believe she is protecting Yuigaoka’s identity, when the school’s deeper spirit may actually support students who seriously pursue a dream.

Kanon’s opposition to Ren is also maturing. In Episode 1, Kanon mostly defended Keke’s right to try. In Episode 4, Kanon defends school idols from within: after performing, she understands that school idols are not cheap amateurism. They are difficult, serious, and emotionally real.

So the Kanon/Ren conflict is becoming:

> **What counts as legitimate music?**
> **What kinds of performance are worthy of Yuigaoka?**
> **Can a school’s tradition live through new forms rather than only formal ones?**

# Updated thematic trajectory

Through Episode 4, *Superstar!!* is building a very clear pattern:

Each new girl is attached to a different kind of blocked self-expression.

Kanon: “I love singing, but my voice fails when judged.”
Keke: “I lived according to family expectations until school idols gave me a dream.”
Chisato: “I support Kanon, but I must honor my own dance commitment.”
Sumire: “I have tried to become a star all my life, but I am always pushed into the background.”
Ren: “I protect the school’s musical dignity, but may be misreading what that dignity means.”

This makes the early series more coherent than a simple “recruit the members” structure. Each recruitment is an answer to a different failure of expression.

Episodes 1–2 ask: can Kanon sing again?
Episode 3 asks: can Kanon sing in front of people?
Episode 4 asks: can Kanon use her recovery to recognize someone else’s despair?

That last step is the crucial one. Kanon is no longer only the girl being saved. She has begun saving others — not by becoming a flawless idol, but by understanding what it feels like to believe your dream has already rejected you.
