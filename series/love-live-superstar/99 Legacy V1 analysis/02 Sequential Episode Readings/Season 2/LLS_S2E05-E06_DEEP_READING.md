# S2E05-E06 V1 Deep Reading

Episodes 5–6 are the **Natsumi Onitsuka integration arc**, but more importantly they make Season 2’s central problem much sharper.

Up to this point, Season 2 has been asking:

> **How does Liella!, a group born from vulnerable beginnings, remain reachable after becoming admired and intimidating?**

Episodes 5–6 add a second problem:

> **What happens when Liella!’s sincerity enters the world of attention, views, monetization, editing, and performance-as-content?**

Natsumi is not just “the money girl.” She introduces a whole different value system into *Superstar!!*: not music as connection, not school idols as dreams, not performance as self-recovery, but performance as a route to survival, independence, and profit. The clever part is that the show first presents this as a gag, then slowly reveals it as a defense mechanism.

# Episode 5: Natsumi turns Liella! into content

Episode 5 begins by following up on Mei and Shiki joining. Liella! is now eight members, and Kinako is overjoyed that she finally has other first-years in the club. But the new first-year trio immediately confronts the gap between themselves and the second-years.

This is one of the episode’s most important early moves. Mei and Shiki are not weak in a generic sense. In fact, both have surprising ability: Mei has music sense, Shiki has physical aptitude, and both have knowledge acquired through private school-idol obsession. But when they compare themselves to the original five, they feel impossibly behind.

That means Kinako’s anxiety was not solved by adding more first-years. It has become a generational problem:

> The first-years admire Liella!, but admiration keeps turning into self-comparison.

That is exactly the vulnerability Natsumi exploits.

## Natsumi’s entrance: “producer,” not member

Natsumi does not come to Liella! as a fan, a dreamer, or a shy aspiring idol. She comes as a **producer**. That distinction matters.

Everyone else joined because school idols touched some hidden or wounded part of them. Natsumi approaches from the outside as an operator. She has seen Liella!’s follower count and views, and she immediately calculates opportunity. Her pitch is modern and plausible: she offers livestreaming support, promotion, online reach, editing, and branding. In other words, she understands the infrastructure of attention.

This makes her a useful foil for Sumire. Sumire also understands show business, but Sumire’s wound is theatrical recognition: she wants to be chosen, centered, and seen. Natsumi’s interest is colder and more transactional. She wants to convert visibility into money.

Her contract is telling: she charges no fee but collects all video revenue as “production costs.” Sumire notices the exploitative structure immediately, because Sumire has showbiz instincts. Keke and Kanon are more naïve because they are not thinking economically. Kanon even says they are not trying to make money, which is true, but also precisely why Natsumi can take advantage.

This is an unusually sharp little moment for *Love Live*: sincerity is admirable, but sincerity can be exploited by someone who understands contracts, platforms, and monetization.

## “Liella!’s everyday life” as demystification

The first video Natsumi films is about Liella!’s everyday life, and despite Natsumi’s motive, it has a real positive effect.

The first-years see the seniors playing cards, bickering, losing games, acting silly, discovering video games, and generally being normal teenagers. This directly addresses the Season 2 intimidation problem. The seniors had become mythic from the outside: Kanon the incredible singer, Chisato the dance champion, Ren the elegant student council president, Sumire the showbiz veteran, Keke the idol fanatic. But in ordinary life they are approachable, flawed, and ridiculous.

So Natsumi’s content is exploitative and useful at the same time.

That is important. The show does not make her wrong about everything. She understands something Liella! does not: public image is not only built from polished performances. Fans and prospective members also need to see ordinary personality, vulnerability, and play.

The video helps the first-years think:

> Maybe the seniors are not unreachable gods.
> Maybe they are people we can stand beside.

That is the good version of Natsumi’s worldview. Media can demystify. It can make a dream feel closer.

## Practice footage: the danger of comparison

The practice-video plan is more dangerous. Once Natsumi starts filming practice, the first-years become afraid that viewers will see the gap between them and the second-years. Their fear is not irrational. Liella! is now a public-facing group; footage is not neutral. A practice video can encourage fans, but it can also turn development into a spectacle and expose weakness before the members are ready.

This is a subtle but important distinction:

-   The everyday-life video makes the seniors less intimidating.

-   The practice video threatens to make the first-years feel inferior in public.


That is why Natsumi’s camera is not just a gag prop. It changes the social meaning of practice. Practice is supposed to be the private space where inadequacy can become growth. Natsumi turns it into content before the first-years have emotionally integrated their own beginner status.

## Kanon’s decision to wait

When Sumire exposes that Natsumi is using Liella! for video revenue, Kanon’s response is consistent with her established leadership style: she says they should wait and see because there is probably a reason.

Sumire is not wrong. In fact, Sumire is morally sharper here than Kanon. Natsumi is exploiting them. The contract is bad. The money motive is hidden. Sumire’s suspicion is justified.

But Kanon is also doing what Kanon always does: she looks for the wound beneath the behavior.

This is both her strength and her weakness. Her compassion lets people enter Liella! without being judged solely by their worst presentation. But it also risks enabling someone who is actively manipulating the group.

Sumire even says Kanon is too nice for her own good. She is right. Kanon’s instinct is beautiful, but not always safe.

This gives the episode a useful internal contrast:

> Sumire sees the scam.
> Kanon sees the possible reason behind the scam.
> Both perspectives are necessary.

That is why Liella! has become healthier as a group. Kanon alone might forgive too easily. Sumire alone might condemn too quickly. Together, they create a more complete moral response.

## Natsumi weaponizes the first-years’ insecurity

The most important turn in Episode 5 is Natsumi persuading the first-years to practice separately.

She frames it in a way that flatters their insecurity: if they train alone, they can avoid dragging down the second-years. This is exactly the wrong solution from Liella!’s perspective, but it feels emotionally plausible to Kinako, Mei, and Shiki. They already fear being exposed as weaker. Natsumi gives that fear a plan.

This is the darker mirror of Season 2’s earlier debate over accessibility. The first-years do not want an easier Liella!. They want to become useful to Liella!. But because they define usefulness through comparison, they become vulnerable to separation.

Chisato allows it because she understands the feeling. She once believed she had to become strong alone before standing beside Kanon. That is a lovely continuity point. She knows this is not ideal, but she also respects that the first-years may need to test themselves.

Episode 5 ends with Natsumi triumphant: she has split them up and can “play” with them. But the viewer already understands the tragic irony. Natsumi thinks she is manipulating naïve girls for money. What she is actually doing is walking into the exact emotional world that will expose her own lack of a dream.

# Episode 6: Natsumi meets dreamers

Episode 6 moves the first-years and Natsumi to Hokkaido, Kinako’s home territory. This is a smart location shift because Hokkaido gives the first-years a physical space separate from the seniors’ aura. The visual openness — fields, sky, rural roads, Kinako’s family homestay — contrasts with Yuigaoka’s prestige and Tokyo intensity. It lets the first-years temporarily ask who they are without standing in the original five’s shadow.

But Natsumi is still trying to frame everything as content.

She wants a unit name, a sister-group structure, daily-life footage, clickbait, and eventually manipulative titles like “Liella!’s Disbandment.” The comedy is broad, but the underlying logic is sharp: Natsumi thinks narrative conflict, separation, and insecurity are useful because they generate attention.

In her worldview, even emotional pain can become material.

That makes her one of the more modern characters in *Love Live*. She is not merely a showbiz girl like Sumire. She belongs to the platform economy: thumbnails, branding, subscriptions, superchats, engagement, clickbait, and monetized personal image.

## The first-years reject becoming a “sister group”

The first scene is important because Kinako, Mei, and Shiki reject Natsumi’s attempt to turn them into a separate unit. They do not want to become Liella!’s sister group. They want to become useful to Liella!.

That might sound unhealthy — and to a degree it is — but it also clarifies their sincerity. They are not trying to escape the seniors or create their own fame. They want to close the gap so the nine-member Liella! can win.

Mei’s line is especially severe: if she cannot be useful to Liella!, she has no intention of continuing as a school idol. That shows how quickly her hidden fandom has turned into responsibility. She does not merely want to enjoy being close to idols. She wants to be worthy of standing with them.

So the first-years’ problem is not lack of seriousness. It is over-identification with usefulness.

This connects them to earlier arcs:

-   Chisato once tied belonging to usefulness.

-   Sumire tied visibility to being chosen.

-   Kanon tied singing to not disappointing others.

-   The first-years now tie membership to helping Liella! win.


The same emotional pattern keeps reappearing in new forms.

## Natsumi’s dreamlessness

The key to Natsumi is that she does not initially understand dreams as something worth trusting. When Kinako asks about her dream, Natsumi says she has none — unless earning money counts. When asked why money, her answer is comically practical: retirement security.

That joke is actually revealing. Natsumi’s worldview is built around future anxiety. Money is not just greed. It is control. It is the one measurable thing she can pursue after other dreams have failed.

Later, she says it explicitly: she has had many dreams, and none came true. So she concluded she is not the kind of dreamer the others are.

That is the emotional center of the arc.

Natsumi’s “money makes the world go round” persona is not pure avarice. It is a defensive philosophy built from failed aspiration:

> Dreams disappoint.
> Money is real.
> Views are measurable.
> Profit is proof.
> Therefore, chase money, not dreams.

That makes her much more interesting than a simple comic scammer.

## Kinako and Natsumi

Kinako is the perfect character to expose Natsumi because Kinako’s sincerity is almost impossible to deflect. She is not clever enough to outmaneuver Natsumi and not suspicious enough to condemn her. She simply asks: what is your dream?

That question pierces Natsumi more than an accusation would.

Kinako’s own dream is also important: she wants to surpass the senior performance and make Liella! even better than before. This is ambitious, maybe naïve, but it is not selfish. It is the dream of a kouhai who wants to add to what she inherited.

Natsumi first mocks or resists that kind of idealism, but when she films the first-years practicing, she sees that their dream is not empty. It is difficult, but not impossible. Her line that if Kinako is just going to give up, she should not talk about having a dream is harsh, but it is also the first time Natsumi speaks from genuine investment rather than profit motive.

This is her turning point before she realizes it.

She is moved by the sight of a possible dream.

## Chisato raises the stakes

Back in Tokyo, Chisato sees the first-years’ video and decides it is time for them to learn the same choreography as the second-years. This is a major Season 2 development.

Previously, Liella! had protected the first-years by reducing pressure. Then the first-years chose the serious path. Now Chisato gives them a concrete standard: same steps, same stage, same group.

This is the right evolution. Chisato is not throwing them into difficulty randomly. She has observed their growth and gives them a goal.

Her line about planning being easier when there is a goal fits the whole season. The first-years need an endpoint that is hard enough to organize their effort. Otherwise “catching up” remains a vague anxiety.

So Episode 6 refines Season 2’s accessibility thesis again:

> A reachable dream still needs a demanding form.
> Without a standard, encouragement becomes fog.

## Kanon’s intervention

Kanon arriving in Hokkaido in a bear suit is comic, but the scene that follows is one of the key moments of Season 2. She has come because she is worried about both the first-years and Natsumi. This is exactly Kanon’s leadership style: she senses the emotional loose thread and physically goes to where the problem is.

Her invitation to Natsumi is also very Kanon:

> If you do not have a dream, why not chase the same one as everyone else?

This is a profound contrast with Natsumi’s worldview. Natsumi thinks dreams are individual bets that fail and leave you alone. Kanon offers a communal dream — not as a replacement for personal ambition, but as a way to start moving before you know what your own dream is.

Kanon then uses her own history again. She says she failed to enter Yuigaoka’s music course, but Keke and the others taught her that if people are together, they can do their best. If they put together their faults and shortcomings, they can chase dreams together.

This is the most explicit statement of Kanon’s mature philosophy so far:

> **A group does not exist because every member is already whole.
> A group exists because incomplete people can combine their shortcomings into forward motion.**

That is the exact opposite of Wien’s “chosen talent” philosophy and the exact opposite of Natsumi’s “money is the only reliable thing” philosophy.

## Natsumi joins Liella!

The actual joining is not framed as Natsumi suddenly becoming pure. She remains embarrassed, uncertain, and still tied to her streaming persona. But the school festival performance makes her realize she has found something.

The staging is important. The nine-member Liella! enters as “a new Liella!” and counts from one to nine. This directly pays off the earlier “nine is the magic number” joke, but also makes the number emotionally real. It is no longer Keke projecting franchise mythology onto the group. It is Liella! actually becoming a nine-member organism.

Natsumi’s presence changes the performance atmosphere. The song and visuals are bright, flashy, almost neon-commercial in a way that fits her. It is not just “Liella! plus Natsumi.” It is Liella! absorbing her content-production, extroverted, attention-grabbing energy and turning it into school-idol expression.

That matters because the show does not reject Natsumi’s media persona. It redeems it.

Her camera, streaming language, self-promotion, and “OniNat-’sup!” energy are not erased. They are repositioned from exploitation toward participation. She becomes the one who can open the performance, frame the event, and bring viewers into the spectacle — but now as a member rather than an extractor.

At the end, she says she found her dream. That lands because the dream is not simply “become a school idol” in the abstract. It is:

> To stand inside the kind of collective dream she had stopped believing could belong to her.

# Natsumi Onitsuka: running profile

Natsumi is one of the most conceptually useful Season 2 additions because she introduces a value system the franchise usually treats only lightly.

Her surface personality:

-   loud

-   opportunistic

-   shamelessly self-promotional

-   money-obsessed

-   media-savvy

-   theatrical

-   evasive when confronted

-   surprisingly hard-working


Her deeper structure:

> **Natsumi is a failed dreamer who replaced aspiration with monetization.**

She has tried many dreams, failed at them, and concluded that she is not “the kind of dreamer” the others are. Money becomes her substitute for a dream because money is concrete. It does not require vulnerability in the same way.

This is why she is not the same type as Sumire. Sumire still believes in stardom even after repeated rejection; her arrogance is the armor of someone who cannot stop wanting the spotlight. Natsumi’s armor is more cynical. She has already retreated from dreaming into calculation.

Sumire says, “I should be seen.”
Natsumi says, “Visibility should pay.”

That difference is important.

## Natsumi’s core wound

Her wound is not poverty in a simple melodramatic sense, though she clearly works hard and worries about money. Her wound is repeated dream failure. She has internalized the idea that dreaming belongs to other people.

So her “money” mantra is not merely greed. It is anti-disappointment technology.

If you chase money, then every view, every like, every metric, every revenue estimate gives feedback. If you chase a dream, you risk discovering that your heart was foolish.

That is what Kanon has to counter.

## Natsumi’s relationship to Liella!

Natsumi initially sees Liella! as an asset. By the end of Episode 6, she sees Liella! as a place where she might become a person with a dream again.

That is the conversion.

But I would not say her arc is complete. She has found the dream, but she has not yet fully integrated her media/commercial instincts into Liella!’s ethical framework. That will probably remain her ongoing role: keeping Liella! visible and contemporary while needing reminders that attention is not the same as connection.

# Kanon Shibuya: updated profile through S2E6

Kanon’s leadership reaches one of its clearest forms here.

In Season 1, Kanon was often the person being saved. Then she became the person who could reinterpret other people’s blocked dreams. In Season 2, she increasingly becomes the person who can invite someone into a dream before that person has a dream of her own.

That is a subtle but important distinction.

Natsumi does not say, “I secretly want to be a school idol.”
She says, “I do not have a dream.”

Kanon’s answer is not, “You must have one deep down.” It is:

> Then borrow ours. Start with us.

This is mature because Kanon understands from experience that desire can be reawakened by participation. She did not recover her own voice in isolation. Keke’s belief came first; the feeling followed. She now offers Natsumi the same structure: enter the shared movement, and perhaps your own desire will appear.

That is exactly what happens.

## Kanon’s compassion and its risks

Episode 5 also makes Kanon’s softness look risky. Sumire is right that Natsumi is exploiting them. Kanon’s “there must be a reason” is emotionally accurate but practically dangerous.

This is a good limitation for her. Kanon’s strongest trait — seeing the wounded self beneath bad behavior — can become naïveté if not balanced by others. She needs Sumire’s suspicion, Chisato’s structure, Ren’s institutional seriousness, and Keke’s intensity.

Kanon is not a flawless moral center. She is an interpretive center. That distinction matters.

## Kanon as the anti-Wien

The contrast with Wien keeps sharpening even though Wien is absent from these episodes.

Wien’s implied worldview: only exceptional individuals deserve the stage.
Natsumi’s initial worldview: dreams are unreliable; monetize attention instead.
Kanon’s worldview: flawed people can chase dreams together.

These are three very different responses to disappointment:

-   Wien intensifies talent and selection.

-   Natsumi retreats into money and metrics.

-   Kanon creates shared beginnings.


That is becoming the philosophical spine of Season 2.

# Chisato: updated profile

Chisato’s presidency continues to matter. In Episode 5, she adjusts training for the first-years; in Episode 6, she decides to raise the stakes once she sees they are ready.

This shows that Chisato is becoming a genuinely good club president. She is not simply gentle. She knows when to protect and when to push. Her Season 1 arc prepared her for this: because she once misunderstood strength as solitary proof, she understands the first-years’ desire to train separately. But because she now understands mutual support, she also knows that they eventually need to stand on the same level as the others.

Her leadership is bodily and developmental:

> Kanon invites emotionally.
> Chisato calibrates the path of training.

That division of labor is increasingly clear.

# Sumire: updated profile

Sumire is excellent in Episode 5 because she is the first to recognize Natsumi’s exploitation. This is not just because she is cynical. It is because she understands show business and money better than most of Liella!.

Her suspicion protects the group.

This continues her Season 2 role as the practical counterweight to Kanon’s generosity. Sumire may be vain and abrasive, but she has worldly instincts the others often lack. She knows that contracts matter, revenue matters, and hidden terms matter. In a story about school idols entering the media economy, Sumire is one of the few characters equipped to smell danger.

# The first-years after Episodes 5–6

Kinako, Mei, and Shiki become more coherent as a sub-unit here, even though they reject the idea of being a separate sister group.

Their shared identity is:

> first-years who want to become useful to Liella! without being carried by the seniors.

Kinako gives sincerity and the dream language.
Mei gives pride, music sensitivity, and fan seriousness.
Shiki gives competence, tools, and analytic framing.

What they lack is confidence that their developing selves are already part of Liella!, not future applicants to Liella!. That is why the school festival matters. The performance forces them onto the same stage, same choreography, same count-off, same name.

They do not become Liella! after they catch up.

They catch up by being Liella!.

# Liella! after Episodes 5–6

With Natsumi joining, the group finally becomes nine. But this matters less as a franchise-number callback than as a structural turning point.

Liella! now contains three different generations of entry into the dream:

The original five: recovered blocked selves through school idols.
Kinako/Mei/Shiki: intimidated first-years learning to enter an already-bright group.
Natsumi: a cynical, media-savvy failed dreamer rediscovering desire through the group.

This makes Liella! much broader than Season 1’s unit. Season 1 was about forming a group from emotional wounds. Season 2 is about turning that group into a culture that can absorb different relationships to aspiration:

-   pure admiration

-   hidden fandom

-   loneliness

-   usefulness anxiety

-   cynicism

-   commercialism

-   failed dreams


That is a big expansion.

# Updated Season 2 thesis

After Episodes 5–6, I would refine the Season 2 thesis again:

> **A successful dream must remain reachable, but it must also survive being seen, marketed, compared, and monetized.**

Natsumi reveals that public visibility is double-edged. Liella!’s videos can inspire people, demystify the seniors, and give the first-years confidence. But they can also expose weakness, create comparison, incentivize clickbait, and convert sincerity into revenue.

The answer is not to reject media. Liella! needs media. Natsumi’s skills matter. The answer is to place media inside the group’s ethic of connection.

That is what Natsumi’s joining accomplishes.

She does not stop being OniNatsu.
She stops standing outside the dream with a camera and starts standing inside it with everyone else.

The strongest statement of these episodes is therefore:

> **Attention becomes dangerous when it extracts from people.
> It becomes beautiful when it helps people join the song.**
