# S2E07-E08 V1 Deep Reading

Episodes 7–8 are quieter than the Natsumi arc, but they are structurally important because they refine Season 2’s central question. Season 2 has been about how Liella! can grow from a five-member miracle into a nine-member school culture. These episodes ask:

> **Can the people who carry Yuigaoka and Liella! allow themselves to be ordinary, vulnerable, and supported by others?**

Episode 7 does that through Ren. Episode 8 does it through Yuigaoka as a whole.

The key movement is:

> Ren stops treating herself as the solitary guardian of her mother’s school.
> Yuigaoka becomes a school where responsibilities, dreams, and stages are shared.

# Episode 7: Ren Hazuki and the shame of being ordinary

Episode 7 initially looks like a comic “Ren becomes addicted to video games” episode, but it is actually a continuation of Ren’s Season 1 wound. Ren’s central problem has always been that she treats Yuigaoka as something she must guard alone. In Season 1, that made her oppose the school idol club because she misunderstood her mother’s legacy. In Season 2, that same seriousness makes her overwork herself as student council president.

The Love Live prelims are announced as fully remote, with free song choice. Chisato suggests the familiar production structure: Kanon writes lyrics, Ren composes. But Ren is exhausted, distracted, and half-asleep. Everyone assumes she is overworked by the student council, which is partly true. But the actual source of her immediate problem is more embarrassing: she has discovered games.

That embarrassment is the point.

Ren can confess grief.
Ren can confess institutional anxiety.
Ren can confess devotion to her mother’s school.
But she cannot confess something as ordinary as “I got hooked on games and stayed up too late.”

That tells us a lot about her self-image.

## Ren’s perfection problem

Ren is not merely responsible. She believes that her responsibility requires an image of near-flawlessness. She says she is the student council president, the person holding the school together. If anyone finds out that she is addicted to games, she imagines disaster: disappointment, loss of authority, even damage to the school idol club.

That is absurd in the literal sense, but emotionally coherent for Ren. She has spent so long being “Hazuki Ren, founder’s daughter, student council president, guardian of Yuigaoka” that ordinary weakness feels like institutional betrayal.

This is why the comedy works. The game addiction is silly, but Ren’s shame is real.

Her fear is not:

> “People will think I like games.”

It is:

> “People will realize I am not the perfect person I think I must be.”

That makes Episode 7 a very natural sequel to her Season 1 arc. She no longer misreads her mother’s dream, but she still misreads what it means to inherit that dream. She thinks inheritance requires solitary dignity. The episode teaches her that inheritance can also mean ordinary happiness with friends.

## Mei as Ren’s unlikely confidante

The choice to have Mei discover Ren’s secret is surprisingly good. Mei understands secret desire. Her whole entry into Liella! was built around being unable to admit that she loved school idols. So when she finds Ren hiding a hobby, she is the right person to understand the shame of wanting something that does not fit one’s self-image.

Mei’s advice to Ren is also emotionally mature. She tells Ren that friends may fight when they learn more about each other, but that is part of growing closer. This directly reflects Mei’s own relationship with Shiki. She and Shiki became closer not because they hid their embarrassing desires, but because they finally exposed them.

So Mei becomes a bridge from the first-year arc into Ren’s arc:

> Mei learned that hidden desire can be accepted.
> Now she helps Ren learn the same thing.

This is one of the better signs that the new members are not just add-ons. They are starting to affect the seniors.

## Kanon as vice president

Kanon’s decision to ask Ren to make her student council vice president is another major leadership moment. It is not glamorous. It is not musical. But it is extremely important.

Kanon sees that Ren is carrying too much, and she does not merely offer emotional support. She offers institutional labor. She wants to help Ren grow the school inherited from Ren’s mother. That line matters because Kanon understands what Yuigaoka means to Ren. She is not just saying, “Let me help with paperwork.” She is saying:

> I understand that this school is your mother’s dream, and I want to help you carry it.

This is a mature evolution of Kanon’s relationship with Ren. In Season 1, Kanon helped Ren reinterpret her mother’s past. In Season 2, Kanon helps Ren bear her mother’s future.

There is also a subtle difference between Kanon and Chisato here. Chisato became school idol club president to support Liella!’s internal organization. Kanon becomes student council vice president to support Yuigaoka as a whole. This expands Kanon’s sphere from group interpreter to school-level participant.

## The misunderstanding comedy

The “forbidden realm” misunderstanding around Ren and Mei is funny, but it also serves a function. It shows that Liella! has become intimate enough to meddle, tease, speculate, and panic about each other’s lives. This is not the solemn Season 1 group formed under existential emotional wounds. It is now a messy, ordinary friend group.

That matters for Ren. She does not need to be loved only as the dignified president. She can be teased, misunderstood, and embarrassed. That is part of ordinary closeness.

## Multiplayer as the episode’s metaphor

The final gaming scene is the emotional solution in miniature. Ren has been stuck on a boss because it is too hard for a solo player. The others join in multiplayer. Keke supports, Natsumi attacks, Kanon turns around when warned, everyone contributes, and together they clear it.

This is not subtle, but it is exactly right for the episode.

Ren’s life has been a solo-player challenge.
The answer is multiplayer.

That metaphor is especially fitting because it translates the school idol ethic into Ren’s new hobby. The lesson is not “stop playing games and return to perfect responsibility.” The lesson is:

> Even the thing you were ashamed of can become a place of connection.

That is a beautiful little resolution. Ren does not have to lock the gaming room and renounce her ordinary interests. She can keep the key. She can enjoy games without letting them consume her. She can be responsible and still be human.

Her final reflection — that something so simple can change how she feels, and that her mother’s school has led her to so many wonderful encounters — is the true endpoint. Yuigaoka is no longer a burden she protects alone. It is the place that gave her friends who can see her imperfectly and still stay.

# Ren Hazuki: updated profile through S2E7

Ren’s profile is now much fuller.

Her original wound:

> She inherited her mother’s dream but misread its meaning.

Her Season 2 wound:

> She still believes she must be worthy of that inheritance by remaining composed, responsible, and flawless.

Episode 7 does not undermine her seriousness. It humanizes it. Ren is still elegant, disciplined, dutiful, and deeply attached to Yuigaoka. But now she is also someone who can get hooked on games, lose sleep, panic about being found out, and need help from her friends.

This is important because Ren’s perfection was always isolating. In Season 1, it separated her from Liella!. In Season 2, it threatens to separate her from ordinary friendship.

Her growth is:

> From solitary guardian
> to shared steward
> to ordinary friend.

That is a strong continuation.

# Episode 8: finding the stage where roads meet

Episode 8 is the district prelim stage episode, and it works as a companion to Episode 12 of Season 1. In Season 1, Yuigaoka’s students built Liella!’s Christmas stage. In Season 2, the question becomes: what stage represents the nine-member Liella! and Yuigaoka now?

The answer is no longer merely “the school supporting Liella!.” It becomes:

> **Yuigaoka as a place where roads, people, dreams, and futures cross.**

This is a very *Superstar!!* idea, and it is one of the cleanest uses of location in the series so far.

## The remote prelim problem

Because the prelims are remote and song choice is unrestricted, stage choice becomes strategically crucial. Keke correctly points out that historical Love Live stages have mattered. Natsumi immediately thinks in terms of appeal, polling, and viewer incentives. Sumire and the others understand that they need impact. But Kanon frames the problem most clearly:

> They need a place that symbolizes Liella!, Yuigaoka, and each of them.

That is a harder standard than “a famous place” or “a flashy place.” It requires interpretation.

The rejected Jingu Stadium idea is funny because it is impossible, but it also shows the temptation to borrow grandeur. A huge venue would make them look important. But it would not necessarily say anything about Liella!.

Natsumi’s vote-for-exclusive-pictures proposal is the opposite temptation: not grandeur, but attention manipulation. Again, the show is keeping her media logic alive. She thinks in terms of incentives and votes. That is useful in moderation, but the episode rejects it as the group’s core strategy.

Liella!’s real answer has to come from meaning, not spectacle or manipulation.

## The open house and Yuigaoka’s maturation

The open house scenes are more than school-event comedy. They show that Yuigaoka has changed.

A magazine describes the school as small but united. Prospective students visit. Clubs are active. The student council is no longer Ren alone. Kinako and Nanami are involved, and Kanon is serving alongside Ren. Students from different courses work together. The school is becoming what Ren’s mother wanted.

That line — “We’ve become the kind of school your mom wished for” — is a quiet but major payoff to Season 1. Yuigaoka’s founding ideal is no longer only a remembered wish in Ren’s family archive. It is becoming visible in everyday school life.

This is also why the school idol club chooses not to hold an open house concert. They have enough status now that a concert would dominate attention and overshadow the other clubs. That is a mature institutional choice.

In Season 1, Liella! needed the school to notice them.
In Season 2, Liella! must avoid swallowing the school’s identity.

That is an important development. The group has become powerful enough that restraint matters.

## Aria’s visit

Aria’s visit is a small but interesting Kanon-family moment. Keke immediately tries to recruit her for next year, and the “both sisters as school idols” joke is obvious. But Aria’s actual contribution is different: she asks why someone decided to build a school there in the first place.

That question unlocks the episode’s interpretive path.

Aria is an outsider-adjacent figure: close enough to Kanon to understand her, but not inside Yuigaoka’s daily emotional economy. Because she has not absorbed the school’s assumptions, she can ask the basic historical question everyone else takes for granted.

This is a nice use of her. She does not need to become a school idol to matter to the story. She helps Kanon think.

## Sunny Passion’s last island stage

Sunny Passion’s video call is one of the episode’s strongest thematic parallels. They show Liella! their stage, built by everyone on the island to fit their image. They explain that this may be the last time they can perform on that island as school idols, because later rounds will take place in Tokyo or at the national venue.

This gives Sunny Passion a gentle melancholy. Their island stage is not just pretty. It is a farewell to performing as local representatives in the place that made them.

Sunny Passion’s relationship to place has always been clearer than Liella!’s. They are island school idols. Their school, island, audience, and stage are organically aligned. They are proud of that rootedness.

Liella! sees this and realizes they need their own equivalent:

> not a borrowed grand venue,
> not a vote-hacking gimmick,
> but a stage that expresses where their feelings gather.

Sunny Passion once again functions as the mature foil. They show Liella! what it looks like when place and performance are unified.

## Kanon and Kinako walking the town

Kanon’s walk with Kinako is the emotional hinge of the episode. Kinako, as the girl from Hokkaido, sees Tokyo/Yuigaoka’s surroundings with fresh eyes: lively streets, fashionable people, filming, smiles, crowds, roads, motion. She is excited because the city still feels new to her.

Kanon, who grew up there, has to learn to see her familiar world again through Kinako’s outsider wonder.

That is crucial. Kanon finds the stage by combining memory and freshness:

-   Kanon knows the area intimately.

-   Kinako sees its liveliness as something special.

-   Aria has asked why the school was built there.

-   Sunny Passion has reminded them that place matters.

-   The student council has gathered stage suggestions from the school.

-   The old roads have existed for over a hundred years.


All of this converges in Kanon’s insight: Yuigaoka was built where roads come together.

This is one of Kanon’s most characteristic interpretive moments. She gathers fragments from other people and turns them into a unifying image.

## The road as stage

Choosing the road is a very strong symbolic choice. Roads are movement, crossing, arrival, departure, encounter. They are not owned by one person. They exist because people pass through them.

That makes the road a perfect stage for Season 2 Liella!.

Season 1 Liella! was born from five girls whose lives converged at Yuigaoka. Season 2 Liella! now includes first-years, the student council, prospective students, the town, and a school whose identity is still forming. The road expresses all of that.

Kanon’s explanation makes it explicit:

> The roads come together here.
> All of us come together here.
> Our dreams and hopes connect here.
> This is what Yuigaoka is about.

That is the episode’s thesis.

The road stage also develops the “Song for me / Song for you / Song for all” chant. Season 1 moved from personal recovery to school connection. Season 2’s road stage expands that connection outward into the town, open house visitors, future students, and those who may cross paths with Yuigaoka.

The performance invitation — “We hope that Liella! and Yuigaoka will cross paths with you” — is exactly right. It is not just “watch us.” It is “come into relation with us.”

# Kanon Shibuya: updated profile through S2E8

Kanon’s Season 2 leadership keeps becoming more institutional and spatial.

In Season 1, she interpreted people.
In Season 2, she increasingly interprets places, roles, and structures.

Episode 7: she notices Ren’s burden and enters the student council.
Episode 8: she interprets Yuigaoka’s location and turns it into Liella!’s stage.

That is a real expansion of her protagonist function. She is no longer only the emotional center of Liella!. She is becoming one of Yuigaoka’s meaning-makers.

## Kanon as vice president

Kanon becoming vice president is more important than it first appears. It formalizes her responsibility to the school, not merely the club. This creates a bridge between Liella! and Yuigaoka’s broader student body.

This also corrects a possible imbalance from Season 1. Kanon’s singing and emotional force brought people together, but Ren carried the school institutionally. Now Kanon helps carry that institutional load. She is not replacing Ren; she is sharing the burden.

That is a mature expression of friendship: not only comforting Ren, but taking on work.

## Kanon as interpreter of place

The road-stage insight is classic Kanon. She does not invent meaning alone. She absorbs what everyone else reveals:

-   Kinako’s outsider excitement.

-   Aria’s question.

-   Sunny Passion’s rootedness.

-   Ren’s mother’s dream.

-   the students’ desire to support Liella!.

-   the history of Omotesando’s roads.


Then she names the connection.

This is the same gift she used when naming Liella!. She takes apparent incoherence — roads, school history, open house, stage ideas, town energy — and turns it into a song/stage concept.

Her creative talent is therefore not only musical. It is synthetic. She finds the shared image that lets everyone move together.

# Ren and Kanon as school stewards

Episodes 7–8 make Ren and Kanon into a complementary pair at the institutional level.

Ren carries memory.
Kanon creates beginnings.

Ren guards Yuigaoka’s inheritance.
Kanon helps that inheritance become livable and shared.

Ren understands the school as her mother’s dream.
Kanon helps connect that dream to the students now present.

This is why Kanon as vice president makes emotional sense. Ren alone risks turning school stewardship into duty. Kanon brings relational warmth and forward motion. Together, they make Yuigaoka less like a memorial and more like a living school.

# Chisato and distributed responsibility

Chisato also quietly benefits from this arc. In Episode 7, she tells Kanon that she challenged herself to become club president, repeating Kanon’s own earlier lesson back to her. In Episode 8, she is part of the emotional response when the students offer to take over student council work so Liella! can focus on Love Live.

This matters because Season 2 has become a story of distributed responsibility:

-   Chisato handles the school idol club.

-   Ren leads the student council.

-   Kanon supports Ren as vice president.

-   Kinako and Nanami enter the council.

-   students help with stage ideas and school work.

-   Liella! carries Yuigaoka into Love Live.

-   Yuigaoka carries Liella! by giving them the stage.


No single person has to hold everything.

This is the direct opposite of early Ren, and also a correction to early Kanon’s tendency to internalize pressure.

# Kinako’s role in Episode 8

Kinako is very important in this episode even though she is not the central emotional focus. Her outsider perspective helps Kanon rediscover her own town. That fits Kinako’s Season 2 role as the permeability figure: she is both inside Liella! and still new enough to see things freshly.

She also symbolizes Yuigaoka becoming a place that people come to from elsewhere. Season 1’s Yuigaoka was Kanon and Ren’s local emotional world. Season 2’s Yuigaoka now includes girls from Hokkaido, Shanghai, and other backgrounds, plus prospective students looking in from outside.

Kinako’s wonder at the city helps Liella! understand that the road stage is not ordinary just because it is familiar to them.

# Natsumi after joining

Natsumi remains wonderfully herself. Her instinct is still to think in terms of virality, breaking exclusive footage, and monetizable attention. But now she is inside the group’s ethical frame. When Sunny Passion shows their stage, Natsumi’s first instinct is to make it viral, but the others stop her because Sunny Passion trusts them.

That is exactly where her ongoing development should be. She still has the media reflex. The group teaches her when not to exploit trust.

This is more interesting than simply purifying her. Natsumi’s instincts remain useful but need moral calibration.

# Liella! after Episodes 7–8

By the end of Episode 8, Liella! is now fully functioning as a nine-member group embedded in a school.

That sounds simple, but it took a lot of structural work:

-   The first-years have joined.

-   Natsumi has joined.

-   Chisato is club president.

-   Kanon is student council vice president.

-   Ren is less isolated.

-   The student council includes younger/general-course students.

-   The school itself supports Liella! without being swallowed by them.

-   The district prelim stage expresses Yuigaoka, not just the idols.


This is very different from Season 1. Season 1 was a formation story. Season 2 is becoming a civic/community story: what happens when the idol group becomes one of the institutions through which a school understands itself?

The remote prelim performance is therefore not simply a competition entry. It is Yuigaoka staging its identity to the outside world.

# Updated Season 2 thesis

After Episodes 7–8, I would refine the Season 2 thesis again:

> **A dream that succeeds must remain reachable, but it also must become shareable as responsibility, not merely admiration.**

Liella! becoming popular was not enough.
Adding first-years was not enough.
Becoming nine members was not enough.
They also had to distribute work, expose imperfections, and find a stage that belonged to everyone.

Episode 7 teaches that even Ren can be ordinary and supported.
Episode 8 teaches that even Liella!’s stage is not theirs alone; it belongs to the school, the town, the roads, and the people whose paths cross there.

That is the deepest continuity with Season 1. Kanon began as a girl whose voice failed when she stood alone. By Season 2 Episode 8, the whole school is built around the opposite truth:

> **No one’s song, dream, school, or future is meant to be carried alone.**
