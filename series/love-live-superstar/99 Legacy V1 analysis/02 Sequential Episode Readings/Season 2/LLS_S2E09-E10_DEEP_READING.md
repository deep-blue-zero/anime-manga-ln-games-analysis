# S2E09-E10 V1 Deep Reading

Episodes 9–10 are probably the strongest Season 2 pair so far, because they finally force the season’s two big pressures into direct conflict:

> **Liella! wants to win, but Liella! cannot win by betraying the reason it exists.**

Up to this point, Season 2 has been about expansion: adding first-years, adding Natsumi, becoming nine, finding a stage, making the dream reachable. Episodes 9–10 ask the more painful question: now that the group is bigger and the stakes are higher, will Liella! protect the whole nine-member dream, or will it sacrifice part of itself in order to maximize its chance of victory?

The answer is not sentimental. The show fully admits that performing with only the second-years might increase their odds. It just says: if they do that, the victory would no longer mean what Liella! needs it to mean.

# Episode 9: when winning threatens the group

Episode 9 opens with triumph: Liella! has passed the district prelims, Yuigaoka celebrates, Natsumi is thrilled by online attention, Mei is embarrassed by being called cute, and the school is proud of them. But the mood turns very quickly when they discover that Sunny Passion did not qualify for the Tokyo regionals.

That is a major shock because Sunny Passion had functioned as the mature ideal of school idolhood: rooted, generous, community-backed, and already proven. Their elimination tells Liella! that the hierarchy they understood has been broken. The opponent who defeated them is no longer the champion they love. It is Wien Margarete.

This changes the emotional structure of the season.

Sunny Passion represented **mature school-idol rootedness**.
Wien represents **solitary overwhelming excellence**.

So when Wien eliminates Sunny Passion, she symbolically defeats one of the very models Liella! has been learning from.

## Sunny Passion’s exit as warning

Sunny Passion’s call is one of the most important scenes in the episode. They do not collapse into bitterness. They congratulate Liella!, explain that they lost because they may have become complacent, and warn them that Love Live only gives you three chances in high school.

That line lands hard because it turns the tournament into time pressure. *Love Live* often has a gentle “follow your dream” atmosphere, but Sunny Passion reminds everyone that youth has an expiration date. You do not get infinite attempts. Even a beloved, accomplished group can end before it reaches the final again.

Their advice — treat every round like it is your last — darkens the season. It makes Keke’s hidden situation feel thematically aligned with Sunny Passion’s loss. For Sunny Passion, time ran out faster than expected. For Keke, time may literally run out if Liella! does not win something.

The episode is therefore about urgency, but also about what urgency does to people.

## Chisato’s diagnosis: keeping up is not winning

Chisato notices the gap between the first-years and second-years. This is not cruelty. It is her job as club president and dance leader to see the group honestly. The first-years are working hard and keeping up, but “keeping up” and “winning” are different things.

This is one of the most honest points Season 2 has made. The show does not pretend that effort erases experience instantly. Liella! becoming nine created a richer group, but it also created a competitive problem. The original five have more training, more stage experience, more developed chemistry, and more mature performance bodies.

So the dilemma becomes real:

> If Liella! performs as nine, it is truer to itself.
> If Liella! performs as five, it may have a better chance to win.

That is the central conflict of Episode 9.

## Keke’s contradiction

Keke’s position is heartbreaking because she is caught between two truths.

On one side, she desperately needs Liella! to win because her family may force her back to Shanghai if she does not produce results. On the other side, her dream of school idols is not simply victory. It is performing happily together with everyone.

That is why she refuses to tell the group about her situation. She does not want Liella!’s performance to become a rescue mission for her. She wants it to remain a joyful shared dream.

But that refusal has a cost. By hiding the stakes, Keke forces Sumire to carry knowledge that affects the entire group. Sumire knows that “let’s all have fun together” is not enough if Keke might disappear. Keke wants to protect the purity of the dream; Sumire wants to protect Keke.

This is the strongest Keke/Sumire material so far.

## Sumire as the willing villain

Sumire’s proposal that only the second-years should perform is not simple selfishness, even though she initially disguises it as showbiz ambition. She says she wants results to get back into show business, and part of that is probably emotionally true — Sumire still wants recognition. But the scene makes clear that this is mostly a mask.

She is trying to become the bad guy.

She knows Keke will not ask the group to sacrifice the first-years. She knows Kanon will resist any exclusionary logic. She knows the first-years are already anxious. So Sumire says the ugly thing aloud: if the second-years give Liella! a better chance of winning, then maybe the first-years should sit out.

The painful thing is that she is not obviously wrong in tactical terms.

This is why the scene works. Sumire is not being cartoonishly cruel. She is articulating the brutal competitive calculation everyone else is trying not to face.

The question becomes:

> What kind of win would Liella! accept?

## The first-years’ self-sacrifice

Kinako, Mei, Shiki, and Natsumi then make the opposite offer: they will voluntarily sit out so the second-years can win for them. This is deeply moving, but it is also wrong in the same way Sumire’s plan is wrong.

The first-years are not being cowardly. They are being too self-effacing. They think: if our presence lowers Liella!’s chance, then love means stepping aside.

This repeats one of *Superstar!!*’s core emotional patterns:

-   Kanon thought leaving Keke would protect Keke’s dream.

-   Chisato thought becoming strong alone was necessary before standing beside Kanon.

-   Sumire thought being handed the center might only burden the group.

-   Natsumi thought staying outside with a camera was safer than dreaming.

-   The first-years now think absence might be their most useful contribution.


Again and again, the show rejects love as disappearance.

Liella!’s ethic is not “I will erase myself for you.”
It is “I will stand with you, even if standing together is harder.”

## Keke and Sumire’s confession

The shrine scene is one of the emotional peaks of the season. Sumire brings out the old Sunny Passion treasure Keke once entrusted to her, saying that Keke saved her with it and that now she wants to return the favor. That object has become the symbol of their bond: Keke’s school-idol faith was once something Sumire found excessive or ridiculous, but it became the thing that pulled Sumire into real sincerity.

Sumire’s desperate line — that she wants to be a school idol with Keke for all three years — finally exposes what is underneath all the bickering.

She does not want Keke protected abstractly.
She does not want Liella! to win only for prestige.
She wants Keke to stay.

Keke’s response — “I hate you… but I love you” — is perfect for them. It preserves the abrasive texture of their relationship while admitting the emotional truth. Their relationship is not soft like Kanon/Chisato, not quietly perceptive like Mei/Shiki, and not senpai-kouhai like Kanon/Kinako. It is hostile intimacy: irritation as attachment, fighting as care, insults as emotional camouflage.

By the end of Episode 9, Keke’s secret is out, and Liella! chooses the only answer that preserves itself:

> They will perform as nine.
> They will win as nine, or lose as nine.

That is the episode’s moral resolution.

# Episode 10: what it really means to sing

Episode 10 moves from the membership crisis into an ideological confrontation with Wien Margarete. The title, **“A Song That Rings Through Shibuya,”** is important: this is not only about advancing in Love Live. It is about defining what kind of song Liella! wants to send into the world.

Wien has repeatedly claimed that she will show Kanon “what it truly means to sing.” Episode 10 finally forces Kanon to answer that challenge.

## Training camp as co-creation

The Hokkaido training camp is structured around pairing first-years and second-years to create the performance together. This is the right continuation from Episode 9. It is not enough to say “we perform as nine.” The performance itself has to be built as nine.

So the episode pairs them across years:

-   Chisato invites Shiki into dance creation.

-   Ren draws Mei into composing.

-   Kanon works with Kinako’s lyric embarrassment.

-   Keke and Sumire continue their chaotic but productive conflict.

-   Natsumi tries to transform everything into content, but gradually gets pulled into the group’s emotional rhythm.


This matters because the first-years are no longer just trying to catch up. They are contributing. They are not passengers on the second-years’ performance; they help make it.

That is the concrete answer to Episode 9’s dilemma.

The solution is not “the first-years become as experienced as the second-years overnight.”
The solution is “the song must be made in a way that requires them.”

## Kinako and lyric vulnerability

Kanon’s scene with Kinako and the old lyric notebook is a lovely small echo of Season 1. Kanon recognizes that showing lyrics is embarrassing because lyrics expose inward feeling before they are protected by music. She admits that she felt the same way, wanting to rip up her own notebook.

This is a very precise depiction of creative vulnerability. Writing lyrics is not just “making words.” It is letting other people see the immature, sentimental, unfinished emotional material inside you.

Kanon’s answer to Kinako is not “your lyrics are definitely great.” It is:

> I want you to feel the joy and excitement that come after the embarrassment.

That is a beautiful continuation of Kanon’s mentorship. She does not erase the embarrassment. She says the embarrassment is part of the process by which private feeling becomes shared song.

This is also what Season 2 has been doing with the first-years generally: letting their private inadequacy, shame, or hidden desire become visible enough to join the group.

## Wien’s press-conference ideology

Wien’s remote press-conference appearance is cold and revealing. She says she is competing to show school idols what they really are — amateurs. Then she disconnects.

That is the distilled version of her worldview.

She does not see Love Live as a community, tradition, school-culture space, or place of youth. She sees it as a stage on which to demonstrate superiority. She treats school idols as inferior performers who mistake enthusiasm for art.

This is why she is so threatening to Liella!: she attacks not just their chance of winning, but the legitimacy of their entire form.

For Wien, singing is proof.
For Liella!, singing is connection.

For Wien, the stage is a battlefield of selection.
For Liella!, the stage is a place where people’s paths, flaws, and hopes gather.

The episode then asks whether Liella! can answer not by argument, but by song.

## Kanon’s crisis: the coldness of victory

Kanon’s conversation in the bath is one of her best analytical moments. She does not deny that Wien is amazing. In fact, she gives her full credit: Wien creates a world that belongs to her, and she must love singing. Kanon is not threatened in a petty way.

But Kanon identifies what feels wrong:

> The emotion she receives from Wien is only the desire to win. A feeling as cold as ice.

This is the core contrast of the episode.

Kanon has learned to want victory, but she does not want victory to become the only emotion in the song. Season 1 ended with Kanon learning that losing hurts because people support you. Season 2 now tests whether that desire to win will freeze into something like Wien’s cold, solitary drive.

Kanon’s answer is no.

The difference is not that Liella! lacks competitive ambition. They very much want to win. The difference is that for Liella!, victory is not the source of the song. It is the hoped-for result of a song already filled with relationships.

## The no-practice day

Kanon’s decision to make the next day “no practice” is one of the most important leadership choices she has made.

On the surface it looks irresponsible. They are at training camp before the Tokyo regionals, facing a terrifying opponent who beat Sunny Passion. But Kanon recognizes that the group has become too fixated on improvement and victory. They are becoming tense, anxious, and instrumentalized. Even their desire to catch up has become oppressive.

So she stops practice.

This is not avoidance. It is recalibration.

She understands that Liella!’s strength is not produced only by repetition, discipline, and efficiency. It is produced by shared joy, memories, play, and the emotional surplus that makes them want to sing together in the first place.

That is why the snowball fight, takoyaki idea, skating, and random winter-camp play are not filler. They generate the feeling that becomes the song. Natsumi thinks it is useless because it does not produce monetizable content. The others realize afterward that it made them excited for practice again.

Kanon’s insight is:

> Rest and play are not outside the song.
> They are sometimes the source of the song.

This is a very mature answer to Wien. Wien intensifies willpower. Kanon restores joy.

## “What it truly means to sing”

The campfire scene gives Liella!’s definition. They talk about doing their best, being supported by everyone, growing together, and feeling a happiness that cannot come from anywhere else. Their songs overflow with that emotion.

That is the answer to Wien:

> Singing is not merely power.
> Singing is the overflow of shared effort, support, joy, pain, and growth.

Kanon realizes that this has been Liella! from the beginning. That is important because the answer is not newly invented to counter Wien. It is the essence of Liella! finally becoming conscious of itself.

From Episode 1 of Season 1 onward, Kanon’s voice has never existed as pure individual power. It returned because Keke believed in her. It stabilized because Chisato remembered her. It grew because Sumire, Ren, Kinako, Mei, Shiki, and Natsumi each brought new incomplete selves into the song. It became school-wide because Yuigaoka supported them.

So when Wien says “songs are power,” Kanon’s “you’re wrong” is not a denial that songs have power. It is a rejection of power as the primary meaning of song.

Songs are powerful because they connect.
They do not become meaningful merely because they overpower.

## Wien’s “Edelstein” versus Liella!’s “Sing! Shine! Smile!”

The contrast between performances is extremely clear.

Wien’s performance is dark, solitary, theatrical, vertically lit, and almost combative. The lyrics speak of ruined city streets, a fading voice, destroying weakness, believing in oneself, and converting tears into passion. It is a stunning performance, and the girls feel it. It has the “aura of a winner.”

But it is also isolating. Wien dominates the stage alone. The world of the song belongs to her.

Liella!’s “Sing! Shine! Smile!” is the opposite: bright, collective, floral, colorful, communal, and openly about connected hearts, this present moment, joy and pain, dreamers captivated by dreams, and not needing a reason to love what one loves.

The lyric “we don’t need a reason to love the things we love the most” is especially important for the whole series. It answers not just Wien, but many earlier character wounds:

-   Kanon did not need a reason to justify singing after failure.

-   Keke did not need a reason to love school idols beyond the fact that they moved her.

-   Sumire did not need to justify wanting the center.

-   Mei did not need to be embarrassed by loving school idols.

-   Natsumi did not need to reduce dreams to profit.

-   Ren did not need to turn inheritance into duty alone.

-   The first-years did not need to earn the right to begin before loving the dream.


The performance is therefore not just happy. It is ideologically precise.

# Kanon Shibuya: updated profile through S2E10

Kanon’s role in Episodes 9–10 is less about personal trauma and more about **protecting the moral meaning of Liella! under pressure**.

In Season 1, Kanon had to learn that she could sing.
In Season 2, Kanon has to defend what singing means.

This is a major evolution.

## Kanon as guardian of the whole group

In Episode 9, Kanon rejects the second-years-only plan immediately because it contradicts Liella!’s identity. This is important because she does not deny the tactical logic. She rejects the moral premise.

Her core belief has become:

> A performance that excludes part of Liella! in order to win is not a Liella! victory.

That is leadership. Not emotional passivity, not generic kindness, but the ability to say what cannot be compromised.

## Kanon as affective strategist

Episode 10 shows a different kind of intelligence. Kanon does not respond to Wien by demanding more practice. She responds by restoring the emotional source of their song.

That is subtle. Most sports/idol narratives would treat a stronger opponent as a reason for harsher training. Kanon realizes that harsher training might make them more technically prepared but emotionally colder. Liella! needs skill, yes, but skill without joy would make them imitate the very thing they are trying to answer.

So Kanon becomes an affective strategist: she manages not only practice but the emotional ecology that makes performance meaningful.

## Kanon and Wien

Wien is a dangerous foil for Kanon because she speaks directly to Kanon’s strongest individual gift: singing. She tries to isolate Kanon from Liella! by framing song as individual power and future-building.

Kanon’s answer is not to deny her own talent. It is to refuse the isolation of that talent.

That is a more mature version of her Season 1 recovery. In Season 1, she learned she could sing because she was not alone. In Season 2, she confronts a genius who says song is power, and replies that the true meaning of song is still not solitary force.

# Keke and Sumire: the season’s strongest relationship thread

Episodes 9–10 make Keke/Sumire one of Season 2’s emotional cores.

Keke’s hidden pressure could have remained a cheap dramatic device, but Sumire makes it relationally rich. She is the one who knows. She is the one Keke rejects. She is the one who tries to solve it badly. She is the one who exposes it. She is also the one who says most clearly that she wants Keke to stay.

Their relationship has become a kind of abrasive mutual salvation.

Keke gave Sumire school-idol sincerity when Sumire was retreating into showbiz self-protection.
Sumire gives Keke worldly stubbornness when Keke hides behind the purity of “everyone having fun.”

Keke wants the dream to remain happy.
Sumire insists that happiness requires confronting the conditions that could destroy it.

That is why “I hate you, but I love you” is not just a funny confession-like line. It is their entire dynamic.

# The first-years: from passengers to contributors

Episodes 9–10 are also crucial for the first-years.

Episode 9 shows them offering to step aside. Episode 10 corrects that by making them contribute to the song’s creation. This is the proper resolution.

The first-years do not prove themselves by sacrificing their place.
They prove themselves by helping create the performance.

That matters for each:

-   Kinako contributes lyric vulnerability and beginner sincerity.

-   Mei contributes musical feeling despite embarrassment.

-   Shiki contributes dance and analytic support despite self-deprecation.

-   Natsumi contributes energy, media instinct, and the reminder that experiences can be shared outward.


By Episode 10, the first-years are not merely “new members Liella! is carrying.” They are part of the song’s authorship.

# Liella! after S2E10

Liella! has now consciously defined itself against two temptations:

1.  **The tactical temptation:** exclude weaker members to increase the chance of winning.

2.  **The ideological temptation:** treat singing as solitary power and victory.


They reject both.

That does not mean they stop wanting to win. In fact, they probably want it more intensely than ever. But they now know what kind of victory they seek.

They want a victory that proves nine people, with unequal ability and different histories, can create a song stronger than solitary brilliance.

That is the core of the Tokyo regional arc.

# Updated Season 2 thesis

After Episodes 9–10, I would refine the Season 2 thesis this way:

> **A successful dream must remain reachable, shareable, and whole under pressure. When victory threatens to divide the group or freeze the song into pure ambition, Liella! must recover the joy, memory, and mutual support that made the dream worth chasing in the first place.**

Wien asks: who has the power to win?

Liella! answers: winning only matters if the song still carries everyone.

That is the deepest development of these episodes. Liella! is no longer simply trying to become stronger. It is learning what forms of strength would actually betray it.
