# S2E03-E04 V1 Deep Reading

Episodes 3–4 are an important pivot because Season 2 stops being only about “can Liella! recruit new first-years?” and becomes a more layered question:

> **What does Liella!’s light look like from the outside — as inspiration, as intimidation, as pressure, as rivalry, or as a place for people who think they do not belong?**

Episode 3 introduces the external competitive standard through Wien Margarete. Episode 4 brings the internal recruitment question back through Mei and Shiki. Together, they sharpen Season 2’s central tension: Liella! must be serious enough to become champions, but open enough that people who feel unqualified can still enter.

# Episode 3: favorites to win, but not winners yet

Episode 3 is titled **“Favorites to Win,”** and the title is almost cruel. Liella! is invited back to the Yoyogi School Idol Festival as the closing act, which means the outside world now treats them as a major group. They are no longer the newcomers struggling for permission to exist. They are expected to perform like a leading contender.

But the episode immediately exposes the uncomfortable truth:

> Liella! has reputation before achievement.

They have attention.
They have admiration.
They have school support.
They have Sunny Passion’s praise.
But they still have not actually won a major competition.

That creates the central anxiety of the episode. They are being treated as “favorites,” yet internally they know their record does not justify that status. This is especially important for Kanon, because Season 1 ended with her learning the pain of losing after everyone supported them. Episode 3 makes that pressure explicit. She wants to live up to the expectations of Sunny Passion, Yuigaoka, and everyone who now sees Liella! as the school’s pride.

## Sunny Passion’s ambition

The conversation with Sunny Passion is doing important work. Kanon asks why they praised Liella! so highly, and Sunny Passion answers that Liella! really did move them, especially on the Christmas stage. But they also make clear that they still intend to win again.

That matters because Sunny Passion are not just benevolent senpai. They are generous, but they are still competitors. Their desire to win back-to-back and carve their school and island into Love Live history gives them a scale of ambition Liella! still lacks.

Kanon’s instinct is still a little too pure: she can admire Sunny Passion’s dream and cheer them on, even though their victory would mean Liella!’s defeat. Sunny Passion gently points this out. This continues the Season 1 finale’s lesson: competition is not only about beautiful feelings. If everyone’s dreams matter, then victory also means denying someone else’s dream.

That is a hard truth for Kanon, because her leadership is built around making room for others. Love Live asks her to accept that making room is not enough. At some point, Liella! must take space.

## Wien Margarete as anti-Liella

Wien Margarete’s entrance is one of the sharpest antagonistic introductions in *Superstar!!* so far. She does not approach Liella! as a fan, a prospective member, or a supportive rival. She approaches Kanon almost like a talent scout or judge:

> “You’re one of the favorites to win, right? Then sing for me.”

That is a direct challenge to Kanon’s Season 1 recovery. Kanon learned that her voice could exist in relation — with Keke, Chisato, Liella!, the school, the audience. Wien isolates the voice again. She looks at Kanon and says, in effect:

> Prove your individual worth.

This is why Wien is immediately threatening. She does not merely want to beat Liella!. She implicitly rejects the collective school-idol logic that has saved Kanon.

Her song, “Butterfly Wing,” reinforces this. The lyrics are full of solitary struggle, enemies, self-polishing, flight without wings, and the idea that only those with fervent desire deserve to be chosen. That is almost the opposite of Liella!’s “connected lights” philosophy. Wien’s world is vertical, selective, aristocratic, and individual. Liella!’s world is horizontal, relational, and communal.

Wien’s challenge is therefore philosophical:

> **Is school idol greatness born from shared connection, or from solitary exceptional talent?**

Liella! loses badly enough that this question cannot be dismissed.

## Liella!’s second Yoyogi failure

Liella! receives honorable mention again. From the outside, that is not shameful. From their own perspective, it is devastating. They entered as the favorites and lost to a middle-schooler.

Kinako blames herself because she is the new, least experienced member. This is exactly the Season 2 problem made painful: if Liella! includes beginners, then those beginners will fear they are holding the group back. Sumire’s answer is important: they were all up there together, so the result belongs to all of them. That is school-idol ethics at its best. It protects Kinako from becoming the scapegoat.

But Wien then worsens the wound by dismissing Love Live itself. If Liella! are supposed favorites, then the competition must be easy. She even says Kanon at least has talent “for singing,” separating Kanon from the group as if the others are just dead weight.

This is a dangerous temptation for Kanon’s story. Season 1 made her the emotional center. Wien tries to convert her into an isolated star.

## Yuigaoka’s response: pride without complacency

The Yuigaoka students’ response is the emotional corrective. They congratulate Liella! for the honorable mention, call them the school’s pride and joy, and tell them to keep aiming to win.

This could have been written as empty consolation, but it lands differently because of the school’s history. Yuigaoka is still young. Its club accomplishments are sparse. Liella!’s achievements matter not because they are trophies, but because they give the school a shared identity.

The key phrase is **“Superstar!!”** The students call Liella! their superstars not because they have already become national champions, but because they have made Yuigaoka feel like a school with a future.

That is a crucial distinction:

> Winning gives external proof.
> Liella! has already given internal pride.

But the episode does not let them stop there. The students’ pride becomes not a permission to relax, but a reason to continue.

## “We’re all the center”

Kanon’s choice to perform without a center is the episode’s strongest answer to Wien. Wien isolates Kanon’s individual singing talent. Kanon responds by refusing a hierarchy inside Liella!.

When she says they are all the center — and that Yuigaoka’s students and the school itself are also center — she is expanding the idea of performance beyond individual prominence. This is not the same as saying individual talent does not matter. Kanon’s singing still matters enormously. But the meaning of the performance comes from the whole field around them.

“Go!! Restart” is therefore not just a recovery song after defeat. It is Liella!’s answer to the Yoyogi loss:

> We failed again, but this pain belongs to all of us.
> So does the restart.

This is also where Kinako is fully absorbed into Liella!’s emotional logic. Her weakness does not make the group less Liella!. The group’s willingness to carry weakness together is part of what makes them Liella!.

# Episode 4: Mei and Shiki, or the girls who think they are not idol-shaped

Episode 4 is titled **“The Two in the Science Room,”** and it returns Season 2 to the recruitment problem. But it is doing something more specific than Episode 1–2. Kinako’s issue was ordinary inadequacy: she admired Liella! but feared she lacked ability. Mei and Shiki’s issue is different:

> They both want connection, but each believes her own nature makes her unsuitable for it.

This is why the episode pairs the school idol club presidency plot with the Mei/Shiki plot. Both are about people saying, “I am not cut out for that,” when the real issue is fear.

## Kanon refuses formal centrality

The episode begins with the question of club president. Everyone assumes Kanon is the obvious choice because she started the group emotionally, brought everyone together, and remains Liella!’s interpretive center. Kinako, especially, sees her as the natural president.

But Kanon hesitates. Her reasoning is extremely important:

> Since they are trying to reinvent themselves as a group, the president should be someone else.

This is one of Kanon’s most mature decisions so far. She recognizes that if Liella! is to become more than the original Kanon-centered miracle, its formal structure cannot simply reproduce Season 1. She was the center of formation, but Season 2 requires distributed leadership.

Her suggestion of Chisato is also meaningful. Chisato has always been the person who gives the group bodily structure: training, rhythm, discipline, choreography, steadiness. Making Chisato president formalizes what she has already been doing.

So Kanon is not abdicating responsibility. She is refusing to let the group’s future collapse into her own centrality.

## Chisato as president

Chisato accepting the presidency is a quiet but major evolution. In Season 1, her wound was that she believed she needed to become strong alone before standing beside Kanon. Now she accepts a role where she must support everyone openly, not as hidden helper but as formal leader.

That is a nice continuation of her arc:

Season 1 Chisato: “I want to become strong enough to support Kanon.”
Season 2 Chisato: “I want to support this whole group as president.”

She is still nervous and says she may not be cut out for it, but she chooses to try. That choice mirrors what the episode later asks of Mei and Shiki.

The show is drawing a line between three “not cut out” claims:

-   Kanon: not cut out to be president.

-   Chisato: not cut out to be president.

-   Mei/Shiki: not cut out to be school idols.


The answer is not that everyone is secretly perfectly suited. The answer is that “not cut out” is often what fear sounds like before effort begins.

# Mei Yoneme: the hidden fan

Mei’s characterization becomes much sharper here. She is not merely a delinquent-looking girl with a temper. She is a painfully self-conscious school idol fan whose face, poor eyesight, and social defensiveness have made her seem hostile when she is actually overwhelmed.

The reveal of her room is very strong: Liella! poster, rare school idol media, collectibles, and intense fan knowledge. Keke’s reaction to the rare DVD is funny, but it also confirms that Mei is not casually interested. She is deeply invested.

That changes every previous Mei scene. Her aggression was not indifference. It was embarrassment.

Her core wound is:

> **She loves school idols, but cannot imagine herself as someone allowed to be one.**

She gives several reasons: her face, her personality, her inability to smile. These are not trivial insecurities. For a school idol, “smile” is almost ideological. To say “I can’t even put on a smile” is to say “I lack the basic expressive language this world requires.”

That makes Mei a strong addition to *Superstar!!*’s cast. She is not blocked by lack of talent exactly. She is blocked by self-image. She believes her outer presentation disqualifies her inner desire.

## Mei and Kanon

Kanon recognizes Mei’s “not cut out” logic because she has heard versions of it all season:

Kanon: I can’t sing.
Sumire: I’ll never be chosen.
Chisato: I can’t stand beside Kanon unless I prove myself.
Ren: I cannot repeat my mother’s school idol memories.
Kinako: I cannot keep up.
Mei: I cannot be a school idol because of my face/personality.

Kanon’s great strength is that she hears the desire underneath the refusal. With Mei, she does not argue “you are obviously idol material.” That would not work. She instead points to Mei’s loneliness and embarrassment, then connects it to Shiki.

Her key insight is that Mei and Shiki are alike:

> Both are easily embarrassed.
> Both feel lonely.
> Both hate that vulnerability.
> Both pretend they would rather be alone.

That is not just recruitment rhetoric. It is a correct diagnosis of their relationship.

# Shiki Wakana: the loner who engineers connection

Shiki initially appears as a deadpan science eccentric: microphones, devices, experiments, emotional flatness, practical observation. But Episode 4 reveals that her detachment is another form of loneliness.

Her backstory with Mei is important. Shiki says she was always alone and fine with that. Mei reached out to her, kept showing up, and eventually even joined the science club despite not caring about science. Shiki’s science room is therefore not simply her eccentric domain. It is the place Mei chose to remain with her.

This is why Shiki’s attempt to push Mei toward the school idol club hurts so much. On the surface, Shiki is sacrificing her own companionship so Mei can pursue her real dream. Emotionally, she is also denying that she needs Mei.

Her cruel line — telling Mei not to come to the science room if she is not really interested — is an attempt to cut the knot cleanly. She thinks she is freeing Mei. But because Mei’s presence is itself an act of care, Shiki is also rejecting the relationship that helped her stop being alone.

Shiki’s core wound:

> **She does not understand why someone would choose to stay with her, so she tries to release that person before becoming a burden.**

That is different from Mei, but complementary.

Mei thinks, “I am not idol material.”
Shiki thinks, “I am not someone worth staying for.”

The episode resolves both by making them admit that they can try only if they try together.

# Mei and Shiki as a pair

This is one of the strongest relationship constructions among the new first-years because neither is simply “saved” by the other. They have been mutually distorting themselves for each other.

Mei joined the science club to stay with Shiki, even though she wanted school idols.
Shiki tried to push Mei away because she thought Mei was sacrificing too much.
Mei interpreted Shiki’s self-erasure as rejection.
Shiki interpreted Mei’s loyalty as obligation.

The truth is simpler and more vulnerable:

> Mei wants school idols, but she also wants Shiki.
> Shiki says she wants to be alone, but she wants Mei.

Their shared confession is deliberately awkward. Mei says she does not really like school idols. Shiki points out that her face is red. Shiki says she cannot smile. Mei tells her she is cute. Shiki replies that Mei is cuter. It is comic, but it is also the first time they stop translating affection into avoidance.

The decisive line is:

> “I think I could try my best if you were there with me.”

That is very *Superstar!!*. Just as Kanon could sing because she was not alone, Mei and Shiki can try school idols because neither has to perform an acceptable self alone.

# Kanon Shibuya: updated profile through S2E4

Kanon’s Season 2 leadership is becoming less about rescuing people directly and more about **creating structures where other people can choose each other**.

In Episode 3, she responds to Wien’s isolating challenge by declaring that everyone is center. In Episode 4, she refuses to become club president and supports Chisato taking the role. Then she helps Mei and Shiki see that their relationship is not an obstacle to school idols, but the very condition that makes school idols possible for them.

This is a subtle but important evolution.

Season 1 Kanon often solved the emotional problem personally.
Season 2 Kanon increasingly distributes agency.

She does not become president.
She does not make herself center.
She does not recruit Mei alone.
She recognizes the bond between Mei and Shiki and lets that bond become the bridge.

That is a more mature form of leadership.

## Kanon versus Wien

Wien also opens a potential new pressure point for Kanon. Kanon has recovered from stage fright, but Wien’s attention singles out her singing talent in a way that could separate her from the group. This is dangerous because Kanon’s identity has been rebuilt relationally. If Wien keeps framing Kanon as the only truly talented one, Kanon may have to confront the question:

> Can she accept her individual excellence without letting it fracture Liella!’s collective identity?

That is probably the next major test for her.

# Chisato: updated profile

Chisato becoming club president is one of the cleanest continuations of her Season 1 arc.

She is no longer just Kanon’s childhood friend, dance coach, or emotional support. She is now the formal organizer of Liella!’s club identity. This is especially fitting because Season 2’s core problem is integration. Kanon is the emotional interpreter, but Chisato may be better suited to daily structure, training, and group balance.

Her presidency also gently decentralizes Kanon. If Kanon remains the emotional center, Chisato becomes the operational center.

That gives Liella! a healthier structure:

-   Kanon: voice, interpretation, emotional direction.

-   Chisato: training, organization, bodily/group cohesion.

-   Ren: institutional connection to the student council and Yuigaoka.

-   Keke: idol faith and dream pressure.

-   Sumire: presentation, showbiz realism, friction.

-   Kinako: beginner’s perspective.

-   Mei/Shiki: concealed desire and mutual dependence entering the group.


# Kinako after S2E4

Kinako continues to function as the bridge between Liella! and ordinary first-years. She is the one who talks about school idols in class, invites classmates to the roof, and accidentally creates recruitment openings. She is also still visibly new enough that Mei and Shiki can see joining as less impossible.

That matters. If only the original five were recruiting, Mei might continue to see Liella! as unreachable. Kinako’s presence proves that a first-year can enter.

Her function is not simply “sixth member.” She is the proof of permeability.

# Natsumi in the background

Natsumi’s brief presence in Episode 4 is useful foreshadowing. She appears exhausted from work, filming, editing, and monetization. While Mei and Shiki are dealing with the problem of belonging, Natsumi is already marked by a different problem:

> performance as labor, content, and money.

The next episode title makes that obvious, but even here she feels like the member whose relationship to performance will be least “pure” in the Love Live sense. That could be valuable, because Season 2 is already dealing with the difference between inspiration, reputation, and results. Natsumi may add the question of value in the economic/media sense.

# Liella! after S2E4

By the end of Episode 4, Liella! has eight members, but more importantly, the group’s structure has changed.

Season 1 Liella! was a five-member unit built around recovered dreams.
Early Season 2 Liella! is becoming a school club that can absorb very different kinds of first-years.

Kinako enters through ordinary admiration and effort.
Mei enters through hidden fandom and shame.
Shiki enters through loneliness, science-room attachment, and the desire to stay with Mei.

This expansion makes the “Song for me / Song for you / Song for all” chant feel different. When Mei awkwardly says “Song for me,” it is not just a ritual. It is a thesis:

> The girl who believed her face and personality made her unfit for school idols is now allowed to begin with herself.

That is the Season 2 version of Kanon’s original recovery.

# Updated Season 2 thesis

Episodes 1–2 established:

> **A dream that succeeds must learn how to remain reachable.**

Episodes 3–4 complicate that:

> **Remaining reachable does not mean lowering the dream. It means protecting the path by which frightened, awkward, ordinary, or self-denying people can choose to climb toward it.**

Wien represents greatness as selection: only the chosen, only the exceptional, only the ones with overwhelming desire.

Liella! represents greatness as connection: people who think they are not chosen can still become part of the song if they are willing to begin together.

That does not make Wien wrong about skill. Liella! lost. Her talent is real. But the philosophical opposition is now clear:

> **Wien asks who deserves to be chosen.
> Liella! asks what people can become when they are connected.**

That is the strongest development of these two episodes, and it gives Season 2 a much sharper conflict than simple member recruitment.
