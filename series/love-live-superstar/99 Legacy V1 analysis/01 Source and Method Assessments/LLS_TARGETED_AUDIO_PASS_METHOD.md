# Targeted Audio Pass Method

I reviewed the manifest. It contains the ten episodes selected for the targeted audio-and-Japanese-dialogue pass, in chronological order:

**S1E1 → S1E10 → S1E11 → S2E4 → S2E6 → S2E11 → S3E2 → S3E6 → S3E8 → S3E12.** manifest

I’ll treat this as a **progressive reconstruction of the cast through performed voice**, rather than merely adding occasional vocal notes to the existing plot analyses.

# Running character-profile method

For each episode, I’ll produce two interconnected layers.

## 1\. Episode-level analysis

The ordinary episode analysis will still cover:

-   narrative and thematic function;

-   visual direction and character staging;

-   relationship development;

-   performance scenes;

-   meaningful Japanese phrasing;

-   how the episode fits the larger seasonal architecture.


Audio will be integrated wherever delivery changes the meaning of the written line: irony, embarrassment, emotional concealment, hesitation, contempt, teasing, authority, vulnerability, and so forth.

## 2\. Cumulative voice-and-personality profiles

Each featured character will receive a running profile covering:

### Vocal baseline

-   perceived pitch range and vocal placement;

-   softness, brightness, roughness, breathiness, resonance, or nasality;

-   conversational speed and rhythmic habits;

-   articulation and sentence completion;

-   characteristic pauses, sighs, laughs, gasps, murmurs, and interjections.


These will be descriptive character-performance observations, not claims about the actor’s physiology.

### Japanese speech identity

-   formality and politeness;

-   sentence-final forms;

-   pronouns and forms of address;

-   dialectal or foreign-language elements;

-   contractions, roughness, feminine styling, theatricality, or unusual lexical choices;

-   repeated expressions and catchphrases.


This will be especially important for characters such as Keke, Sumire, Mei, Shiki, Natsumi, Wien, and Tomari, whose personalities are partly constructed through stylized speech.

### Emotional leakage

I’ll track the points where the voice reveals more than the words admit:

-   Wien sounding affected while verbally dismissing something;

-   Sumire losing theatrical projection when she anticipates rejection;

-   Mei becoming louder or rougher to conceal embarrassment;

-   Shiki’s minute changes around Mei;

-   Natsumi slipping from content-creator performance into sincerity;

-   Tomari’s composure becoming protective fear;

-   Chisato’s gentleness acquiring unmistakable authority.


### Public, private, and relational voices

A character may not have one stable “voice.” I’ll distinguish how she sounds:

-   in public versus alone;

-   during club business versus emotional confrontation;

-   with juniors versus seniors;

-   with her closest relational partner;

-   before, during, and after a performance;

-   when leading, following, teasing, defending, or asking for help.


This should make relationships audible rather than relying only on dialogue content. Keke’s voice with Sumire, Chisato’s voice with Kanon, Shiki’s voice with Mei, Natsumi’s voice with Tomari, and Wien’s voice with Kanon should each have their own grammar.

### Speaking voice versus singing identity

For songs and rehearsals, I’ll examine:

-   whether speaking traits survive into singing;

-   who projects outward and who sings inwardly;

-   softness, precision, force, theatricality, restraint, or emotional openness;

-   line distribution and vocal pairing;

-   how arrangement and staging position a character within the collective;

-   changes in confidence across the selected episodes.


I will keep a clear distinction between **diegetic characterization** and the polished studio production of the seiyuu performances.

# Progressive evidence standard

I’ll rebuild the profiles in episode order rather than allowing later characterization to overwrite the first impression immediately.

Each update will be marked implicitly or explicitly as one of four things:

-   **Established:** repeatedly audible and visually supported.

-   **Refined:** an existing interpretation gains a more precise vocal explanation.

-   **Revised:** the audio complicates or corrects something inferred from subtitles and frames.

-   **Provisional:** an intriguing pattern that needs another episode before being treated as characteristic.


For example, S1E1 will establish Kanon and Keke without importing everything we already know from Season 3. S1E11 can then show exactly what changed in Kanon’s voice. Later episodes may reveal that an early habit was situational rather than a permanent personality trait.

# The longitudinal comparisons built into this set

The manifest is particularly strong because it contains several useful before-and-after structures.

**S1E1 → S1E11:**
Kanon’s self-protective baseline, performance anxiety, first relational opening, and ability to sing while physically alone but psychologically accompanied.

**S1E10 → S3E6:**
Keke and Sumire’s early hostile trust versus their mature intimacy, concern, and post-graduation anxieties.

**S2E4:**
The strongest concentrated baseline for Mei and Shiki: rough emotional concealment beside minimal, precision-coded expression.

**S2E6 → S3E8:**
Natsumi as monetized media persona versus Natsumi speaking directly as a dreamer and older sister; Tomari’s skepticism can then be read as both philosophy and protective love.

**S2E11 → S3E2:**
Wien as solitary proof-seeker and contemptuous rival versus Wien inside an actual relational performance structure, while retaining her severity and characteristic 「ふん！」.

**S3E12:**
A final-state audit of the entire cast: who has softened, who has become more authoritative, which mannerisms remain, and whether inheritance produces imitation or distinct new voices.
