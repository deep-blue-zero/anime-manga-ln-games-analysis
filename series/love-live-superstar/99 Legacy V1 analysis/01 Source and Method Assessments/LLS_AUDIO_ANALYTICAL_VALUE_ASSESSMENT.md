# Audio Analytical Value Assessment

Yes. **Complete episode audio would materially improve characterization**, especially for characters whose personality is carried as much by delivery as by literal dialogue. The current *Superstar!!* source already gives us a strong account of each character’s personality, wound, artistic philosophy, and relational function; audio would add a more precise **paralinguistic layer**—how those traits are enacted moment by moment rather than merely stated. Love Live Superstar Understandi…

It would probably refine the existing interpretation rather than overturn it, but in several cases the refinement could be substantial.

# What audio reveals that transcripts and frames cannot

## Prosody as personality

A transcript records what someone says. Audio reveals:

-   whether the line is teasing, defensive, contemptuous, exhausted, performative, or sincere;

-   how quickly the character speaks;

-   where she hesitates;

-   which words she stresses;

-   whether her voice rises, tightens, cracks, softens, or becomes breathless;

-   whether a pause reflects uncertainty, calculation, embarrassment, or suppressed feeling;

-   how far her public voice differs from her private voice.


That is especially valuable in *Superstar!!*, where several girls use a habitual persona to protect something more vulnerable.

Sumire’s theatrical grandiosity, Natsumi’s influencer voice, Ren’s formality, Mei’s roughness, Shiki’s flatness, and Wien’s contempt are not merely semantic traits. They are performed vocal systems.

## Micro-reactions

Frames can show that Wien turns away or narrows her eyes. A transcript can show that she says “hmph.” Audio tells us whether the sound is:

-   genuinely contemptuous;

-   irritated;

-   defensive;

-   embarrassed;

-   reluctantly impressed;

-   nearly affectionate.


Wien’s recurring **「ふん！」** is the clearest example. By comparing instances across Seasons 2 and 3, we could trace whether the same vocalization changes meaning over time:

> early: “You are beneath me.”
> middle: “I refuse to admit that affected me.”
> late: “Do not think caring has made me less severe.”

The word does not change. Its **intonational function** does. That kind of development is almost invisible in subtitle-only analysis.

## Relationship grammar

Audio can identify how characters sound specifically around different people.

Sumire may use a different defensive rhythm with Keke than with Kanon.
Chisato may sound softer with Kanon but firmer when acting as club president.
Wien may become more clipped around Liella! as rivals, then less rigid when living with Kanon.
Tomari’s analytical composure may change much more around Natsumi than around the other girls.

This lets us distinguish a generic personality trait from a relationship-specific behavior.

# Characters who would benefit most

## Wien Margarete

Probably the single largest beneficiary.

Her characterization depends heavily on:

-   clipped dismissal;

-   prideful pauses;

-   the “hmph” vocal tic;

-   contempt shading into embarrassment;

-   a refusal to sound emotionally dependent;

-   gradual relaxation without losing sharpness.


Her development is intentionally not expressed by turning her into a conventionally sweet character. It is expressed through **small reductions in hostility**, growing willingness to answer, less rigid denial, and warmth leaking through the same severe voice.

Audio would help us describe her “tsun” quality much more precisely than the transcripts alone.

## Shiki Wakana

Shiki is another high-value case because a transcript may make her sound simply flat. Audio can reveal the micro-inflections inside that flatness.

Questions audio could answer include:

-   How emotionally neutral is she really?

-   Does she become fractionally faster or softer around Mei?

-   When does deadpan delivery function as comedy, and when as self-protection?

-   How much emotion is present in pauses rather than pitch?

-   Does becoming center alter her performance voice?


For Shiki, the difference between “emotionless” and “minimally but precisely expressive” is central to understanding her.

## Mei Yoneme

Mei’s rough speech is only half the joke. The other half is the mismatch between her intimidating surface and her uncontrollable fan reactions.

Audio would clarify:

-   how quickly aggression collapses into fluster;

-   whether she raises her volume to hide embarrassment;

-   how her school-idol knowledge leaks into her voice;

-   the contrast between speaking about idols generally and addressing Shiki directly;

-   whether her eventual confidence sounds like genuine ease or courageous effort through embarrassment.


Her voice is likely one of the clearest expressions of her theme: hidden love repeatedly escaping its defensive enclosure.

## Natsumi Onitsuka

Natsumi has multiple vocal selves:

-   the OniNatsu content-creator persona;

-   the calculating business voice;

-   the insecure failed dreamer;

-   the increasingly serious Liella! member;

-   the older sister speaking directly to Tomari.


The transition between “performing sincerity for an audience” and “speaking sincerely without an audience” is essential to her arc. Audio would make those layers far easier to separate.

Her confession to Tomari in Season 3 would be particularly valuable because the content is already direct; the delivery would tell us how difficult direct emotional language is for someone accustomed to converting everything into persona and content.

## Keke Tang

Keke’s energy is not simply that she talks loudly. Her characterization likely depends on tempo, breath, emotional acceleration, code-switching, and the way idol enthusiasm overwhelms ordinary conversational pacing.

Audio would help distinguish:

-   comic fanaticism from serious conviction;

-   performative outrage at Sumire from genuine hurt;

-   homesickness and family anxiety beneath exuberance;

-   the degree to which her Japanese voice changes in Shanghai or with family;

-   the emotional difference between “school idols are wonderful” as fandom and as an adult vocational claim.


Keke’s surface is so animated that the quiet moments are disproportionately important.

## Sumire Heanna

Sumire’s voice should reveal the distance between **performed stardom** and the girl underneath.

Her most useful contrasts would be:

-   “Galaxy!” projection versus ordinary conversation;

-   professional showbiz explanation versus defensive boasting;

-   sharp banter with Keke versus genuine concern for Keke;

-   confident demands for the center versus the tonal collapse when she expects not to be chosen;

-   her public stage voice versus private uncertainty about her future.


Her catchphrase and poses tell us that she creates herself theatrically. Audio would tell us when the theatrical self is empowering and when it is compensatory armor.

## Chisato Arashi

Chisato’s subtleties are easy to understate because she speaks gently. Audio could reveal how much authority exists beneath that gentleness.

The important distinctions are:

-   playful childhood-friend warmth;

-   quiet personal concern;

-   uncompromising dance discipline;

-   club-president authority;

-   the tone she uses when encouraging Kanon versus releasing Kanon;

-   whether she becomes more formal or more direct when she stops acting as only Kanon’s supporter.


Her voice could help substantiate the idea that she is not merely soft; she is **softly severe**.

## Ren Hazuki

Ren’s characterization depends strongly on register and composure.

Audio would sharpen:

-   her formal, refined public voice;

-   how much of that voice is upbringing and how much is defensive duty;

-   the moment grief breaks through the institutional mask;

-   the contrast between student-council authority and gaming embarrassment;

-   her gradual ability to sound like an ordinary teenager;

-   the warmth in her later conversations with Kinako.


Ren’s comedy is especially dependent on hearing the dignified voice placed in undignified situations.

## Kanon Shibuya

Kanon may gain less in terms of basic identification—her psychology is already very legible—but audio would deepen her tremendously as a **voice-centered protagonist**.

We could examine:

-   how she minimizes her hopes conversationally;

-   the dry or self-deprecating quality of her early speech;

-   how her breathing and vocal tension change around evaluation;

-   the difference between talking for herself and speaking for someone else;

-   when hesitation turns into moral clarity;

-   how her public-speaking voice develops from Season 1 through graduation;

-   whether her sung voice becomes more open, forward, or emotionally relaxed across the series.


Kanon’s arc is literally about recovering and then entrusting a voice. Hearing that voice across three seasons would give the analysis a much firmer performance basis.

# Singing deserves a separate analysis layer

Complete audio would also allow a distinction between **character as speaker** and **character as singer**.

Useful questions would include:

-   Does a character preserve her speaking personality when singing?

-   Who sings with the most direct projection, softness, precision, theatricality, or restraint?

-   How does Wien’s elite-coded intensity differ from Kanon’s relational warmth?

-   Does Sumire sound more secure when performing than when speaking privately?

-   Does Shiki’s understated personality become more expressive in song?

-   Does Kinako’s vocal confidence change as she becomes a center and successor?

-   How are line distributions used to express narrative authority?

-   Which voices blend, contrast, answer, or support one another?


The songs are studio-produced and should not be treated as transparent measurements of fictional technical ability. But they are still authored performances. Vocal arrangement, line assignment, phrasing, and contrast are part of the series’ characterization.

# Full audio versus selected video clips

Both are useful, but for different reasons.

## Complete audio tracks

These are best for:

-   detecting repeated vocal habits;

-   tracing changes across seasons;

-   checking offscreen speakers;

-   recovering laughter, sighs, breaths, murmurs, and reactions omitted from subtitles;

-   comparing the same character across many contexts;

-   auditing whether a supposed trait is recurring or based on one memorable scene.


The full track serves as the evidentiary archive.

## Targeted video clips

These provide the highest value per minute because they preserve voice, facial expression, posture, editing, distance, and reaction shots together.

The most useful clips would be approximately **20–90 seconds**, centered on:

-   emotional reversals;

-   private conversations;

-   performance rehearsals;

-   arguments;

-   comic persona breaks;

-   recurring catchphrases;

-   major speeches;

-   the transition immediately before and after a song.


For example, Wien’s “hmph” works best when we can hear the sound and see whether she raises her chin, looks away, closes her eyes, or reacts after being praised. Voice alone gives prosody; video gives the complete defensive gesture.

# The ideal bundle format

The strongest bundle would contain:

1.  the complete episode audio track;

2.  subtitle and dialogue indexes with accurate start/end timestamps;

3.  contact sheets and full-resolution frames;

4.  a small folder of manually or automatically extracted key clips;

5.  markers for songs, rehearsals, arguments, and emotional climax scenes.


The full audio does not need to be examined linearly with equal attention. The efficient method is:

> transcript identifies important scene → dialogue index gives timestamp → audio/video clip is audited → repeated behavior is compared across episodes.

That turns audio into a systematic characterization source rather than an impressionistic extra.

# Expected impact

I would rank the likely gains this way:

**Very high:** vocal mannerisms, public/private persona differences, comedy, relationship-specific delivery, Wien’s tsun characterization, Shiki’s micro-expression, Natsumi’s persona layers.

**High:** emotional escalation, leadership tone, vulnerability, speaker attribution, recurring catchphrases, dialect/register, offscreen reactions.

**Moderate to high:** fictional performance identity through singing, provided we distinguish studio production from diegetic ability.

**Lower:** large-scale thematic interpretation. Audio probably would not overturn the central reading of *Superstar!!* as a story of wounded voice, group formation, expansion, and inheritance. It would make that reading **more embodied, character-specific, and evidentially precise**.

The best formulation is:

> Frames tell us how a character is staged.
> Transcripts tell us what she says.
> Audio tells us how much of herself she permits the line to reveal.
