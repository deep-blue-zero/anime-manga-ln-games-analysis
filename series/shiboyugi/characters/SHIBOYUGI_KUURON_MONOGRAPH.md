# Kūron

九龍 carries the ruby クーロン and identifies the person who oversees the organization conducting the lethal games. He introduces it as his only name. An earlier illustrated account in V02 gives Yūki's knowledge of a rumored founder called Kūron. In the V08 and V09 scenes where he is directly encountered, he looks like a boy and speaks with the ease of a practiced adult. He gives the player Shiro help interpreting her family archive after having her brought to him under coercion. Hakushi, a retired player investigating the institution, meets him as someone with information of her own to test. Together the encounters establish a man who makes concessions about what someone may learn and tests what they will do with that knowledge. His claims to historical continuity and kinship allow narrower conclusions than the evidence for his current office. This portrait contains spoilers through the Japanese V10 endpoint. V02 S03-I01, V08 S20 P0053–0162/S22 P0109–0177, V09 S24 P0003–0038.

## A family resemblance and a historical claim

Shiro finds archaic publications in an old house belonging to relatives. She is stopped and taken by people she recognizes from their clothing and vehicle as associated with the games. She wakes bound to a chair. The boy across from her identifies her first as 蔵里士郎, Kurari Shirō, and then identifies himself. His greeting, plain 「君」 and apology for appearing in a child's body make an oddly civil opening to a meeting whose terms he controls. He says he took notice while looking through newcomers' profiles, recognized her surname and investigated. Her own discoveries have created an institutional risk. He also admits he had not worked out how to respond and had not long known someone like her existed. His ability to find and retain a specific audience is demonstrated. The account does not support an administrator who already sees the full significance of everything he finds. V08 S20 P0053–0090/P0109–0116. [The coerced meeting](../readings/SHIBOYUGI_V08_DEEP_READING.md#sby-v08-e007--the-archive-and-the-person-who-can-read-it).

He asks Shiro to verify 九重津々二, Kokonoe Tsutsuji, in the recoverable family register. She recognizes that name. Kūron calls Tsutsuji his great-grandchild and says 九重国辰, Kokonoe Kunitatsu, describes his own place in that social frame. He counts the relation to Shiro and treats her suggestion of 襲名, succession to a name, as a sufficiently useful explanation, then calls the actual matter more complicated. In the same conversation he refers to 代替わり, a predecessor and an original holder, sometimes using 九龍 in the third person. Roughly a year later Shiro sees the same person with a year's apparent physical growth. He later speaks of having waited nearly three hundred years. A verified historical name, kinship testimony, changing appearance and a long first-person timescale support a deliberate complication of identity. The novel supplies no mechanism that makes these statements all literal about one body's history, and Kunitatsu remains a proposed historical identification rather than a confirmed present surname. V08 S20 P0073–0116/P0956–0963, S22 P0114–0119/P0172–0177.

Kūron offers a present-language rendering of an old handbill and proceeds to explain why the publications matter. He identifies their issuers as a body in the organizers' lineage and corrects himself toward that formulation because the institution took different forms over time. His rationale for disclosure is practical. Shiro can keep searching, exposing the material is costly to the organization, and she could already have copied it. He can avoid letting her search alone and give her a reason to act on a project he understands. His explicit claim that arbitrary seizure of someone's property is against their way of doing things sits beside the fact of the forcible meeting. The two acts narrow what readers can infer about how he applies the claim. Shiro keeps the materials, receives information few players have and then seeks independent corroboration among agents, spectators, retired players and other connected people. In her view no later game becomes easier because of kinship. Access to history is actual assistance. Preferential play and the precise origin story remain unproven. V08 S20 P0124–0162.

## Interest in what ambitious people do

Kūron treats an exceptional appetite for distinction as something he knows from himself. With Shiro he proposes a similarity, 「僕もそうだったからだ」, and makes disclosure a prospective stimulus. After her dispute with Awahime has cost her a route to Yūki, he finds her again. He now addresses Shiro as 「我が子孫」, declares that out-of-game disputes are free except where they threaten the organization and calls his presence mere curiosity. He also admits he lacks some details, mocks her transparent appetite and recalls that she similarly failed to recruit Hakushi. The encounter has the consequence of Shiro recovering enough purpose to declare herself his future successor and say she will find another candidate. He answers that he will go on expecting things from her. The possible motives include sympathy, amusement, concern for finding a successor and a deliberately provoking intervention. The effect on Shiro is clearer than the distribution of those motives or the extent of his surveillance. V08 S20 P0141–0148/P0956–0988. [What the incumbent tells Shiro and Hakushi](../readings/SHIBOYUGI_V08_DEEP_READING.md#sby-v08-e012--the-reward-and-the-contest).

Hakushi's meeting gives Kūron a different kind of interlocutor. He comes to a public bench with a cartoon bag as their agreed identifying mark, repeats her greeting 「ごきげんよう」 and answers surprise at his appearance with an apology. Later he raises the possibility that she is goading the organization to kill her or has never fully entrusted her aim to her pupil. Hakushi promptly rejects the account of her motives and asks whether it reflects trouble finding his own successor. He laughs and says 「一本取られた」, then shifts away from a line of questioning in which he thinks he may receive more damage than she does. This is a real change in conversational behavior under counterpressure. He does not need unquestioned deference to continue a useful exchange. He also tells her he does not know Shiro's exact words in the rejected bargain with her. The bag, bench and easy laugh give a limited ordinary counterpart to the interrogation room. The selection of a bag as a marker does not settle private tastes, and the source leaves household life and erotic or intimate history unknown. His own remark about a founder's lack of family interest sits uneasily beside the kinship he personally performs with Shiro. V08 S20 P0109–0114, S22 P0109–0156/P0172–0177.

## Sharing evidence and keeping the office

Kūron calls game ninety-nine a 継承戦, a succession contest. He says the target comes from a time when a dangerous performance was easier and less routinely lethal. He accepts that a change is possible and that the current threshold is severe, but asks how past candidates would feel if the requirement were reduced after their attempts. Hakushi says she would be unable to accept it. His argument is intelligible as fidelity to prior challengers and also leaves intact a bar he says has stood since the earlier form of the games. He expresses dislike of Shiro and Yūki fighting outside the games over the prize. He reads their estrangement as carrying a possible exclusion from game ninety-nine, with carefully qualified knowledge of what Yūki understands. The reader later sees that Shiro had in fact already told Yūki about reported nomination and sharing provisions in an earlier private call. The evidence for those particulars is Shiro's cross-checked account. Kūron's words help explain the succession goal and who matters, without demonstrating a completed nomination, allocation or handover. A different, young-looking official visibly adjudicates a case and orders treatment during V10's game; appearance supplies no identity link to Kūron or transfer of his authority. V08 S22 P0150–0177, V09 S16 P0010–0043, V10 S23 P0357–0376 and S26 P0007–0024.

The question of the 「三十の壁」 shows him capable of a more testable answer. Asked whether the supposed curse near a player's thirtieth outing exists, he distinguishes a shared belief from a departure deliberately imposed by the organizers or detectable, he says, in records of survival and injury. He actually hands Hakushi a tablet with a document full of graphs. She has her own experience and testimony from other players, so he asks about the strength of the impression rather than stopping at institutional denial. He suggests that once a participant is highly accomplished, defeats tend to be surprising and especially hard to account for. He tests the idea against Hakushi's body, and her inward recall of her eighty-third and ninetieth games offers particular cases. This does not disclose the study's sample, metric or controls or establish that experience can be fully explained by his proposal. The act of furnishing material and listening to contrary experience is firmer evidence for Kūron's method than the asserted result is for institution-wide fairness. V09 S24 P0008–0038. [The graphs and the Wall of Thirty](../readings/SHIBOYUGI_V09_DEEP_READING.md#sby-v09-e008--the-wall-of-thirty-as-belief-and-as-claim).

He refers to himself as 「僕」 when offering a guess or a personal stake, 「我々」 or 「僕ら」 when explaining an organization, and 「我が子孫」 when situating Shiro inside alleged shared history. His individual and collective assertions require their own evidence even when he moves between them quickly. He is measured, capable of quietly cutting language with Shiro and capable of taking a sharp answer from Hakushi. There is no uniform register that makes every formal statement authoritative. The recorded wording and narratorial descriptions are prose evidence of voice. Performance is outside this witness. When an inquiry risks independently exposing the organization, the strongest prior case favors a controlled encounter and enough disclosure to retain leverage. When a skilled interlocutor arrives with her own evidence, he can adjust his hypothesis and share internal material without granting unrestricted access. For a consequential new exception, a precedent involving the same authority and a real decision would matter more than inference from his apparent age, the extent of the organization, or his descriptions of what it normally does.
