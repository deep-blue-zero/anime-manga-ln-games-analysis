# Chie

Chie (智恵, チエ; SBY-P019) calls herself an ordinarily capable person whose skills fail to stand out. Scrap Building turns that description into a liability when it makes the survivors assign one of their peers the smallest contribution. By then Chie has taken the first-floor lead, carried the player whose earlier rescue she would have declined, and placed her in a voting booth she could not enter and use alone. Chie has a sharp case against the way contributions are counted. She also has a stake in discounting the kinds of danger that other people bore. Both matter to how she dies. This portrait contains spoilers through V10 of the Japanese novels. Chie's direct evidence is concentrated in V02. The ordinary life and earlier games that brought her there receive no independent scene.

## A plausible place in several groups

“智恵でーす” and “器用貧乏” are her own introduction. She thinks this is her fourth game. She advertises enough practical breadth to fill a gap without demanding a position from the people she has just met. Yūki sees a girl who could fit into any clique without belonging to one, a schoolroom analogy prompted by what she sees and hears there. Their actual schooling, Chie’s finances, and what led her to enter the games remain unshown. Doing a little of everything is a self-placement within an impromptu team. The story subsequently tests it with actions instead of confirming a school biography. V02 S12 P0085–0088. [V02, first introductions](../readings/SHIBOYUGI_V02_DEEP_READING.md#sby-v02-e001--who-can-claim-experience)

Chie gives the initial anti-rescue position a social premise. She acknowledges 仲間意識, a sense of fellowship, then describes the group as a means of surviving together. Finding the separated Kotoha would cost scarce light and expose more people, and Chie also attributes the predicament to Kotoha’s own blunder. Keito seconds the view and emphasizes resource depletion. Chie brings in personal responsibility and the limit of the obligation. Her “しょうがないんじゃないですかね” and “ばちは当たらない” make abandonment sound permissible and regrettable at the same time. Those phrases soften the way she delivers the judgment while preserving its substance. Yūki considers the survivors’ calculation reasonable, then undertakes the rescue anyway. Chie’s acquiescence to their practical assessment has a firmer basis than any claim that she would always prefer to let others die. V02 S12 P0375–0394. [V02, the cost of rescue](../readings/SHIBOYUGI_V02_DEEP_READING.md#sby-v02-e003--when-the-cost-of-help-includes-the-judge)

She and Keito become separated from Mishiro during the encounter with the beast. Keito gives the account to Yūki. In the same reunion Chie can answer Kotoha’s genuine question about whether hiding should have failed because of smell. Her casual reference to 防腐処理 brings a familiar feature of the altered body to bear on a specific observation. Their earlier hiding worked, and Yūki independently finds the explanation plausible. Chie knows how to make a very practical contribution to somebody else's reasoning. When Yūki later sets out for Mishiro, Chie asks what in their encounters could possibly make her want to save the woman who challenged her. Chie accepts Kotoha’s weight before she understands the motive for Yūki’s decision. It is an actual change of circumstances. Carrying someone already out of immediate trouble, with the group heading on, is a different cost from sending people back into a hazardous floor to find them. Her surprise at Yūki’s choice and the help she gives can coexist. V02 S12 P0692–0707/P0739–0752. [V02, choosing to return](../readings/SHIBOYUGI_V02_DEEP_READING.md#sby-v02-e004--giving-up-an-advantage-and-choosing-to-return)

## What the person on her back knows

On the first floor Chie walks in front with Kotoha on her back and Keito well behind because appearances of safety can lie. While carrying Kotoha, she evaluates Yūki as お人好し, a soft touch, on the strength of her rescues. Kotoha disagrees and uses Chie's curiosity to set up a private exchange. Chie says “聞きたい聞きたい” easily. She lowers her voice when she realizes the answer has strings attached, asks what the proposed favor means, then assents to treating the information as a verbal debt before hearing it. Their exchange has the conversational shape of two people finding out how much of a situation they share. Chie can retrieve and inspect her paper, challenge Kotoha’s claim that the outfit itself is meaningful, admit unfamiliar words, and let the explanation reach a conclusion that visibly frightens her. Her statement that she has never voted in an election explains one missed association. It gives neither an age nor a position on politics. Kotoha’s thought about the ignorance of young people belongs to Kotoha, whose access to the clue was superior here.

Chie learns that Kotoha expected a forced selection, inferred from the kit and the design of the building, and kept that inference to herself. Kotoha thanks her in the concrete form of a promise to withhold a vote against her and asks for the same consideration. Others arrive before the passage shows a completed reply to that specific request. The prior agreement to hear the information and the later statement about Chie's ballot establish different things. Kotoha subsequently reports honoring her promise. Chie helps her into a chair anyway, at the last point when the booths still allow contact. The scene supplies both a plausible instrumental reason to help and an instance of dependence with consequences beyond a moment of politeness. Historical knowledge of the final test is a differential advantage. Readiness to move a companion across a floor is Chie’s contribution to getting them both there. V02 S12 P0925–0971/P1002–1006/P1099–1103. [V02, the vote and the promise](../readings/SHIBOYUGI_V02_DEEP_READING.md#sby-v02-e005--taking-a-secret-ballot-at-its-word)

## Losing the means of negotiation

The briefing presses the players to choose the person whose contribution they judge smallest. Earlier they assigned leads by rock-paper-scissors, which made their chances of taking evident risks unequal. Mishiro reasons afterward that Keito, Kotoha, Mishiro herself and Yūki contributed in obvious ways and that Chie did little beyond carrying Kotoha. The reader has seen Chie lead on the safest-looking floor and get Kotoha into her booth. The strength of Mishiro’s point is the difference in demonstrated exposure. Its weakness is how much valuable work it treats as negligible once the most dramatic sacrifices are available for comparison. Chie’s “じゃんけんでランダムに決めた” answers that weakness directly. Earlier she had herself appealed to decisive individual responsibility in the case of Kotoha’s injury. At the ballot she distinguishes an allocated chance to take risk from individual blame. Those views can be reconciled as claims about different responsibilities. Her position is also plainly the one that might save her now.

Chie says she voted for Keito and suspects Kotoha broke her promise. She then offers a different criterion and a personal indictment, claiming that Keito abandoned Mishiro first and smiled while escaping. The smile and the relief Chie reads into it are attributed to Chie. The disclosed events establish the split and later return, then Keito’s self-reported vote for Chie, with no independent view of the alleged expression. Chie’s objection to the group’s verdict need not depend on treating that allegation as settled. Her proposal to vote for someone a person actually wants gone is harsher than an appeal to equal opportunity. It also exposes the difference between the apparently neutral criterion and the hostility the process may authorize. Mishiro can already recognize her own use of a convenient scapegoat. Yūki later wonders whether her own way of deciding was irresponsible. Nobody in the locked booth responds where Chie can use the response.

Chie moves from intimate appeals over the supposed promise to the blunt “なんとか言えよ” and repeated threats of a curse. The switch is an observed written response to mortal pressure. Her early drawn-out friendliness, quietly repeated question about Yūki, and last short imperatives belong to the same person in successive information states. In Mishiro's hearing, the sound of Chie's voice and pounding stops, a brief sound of crying follows, and she falls silent. Four emerge and Chie’s door stays closed. The death is established by the sequence and subsequent treatment. Mishiro could not see the release in Chie’s booth or inspect it, and the survivors’ recollections give a strong reconstruction of the vote without a checked ballot record. No authority announces the final individual tally to the witnesses. V02 S12 P1063–1085/P1099–1117. ## The boundary of the portrait

Chie is likely to join a cooperative arrangement expressed in reciprocal terms, to question a discretionary rescue when she sees high group cost and personal blame, and to accept a concrete burden that falls within the ongoing route. The building supports that narrower contrast between perceived obligations. If a colleague offers new information privately and makes its terms legible, Chie shows curiosity and enough pliancy to pause over the price and continue. Whether she would obtain that information without a companion or honor any stronger agreement in another state is unknown. Terminal accusation should not be treated as evidence of her everyday baseline. Her continuing role is instead the way a participant’s social competence and practical maintenance are rendered uncountable at the moment her life is made to depend on comparison.

We hear her adaptation to an informal companion, to a veteran, and finally to adversaries she cannot see. Her reported voting inexperience and the ordinary verbal cadence give modest evidence of how she talks about herself. The novels do not establish her leisure, food, stable aesthetic tastes, romantic interests, intimate history, or what care in her civilian relationships looks like. The words and their written emphasis are available evidence. They supply no performed voice.
