# Apprenticeship and succession

The industry is dangerous enough that raw talent and solitary effort cannot be relied on to carry an entrant through. Training is generally acquired through people who have their own incomplete accounts of what works. An actual encounter between two skilled pupils can put outside help, bodily cost, evaluation and their relationship to their teacher on different sides of a contest. A person can hand on a commitment without disclosing a technique, and can teach generously while wanting another person's future act to count as her own. The distinct mechanisms are tested best across lineages. It matters that this work precedes the later prospect of succeeding to an office. An old 99-clear objective can shape Yūki without visibly conferring any right over the institution she wants to survive. ## Hakushi and the successors who depart

The actual origin of apprenticeship is shown four volumes after the former mentor first appears. Hakushi catches Yūki under real conditions and sees an advantage that the young person does not know how to use fully. The supposed naïveté turns out to include a strong aversion to being told what to do; Yūki challenges the legitimacy of the offer when it is first made as a declaration. Hakushi's developed case for teaching is a reason to keep someone alive long enough to want a later choice. She chooses to give the novice that choice after the beginner protests. The target they are later known to share is then absent. Having a way out of the game left open after defeat, having guidance available and wanting the same career make separate changes in the person's life. V05 S16 P0038–0146. The teacher's curriculum mixes pragmatic breadth with keeping important asymmetries intact. Notes permit reviewing rare errors. She avoids reentering until she thinks she can perform, although her pupil worries about losing rhythm. Hakushi describes repeated alterations that kept her able to compete as the damage accumulated. The agent sees her survive Candle Woods. Etsusei later exploits a similarly altered body for a deliberate tactical feint. Hakushi says she taught the modification to neither pupil. The actual life given to an old objective starts from Yūki's response to believing she has lost her teacher at Candle Woods. Even after this is disproved, she takes a goal she now uses seriously. The mentor wants to see it achieved, but says a student may stop and needs to be unconcerned with somebody else's preference. Actual release can be qualified materially. A pupil who knows the teacher’s habits still knows where to look for convincing evidence in Snow Room, and Hakushi will still accept a credible pressure test for a person she thinks may need it. The durable part of the training is a way to respond to opponents and uncertainty where the mentor is absent or mistaken. ## Kyara, Moegi and the danger of a model

Kyara’s mastery of concealment and extreme action makes her an example Moegi has reason both to revere and fear. An earlier understanding of a teacher’s readiness to protect is retested when Airi describes a scene. Moegi makes a good inference about what has happened and about a future effect on her own team's opportunity to clear. Part of the effect of teaching in that scene is reasoning independently, including when the teacher is potentially a danger. She acts toward other people in ways she had no unambiguous order to choose. In the fight with Yūki, fear, sustained resistance, upset and relief at dropping an expected performance do not add up to one consistent feeling about being beaten. The teacher later says what she chose not to do for her pupil. Claims of inattention are present while the learner needed a relation that could matter. Kyara registers that Hakushi too has a student and can be pleased or interested at hearing Yūki adopt the objective. No resulting common ethics between them sits behind that recognition. ## Mishiro and the attempt to extend a result into another person

Mishiro has bodily experience of how someone else's help can recast status in Scrap Building. Her later recruitment of Riko combines relief, fitting of injury and a genuine vocation for the younger woman. It also contains a striking conditional assignment. Riko can defeat the person Mishiro cannot and have that victory count toward her own position. A pupil grateful for real care can want that transmission to be mutual. Riko's actual methods depend on her own observations, assessments and resources after she gets a chance to challenge Yūki. Failure shifts the meaning of a loss across them and does not establish its full price for Mishiro. The story then gives an independent contrast in Mitsuba. A pupil can admire a kind of intense interest, refuse to inherit an animus and test a supposed rival for her own reasons. Mitsuba's understanding of what other disciples were told is evidence of her stored relation to the project, with its own knowledge limits. ## Yūki, Tamamo and Rinrin

Tamamo initially wants both skill and nearness. Yūki believes a second lesson was not intended and is obliged by what she has already agreed to. The actual work in the apartment makes help, enthusiasm and intrusion difficult to distinguish from gestures alone. Yūki takes the work seriously and stresses the consequences of her observations over the future availability of a personal protector. She can teach across kinds of mobility, choose situations and real support appropriate to her pupil’s stamina, and make an independent style a personal consequence of reaching further than her own known mentor. Sometimes this is good advice accompanied by a motive to reduce involvement. Tamamo has given time and aid and does not consent to losing all contact with the same interest in independence. ## A purpose may be transferred with the means left open

The word successor may appear to make everyone part of a single pursuit, especially after Shiro tries to find someone whose tally will give her a route to the organization's control. That analogy illuminates the material part of the profession: bodies can wear out, prestige sometimes transmits through a student, and candidates bring knowledge and resources that another person cannot simply reproduce. The decisive relationships in these earlier cases concern deciding how much authority a teacher or benefit actually gives someone. Hakushi’s stand on apprenticeship survives transferable goals in part because she relinquishes rank as the meaning of a result. Kyara can give another person a way of thinking without choosing for her, while Mishiro actively tries to make another's action extend her own. Tamamo and Yūki show how actual dependence outlasts a decision to sever it. A purely imitative account cannot handle different skills and explicit refusal, while a purely voluntary account overlooks how much earned care and fear can condition acceptance. The comparison earns its own responsibility alongside the monographs because each lineage supplies a counterexample to an apparently sufficient explanation of the others.
