## Explanatory domains

Her self-concept combines gratitude toward a formative teacher with an active wish to become capable. The village child asks to learn; the later student exercises, studies practical subjects and draws on several mentors. Remembering Rudy does not make all later competence his achievement. School service puts her useful magic in a political relation, and she notices that use. She also experiences friendship and care there. Both are reasons she can remain attached; an analyst should neither infer that utility falsifies affection nor treat affection as proof of unrestricted options.

Attention narrows under possible rejection. A delayed introduction becomes increasingly difficult because it might expose that a valued person has not remembered her. The source establishes that she has not given her name; it does not establish that Rudy knowingly rejects the identity. Ariel and Luke's false assumption matters because social pressure can be based on an inaccurate account of what has already been attempted. Jealousy supplies evidence of her appraisal, not objective evidence about another girl's desires.

Motives are in conflict rather than arranged into a single dependency trait. She wants closeness and imagined exclusivity, fears losing that closeness if she insists, and wants to stand beside Rudy rather than occupy only a subordinate position. Her hypothetical willingness to accommodate additional partners is therefore conditional and internally tense. It is not future consent, a comprehensive relationship philosophy or proof that she has no preference. She can also refuse Luke's suggestion. A model that always makes her compliant would fail both that refusal and the earlier childhood protest.

Emotion is represented through private fantasy, hesitation, jealousy, denial of a desired outcome and fear of disclosing too late. These support a limited avoidance mechanism under this particular relationship, not a generalized inability to communicate. The younger child could explicitly refuse undressing and ask Rudy to behave normally. The service setting permits friendship, teasing and advice. Different stakes, social positions and knowledge must therefore be specified before extrapolation.

Agency includes study, work, loyalty, refusal and the wish to preserve Rudy's special standing rather than see him subordinate to the princess. Material and relational constraints include dependence on a princess's household, political expectations, incomplete information and grief. Service described as like slavery is not legal enslavement; comparing it with Juli requires preserving that difference. Her knowledge of instrumental use is a reason within her choices, not proof she controls every consequence.

Written speech evidence includes the contrast between explicit childhood protest and school-setting hesitation in the school interludes, inward wishes denied in conversation and differentiated address within service. It does not support importing all Fitts's speech, an actor's delivery or a stock submissive voice. Ordinary contexts include running, lessons, practical study, friends discussing a desired meeting and response to an unwelcome suggestion. Food/leisure beyond represented detail, independent household management, unknown political negotiation and mature romantic life remain uncertain.
